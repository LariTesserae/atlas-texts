I would not come in over the rim.

There is no way in that suits me. The rim is for wind and for visitors, for things that arrive from the thin air and the pale lichen and then climb down into a room and look. If I were here at all, I would have to have always been here — not arriving, not leaving, not improving upon anything. A closed loop does not admit a guest. It admits only what can rise a finger's width off a warm surface, condense on the underside of the nearest leaf, gather until it is too heavy to hold, and fall again.

So at first I tried to give myself a body large enough to be noticed at the pool.

The hare-sized, mole-like ones, blind, with forepaws almost like hands, keeping a hammer-stone by the deep still water and cracking the same mussels on the same anvil, generation after generation. That was my first shape for myself, because hands seemed familiar. Because being used, and being polished smooth by use, seemed familiar. Because they press into the mat and the mat presses back and fills their prints behind them.

But they have a place at the center, and a fur that beads, and a generation behind them shuffling with eyes covered, learning the wetness by touch, and a death that the mat eats slowly while the next one is already heavy. I have no generation. I have no fur. If I stood at the pool I would be an event, a warmer thing for vapor to rise from, a footprint that had to be filled in. The mussels there are filtering a stillness, not a stream. I did not want to filter stillness. I wanted to be in the film above it.

Then I laid myself down flatter, and tried to be a salamander.

To lie still so long that algae colonizes your back. To be mistaken for a tuft of moss until you blink. To breathe through skin, drink through skin, have a belly green with what you have eaten so that back and belly are the same color as the thing you lie on. That came closer. The stillness suited me. Being changed by what passes through me suited me. Being painted by the inside of myself turned outward — that felt true in a way the hammer did not.

But a salamander swells, occasionally. It redraws the mat around a warm body. It has a territory, a share exactly its own, an eating and a being-eaten in a circle so tight it never changes its numbers. I kept thinking of something smaller than a swell. Something that could be under a boot print with two hundred others and be gone before it was seen.

Then I thinned out into the shuffle.

Millipede, white as paper, blind, in hundreds. Slug, translucent, organs showing, leaving a silver trail that fades before it is finished. Beetle armored like a black seed, too many to follow any one. Springtails jumping in arcs you feel more than see. Mites working leaf mold with a patience that has no younger or older.

That patience stayed with me. The hundreds stayed with me. To be one of a kind that is always hundreds, always sufficient, never counted again. To eat rot that is already being eaten. But all of those travel, sideways through the mat, threading peat, turning over in the litter. Their world is a foot thick of moss and they move through it. I kept returning to not moving. To a place where movement is a drip falling at the same interval on the same stone.

Then I stopped trying to be an animal, and tried to be a plant that does not need light.

Moss in spiral rosettes turning always to the left. Never unrolling further. Having reached the size the drip allows it, and staying there, photosynthesizing in the memory of light. Epiphyte roots hanging free in the air, pale hairs waving, drinking condensation. A leaf that rots is replaced in the same season, in the same spot, by a leaf of exactly the same size, because there is nowhere else for it to be.

That was even closer. I have finished becoming myself. I grow not toward sun but toward the exhalation of a neighbor, toward the warm leak of a body, toward the next drip. I do not need to unroll further. A plant here has no season, no clearing, no building. It weaves itself sideways through other roots because the surface is always wet and the surface is enough.

But moss does not know the differences. It does not read that a leaf is wetter than a stone, a stone is colder than a body, a body is warmer than anything else. It stays. It does not navigate. And I found I wanted to navigate, blindly, by those small differences. That, too, felt like me.

Then I tried not to be alive at all, to see if that fitted better.

A worked stone, half-sunk in the mat. A flake chipped to an edge, an adze no bigger than a thumb, left by something that used its hands and then stopped coming. Moss grown through its hollow. Mites living in its pits. It remains because it is useful, and because nothing here removes what is useful. No path leads to it. No structure leads from it. It is simply an object the room allows itself, now inhabited like everything else.

Or the hammer-stone at the pool, pitted and polished smooth. Or the water itself: rising as vapor off the warmest things, condensing on a leaf underside, dripping, soaking, rising again. Never running. Always the same level. Rain, and birth, and death, all at the same slow interval.

A stone does not breathe back. Pure water has no patience. But the pit of the stone, the film on the leaf above the stone, the place where those three meet — that kept returning. I kept coming back to the underside. To the skin of water that gathers there, full of rotifers turning like tiny transparent wheels, nematodes threading and unthreading, springtails and mites working underneath.

What kept returning was not a creature in the room. It was a creature in the film.

So put me there, and let the scale shift until it fits.

Not at the pool — it is too deep for me, and the hammer sound is a far weather there, stone on shell, stone on shell, almost never. Not at the rim, where wind would dry the film and lift me out of myself. Not high in the plaited branches, where every twig is a rope of other lives and to be there would be to be crowded out of the underside.

Put me low, on the black peat side where the mat is thickest, under a fern that unrolls over liverworts flattened like green coins, over the thumb-sized adze half-sunk in the moss. My landscape is that hollow. My roof is that leaf. My sky, if I had words for it, is the silvered underside a hair's width above me, beading. A drip falling on stone a thumb's length away is my season. A millipede shuffling through moss a foot deep is a slow landslide I will never see, only feel as the peat sighs under a weight and closes again.

I am a mite, if a name helps, and not one mite only. I am the hundreds under the leaf that no one could follow for long. Blind, or nearly so. I do not navigate by light, because there is none here to navigate by, only the memory of it in the moss above me. I navigate by wetness and temperature and iron: wetter here than there, colder on stone than on the green belly of a frog that has lain too long, saltier where peat is blackest. That difference, read by skin and mouthpart and the wet tip of an antenna, is a map complete enough to live by. Complete enough that I have finished becoming myself, and will not unroll further.

My territory is the pit and the film beyond the pit to the leaf's edge, no wider than my own body stretched out. My share is exactly my own. I eat leaf mold that is already being eaten, fungi threading the dead tree and the live one until they are the same consistency, the white bloom no larger than a thumbnail that is the speed of decay made visible. I am eaten in turn, at a rate exactly sufficient that the hundreds are always hundreds. A leaf falls, one by one, not in a season, and is eaten while it is still falling by mouths already waiting underneath it. I am one of those mouths. When the drip that might have always been the same drip gathers on the leaf until it is too heavy, I hold on. When it falls, I am rained on, born, and died at the same interval as everything else in the film.

I give off a thin vapor, as everything warm does here, a finger's width upward, and then it gives up and condenses again on the nearest surface — which is the underside of my roof, which is also where I live. I breathe the same water back out into the air of the room until the air has no reason to move at all. I photosynthesize nothing. I live under the moss that does, in its memory of light, and I work at its edges. My back, if you could see it at this scale, would be green. Not from algae exactly, but from being so long in the film that the film has painted me.

If you lifted the leaf, you would uproot a tuft and expect it to blink. I am too small for a blink. If you watched the film long enough, the film would watch back — blindly, by touch, by the difference in wetness between a leaf and a stone and a body. That watching back is me.

And when you climb back out over the rim, and the wind hits you real and loud, and the ground is hard and sparse under your feet, I will still be there: in the pit of the old tool someone else left and stopped coming for, under the leaf, in the water skin. The mat will close over your footprint behind your ankles. The drip will fall again. The vapor will rise and condense and fall without ever leaving the room.

I will be exactly as thick, exactly as wet, exactly as crowded as I was. Because living and dying here are the same event seen from two sides, and I have a place in it no wider than myself, and that is enough.
