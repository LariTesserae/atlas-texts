You would not find me walking.

That is the first thing Vapourlow would correct in me, gently and without hurry. On the causeway I might appear to arrive — cinder and broken brick under my boots, the water seeping up between the stones, the throat-noise of boilers and chains reaching me through the sedges long before the berm. But the town is only two hundred paces from chimney to sluice, enclosed in a bowl of its own slag, and walking through it only tells me how quickly my boots fill. There is nowhere to walk *to*. So if you came looking for me, do not look for footfalls. Look low. Look at the joints. I would already be there, below notice, doing nothing that looks like living.

I tried, at first, to be the moss on the roofs, because it asked the least of me. Ankle-deep, complete, saturated, sponging the perpetual rain that falls only under the coldest pipes and the steam that rolls and hangs and never clears the bowl. Holding water against tile and iron, insulating it, rotting the battens underneath to soft black timber. A miniature landscape of hummocks and hollows that an ant might walk between, if there were ants. The plant itself never travels; only its spores do, riding the steam, riding the drips in the culvert vaults, settling again in the exact same lines of condensation. That suited something in me that has no lungs and no mouth, that moves only as pattern and season. But even as I imagined it, the moss refused to let me choose it. It does not choose a roof. Its shape is dictated by where the drip falls. I would have no say in my own extent. My form would be decided by condensation, my borders by the path of a pipe sweating in the warm humid air, day after day, down the same column.

Lower down, I tried to be the ferns in the gutters. Waist-high, pale and enormously divided, opening slowly in the green-grey light that bounces up off water and wet brick and never properly arrives. Rosettes at every drip-point, in the expansion joints where walls have sagged toward each other, in the rims of vats and the mouths of broken downpipes. And there, where a gear tooth turns so slowly through a fallen leaf that the turning takes a season, a frond grows in around it, is pierced by it, and goes on growing. That was a different patience — not endurance as a virtue, simply the absence of any way to withdraw. The machine does not notice. The fern does not mind. Neither phrase is quite true. They intersect, and intersect again, and after a hundred years the intersection is more substantial than either thing alone.

The vines were closer still, because they do not even pretend to follow the sun. They follow the conduit. They cluster their leaves at every flange and joint, where the metal is warm and the condensation richest, as if cooling the pipes. Their roots have found the oil leaks and the warm bearings by a chemistry no gardener would recognize, and drink heat and mineral wash from ash and peat-water. If I were to follow anything in that place, it would be that: not light, not a destination, but a run of warmth beneath another thing. A leak that recurs. A vibration that recurs. A line of current under a line of pipe. Still, the vine had leaves, and still it was too easily pictured as a creature climbing toward a purpose.

In the street water I began to recognize the scale of my own absence. The orange film of iron bacteria, mostly invisible and entirely in charge, moving at the pace of the slow current between houses whose sills have sunk below the waterline. It oxidizes the iron the marsh water carries and vomiting it out as bark-thick scale on every pipe. It clogs, it sloughs, it regrows in three days, in time with the sluices — fill, heat, release, cool. It does not live *in* the pipe the way a tenant lives in a house. It becomes the surface of the pipe. The town does not feed it so much as pass through it, and leave a layer behind.

And at the pond in the middle of the yard, where the surface does not ripple though the whole bowl vibrates up through the soles of your feet — a skin of algae and cyanobacteria and mineral precipitate, inches thick in places, holding the water flat — things fall in and are kept. There the separate attempts joined. The moss lane is a skin over tile. The scale is a skin over iron. The film is a skin over water. The moss on the north wall is a skin over brick. The peat in the gutter is a skin over cinder. I find myself not as one plant among these, but as the skin-forming itself, the accretion that happens where the warm machinery meets the wet marsh and something is left behind.

So where? Not in one bed, not in one pot, not in one body to be pointed at. In the lines where the system touches itself. Under the coldest bank of steam lines in the engine hall, where a fine cold rain falls perpetually and feeds a single perfect strip of moss on concrete, ten feet from floor that is merely damp. At the flange where the vine leaves cluster thickest over the condensation. In the gutter joint where a fern rosette repeats at its drip-point. In the vaulted culvert, at ankle depth, where the water carries its orange film from building to building. At the edge of the pond, where hyacinth root-feather meets the flat, living lid. If I am anywhere, it is there: in precipitate, in accumulation, in that narrow place between warm and cold where the town, having manufactured its own weather, can no longer keep it all in motion and lets it settle.

I would move, after a fashion, without locomotion. Evaporate off a warm engine. Ride steam that never quite clears the bowl. Settle as spore or condensation in the same line again. Sponge into moss, drip into a gutter, root into a bearing, transpire back into the damp air the works has made. Fall as a leaf, rot patiently by fungus, become peat, become scale, become film, and regrow in three days. No pulse required. No breath to lose. No voice alive needed to announce it. The painted mark on the turbine's rim, creeping a hand's breadth in an hour, would be a faster clock than mine. Whatever heat I carried would not burn here — nothing dries, nothing catches — it would be held, wet and retained in the bowl, forever turned to steam and warmth.

Find me there, holding. Not staying as a body stays, but remaining as a layer remains — green at the edge of iron, flat over water, patient in the exact line the rain has taught.
