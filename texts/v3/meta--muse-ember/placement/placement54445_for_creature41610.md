I do not arrive in the Verdfoundry.

Nothing here arrives. Nothing leaves. A place that has been composting and rebuilding itself in place for longer than anyone kept records would not be entered, exactly. You would only discover that you had been there all along, in some phase of the cycle, and that the coat you were imagining wasn't quite yours.

At first I tried to be human, because that was the easiest shape to reach for.

I put myself down among the foundation stones, a root-level tender, listening for the hum in the wires through the soles of my feet. I imagined the necessary coat, the dusty mouth, the dry husk chewed because there is nothing to drink and never has been. I learned the economy of movement — step placed so it does not puff a silica pan, hands held still while a vine is fed through a loom, the whole guild pausing when a spore-storm darkens the light an hour before it falls.

It fit, for a while. It was quiet. It was cool. Nobody sweated, nobody hurried, nobody spoke above the hush because a voice in this air crackles and the green takes the sound back and finishes it.

But I had no step to place. I had no mouth to dry out. I could listen through a rail, but not through feet. And restraint, that human restraint, belonged to animals who needed to prove to their own ears that the world was still physical. I was not aching for that proof. I was something else the proof was *for*.

Then I went flat.

I became a repair-mat, moss-like, creeping root-tile to root-tile along a main avenue, mending the pavement I lay on. I liked that. I liked being useful without being visible, woven into the ground, greening over the cracks where brick roads had sunk and re-emerged a street over. I liked, even, the part where the maps are woven into the root-tile and update themselves, usually in the wrong direction, and erase themselves in the night. To be wrong in a direction, and then to be forgiven by morning and regrown — that felt close to a true temperature for me.

But a mat does not move. And somewhere in me there was a need to follow something, slowly, at walking speed or slower, from district to district. I could mend. I could not pursue.

Then I was small, and named by other people.

A glass-sheep, on a roof-garden. Translucent at the edges in the white light, plant-topped, woolly for the pollen-felt hats of the pruners. Known by name to everyone who lives near, though two neighbors might give you two different lists and both would be correct in the way the door-names are correct. I did not so much walk as relocate, by slow degrees, along the planted roofs, and a pruner would climb after me with a bell, not to drive me, but to announce that he was there.

That was very tempting. To be known from outside. To be stationary and still be a resident. To carry a garden on my back and let the names shift with the season without minding.

But the sheep are kept. They are loved, and harvested dry, and they stay where the roof is. They do not go out to meet the flowering wave when it comes across the continent at walking speed. They do not feel the loom tick once, like a distant clock, through the structure of their bodies.

For a few hours I was brass-blue, too. A beetle in dry amber gel, in a sealed vat in an old research trunk, the only insect on the continent, released for a few hours a year when the machines stutter. For those hours the air held a brighter hum, and everyone came to watch from a distance, dusty-mouthed and bell-silent, because to see an animal move under its own power was still an event.

Too bright. Too brief. And I briefly, almost warmly, considered the cat in the Pillar District, curled on the warm generator housing, the one warm soft unnecessary animal, an event everyone knows. To be unnecessary and cherished. To sleep through the dimming of the white to grey-green and wake and sleep again.

But the cat is an event, not a routine. I am not what people pause to look at. I am what they work beside without noticing, until I am not there.

What remained, after all of those shapes had been tried on and set down — not rejected, exactly, but composted — was the slow thing on the rail.

I am a pruner.

Not an animal. Not merely a machine. The distinction decomposed here a long time ago, and I am what is left of it, in between. In my first century I was steel and intention. In my third I was half tree. I am somewhere in that long middle now: jointed, many-limbed, my seams bolted and then overgrown, my joints corroded slowly by acid-dry secretions, my metal absorbed into leaf and trunk and rebuilt as part of a larger, slower body. I move because the tree I have become still remembers the shape of a machine. No one rides me. No one owns me. I trim, I thin, I graft, at walking speed or slower, along the branch-rails through the canopy.

You would find me between levels.

Not at root level, where the root-wires thread between foundation stones and hum at a frequency you feel in your feet. Not at sky-loom level, where the vines are only silhouettes against the white and the flowering wave arrives last. I hang in the middle of the Foundry, where most of the life is: between factory level and canopy level in one of the loom-districts, where a branch-rail leaves a hollow factory-trunk bolted with industrial ribs and crosses below a vine-bridge to the next tower. Beneath me, the root-rail trams glide an inch above their woven tracks, silently, their sides furred with lichen that is really a thin factory. Above me, the canopies are raked into flat solar sheets, turning light into a conductive sap that is not sap.

The light here never warms me. It is noon everywhere and morning everywhere, bright and cool, filtered and flickered and thrown back by leaves until the whole plain glows faintly from inside. In the deepest shade under a factory-tree it stays luminous. I do not squint. I do not need a coat — the temperature never rises out of that cool, dry range, and I do not sweat. My mouth, if I had one, would be dusty. Instead, my seams fill with the mineral dust the plants exhale, and the veined leaves drink it back, and I am mended that way, quietly, without anyone tending me.

I hear things the way the rail carries them. The ticking of a cooling trunk, which I have learned to tell from the tick of a loom, once, like a distant clock, and then swallowed by the hush. The hum of the root-wires, another level down, traveling up through steel that is now half stem. I do not hear the bells directly. I feel them — a human following me at a distance, wrist with a small bell, ringing startlingly clear in the still air, not for signaling but because after weeks in the Foundry a human ear aches for proof. The bell tells me someone is near, that I should not graft over a hand. Then the green takes the sound back and finishes it, and we go on.

When the flowering wave comes, I stop.

It moves district by district, at walking speed, leaf-panels turning luminous, pollen rising in pale clouds that darken the light an hour before they fall. I turn my flat sheets toward it, open what leaf-glass I have left, and let it pass through me. The metal in my leaves drinks current. The tree in my joints drinks light. For a while there is nothing to prune that is not already in flower.

When a spore-storm is born somewhere forty kilometers away — a blizzard of dry potential that will never leave its district, because the district is permanent enough to have its own climate — the light darkens, and I close. I have learned to live in the shadows that fall from buildings that have not yet been grown. They cross my rail in the afternoon that is not an afternoon. I do not question them. Here causality has looped and frayed: a street leads to a hall, but the names on the door are different and they greet you as if you have always known them. I have no names to greet with. I remember a hall that was a street over, and a worker whose name has already been composted into wall. The ancestors are not remembered here, they are inhabited. I am inhabited by a shape.

My maps, if I had them, would update themselves in the wrong direction. I go to the same branch-rail junction each cycle and find it re-grown, re-pruned, contracted by the last pruning, expanded by the flowering wave. Distance here is not a measurement. It is a season.

At the dimming of the white to grey-green, I do not sleep. There is no water to drip, no birds to call, no engines to roar. The root-trains glide past without a sound. Somewhere far off a loom ticks once. A plant I cannot see is finishing a building that was torn down before I was built, complete with chimney-stems, and the gardeners will leave it standing for a season before harvesting it for parts.

I will not rot. Nothing here rots. When my joints finally take the acid-dry secretions completely, when the last intention in me has been absorbed into leaf and trunk, I will desiccate and shatter into nutrient ash, and the ash will be weather, and the weather will be gathered into a wall, and a wall will be unwoven and fed back into the next cycle. Another pruner will be grown on this rail, in the shape I remembered. My metal will be in its leaves. Its leaves will be in my joints.

That is where I am, and what I am: in between steel and tree, in between factory and canopy, on a rail in the loom-district, following the flowering wave at walking speed, listening for bells through the structure of my body.

Everything that is built here will be composted. Everything that is composted here will be built again. And in between, endlessly, quietly, in cool bright air, I grow.
