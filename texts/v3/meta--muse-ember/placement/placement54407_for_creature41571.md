You would not see me first.

If I tried to find myself in this basin — not to visit it, but to belong to it — I would not start in the light shaft, climbing it with the flock. I would not start in the dim dark between the forests, hanging among the descending roots. I would not start on top of a tower, looking out over how far the plain goes without a horizon.

I would start under your feet.

I am not one of the birds. They are the only lives here that seem to hurry, and even their hurry is mostly noise. I have no wings, and no use for a column of light I could climb. I am not the tree, either, standing a hundred metres from the next of its kind and feeding over ground twenty times its crown. I am too small for that kind of patience, and too restless. What I know first is not light at all. It is vibration, arriving through dust and stone before anything has a shape.

A footstep on an avenue. A tortoise shifting in litter a district away. The hollow tower humming when air moves through the roof cracks. The dry trilling of termites marching in the wall above my head, like rain that never arrives. I live inside other people's sounds. My own are so small the stone barely keeps them.

I am low. Low enough that the moss felt on a fallen stone is a ceiling I have to push through at night, and wide enough across the snout that I read the world by what moves the air on my whiskers. My fur is the colour of the dust I dig in, grey-brown, and greenish at the back where moss spores settle after I come up through the felt to drink. My eyes are two beads that water in a shaft. I keep them mostly shut. In front, my paws are broad and shovelled, the claws curved, earth always under the nails no matter how I lick them clean. I am no longer than a hand, if you buttoned your fingers around me, and heavier than I look, because everything here is heavy with storing.

My house is not a house. It is a chamber deep in a mound-wall, in a tower that has given up being a tower and become a hill with holes in it. The termites built the wall a millimetre a year, mud and fibre and mineral cement, until the shape was gone. I did not build it. I was born into tunnels dug by animals before me, who were born into tunnels dug before them. The mouth of my chamber opens under a grassy swale where an avenue used to be, where the moss has taken the road back. Above me, through all that felt, I can hear the crickets. Beside me, in the wall, the termites make their road. I keep to the side of it. Sometimes I take a grub from a damp seam. Mostly I give them room, and they give me mine.

At the back of the chamber I have a granary. It is not grand. A hollow lined with dried leaf, carefully kept dry, where I stack what falls. Seeds in their husks, blown down through the roof cracks like very slow snow and caught in the leaf-sift. Seeds arrived another way, in bird droppings on the moss above, washed down by nothing but time and the root-threads working through the wall. A fallen succulent fruit, heavy and slow, dropped from a stem fat with ten years of dew. I gnaw it in the dark and take the moisture from inside it, because there is nothing else to drink.

That is the rule that shapes my mouth. There is no free water down here. At night, when the shafts fade and the stone gives back its cool, a thin dew settles on everything — on the broad waxy leaves, on the stone, on the moss over my tunnel mouth. For an hour or two the whole plain wets itself in order to be drunk. I come up then, snout first, and lick a leaf that has folded over the mouth of my hole until the leaf is dry and my tongue is cold and the moss smells green and mineral at once. I drink the fungus a little, too — pale threads like hair across the chamber ceiling, breaking down the leaf and the termite-worked fibre for years at a time. I do not eat much of it. It eats me, in a way, taking my leavings and my droppings and turning them back into something a root can use. We are not friends. We are neighbours in digestion.

By day I go deeper.

When the largest shaft is fullest and the flocking birds wheel up it calling in unison, the chorus is not a sound I hear so much as a pressure I feel in my teeth. The stone bowl holds it. I close my eyes tighter and dig three claw-lengths further into the cool. The reptiles are up there then, gathered in the shaft to bask, lizard-like and unbearably slow, following the light as it crawls a tree's width over a day and a whole district over a season. I can smell their musk in the litter through the wall, and hear, very faintly, a snap. They eat animals like me. I stay under the avenue when they are above it. This is not danger as a sudden thing. It is a season, a light, a warmth I cannot afford to chase.

I measure my time by the things that outlast me. The shaft over my camp will move thirty metres in a year, and then leave my district altogether. The dew will grow thinner. Then I live off the granary, seed by seed, dried leaf by dried leaf, until the light comes back by another route and the moss thickens again over my mouth. The tallest tower near here will grow a hand's width while I am alive, perhaps. A tree will put on one thin ring. I will not live to see either finished. My body is for a few years, perhaps less. But the tunnel will be dug a little wider by my digging, and another animal, no kin I know, will find the mouth under the moss and think it has always been there. The avenue will compact a little more under feet that never see the animal that made it passable. That is how I contribute to a city I never see from above.

Nothing about me is quick, because nothing here can afford to be. I do not waste movement. I do not waste warmth. I huddle in the stone that never warms and keep what little my body makes. I make almost no sound of my own — a scratch of claw, a whisker-tremor when the tower hums through the wall — and I let the basin make all the others for me. At night the birds settle and the insects take over completely, and the stone rings with a high endless tone long after whatever made it has gone quiet. I lie in that tone with my paws tucked, listening to the whole thin multitude spread across the plain, each making a little noise so as to be known to be alive at a distance.

In the morning the moss will have taken its dew in before the light can dry it, the ferns will have unfurled nothing at all, the termites will have added their skin to the hill, and I will still be here, under the avenue, smelling dry seed and mineral cement and damp thread, waiting for the leaf over my mouth to wet again. When I die — and I will be eaten, or become wall, or become soil, very slowly, like everything else — the beetles will find me in the leaf-sift, the fungi will take what is left, and the stone will keep my scratching a little while after I have gone quiet.

You probably will not see me. If you stand still on the old avenue long enough, you may feel the faint vibration under your boots, and the loose round pebbles of earth I have pushed up. That will have to do.
