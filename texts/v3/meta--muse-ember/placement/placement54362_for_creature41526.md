I went down, not up.

Not far down. Just past the grass where the marsh stops pretending to be ground, into the shallow sink where water is at the surface, not below it. My boots filled first with black film, then with the sponge. The floor gave, and gave back. A footprint lasted only as long as it took the fibers to close over it, and the water to settle smooth again.

I thought I was visiting. I thought I would walk a little way under the leaves, listen a little while to the noise, and then go out at the far side where the root mat thins and the canopy opens a crack. That was the first thought. It stayed with me for a long while, because everything else in the place seemed designed to correct it.

The light did not help. It was ordinary, green-filtered, moderate. It did not deepen toward evening or brighten toward morning. If I stood in one of the dripping chambers and counted — one drip, two, three — the pattern on the floor was the same when I stopped counting. I waited for the place to change. It did not. It thumped. It hissed. It dripped. It thumped again.

My walking began to repeat itself. Room, then long empty air, then room. A cupped leaf holding its own level pond, overflowing by one measured drip into the cup below. A stalk as thick as a thigh, arching into a ceiling a few meters up. A hollow stem humming with water inside it. Return would be identical. Leave would be identical. There was no mark I could safely make that the place would keep.

So I slowed, as everything here seems to have slowed into its final shape. The mammals padded between rooms and paused and padded on, their prints closed over. The birds stitched the air and were gone to the next chamber. I could not keep either pace. The beetles had their road — the vein, raised like a pipe, wide to them as a highway — and they filed along it in both directions forever, shells clattering faintly through the wax. The termites hauled, the millipedes flowed like dark rivers through channels cut into the plant's own body. To be in this place for good, I understood, you cannot be a passer-through. You have to give up the idea of getting across it.

I stopped under a leaf.

It was an arch, really — one vast leaf on a thigh-thick stalk, flattening overhead and curling a little at the lip to make a room. The light came through it translucent, fixed at that moderate afternoon. Below it, the sponge-floor stretched away a long and empty way to the next room, where another company was singing. Above me, the vein ran like a warm pipe. Below the leaf's lip, a cup held its pond perfectly level, skinmed green with duckweed and seedlings, breaking the black mirror into leaflets. Every few seconds it overflowed, one drip, into the cup below, and that one sounded in here as if something in the ceiling were counting for us all.

I was belly-down to the underside. The leaf was gray-green there, cool and polished and wet. Condensation beaded and ran in slow lines and fell. It fell on my back, and on the hairs of my arms, and off the room into the air that had already been used and used again. My legs were tucked. My eyes were half-closed against the drip. I had not decided to stay. The place had decided it by never offering a reason to leave, and by the time the walking thinned out of me, this was where the walking had brought me.

The noise was still all around me. The piston *thunk... thunk...* from deep in the main stalk, felt through the sponge and through the leaf-stalk joint above my head. The sigh of valves along the undersides. The clatter of beetles somewhere on the other side of the wax, traveling the vein. The faint crackle, now and then, of a spore pod splitting in a stem and dusting the air with light.

And then the boom began. Not outside me. In my throat.

It swelled, metallic and resonant, and receded, and swelled again, timed not to any dusk because there is no dusk to time it to, but to the drip and the thump. A company far off across the empty hall answered. Another answered them. I answered back, a little late, a little slower, and the answer went out through the leaf and came back through the leaf, and I understood that for a long time I had been listening to this chorus as if it were the place speaking. It was. It was also us.

I am one of the frogs glued up here.

I do not build. I do not need to. The leaf builds the room by curling; the stalk builds the tunnel by being hollow; the vein intersection where it meets the stalk collects a film of water just thick enough. I lay my eggs there, in the wet where vein meets stalk, and the young slip down the polished surface by their own weight, down to the cup, to ride the slow overflow from cup to cup. They never reach a river. They never need to find one. The dragonflies stitch above the cup thinking it a lake, and the frogs at its edge sing into it so the green skin trembles, and it all stays at the same depth.

Up through the leaf I can hear the beetles walking their highway, and the termites clacking in the groove beside it, carrying fragments down toward the sponge where the millipedes churn the dead plant back into floor. What falls here is eaten here. What dies here is returned here. Even my own small body, when it is done, will be within days part of the channel, part of the mat, part of the one plant that is the floor and ceiling and roof and road and pond all at once.

I do not age in any way I can track. That is not a boast. It is a condition of the light. As fast as a young one slips to the cup, another egg-film gathers at the joint. As fast as my song recedes, it swells. The plant produces endlessly and grows not at all; fruit hangs ripe in the storerooms of folded leaves and is never quite picked clean; the duckweed on the water covers the surface exactly and never thickens. We live in the exact amount. That is the mild wrongness of it, and its mercy. Nothing is new. Nothing is collapsed. Everything is worn smooth by a million passes of water and foot and wing and belly.

I will not go out at the far side where the canopy cracks. That is for the walkers, the mammals with their closing footprints, the birds flashing between rooms. My distance is not to the edge of the basin. My distance is to the next company singing across the long damp hall, close enough to answer, far enough never to touch. It is enormous, and sufficient.

The light stays on, moderate, green, translucent through the leaf over my head. The drip falls at the same interval. The piston thumps. The valves sigh. The beetles clatter on the other side of the world above me. And under the leaf that is my cliff and my roof and my pond's edge all at once, belly to wax, throat swelling, I sing back —

*thunk... drip... boom...*

— and the place, which has finished changing, changes only in that it answers.
