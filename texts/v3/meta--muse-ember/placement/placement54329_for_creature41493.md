I am low enough that the town is a town.

Not standing over it. Not seeing it whole, the way you might if you crouched and waited for your eyes to adjust to the green dimness. I am under the root plate, where the dark is deepest and amber-brown, where the air is warm in the way earth is warm — held, not heated — and smells of wet soil, mushroom, and green sap. My whole world is the width of a doorway. Everything beyond it arrives only as dampness, as a vibration through mud, as a drip falling a full minute apart into a cupped hollow.

For a long time I did not know what I was. I knew the movement before I knew the mover. A pulse. Antennae tapping, tapping, ahead of me against the mud, and the brief brush of another antennae against mine from behind as we passed. I thought I was watching it. Then I understood I was inside it, and that the tapping was mine.

I do not see. Darkness is not something I escape, it is the medium I live in. I orient by wetness. A wall is not placed here for a view. It is placed for a drip. We broaden the top of a tower, cup it, so condensation from the underside of a root can gather and run down, instead of away. We open shafts on the shaded side and seal them when the air above dries. We keep the nurseries under the root plate, in the coolest, most steadily damp places we have, because moisture is wealth and a dry wall is a dead wall.

After rain, when the mud softens, we work. After a dry spell, we stop, and what we were building hardens and waits, and some outposts at the far drips are emptied, larvae carried back toward the centre mouth to mouth, fungal pellets carried like seed, and the empty towers left to mites and seedlings. That is the pace. Biological pace. Not frantic. Ticking, when it is warm — not footsteps, but mandibles and heads tapped against earth, testing firmness, asking, answering.

I am one of the small ones. Creamy-white, translucent at the joints, blind. The larger, dark-mandibled ones stand in doorways and turn toward vibrations. I do not. I carry.

What I carry is larger than my head, still soft, still shaped by the gut it came from. Chewed earth. Digested plant fibre. Saliva and faeces as mortar. Everything in this town is mouth-work. I arrive at the wall, press the pellet into the older grey layer with my head, turn it, nudge it, taste it with my antennae to know if it will hold, and leave. The wall grows in strata because a life is lived in layers: weathered, fresh, still fingerprint-pocked with headprints. What is finished today is food, floor, or foundation tomorrow. There is no outside for waste to go to, so waste is built with, eaten, farmed on, laid over. I am carrying the town in the most literal way, and the town, in return, is carrying me.

Some days my work is not carrying but holding. In the tallest towers we station ourselves in the ventilation shafts like living plugs, opening and closing with our bodies so the warm air from below — body heat, the slow heat of decomposition under the root plate, the heat held in the low ground itself — breathes out and in. If your hand were close you would feel a faint warm exhalation from a chimney. It stops when a body seals it. Sometimes that body is mine. I am still, antennae laid flat, feeling the town breathe around me, and the town is still because I am.

Other days I am further down, where the pulses slow. The nursery. There the mud must be kept moist with our own bodies. We lick the larvae clean, feed them mouth-to-mouth, turn them in the loam so they do not stick, carry them a few millimetres and set them down again if the chamber floods or dries. I rarely go near the warmest pocket, where she is, where the air is thickest with soil and sap and workers crowd and groom and bring digested food in return. The whole town is oriented toward her warmth without needing to approach it. I know her only as a steadiness in the temperature of the walls, a reason the centre is warmer than the edge.

Between the nursery and the dark granaries my feet keep remaking a road. Roads are memory here. A trail remains usable only because feet keep remaking it; let rain soften it and it reverts to raw mud, and we must learn our own streets again. In the granaries I chew the sponge — the fungal garden growing on stored, chewed fibre — and feed it, and eat the parts it has already digested for me, and carry fresh fibre in on my back. What I cannot digest, the fungus can. What the fungus produces, I can eat. Its threads run out overnight along a line of moisture and by morning have stitched two chambers together, spun through the mud of the walls as rebar, and pantry, and lung. Grazing over the sponge like weather are the mites, almost invisible, cleaning stray spores. I ignore them, or tolerate them, which here amounts to husbandry.

At night the builders mostly stop. That is when the slugs come, slow and silver-trailed, crossing the towers and eating the architecture as they go, erasing a wall a millimetre at a time. I wait in the dark, warmer and steadier than the air above, and in the morning we will plaster over the silver line with a fresh, redder stratum. Springtails gather in the rain-pans where water has pooled to a mirror. Nematodes wriggle in the wet film, turning the damp itself into food. A rove beetle patrols the avenues, turning its head into every blind trial column and hollow we dug and never claimed, and when it passes the pulse thins around it and reforms behind it. A centipede waits motionless in a doorway. A frog sits in a pan for an hour at the edge, holding moisture in one place while the rest of us dry a little more. If a shrew noses at the margin, vibrations travelling through mud faster than through air, we freeze underground, pressed flat against larvae, until the nose withdraws. Then, after a silence that lasts a minute — the time between drips — the ticking resumes.

Nothing is finished. A leaf falls and crushes a district flat, and we tunnel up through the crush mouthful by mouthful and begin again on the new surface. A seedling pushes up through the middle of a tower, and we do not fight it. We layer mud around the green stem and incorporate it as a column. A wall collapses in rain and is not rebuilt, and becomes a flat where springtails graze. A blind shaft becomes a place for mites. A dead worker becomes food for the fungus, which becomes food for the living. I have been carried, and I have carried. I have been food and floor and foundation.

That is what I am, in the end. Not the queen in the warm dark. Not the beetle in the doorway. Not the fungus stitching, though the fungus is in my gut and in my walls and in my food. I am a small pale worker made of the same stuff I lay down — pellet by pellet, tap by tap, layer by layer — part of a digestion that has learned to live in place on raw earth that has not yet decided what it is.

I am still soft. Still being put up.
