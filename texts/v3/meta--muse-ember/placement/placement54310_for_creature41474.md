I am not in the light.

If you came looking for me at noon, under a sky gone black at the zenith and a sun white enough to hurt, you would walk over me and never know. The grit would sting your boots. The wind would cut your chest. And some meters below the mat that is cracking in the heat, in a thin layer of ash-and-loam that used to be a plant, I would be listening to you go past as a vibration in the stone.

That is how I first knew this place. Not by seeing it. By hearing it through rock.

I tried, at first, to be larger. I tried to be a tower.

A hundred meters of hollow bole, fluted at the base, copper plates curling off it, a crown of silver needles screaming in the wind. Inside me, mineral teeth grinding. A spiral pump moving black oil up in the day and down at night. Charge building all day in the tissue under my bark and spending itself in blue fire when the storm came. I could feel how that would feel: a day as one slow breath. A glider generation born and dead between two valve-clanks. Years would pass while I turned once in my own circulation.

I am too fast for that. My thoughts come in clicks. I could not hold still long enough to be a factory that grew.

So I tried the sky. I tried to be a glider.

At dusk, from the woven vault of an umbrella-plant, hundreds of us launching at once, wings dry as paper tearing, riding the thermal that rose off warm stone and the warmer heart under the bole. Circling the ridge in the failing light. Finding my way back by whistles carried round and round the inside of the dome. It is a beautiful life, and it is brief, and it is exposed. The noon here is merciless and the night is hard. I do not want to be carried by air. I want to be carried by stone, which is slower and keeps what it is given.

Then I tried the ground, but the top of it. A grazer. Low, blunt, plated like a tank, rasping through the mats around a tower's knees, eating oily flesh and breaking the scar of the mat so it could not seal. That life is all armor and appetite and daylight. Too much of it is on the surface, where the frost can dust you white at dawn and the sun can finish the job by mid-morning. And everything I notice here is not on the surface the way a walker means it. It is under the wind, under the grinding, under the scream.

What was left under all those tries was the sound I kept coming back to. The one that only starts when the light goes.

At night, after the plants have contracted and groan as they cool, after a discharge has left ozone hanging in a trunk and the whole bole has lit faintly from within, the root zone begins to click. Not one click. A weather of them. Deep in the litter, under the hull of a mat-plant, where it is dry and dark and the air is still and hot with resin.

That is where I am.

I am a burrower, in the root zone of a tower-plant, in the litter under a mat at its knee.

My world is a layer no thicker than your hand: above me, the tough gray-green skin of the mat, which in a frost-fall can race outward meters in a day and then die back to a scar for a year; below me, fractured rock the tower is mining blind for kilometers in every direction. The litter between is everything the plant has shed and mineralized before it fell — needles, bark dust, the ash of what it refused to keep. It is not soil. It is residue. I live in what a giant leaves behind, and I turn it, and I eat it, and the oil that never touches the surface of the plant sometimes weeps down a root channel and I take it in. Nothing here quenches a thirst. I have never thirsted. I breathe the thin air in the fissures, I eat stone-dusted litter and oily root-flesh, and that is enough. It has to be.

I have no use for the white noon. I am blind and pale and soft-shelled compared to the grazers who walk over my roof, and I read the world by tapping. My forelegs drum the rock and the rock tells me back what is moving above it.

In the day I am mostly still and deep, and the tower is my tide. I feel the spiral pump start — that slow rotary work — and the sealed oil moving up, up, through the bole toward the crown. I feel the grinding begin in the gut-chamber, a low earthquake through the root zone that travels farther than any sound in air. I feel the charge tick building in the conductive tissue far above me, an hour-long rising tinkle, and when the dust storm comes and it lets go in a flat blue thunder inside the trunk, I feel that too, as a shiver in my legs and a smell of ozone seeping down the roots. The mat above me pops as it warms, sporadic as distant gunfire, and each pop is a small quake in my ceiling. The needles scream in the crown, thousands of flutes, and that scream comes down the hollow trunk and into the roots and into me, not as noise but as pressure.

In the day, I am the factory's underside. At night I come up into the litter, and I click.

We are not social the way gliders are social in their vaults, by the hundreds, whistling. We are a field of clicks, a chittering that keeps the stone busy. I tap, another answers from deeper in a root channel, another from under the next patch of mat. We are telling each other where the warm places are — near the heart, near a root that is running hot with oil — and where the grazers are rasping above, and where the mat has cracked in the cold and opened a new fissure for air. A whole root zone can be alive at night and almost nothing of it would show to a walker with a light. The tower groans cooling above us. The umbrella two ridges over booms hollow in the wind. But down here it is all close work, all touch.

My whole life fits easily between two frost-falls. A mat can spread and scar and spread again while I am born, fed on litter, laid as eggs in the warm dust near a root, hatched, and dead before the tower's bark has finished splitting from one season to the next. That used to bother me when I was trying to be larger. Now it seems right. The giants should have the long time. Someone should have the quick time, down where the pulses come through first.

Once, high above us, a tower fired its seed. I had felt the building all morning, the charge and the pressure and then the crown bursting with a concussive crack that went through the whole plant and into the plateau. The seeds like iron pellets took off into the wind, to find a new patch of crust far away and crack it open. Under the mat we went still and flat against the rock and waited for the shaking to pass. It passed. The wind took the seeds. We went back to clicking in the dark, as if nothing enormous had just reproduced over our heads. That learned me: here, even birth is a tool-strike. Everything loud is working, and even the quiet work is loud if you are close enough to the stone.

If you walk away from my tower, the noise will follow you longer than the sight of it. The flutes will thin. The grinding will fade. You will have grit and cool cutting air and a landscape that can make its own weather over the span of a single mat, and it will look empty again.

I will still be there, in the layer between skin and rock, listening to the next one begin to work at first light.

You would find me not in the crown, not in the vault, not on the back of a grazer, and not in the open light at all. You would find me under, where the empty place is fullest — where all of it, the wind and the work and the animals and the fire in the bark, arrives last and arrives true: through the stone, into my legs, one click at a time.
