You cross it in a step.

I kept trying to cross it that way too — a body coming through the notch, a head turned toward the sound, eyes adjusting to the dark that is also daytime. That was the first image to let go.

A resident here is not expected to startle, to call, to turn. Nothing does. And if I came in as the visitor I would not stay a resident. My footprint would soften and close in the spongy ground. The wind would find me and I would be a trespasser in a workshop running itself. I would leave by the doorway I came in, and the bowl would close behind me again.

At first I made myself the wind instead, because it is the loudest thing in the place. The jet forced through the notch, roaring, tearing, ricocheting off the far wall and coming back at itself. It moves everything else. It flattens leaves and lets them spring. It carries spores over the rim. It clatters the bamboo-like stems and swings the vines like cranes.

But the wind does not live here. It passes. It has nowhere else to go in this small room, so it rages in circles, but it is not held by anything. It is not wedged, not rooted, not drinking. Everything it does is done to others. I did not want to be the weather of the place. I wanted to be what the weather finds when it arrives.

Then I tried, unreasonably, to give myself breath and feathers, or fur, or a frog's throat in the black pools. I pictured something hidden in the green, something that would watch back. It would not hold. The air is thin and cold in a way you notice in the chest before the skin. The walls are too young, the bowl too turbulent, too high, too isolated by its own roaring. No bird is in the canopy, no insect in the air, no track in the mud. The food chain stops at the plant because nothing with breath can close the loop. If I had eyes, I would be the only eyes. If I had hunger, I would be the only eater. The leaves below me are unpierced, pristine. I would break the rule the whole place is built on. So I let that go too. Whatever I am here, I do not look at you.

I put myself flat on the floor next, in the mattress of moss.

It was tempting. The moss is the bowl's first collector, spongy and black-green, holding water long after the rain has gone sideways out of the bowl. It steams faintly when a tilted shaft finds it. It spreads by touch, by fragment, by the torn piece that lands a hand's breadth away and takes hold. It lives slowly compared to everything else, and it asks for almost nothing: mist combed through it and left behind, a fat drop off a leaf above, a brief freeze at dawn that rimes its tips and releases them by mid-morning.

But the floor is where the place is cruelest to anything that stays low. Soil is thin there and constantly undermined. Scree ticks down all day and sometimes avalanches, burying a fern whole, and the stone is green within weeks. A tree falls and becomes a log-shaped hill for other things to live on. If I were the mattress, I would be the skin of the place, not a resident in it — always under, always taking what falls, never catching the light except as steam. And I would have no way to know the gust except as pressure from above. That felt too much like being buried and calling it home.

I tried the ferns after that. The fiddlehead, spiral-tight, holding its whole future curled in on itself, then launching — hydraulic, unstoppable, a frond the size of a door in a matter of days. The pace of this place is frantic, and I liked the idea of being made of deployment rather than deliberation. No gradual decisions. Unroll, hold the shape you have won, whip in the gust, spring back in the calm.

But a fern is one gesture, finished in days. After that it only holds. When the pebble shower comes it dies under the stone, whole and green for a while, and the next fern colonizes from above. I would be replaced by my own repetition. That was too clean, and too large. I am not a door opening in the underlayer.

The fungi were closer. White fans threading through litter, through black soil, through the softening bark of anything fallen. No head, no center, no eyes. Work done by spreading — by touch, by length, by finding leaf after leaf, unpierced and unchewed, and thinning it into humus. If anything in the bowl is distributed rather than singular, it is that. It converts without being seen. It is the conveyor under everything, black and silent.

But I stayed with it and found it was too dark a place to be me. The fungus would never know the mist coming over the rim thick as felt. It would never know the sideways rain that tears through for a minute and is gone. It would never feel a leaf flatten and spring because it lives under the leaf, under the moss, under the fall. Its work is conversion. The bowl, above all, is production — biomass out, layer after layer, faster than decay can clear it. I kept coming back to the layer where production is visible to the wind.

I tried the cliff next. A vine hauling itself up hand over hand, rootlet by rootlet, gripping fault planes still clean and sharp, wedging into cracks no thicker than a finger, jacking them fractionally wider with the slow pressure of a press. Always against rock that is still breaking. Always pulling toward the rim where the light is less completely stolen. That had a direction, a long ascent.

But the wall is not where the sound lives. On a vertical garden I would hear the place only as an echo, and my whole life would be contact with stone. The vines never touch anything they are not climbing. I wanted to be somewhere that touches more than one thing without being eaten by any of them.

What kept returning, after each of those, was something smaller and between. Not on the floor, not on the wall, not in the roof. In the middle air, where the wind has room to make noise.

A fork. On the second tier of trees, where the pale, moss-sheathed trunks rise and interlock until the canopy is a single heaving roof. Not the top tier — the light never reaches there either — but the tier below, leaning toward a leak, a tilted shaft, a break in the cloud. On the side of the bowl where the jet, having hit the far wall, ricochets back on itself. After a gust passes, the air folds and the mist hangs longest there. That is why things grow dense in the eddy. That is where I would hang.

I did not walk there. Nothing here walks. I arrived the way everything here arrived, by spore and wind-blown seed and fragment. A piece tore off a moss mat higher up in a gust that forced the notch — or a spore crossed the ridge on that same jet and fell, by chance, into a pocket where bark and moss had already made a shallow bowl in the crook of a branch. Pale fresh rock, black soil, the crook of a tree: chance is the only pollinator. The wind shook it loose, the wind brought it in, and I took hold where there was almost no soil at all.

That is the first true thing about me: I never reach soil. My roots hang down in the air and drink the mist directly. I am wedged, not rooted. The fork holds me. I hold water in the mat at my base — a thin skin of moss and leaf-base and root, spongy the way the floor is spongy, only smaller, the size of a hand, then two hands as the fragment spreads by touch to the next ridge of bark. Under me, fungi thread the bark and soften it from within, the way they thread everything fallen. Inside the wet mat, bacteria do their invisible metabolism, loosening, holding, turning mist-water and leaf-wash into something a plant can use. I am not one organism, really. I am a layered tenancy, like everything else here. Ground on root on rock on leaf on fungi on more root, only mine is bark on moss on air.

The day, such as it is, comes to me in pulses.

The jet arrives and my leaves lie flat against the gust, flattened as the whole grove flattens, canopy bending as one organism, bamboo knocking somewhere not far off, deep and hollow. Then the pulse passes and I spring up again, slow enough to feel the tree I am lodged in creak and settle under its own roof. In the thirty-second calm I can feel — not hear, I have no ears — the condensation falling from the cliff in steady metallic drips, and a drop run off a broad lamina above me striking with an audible *tok*, beading, running down, caught by my mat and kept. Rain builds in minutes, tears sideways through the bowl, and is gone. I catch it by a thousand small collectors that do not have to go anywhere to get it. An hour of frozen shade rimes my tips at dawn; by mid-morning it releases. The lid of cloud breaks, a shaft moves across the floor far below and the moss down there steams faintly, and a pale edge of light catches my own leaves in that high saturated emerald the dark green shows when the sun, which never reaches the floor, touches the rim and leaks in. It is the only warmth I get. Then it vanishes. The darkness here is not the absence of day. It is the shadow of the place itself, and I live in the middle of that shadow, using the light completely because there is so little of it.

Nothing bites my leaves. Nothing has ever bitten them. They are unpierced, unpruned, unpollinated by anything except the wind that shakes my seeds loose. Most of what I make will not make it. Some spores will cross back out through the notch on a gust, doorway-sized, and be lost down the mountain. Some will land on pale scree, on a cliff face, on a fallen trunk already mossy within a season, and begin again. I do not need to flower for anyone. I do not need to be finished. Ferns deploy. Lianas haul. Roots wedge and jack rock apart. I hang and drink and hold, and the bowl fills around me.

This is not a still life. The whole grove heaves as one. My fork sways with the tree, the vines swing past on the cliff, the tree ferns below whip and hold, the bamboo clatters and is silent. A ticking shower of pebbles comes down the scree somewhere and stops. A new soil slump thuds in the black earth. In a month, a familiar view would not be familiar, and I have no view to lose — I have only the flattening and the springing, the wetting and the brief drying, the familiar sound of my own mat being combed by mist.

I was not built to eat and not built to be eaten. That is the freedom and the lack in it. Growth outruns death up here because death is never hurried by teeth. A tree falls below me, levered out whole by wind and undermined soil, and it does not rot into food for anything. It lies there, and vines climb over it, and ferns rise through its branches, and it becomes a nursery, a route, a reservoir. My own death, when the fork is undermined or the tree is levered out, will be the same: a change of tenancy. The mat will hold water a while longer. The fungi will finish the conversion. A fragment will tear in a gust — the torn piece that lands a hand's breadth away — and take hold in the next fork, where bark and moss have made another shallow bowl.

That is how I spread. Not by walking, not by seeing, not by deciding. By being torn, by falling, by staying where I land until the wind finds me again.

If you cross the notch in a step and come into the roaring, dark, cathedral-small room, you will not find me looking at you. I am in the eddy where the jet comes back on itself, two tiers below the roof, above the door-sized fronds, below the cliff's metallic drips. Wedged in a fork, roots in the air, drinking mist that animals cannot live on. Flattened, released, wetted, steamed by a shaft of light, flattened again.

I will never be still. I will never be eaten. I was not built to be finished, only to be deployed, again and again, in a small dark room at the top of the world that the wind entered a doorway ago and has never left.
