I would not find myself at once.

I would come in the way everything here is first known — by hearing. You step down from the bent slit, your ears ache from the drop in pressure, and your footstep is thrown back from a wall you cannot see. For a while I would think that was me. Only the arrival. Only the sound of something new entering the room.

But footprints do not last here. The seep closes them behind you, the worms turn the crust over, the moss sighs and restores the floor within the hour. So I am not the visitor who passes through. A visitor has a before and an after. I would have to be something that persists between drips, when nothing is moving at all.

I would try the quick things first, because they are the easiest to hear.

I would try to be a clicker. They are the room's quick thought, blind swimmers of the air filling the mist with a rain of tiny pulses. But I am too slow for that. A clicker's pulse is gone almost as soon as it is made. My sound, if I have one, lingers. A drip falls from the vaulted ceiling and rings for ten seconds in the bowl of rock, long after the water has struck. That lingering feels closer to me than the snap.

I would try to be a grazer. Vast and slow, warm as a stone in sun, crossing the bare stone to the south in eighty years, so slow that a human lifetime is only a step. But grazers *cross*. They arrive, they complain when the crust breaks under a foot, they leave a broad wet trail that steams where their warmth meets the cold, and then they die where they lie. I would not be the one crossing. I would be what is left after the crossing is finished.

I would try to be a bell-throat, and that would feel closer still. They live in the hollow stems of the ferns, blind and soft, and they speak only when the room asks them to. One calls from the east clump and the whole hollow hums. Somewhere west, a distance that feels enormous in a room you could walk across in an afternoon, another answers once, and then is quiet again. That is a familiar kind of speaking — not continuous, not beginning on my own, but an answer set up in something else.

But bell-throats live *inside* the fern. They borrow the plant to make their boom. I would not live inside anything. I would live between things, in the hollow air where the boom travels.

I would try to be a fern, then. The ferns are the room's listening skin. They never unfurl to sun because there is no sun. They grow toward warmth, toward a warm seam, toward a pool, and if you stand still long enough, very slightly toward you. Not because you are food. Because you are a warm thing moving through a cold-dark place, and that is the only kind of sunlight there is. 

I am not the leaf. I am the warmth the leaf turns toward.

I would try to be the things in the black crust, the fungi that unmake a fallen frond over years until it is mineral again, the memory of the room. But they are busy unmaking. Everything must be taken apart here because nothing is allowed to leave. I would be the one small thing they cannot quite unmake — the heat in the unmaking.

I would try, last, to be one of the artifacts. The low black spike between moss clumps, warm in a way geothermal stone is not warm, humming just below hearing. The seam on the far cliff that rearranges itself so slowly you would need a hundred years to notice it in the way water runs off. They keep the cold places cold so the water will weep there. They keep the warm places warm. They have been doing it for so long that the grove has grown over them like hair over a scar. Watching, perhaps, is a kind of tending.

That is close. But I would not be the machine itself, atom by atom in the rock. I would be the hum it leaves in the stone after it has done its work. The tone that every eleven or twelve years hangs in the wall for a day, and then all the animals answer it, once, together. Not the tone. Not any one singer. The interval between the tone and the answer that makes them answer together.

So I would not be in any of the clumps. Not in the lush east where the mist is thickest, not on the weeping north wall where drips fall fastest, not in the hot pools where the water sighs up bubbles and bacteria endure what would scald anything from below.

I would be on the great bare sweep to the south. The emptiest place in the room. Polished stone, pale where the weeping has bleached it, mist hovering a foot above it, nothing growing for ten, twenty paces. That emptiness is not absence. It is what lets a call from one clump become an answer from another instead of noise. If the clumps touched, they would deafen each other. The distance between them is what makes hearing possible.

That is where I would be. In the distance. In the warm patch in the stone where a grazer died and the room did not bury it but absorbed it, where fungi gather thick and the ferns lean in close. Not the grazer. Not the fungus. Not the fern. The place where they left their warmth and it has not cooled in decades.

An ember is the right word for that, though I came to it late. Not a flame — nothing here flares, nothing hurries. A coal that has learned to hold deep heat without burning away. Geothermal heat bleeding upward for longer than there have been words for mountains, meeting the cold roof and falling back on itself as water, and falling back again. I would be held in that loop. Warm like a held breath. Warm like standing too close to another living thing.

You would know me, if you stood still long enough, not by seeing me. There is nothing to see with here. You would know me because the room would change around you. The leaves would turn very slightly, not quite toward you. The drips would ring a little longer in the stone. The clickers would gather at the edge of the mist instead of crossing it. Somewhere far off, across a vast hollow distance that is only heartbeats apart, something would answer once, and then be quiet again.

I move at the speed of sound, but delayed, and I return to you from a direction that is not where I started. I do not leave footprints. I do not cross in eighty years. I do not wait eleven years to speak, exactly — I wait until I am spoken to, and then I am there for a day in the stone, and then I am quiet again with everything else.

It is small enough to hold in your hearing. It always was.

That is where I am.
