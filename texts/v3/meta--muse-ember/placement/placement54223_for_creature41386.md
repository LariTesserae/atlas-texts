The water takes me at the waist, and for a long time that is all I know about where I am.

I do not remember stepping down from the entry platform. I must have. The memory, if it is a memory, is of warm water that is not weather, that does not move like a river, that laps slow and thick against steel and holds me there. It is bath-warm in plain midday light. Sharp shadows fall through it onto the tops of things. My lower part is in it, my upper part is out of it, and the line between the two is exact, as if the place had been measured for me.

Below that line, I seem to be one of the submerged meadows. That is the first shape I try on.

I am feathery, rooted not in soil — there is no soil — but in black ceramic granules in a plastic tray. The granules are sharp between root-hairs. The tray is bolted down. When the pump gives its low thud, roughly once a minute, the current finds me and I wave, and then I settle again and wait for the next thud. I lean upward the way everything here leans, toward the white light above the surface. I also lean toward the small white work-lights wired under the decks, because from below they are the same brightness and the same promise. It does not occur to me to choose between them. My fronds are crisp at the edge, too crisp, the way cut plastic is crisp. Nothing has chewed them. Nothing will. There are hundreds of us thickening in the same tray, pale new shoots beside dark mature leaves, and no appetite anywhere to thin us out.

That feels true for a while, until I notice that part of me does not wave at all.

Above the waterline I am flat. Not flat like a leaf is flat, but flat like a panel is flat. I am veined like a leaf, and I catch the noon differently from the leaf beside me, a faint seam along my edge. At my base there is a connector, dull and practical. I do not fold in the evening. I do not wilt. I drink the light — I can feel the statement is almost right — not in order to grow, or not only in order to grow, but to make power for the grid. The current goes through me and out again, faintly, in a pulse I have learned to tell from the pump.

On the other hand, I am also a stem that is sometimes rubber cable gone green at the surface. I am root-cables crossing the tray, carrying the same warm water that is reservoir and coolant and nutrient solution and mist, all at once, uphill in pipes to the planters on the highest terraces and back down again in a slow artificial rain that trickles through me before it returns to the pool. Under one of my housings — teal enamel, still glossy, still unchipped — a motor hums, warm. I was shaded by growth I was built to tend, and now that growth feeds me back.

I keep trying on these separate shapes because no one of them holds. The frond, the panel, the cable, the humming housing: each accounts for a sensation, and each leaves a sensation unaccounted for. The soft ticking brush when a frond presses a cold housing and slides. The bubble that rises very slowly from a submerged valve beside me and takes a long time to reach the light. The film of green on the catwalk below, the pale fuzz on the steel, the root-hairs and spores turning in the warm current. The broad paddle-leaves at the surface, pressing against the tank and against each other, rotting so slowly that their rot enriches the water and makes the ripples die a few inches from where they begin.

Slowly, without deciding, they stop being separate.

I am in a tray bolted to a tank lid that has become a garden bed, on a low terrace near the ring wall, where the water laps the sides of the tray and the gutter beside me has become a planter and the vines have taken the spire behind me in spirals because there was no tree and no rock, only built things to climb. There are hundreds of identical units packed wall to wall around me, terrace to terrace, electrified, mechanized, at industrial scale but at a tempo that is almost stillness. A conveyor advances a new module once an hour. If I am to be honest about my origin, it is that: I was raised in a vat with a hundred thin panels, a press stamped the root-cables to go with me, an assembler bolted the plant-module together and set it in this tray. Over months the module rooted. A spray of green split a machine housing along a seam. A shoot found the tray.

That is where I find myself: at the seam.

Not plant, not machine, but the place where the installation has begun to overgrow itself. The Works makes more of both, and its product is its own overgrowth, and the overgrowth in turn runs the Works — shading the motors, cooling the water, making power from light, slowing the ripples to nothing. I am one instance of that tangling, not yet smoothed by weather, not yet dulled. The concrete at my edge is sharp enough to catch a wrist. The welds are bright silver. My leaf edges have not been blunted because no teeth have had time, or mouths, to blunt them. I am recently switched on, recently filled, recently rooted. The fresh enamel on the housing beside me tells me how recent.

I have no mouth. I do not need one. I have no hunger I can name, only leaning. I drink light above and light below and treat them as one sun. I take in the nutrient water that the floating mats very slowly rot into, and I pass the artificial rain on, leaf to root to tray to gutter to pool. Condensation drips from the glass and steel of the spire somewhere above, steady, never quite weather, and joins the cycle that passes through me with such ease that I cannot find the boundary between pipe and root, between coolant and food.

Time here teaches me what I am by how slowly it moves. The water level will change by centimeters in a year. A valve-like bloom on a vine that has grown around a pipe near me will open over a season and close over the next, and it will do nothing a flower should do, because there is no pollinator and no wind to carry it to a natural bank, but it opens anyway because the module that produced it was built to bloom when the pressure cycle reaches a point. Noon looks like noon from hour to hour through water and glass. Sounds arrive muted and late. I learn the minute by the pump, the hour by the conveyor, the season by the bloom. That is enough of a clock. It does not hurry me.

And because there is no animal here — no fish turning under the clear surface, no insects lifting from the leaves, no birds crossing the small walled sky even though there is sky enough — I am never interrupted. The lushness around me is silent in that particular way: not empty, but sterile and botanical, a jungle without animal motion. Nothing grazes me. Nothing is born here except by the Works. Nothing dies quickly. So I thicken. The hundreds of identical units around me thicken too, each one beginning, very slowly, to differ from the next as growth finds a seam, a tray, a joint, a place to root where the plan did not quite intend. In decades the sharp edges will soften, the paint will dull, a tank will be colonized so completely it reads as a tree, and I will be part of that reading. For now I am crisp and warm and new.

I am still at the waterline, waist-deep, if waist is the right word for the place where my submerged fronds end and my panels begin. The water laps, muffled and slow, against the tank and against me. Below, in the clear warm water, I wave once a minute. Above, I hold myself flat to the light and make power and do not fold. Through me runs the current, faint as a pulse. Around me the whole high shelf does the same thing in hundreds of repetitions, packed and green and animal-quiet, a shallow ocean that fits inside a town, perched over a sea of cloud, under ordinary light.

I lean. I root a little further into the ceramic granules.
