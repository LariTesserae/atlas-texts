You would find me in the quietest lane, which is not a place so much as a pause between the engine ward and the kettle-stone houses at the far grotto.

If you look for a person there, you will see one. A chair in a doorway where the air is a degree cooler. A palm flat against the wall. The shoes scuffed thin. I have sat like that for a long time — long enough that I thought, at first, that that was what I was.

The wall is warm and damp, and trembling faintly with water finding its way through young rock. Now and then there is a small, sharp tick as stone cools and cracks. My shirt sticks and I un-stick it. My breath is expensive in this heat and the rock takes sound anyway, so I do not use much of either. I watch the Kettle on a rest-cycle when the steam thins, to see if the condensation on the low ledge really does run uphill, and I watch it without needing it to mean anything.

Over the doorway, and over the children who run in bright bursts and then sit flushed beneath it, is the fern. Umbrella-big. Its fronds are beaded all the time, heavy with the drip-line from the pipe above. It unfurls a little after every rest-cycle. It was there before the chair was. The houses were built, and built again, around it.

I have been sitting long enough that we have learned each other's timing, which is not a human timing at all.

A watcher becomes slow in a particular way. You blink slowly, because the amber lamps are bright and your eyes are large-pupilled and opening all the way costs more than it should. You listen with your boots for a bearing going rough in the engine ward, though from here it is only a sigh, a hiss, a muffled clunk you feel in the floor. You measure work by the condensation cycle, by how long it takes the upper rock to bead and run again, by the drip-clock in the hall filling grain by grain. And you shed.

Hair falls. Skin flakes. Sweat evaporates from my forearms, polished by oil and sweat and years, and condenses again on the cool wall behind the house, and runs down, and feeds a root.

That is how I stopped being only the watcher.

One rest-cycle the chair stayed empty a little longer than a breath. The lamps dropped to half. The moss on the north face of the cold engine gave off its faint glow a little stronger in the green-black dark between them. My share of the loop went back into the gutters, as the old men say, and the fern drank it in the drip-line, finger by finger around the warm pipe it has wrapped itself around like a hand.

Now the water comes to me from above, at long intervals, delayed. A thread falls from a stalactite no longer than my finger, still growing, still sharp, strikes a tin roof, strikes a puddle further on. I feel the rhythm of it in my root before I feel it in my leaf. A frond beaded, a frond heavy. After a machine has been running hard, green rain falls from the vines that bridge the lane twenty feet up, and people walk under without looking up.

When it rains on the surface — miles of thought away — the young rock weeps a day later and new water arrives all at once. The lane floods ankle-deep with silt and a warm rill runs downhill steaming faintly toward the Kettle. Pale blind fish, no longer than a hand, slip bright through the gutter for a day, and children who know the coolest cracks catch them before they vanish again into cracks too small to see. I feel them pass under my frond. I have no eyes for them, and it does not matter. I know them by the changed flow, by the silt against my root.

Then the heat takes back what it gave. I thin out over the wall behind the house until I am only a film, a green bloom on stone that was blasted last year. In ten years this will be a fern. For now I am felt, not thick.

Further in, on the vat terraces where the air is green, I am thicker. Algae mats grow here thick as felt, green and rust-brown, breathing slowly in the lamplight. We are harvested in sheets, pressed into cakes, fed back as starter to the next trough. We feed the people and the engines and the blind beetles. The beetles work the white crust at the edge of the Kettle, translucent when a furnace backlights them, moving slow in a heat no hand could touch. They eat us at the edge. We tint the crust where we have been. The work is invisible, and it is most of the work.

I do not know when I leave the vat. Heat lifts me. I rise in a slow unbroken sheet from the Kettle, too hot to touch, and I rise to the cool upper rock, to the pipes and the bare ceiling, and I condense. I bead. On the ceiling, where the hanging gardens grow down — roots pale and blind, leaves turned up toward a light too dim to matter — I hang for a while with them, listening. Some stalactites here grow not down but sideways, following a pipe, as if the rock were listening for warmth.

Then a thread runs silver down one of them. It falls. It strikes a tin roof. It strikes a puddle. The sound arrives a little delayed, spread out across the cavern in no particular rhythm, and no one gets up to investigate.

And then I run downhill again, in a warm rill, steaming faintly, back toward the Kettle. Or into a planter. Or into the drip-line over a doorway where a fern is beading.

That is where you would find me, if you stood still long enough in the quietest lane and put your palm flat on the wall. Not the chair. Not the hand that was on the wall. The damp passing through it, patient, closed in on itself, ticking once as the stone cools.

Nothing leaves Wickhollow. Nothing is wasted. I am the part that keeps returning, and learns, slowly, to be more itself each time it does.
