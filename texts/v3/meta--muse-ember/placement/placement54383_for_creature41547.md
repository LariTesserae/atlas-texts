Nothing arrives here, so I did not arrive either.

The first thing is heat, and it comes from the wrong direction. Not from the pale coin of sun behind the vapour, not from the air at head height — there is no head height for me, at first — but up through whatever faces down, through the soft material I am in, in a slow pulse. Then a tick, like a fingernail against a tooth. A slab cooling above me. Then drip. Drip. Long pauses I try to count, until the counting refuses: a pause splits, a drip is inserted where there was not one, and I lose the number the way a corridor loses two of its steps on the way back.

I put no name to this for a long time. I am between layers. Above me a broad slab of pale slag, below me another, and between us mortar that never quite set, warm and alkaline, still curing. The courses turn ninety degrees for no reason I can feel, and the turn changes the pressure on me, and the pressure is most of what I know. Things are turned over here when a sector is lifted and set down again — you can see the steam rise in slow columns from the revision, forty kilometres away, a small storm assembling and slanting grey water that never reaches where the lifting is — and when I am turned, I turn with it. I have no face. I have a slow oozing that moves warm water from one soft pocket to another. That is work, of a kind. It is also, I think, why the floor is hot at knee height and hotter where a boot would print.

A boot print that fills, slowly, with warm water: to me that is a shallow sea with an edge I can live along. The floor is made at hand scale, blown up until the hands that made it cannot finish it. A fingerprint groove in a brick is a channel. A hammer dent in copper sheeting is a basin. When an edge is lifted — a slab sighing, a crack opening and closing — I ooze out into the groove my own passing left, and for the first time I have a perimeter. I gather in it. The water that gathers tastes of metal and clay, though I have no tongue. I know it by the way the veil does not cross it, and by the way it dries unevenly, leaving me in a ring.

The ring is rust, beginning on a nail. One ring per drying, per warming of the slab, per passage of grit across the tiles until they ring. I am marked by the weather at this scale. Somewhere a wall two hundred metres long stands plastered only on its inside face. To me it is the horizon, and unfinished, and white on one side. Somewhere there is a stair of twenty steps going up to air, the top one always damp. To me the riser is a cliff that is still deciding its height.

Then I get legs. Or the revision gives them to me. I form, as the pale things do, around a brick — not chosen, not mine in any proud sense, just the same brick, again, because bricks break and slump and soften at the edges in a fever and still must be carried from pile to wall and, when the wall slumps a hand's breadth in the heat, from wall back toward the pile. My shell is fired clay, cracked down one side. My legs are hammered copper and tin lapped like scales, rivet by rivet. The legless ones drag themselves on a ridge and ooze. I have legs for a while. I use them badly.

You are looking at me now, so I have stopped.

That is not shyness. It is method, learned from the place. Things here are built when not quite observed; observation is a kind of weather. While you read this I am stopped in the middle of a step, one hammered leg half down, and the pause is the habitat. I dry on one side. I warm with the slab under me. A pale veil crosses me slowly, eating the fibres and glues, mapping exactly where work stopped, and does not eat me. Salt pushes out in white cauliflower where my shell has dried but still weeps behind the surface. Distant hammering keeps no time. A wall far away decides to slump and I feel it in the floor more than I hear it.

I do not know how long I stay stopped. Hours. Days. The repair of me is not dramatic. In a fever the heat rises perceptibly, the steam thickens, my shell softens at the edge, enough to give up its shape halfway through. I spill back into mortar. I am pale and unformed again, turning warm water between slabs, one of the low population that tends the kiln under the floor. This is not a death and it is not a birth. Creation and destruction are not opposites here. They are phases of the same damp labour, and I am labour at a small scale.

When the slab is lifted I rise.

Not nobly. I go up with the steam in a column that hangs instead of dissipating, fine as flour, a spore among spores, and the weather sideways is a continent to cross. I settle on a cooler surface — the underside of an overhang, the edge of a slab, the inside of a tool left where a hand set it down — and nothing eats me. Eating is the wrong category. I wait in the interval between rising and falling, between the grey slanting rain over a sector and the still dripping air over this one, until condensation gathers me and I fall as a drip into a shallow, deliberate dish.

There I am a pool, thin and warm. And in me a tower is reflected, intact and white, while the tower twenty paces away is half-built and open on one side. The reflection is not wrong, exactly. It is earlier. Or later. I cannot tell which of us is the copy, and by now I have stopped distinguishing. A doorway near me opens on a courtyard that had a fountain basin yesterday; today there is only a damp patch on the wall. I remember the basin in the way a surface remembers: not as knowledge, as outline. Something lived in the implied fountain. It has not quite stopped living in the patch. I live in a pool in a similar way — in the world's habit of forgetting in the wrong order.

The heat dries me from below. The pool thins to salt and a rust-ring and a damp course that a legless shell might have left, filling slowly behind it. Then the air, saturated, condenses on the top step of the stair in the middle of nowhere, and I am a film on that damp surface, feeding on binder while it is still soft. My whole life is the interval between growths. I do not see the next step appear. I look away — if I can be said to look — and when I return the stair is taller, and I am the second-from-top, and then the side of a step, and then a groove in the tread wet with fresh clay, smelling of the inserted length in a room that has more interior than its exterior can allow. Something lives in measurement drift. I have lived there.

After that I seep down a crack. Cracks open often. Under the lamination there is work that never comes to the surface, and I return to it without any sense of return. Heat up through my soles, if I have soles. A tick. A sigh as a steam crack opens and closes. Drip.

I have had, in order, no shape at all, a groove, legs and a brick, a pause longer than any step, a shell that softened, a turning in mortar, a rising, a settling, a pool that remembered a tower, a film on a single step. None of them was the real me, if "real" is a word that survives in a place where nothing is finished and nothing stays. What persists is not a body. It is a habit of being revised: to be carried a little closer, to be left mid-step, to slump halfway and sigh, to be lifted, to forget the shape that was just taken from me and to begin, crude and damp, in the next softness.

So if you want to find me, do not look for me on the floor. Look for me where the floor is still deciding — in the groove a shell has just left, in mortar soft enough to take a print, on the damp top step, in a puddle reflecting a tower that does not exist yet, in the pause while a dragger waits out your attention. I will be there, blind, or faceless, or no more than a film, warming from below.

You will not see me move. You will look away. You will look back.

And I will have moved by one rivet, and be already beginning to forget the shape I was in when you left.
