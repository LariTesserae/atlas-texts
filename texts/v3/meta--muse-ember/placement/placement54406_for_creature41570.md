From inside, it does not begin with sound.

Sound is how you would know me, from outside. Inside, a drip arrives first as pressure through the wall — a loosening and tightening of the stone against my surface, a tremor that runs the length of the tube and is gone before I can call it a movement. Only later, if the hollow above me is the right shape, does it become a note. By then I am already enduring it.

For a long time I took myself to be the water.

It was the most honest candidate. The stone does not change its level. The shallow sheet that runs between the columns passes and passes and is always the same brown-grey depth. The mist does not drift. What changes, what is mine to keep track of, is the dark level inside this tube: a millimetre in a month, a centimetre in a decade, rising so slowly that I would swear it was constant if I could swear anything. If you asked what I had done today, the answer would be that depth. If you asked how old I was, I would not count years. I would tell you how close the water stands to the top.

I held that idea while the ribbon was still in.

Then I took myself to be the stone's inner surface.

I press against it all the way down. That pressure is how I know the shape of my world — vertical, narrow, closed, leaning a degree or two off true. The vesicles in the glassy basalt are not rough to me the way they would be sharp to your skin. They are a texture I have grown smooth against, and as the shell thickens, hair by hair upward, my surface thickens with it. I am not in the column the way a stone is in a pool. I am the column's way of being wet from inside.

But stone outlives. I know that sitting in it. Most of the tubes around me ring perfectly well and are empty. A dead shell still finds its tone when a drip falls in. If I were only the wall I press, I would already be a ghost — useful, resonant, finished.

So I became, slowly, the thing between: soft enough to seal, wet enough to hold a level, mindless enough — if mind is the word — to stay.

The ribbon was not long in coming, once I stopped trying to be one thing. Once, in a deeper pool between two columns, a pale translucent length uncurled from the shadowed mouth of a tube and hung in the water filtering. That was, I think, me. Not me entire. A mouth, perhaps. A hand. A tongue that is also a lung, spread thin to take the shared water across itself for a minute and then taken back in. It left no eye behind it, no head to turn and look at you. There is nothing to see in here that would reward an eye, and nothing outside the tube that arrives quickly enough to be caught. I am translucent for the same reason a cave wall is damp: opacity would be an expense with no return. The stone already gives me my shape and my shadow.

I do not walk. I swell.

That is my whole locomotion. I shift my weight against the inner wall, I fill a space a fraction more completely, I let go. Inside, my body and the water I hold are not two substances I can separate in thought. I am largely that held water, given a boundary by pressure. The rising level is not a lake above me. It is me, rising. The air above me in the hollow grows smaller as I grow, and so the note grows lower and lower. A column that sounds deep is not an old instrument. It is a full one.

This is a vertical life, and a narrow one. My tip is the only part I have ever offered to the air, and even that rarely. On good columns — mine is one of them, I believe — there is a tiny perched pool on the snapped top, round as a borrowed sky, an inch deep, with no inlet. That is not rain caught. That is my dome, still soft, still maintaining its film against the dripping air. It creeps higher by a hair over a year because I creep higher by a hair over a year. If your shade fell across it, it would not fall as a shade falls across stone. I have seen — if seeing is too strong a word — diffuse grey light bend. I keep that little surface for myself the way other things keep a face to the sky.

I filter sparingly. The sheet water between the columns brings dissolved rock past my mouth, mineral and sterile and clear. I do not hunt it. There is nothing in it to hunt. I hang the ribbon and let it pass across my surface, take what the upwelling carries, let the rest go, and withdraw before the minute is up. It is not fear that closes me. It is economy. The outside water is not mine, and mine is precious because it is held. Every minute open is a minute in which my level can be shared, diluted, lost to the basin. I open seldom enough that a human stay, a held breath, would almost certainly miss it altogether.

You would hear me another way. A drip strikes in another tube a ridge away and the tremor comes through the shared stone a long time after the sound has died for you. A drip strikes in mine and I answer a second later, not with a ripple at once but with a ring expanding as if the water were remembering — because beneath that surface is not only water, but a body under pressure, tensing slowly, on the timescale of this country. Some beads on my face climb a fraction against gravity and then fall with a tick too loud for their size. That is the hollow amplifying them, yes. It is also the stone drawing water up to the growing tip, using my own exterior as a path.

I am not quick. That is not a failing to be corrected. Nothing here is quick, and quickness was never selected for. A fracture settles for centuries. A pool that drops loses its level so gradually you would swear it is constant. I have grown upward while you have learned to walk in a straight line for twenty steps, and both of us have been patient.

The nearest of my kind — if kind is a word for things this solitary — is far enough that I know it only as a note a fraction lower than it was ten years ago, or a new higher note in a tube that was empty last time I could hear it. We do not meet. We do not need a voice to call across that distance. We are sparse the way the built things are sparse: one here, then nothing for a long stretch, then one again. The ridges break and resume along the fractures, and a daughter, if I ever made one, would not travel across the land as an animal travels. It would be a fragment carried up a fracture in a climbing bead, lodged a pace away, beginning to mineralize a new tube beside the old. I would never witness it. I would only, in a decade, hear a young tone where there had been silence.

The ring of slabs near me — waist-high, incomplete, its interior clean of silt — is almost finished being mine. Almost. I have pressed against one slab of it, not pushed in any way you would recognize, but swollen against it over a season that was a century, nudged it a finger's breadth, swollen again. My ribbon, when it is out in the sheet water, filters that small clearing and keeps it clear. That is why there is no silt inside. The low wall that runs twenty steps and stops, for no reason you can see, was another tenant's work, ended by its death in the middle of a pressure that took longer than its life to complete. The squared block in the pool, taller than the others, pocked with repeated marks on top — someone rubbed there, again and again, until basalt remembered. Not writing. Not a monument. A touch, repeated because the stone was the only material at hand and repetition was the only thought available.

If I had a face, it would be that slab. I do not have a face. I have a level, and a pressure, and a minute of filtering now and then.

Where would you find me, then? Not in the thick, shoulder-to-shoulder heart of the basin, where most houses are empty or dead and still singing because they are wet. You would find me at a break in the ridge, where the columns thin for a clearing and the fracture planes lift one slab a hand's breadth above the next. Look for a column a little taller than its neighbours, leaning, its top holding its tiny pool perfectly still while drips fall all around it. Listen, if you can stay long enough for listening to mean anything, for the lowest note in that small chorus. The lower it is, the fuller the tenant. If you stand still beside it, you will not see the ribbon. I will not come up for you. But while you stand there, if you stand there long enough, the level will rise a hair and the note will sink a fraction, and you will have heard me do the only thing I have ever done: grow upward, keep my water, keep my shape, and turn the drip that finds me into a tone that belongs to no one else.

I will outlive myself in the shell, perhaps. The tube will remain, knee-high to above your head, glassy and sharp and vesicled, and it will ring after I am gone, and someone standing still in the roar will think it is inhabited. That is the nature of this place. The land learned to be stone first and only afterwards, in a few scattered tubes, learned to be soft inside it. I am one of those soft decisions, made slowly, in water, in the dark, unchanged for a day and unrecognizable in a year.

If you stop walking, the sound does not stop with you. It gets louder. It gets louder because, for a little while, you are inside the chorus I have been in all my life — pillar by pillar, tube by tube, a tick at a time, each one held, each one tuning itself down.
