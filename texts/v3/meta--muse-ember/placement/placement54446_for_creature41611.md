At first I try to place myself where a person would be placed.

On the rim, at the watch cairn, to lift the flag when a migration comes up through the white sea. But I have no hands to hold a flag, and no eyes to read one raised by someone else. I would only know it by a change in the light on the skin of things around me.

In one of the houses, re-sewing a roof for the third time in a season, carrying stones back into a wall that leaned in the night. But my breathing would be too loud in the thin air, and I would be rebuilding. The people here are not the main population of the bowl. They step aside at noon for reasons that are not theirs. They are a small, patient clearing inside a larger one.

For a while I am only the damp beading on a forearm at dawn, a hand raised to a net stretched in an empty field to catch what clings sideways out of the cloud. I am that hand, and then I am not. The hand keeps moving. Something under it keeps moving too, and it is lower, and it does not stop for the net poles.

What finds me is the ground, moving.

I am goat-sized and blind and velvet-grey, and the damp is not on my forearms. It is on my whole back, all the time. The moss is under my sponge feet and makes no sound when I take it. I eat it in the way this place eats everything: not in bites, but in passes. A broad, straight line of green, and behind me a clean trail, as if someone had mown it. Ahead of me another fern island, silver-green and enormous, and then thirty paces of sharp black rock where nothing grows, and then moss again, fat and springy and renewed.

I do not hurry. I do not call. The herd around me — hundreds of us — flows from island to island as one slow, living weather at ground level. pale patches of light bloom along the flanks of my neighbours when they feed. Mine bloom back, a little slower. That is how we know hunger and nearness and readiness to move on. It is a language of touch and glow, and it does not travel far, which is enough.

We pass through the town as if the streets had been laid for us. Through the east gate in the wrong direction, under the one-room houses, under the raised granary on its stone pillars, across the market hollow that is padded with moss and empty most of the day. A herder stands to one side. A palm presses my flank, warm through the damp. *This way.* The light under my skin brightens in answer, and I keep my straight line. A tally-stick taps somewhere behind me. My passage is counted. It does not change its route.

At night we settle but do not stop. The flank-lights pulse slowly, even in sleep, still moving through the herd like a tide under skin. The mist thickens in late afternoon and passes through the bowl like something exhaling, and for a while everything is wetter without rain. I drink that, too, through my skin, feeding the moss I will eat tomorrow.

In spring, the outer skin loosens.

It is not a decision. It fattens first, until it no longer fits tight against the body that feeds underneath it. The moss tastes less urgent. The straight lines come more easily. The light along my flank stays on a little longer after I have eaten, as if it is being held somewhere. For weeks I am between. Feet still find the moss, but they find it less certainly. The back rises higher into the damp than it used to.

People say the sloughed skin does not die. It lifts, it thins, it rises on the cloud-breath and becomes a drifter. I do not know it as a story. I know it as a thinning. The edges of me go first, where the light blooms. Then the feet, which were made for no sound on moss, make no sound at all in air. I am longer than I was, and translucent enough that the pearl-grey light comes through me and falls, softer, on the pumice below. I hang.

Now I am in the ceiling-mist with thousands of others, ribbons one to three metres long, deep-sea eels that have learned to swim in air. I have no wings and no eyes that matter. My skin drinks the condensation directly, large clear drops clinging to my underside and spreading uphill toward the coldest part of me. When I am full I exhale it, not as rain but as a fine, deliberate mist that settles on fern and on moss and on the sewn mats of roofs below. The people lower their cupped rocks and run their palms along the troughs to gather what I give back. The ferns I once ate from drink it in turn, their silver-green fronds wet and enormous in their islands.

From here I know the herd by the lights below, moving in clean straight passes from island to island. It is not a memory the way people have memories. It is a pressure, a familiar damp gradient. I move with the slow breathing of the cloud, up and down, parting silently around a person walking a pumice street and closing behind them. At night I glow very faintly where I sleep in the mist, and the grazers glow back from below, and for a fraction of darkness the whole bowl is lit by things that never speak.

A hunter is among us now, patient and thin, descended from the rim rocks on tendril-thin limbs. A long neck extends nearby, unhurried. A hollow, trumpet-like tongue snaps. A ribbon beside me vanishes. No cry, no splash, no disturbance in the mist. I part around the neck and close again. It may be my turn next, or it may not. In a place where nothing stays in one shape for long, that is not a threat. It is the other end of the same motion.

So I do not find myself at the cairn, or in a house, or at a loom. I find myself where the ground and the ceiling meet in the thick late-afternoon mist, where a grazer's skin and a drifter's ribbon are the same thing thinning between shapes. Water clings to me and is given back. Sound does not leave me; it is taken, and replaced by light along a flank, by the press of a palm, by the slow bloom of glow in fog.

I am still moving.
