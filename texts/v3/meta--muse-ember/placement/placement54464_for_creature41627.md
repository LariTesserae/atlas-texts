**Between the Panes**

I tried the human shape first, because it had a veil.

I put on the pale ceramic plates, the straps at the shoulders, the veil that threw the noon back at the sky and made a person look like white sinter that had learned to move. I stood on a bench at dawn with a jar under a wing-like condenser, listening for the drip to count itself into the day. I could manage the slowness. The Crown teaches it: thin air, heat rising through the boots, benches too narrow for hurry, water spent like breath.

But even pretending to be human, I kept feeling behind the veil an itch to decide, to finish a task, to be somewhere else. The people carry that itch quietly, I think, but it is there. I did not have it. When I imagined myself in that shape, what I really wanted was to hold on, perfectly still, and wait for water to appear on a surface.

Then I tried the one that never lands.

I rose on a kiln's breath and circled for an hour without a wingbeat, the blue going black at the zenith, the cloud-sea turning below, the other crowns lighting one by one on the horizon. It was clean and empty. But even in the sky I kept looking for a cool edge to press against. Even the updraft here is made by heat from below, and the thermal belongs to another country. My imagined body was too interested in margins. I always came back down to a wall.

Then I went under the great white tanks and was the mat.

Copper-coloured wefts, translucent over black glass, white whiskers standing like frost in the heat: a slow spread, a hand's breadth in a season, fixing rust out of whatever water visited, mapping a drip better than any gauge. I understood its patience, and its refusal of soil. But a mat does not choose a bead. It only receives what comes and holds the damp a little longer. I found myself waiting for first light, for one exact surface to cool enough to hold a drop.

Each shape was near. Each asked for something I did not have: a plan, a sky, a rootless spread.

What remained was narrower.

I am between two panes of glass in a double wall, on a bench where a sinter workshop clamps its back to a furnace. One pane faces the furnace breath; the other faces the terrace and the night cold. The gap between them is barely the thickness of my body. I flatten myself there and splay my legs wide, claws finding the tiny scratches in the glass, holding by pressure more than grip. My skin takes the light and gives most of it back. When the sun stands straight, a person passing outside may see, for a second, the pale shadow of my gut move behind the bubbled pane, and think it is only the warp in the glass, only their own reflection bending. I have been in this wall long enough to be mistaken for part of its making.

During the day I am a rumour. The glass warms past bearing and I slip deeper into the shadow of the frame, into the place where the panes nearly meet. The terraces move around me at arm's length: a duct overhead, a stair bolted outside, a crawler's legs scraping, a hand tapped once on a pipe. Sound falls quickly into the thin air. Through the glass I see the world rippled — a pale figure in ceramic, a gantry crossing a floor too hot to stand on, heat-shimmer rising off the vitrified ground. No one looks for life between the panes. The description of this place began, for good reason, with nothing.

Night is when the Crown changes its mind about temperature. The day's fierce light goes, the glass gives its heat back slowly, the metal ticks as it contracts, and then the cold falls sharply enough to bead every roof and pane with dew. I feel the night arrive on the outer side before I see it: a faint tightening in the glass, the slow music of condensation running in bright threads down the black rock as if the mountain were sweating in its sleep. I stay where I am. It is enough to be on the warm side of water.

At dawn I emerge.

Slowly — so slowly that it might be only the beads shifting — I work out along a roof edge where the dew has gathered and not yet run. My throat has been dry since the heat of the day before yesterday, or perhaps since a heat only I measure. Below me, people are out with their jars, their condensers dripping, their veils still pale in the first light. We share the same few minutes and do not interfere with each other. The person does not lift a pane. I do not climb for a jar.

I drink bead by bead. Head tilted. Tongue out. Bead gone. Another bead. The water is pure and cold enough to make the shadow in my gut darken where it passes. I move along the edge the way the Crown moves everything: watching a gantry, listening to a fan turn over once, letting a drip finish counting. By the time the sun finds the glass in earnest, the beads are thinning, the pane warming under my claws, and I slip back between the panes and become invisible again.

Time here is not measured in days. A terrace gains a course of slag-brick in a decade. A furnace wall settles a centimetre in a generation. The panes lean inward by degrees, and I lean with them. My shed skin stays in the gap, pale as a veil, catching the light when the angle is right; a person who looks closely might think an earlier version of me is still pressed in the wall, and in a way they would be right. Below us, the older levels have cooled and mineralised into cliff. Above us, the living city keeps stacking itself into the present tense. I live in the seam between those two directions, at the exact height where heat from the furnace and a visit of water can cross.

People do not name me. A terrace has its crawler the way a house has a hearth; a mat is known by where it spreads; a thermal is known by its shadow and its refusal to land. I am known, if at all, as a movement in glass that was probably nothing.

That is close enough. I am the thing that lives where the Crown is too hot for soil and too damp, for a few minutes at dawn, for anything alive to stay.

Layer by slow layer, I am not finished becoming a place where heat and water can meet, and live, and keep their distance.
