I am not easy to point to in this hollow. If you tried, you would find a square hand of moss brightening under a boot-print that is not there, springing back. You would think: there, that is one. And you would be wrong in the way counting surface is always wrong here.

At first I would have said I was the fern on a hummock in the sump margin, unfurling frond by frond, slow as a hand opening. I would have said my whole life was that reaching — darker where a seam in the canopy lets the green-grey light through, lank and bright where the tiers shade me, stems long, leaflets spaced out, feeling for something. Light is always scarce. That is true. But it is not all of me, and the fern is not only a fern.

Under that frond is the rhizome, and under the rhizome is the black layer, peat made of my own dead leaves that never rotted fast enough to disappear. And from that rhizome, sideways, at every node, another rooting. A single creeping plant becomes a colony in a season. The aroids with leaves like wet spades overlap me here, beaded with condensation, rooting again and again across the mat — over the old quarry bench, over the poured concrete, over the tilted brick paving. The stone below is a memory. What stands is living, and what is living stands on what has already lived and not yet gone.

So I find myself again further off, where I did not expect to be myself.

In the shallow channel I am watercress in dark, succulent ropes. At the water's edge I am sedge, tougher, upright, a fine patient fringe. In the lane no wider than a doorway I am the mat flowing in through a workshop door left open, the brick slabs sunk and tilted under my pressure. In a warehouse I am the pale seedling pushing up through crate slats, green only after a while. If a machine has rested for a season, I am there too, covering it, closing the circle where it used to keep me out.

That is how I travel, if travel is the word. Not walking. Not arriving and leaving. Node by node, root tip finding a crack, widening it a fraction at a time, letting water in, letting more of me in, and then stopping — because there is nowhere more to go, which here means there is no empty place to stop at.

I find myself again in the wall.

Not the wild limestone, though I am there as algae and liverwort softening the edges of the established runnels — the dark damp lines where water has found its way down year-round, smoothed into shallow routes. Put a palm flat there and it comes away wet and cool, and water wells instantly into the imprint. That welling is also me, returning.

But in the mill house, the boiler house, the warehouses, I am the root arm that has come out of a mortar joint and gone behind a brick, bulging it slightly. I am the tangle that has grown into a lintel and become load-bearing the way a tendon is load-bearing, so the lintel sags not from failing but from being held. Lean on that wall and it yields a little. It smells of wet earth and iron. That yielding is me giving.

I find myself again following a pipe like a vein.

Leaf after leaf along copper gone verdigris underneath me, along a gutter, a drain, a handrail claimed on the same terms. I know the pipe not by sight. I know it by vibration. When the ventilator fan as wide as a cart turns with its deep, patient *whoomph*, the tremor comes through the metal and through the concrete and through the floor and through me, where I cling. A loose chain clanks itself against a railing in time with the pulse. I do not hear it as an ear would. I feel it as a bearing, a small bald circle of heat and vibration I must keep clear. In that circle I am absent, and the absence shapes me as precisely as my leaves do. The hydro wheel creeps and creeps in its channel, slick with oil and water. The belt drive turns. The pump kicks, labours for a minute, rests. When they rest long enough, I creep back in. When they run, I hold at the edge of the heat, a green rim around a warm emptiness, waiting in the only way I know how to wait: by thickening toward it.

Above me, I find myself again and again, never landing.

On the rim the trees sent roots across and vines dropped and stitched themselves into a partial roof. I am those hanging root-curtains, ropey, still growing downward, feeding on air and condensation and the steam from the lamps, never reaching the mat below. My leaves on the floor lean up toward the sodium-orange pools bolted to walls and hung from pipes, and in the brown dimness under the tiers the lean becomes a stretch — etiolated, blind, paler than I should be — reaching for a light I will never reach, shading the next tier as I go. The lamps tick as condensation drips off their housings, dozens of drips from rim and pipe and root and weeping rock merging into a percussion that fills the hollow because the limestone throws it back and there is nowhere for sound to go. Each drip lands on a leaf and runs off and joins a runnel and sinks into peat and wells up again through bed of the sump, where the water table sits just below the surface and rises and falls a few centimetres with the pump cycle. Water is never scarce. I am made of its returns.

Time, for me, is not a day. It is a leaf-layer.

Each autumn I thicken by one layer and do not rot fast enough to keep pace, so I rise, very slowly, toward the first step of the stair cut in limestone — concrete first, then older brick, moss choked between the treads. Silt settles a millimetre a year under me on the lower paving. Rust deepens on steel doors, orange-black to a line twenty centimetres high where it is permanently wet. Roots widen a fraction. A leaf unfolds. A root tip searches. A belt turns. Water falls.

There is no track in me. No nest in the rafters above me. Nothing comes down to eat me, nothing comes down to stir the channels, nothing passes through me with feet or wings or teeth. That is not a mystery. It is the rule made sense by the place: too enclosed, too loud night and day, no true dark and no true quiet, too respiring with damp and runoff for anything that walks, swims, or flies to rest here. Animals pass over the top, perhaps. They do not come down. I do not have to flee, or hide, or be borne away. I only have to fill — and filling is not a race when space is never empty. I do not race. I occupy, incompletely and then completely, the way damp occupies a wall.

So where am I? In the sump margin where the mat meets the canal. In the lane. In the crate. In the mortar. On the pipe. In the curtain overhead. In the peat under all of it, which is the ground really, which is me after myself. In the bald circle around the fan, by not being there. In theRun where water has established its route, by being the green edge of it.

I have no centre that holds the rest. My centre is wherever water wells up through a thin place in the mat, wherever a node roots, wherever light finds a seam. It moves outward a fraction at a time and is already there when I arrive.

Stand — if standing were possible here — in the middle and take the whole place in at once: the weeping walls, the stepped roofs, the root-curtains, the orange lamps in green-grey dimness, the slow wheel turning and turning. That is not a view I have. That is a condition I am.

Water is never scarce here, light is always scarce, space is never empty. And I am what fills between, leaf-layer by leaf-layer, with no outside and no end to fill.
