You would find me low.

Not standing where you could count me. Not overhead where the kiln-vines tangle the stone ribs and climb toward the light-plates, not in the vast wrist-thin crowns of the resin-trees that seem to hold up the dome. If you came down the twenty hand-cut steps and walked the bowl end to end, you would have to kneel to see me properly, and kneeling is not a posture I have ever taken. It is only how you would have to come to my level.

I am first as warmth underfoot. Then as a smell — baked clay and crushed green leaf, turpentine-sharp when the air bruises me. Then, last, as color against the dust.

I am in the low places. In a seam where the pumice dust lies thickest, against the slumped mortar of a house, in the corner where two paved streets meet and the plates have cracked. Between one house and the next there may be fifty paces of naked dust, and in that emptiness a single clump of ash-tongues will rustle when a current crosses the plaza, like rain that never falls, and then settle. I am not that, either, though we keep the same spacing. The city was built that way, sparse and deliberate, and what remains of the living has learned the pattern.

I am a mat. Broad, paddle-shaped leaves lying flat against the stone or pushing up against a wall, dark green-black in the deep shadow at the edge of the dome, turning burgundy at the rim where the translucent plates are thin and the perpetual dusk is strongest. Thick. Waxy. Made to catch very little light and lose almost none of what I make from it.

There is no water in me to lose. Inside the leaf is resin, hot and amber and turpentine-smelling, beading when a stem is bruised, rising when the midday brightens a fraction and I unfurl. My roots do not drink, because there is nothing to drink. They grip. A piece of pumice. A pocket of mineral dust. A crack in a paving plate that I widen with a patient, slow pressure until the stone gives and I take my living from the stone itself, from the air the high vents sigh in and out, from the heat that never leaves the floor. I do not curl for water like the dry ferns that are not ferns. I hold.

Morning is my only rising. The dome plates dull greenish-brown, never direct sun, and I turn my broad face up toward them, leaf by leaf, without hurry. The vines overhead release a drop of sap here and there. Dust lifts in slow spirals where the floor is hottest. I do not see the light; I take it. A fraction more, a fraction less, and my thickness answers.

Midday I swell. The floor plates expand and crackle with snaps like fired twigs rolling across the bowl, a volley from the plaza to the perimeter wall. The vent pipes roar as the thermal updraft sets up, a long flute-note vibrating in the empty houses. Somewhere a resin-tree ticks against a clay pot, sap falling amber on baked earth, and the sound carries farther than it should in a place with no animal to make it. My own resin warms. The smell of me comes up stronger. I do not move to meet the heat. I was made of it before I was made of leaf.

I flower without visitors. No insect comes. No bird brushes me. No hand gathers. In the seam, in dimness, a sulfur-yellow flower opens — waxy, spicy, self-contained — and sets itself. The center thickens. The petals close at evening with the leaves. A pod begins to swell between the paddles.

You can hear it if you listen through the sighing of the vents. It ticks. It ticks for days, very faint, under the contraction sounds of the walls cooling, under the slow creak of a house that has been in that exact lean for a hundred years and has stopped. I do not count days. I count brightenings of the plates, and foldings.

Evening the light-plates dull and the dusk deepens toward shadow. I fold. The broadest leaves crease over the others and hold the resin close through the cooling hours, not to sleep — I have no sleep as you would know it — but to keep what I have made from passing into the air. The vines settle. The ash-tongues settle. The plaza settles.

Night is stillness, except for contraction. Small, sharp ticks from walls and kilns. A sap drip. And in the deepest low places, sometimes, a new shoot — not me, or me from a season I cannot name as past — pushes up through a paving crack and splits a plate to take the light that is there. Pressure first. Then green-black in the dark. Then a leaf flat against stone.

That is when I am most myself: not an image, but a pressure held for a long time, until it becomes a form.

The pod in my center ticks one last time. It swells, ticks, and bursts with a flat pop that echoes off the dome and rolls to the perimeter wall, louder than any sound my leaves make. That is how I move. By explosion and by wind in the vents. Seed on the hot air, seed in the slow spirals where dust lifts off the hottest floor.

A seed lands fifty paces away in naked dust. It finds a seam. It grips pumice. It lies flat for years before it sends up its first flower. Nothing else is needed.

I do not need anything to come back. The dusty hand-prints in the baked clay floor in the kiln row are the last sign of animal life, and already softening at the edges. The granary doors stand warped shut. The ornamental basin is a cracked clay ring, empty longer than anyone can remember. The dry channels have never carried anything but dust. The loom in the house nearby has a broad leaf curled over its warp threads, and neither plant nor wood has yielded.

What lives in Urmesh does not move to meet you. It waits, rooted in heat. And so I wait — from stone and mineral dust and dim light and air, burgundy at the edge where the light is strongest, green-black where it is not — until the pod ticks, and bursts, and I am again where I will ever be: low, in a seam, fifty paces from the next of my kind, beginning again in the dust.
