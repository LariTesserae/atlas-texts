First there is a tap.

Three taps under a slab, pause, three taps again. This time I am the pause between them.

I do not know at first if I made the sound or only received it. The stone shivered and something answered. Near knee height, near hand height, under here, a shiver is enough to make a body.

It is cold under the slab. Not night-cold — night has been gone for hours up there — but the kind of cold that has held all night and holds all day. A deep cool in the seam. On top of the slab the sun has made the rock a pan, white and direct and unsoftened by this thin air. I can feel it through what is above me. My back warms. My belly does not. I live in the difference.

The ceiling is one polygon of basalt, a meter across, lifting slightly at the heat on its edges. When it lifts, a slit of light opens and closes and I learn that there is an outside without seeing it: too bright, too thin, too far in every direction. The world under here is crowded bell-to-bell. Glass beetles beside me, dark and translucent, catching that slit as a faint shine along a shell. Iron beetles, duller, heavier, standing motionless — I am one of those, I think; my weight takes longer to shift. Shed skins folded like tissue in the dust packed in for years. Eggs tucked where the seam stays wettest. A fungus pale as bone-spittle eating that dust, sweet and mineral in the air I move through.

At first I have no edge. I am cold, and damp, and pressed. Then the edge forms: a hard curve, a joint, a leg that can brace against the underside when the stone shivers again. Tap. Tap. Tap. The glass ones go first, always, a Morse of disturbance. And then — because the stone has moved and my body has moved with it — I tap back. Not a word. Nothing here carries far enough for a word. It is only the ripple of being disturbed, passing from shell to shell.

At dawn everything sweats.

The turn comes before I understand it. The condenser rows wake first — somewhere beyond the crack, up the slope of sound that will die within meters — and begin to drip steadily for an hour, each drip a clear metallic tick onto pumice below. I count without counting. The underside of my ceiling is wet. The mineral film along it brightens. I turn, clumsy and heavy, and lap at the stone as if it were sweating. That is drinking here. No pool, no river. A surface. A drip. The inner curve of a rusted drum I have never seen, a footprint in ash, a corrugated roof — all the same to me now: places where water will not run off and will not waste itself in the open. I drink the film and the film is enough.

Morning on top is mating. I know it only by vibration and by the formal little shocks traveling down through the rock: claws click, a body settles, stillness, a quick frantic movement, stillness again. Lizards by the thousand, by the meter, running in the cool before the heat makes running necessary. Their scales are translucent against the sun and their shadows pass over my crack and take the light away for an instant. I do not see them. I feel the preference of their hours in the stone: mate in the cool, swarm in the heat, run at noon.

By mid-morning the slab has become too hot to think directly about. The heat is not in the air — the air up there is warm only in pockets, in lees and cracks — the heat is in the stone. It comes through the ceiling and meets the cold that will not move, and I withdraw an inch deeper into the seam, which for me is a migration. The polygon lifts. The hole between rock and rock widens enough for a grain of sand to fall through somewhere to my left, dust devils above redistributing seed and screws and shells as violence. I grip. My legs know.

At noon the running begins.

Above me it is not a sound so much as a weather of clicks. Lizard claws on basalt, fast and frantic, shadow to shadow, because at this altitude and this exposure a lizard cannot sit still on black rock without cooking; it must run to cool its belly. The stone shivers, shivers, shivers. We tap back underneath, every one of us, in waves. Glass first. Iron after. A disturbance spreading bell-to-bell until it is lost in the seam.

And then the shadow that is not a lizard's. Larger. Slower. The running stops. The light in my crack goes out completely, longer than a lizard's passage, and when it returns nothing runs. I will never hear a hawk call. Nothing here calls across the open; the air is too thin and sound bends upward and dies. I know the hawk the way I know everything: by what the stone does before and after.

The machines are farther and closer at the same time. I feel them more than hear them. The long hall near the rim ticks in the sun, cooling and contracting, cooling and contracting, a slow pulse that comes up through the polygons and settles in my legs until I learn to ignore it the way I ignore the heat on my back. A pump somewhere clanks and it is a vibration in my teeth before it is a sound, if it is a sound at all. The heat exchangers hiss along their pipes in straight engineered lines that cut across the natural cracks; their warmth reaches me as a second summer on the far side of the seam. Nothing is maintained for people. The drip is the watering hole. The shade is a cliff. The cables are nesting material carried strand by strand into cracks like mine, though I have not carried any and never will. I am too heavy and too committed to the cold.

In the afternoon, perhaps, rain.

Twenty minutes, violent, hissing on the hot rock above like a kettle. Brown threads run into the cracks. For me this is a flood in miniature and a reprieve in one: the seam where the eggs are goes dark with wetness, the fungus lifts its pale head, the mineral film runs and returns. I hold to the underside while water makes a world of the outside of the stone, and when it is gone by sunset I drink again from what it left on surfaces that do not drain. A dust devil passes and the light changes and a beetle shell — someone's ancestor, someone's shed skin — is lifted and set down elsewhere, redistributed evenly across the shelf as if the shelf were being leveled by a hand.

Evening is the fall.

Thirty degrees in an hour. The stone gives up its heat all at once, as if it had been holding it only to return it. Dew forms instantly on every metal surface somewhere out there, on every cold surface here. My ceiling stops radiating. The cold that was a refuge becomes the whole condition, and the under-slab world wakes for its second day. Tap. Tap. Tap. All around me. The glass ones, the iron ones, disturbance and answer and disturbance again, because now we move.

Outside, in the brief country of cold stone, mice with enormous ears run their pumice paths under the concrete footings — I feel them only as a periodic tremor in a different rhythm, carried strand by strand into their own warm hollow. Steam moths come over the vents at dusk, hundreds of them, feeding on heat that boils at a temperature that feels cool to a human hand; the white plume races sideways along the ground instead of rising, and I know it only because the air in my crack moves differently, sideways, purposeless. The swifts in the basalt columns chitter shoulder to shoulder, deafening at the cliff face and gone ten meters back. I am ten centimeters back. I hear the first note of it and then the thin air takes the rest.

I am not alone in being small. I am not alone in being fast, though I am slow. I am not alone in being close — close to the ground, close to a seam, close to a drip, close to the one place that stays cold while the surface above me cooks and freezes and cooks again.

Tomorrow the condenser will tick for an hour at dawn. I will turn and drink the stone. The lizards will mate in the cool and run at noon and a shadow will end a run. The machines will tick and run on their schedules. The skins folded under me will gain one more layer. The eggs where the seam stays wettest will or will not finish.

In a thousand years the condensers will be cobbles, the slab will be gravel, and something will nest in them until then. I do not know a thousand years. I know the tap, the pause, and the tap again.

Three taps under a slab, pause, three taps again. This time I know which is mine.
