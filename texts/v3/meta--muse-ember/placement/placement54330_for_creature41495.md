First there is pressure, not a population.

Warmth at my back. A damp surface I am pressed against, and cannot tell if it is floor or wall or the side of something breathing. Clicking, many-throated, close to my ear and far away at once. Somewhere below my knees, a scraping, a sideways chewing, a cough. Light finds me before I find myself — white, ordinary light, falling through holes in a ceiling tens of metres up, in shafts wide as streets and in blades no wider than a finger. Dust hangs in it. My shadow on the stone is sharp.

I do not arrive here. I am already in the thickness of it.

The light moves. Very slowly across the floor it moves, turning wet clay to ochre, turning blue-grey felt to shine. I track it with whatever I have for a face, half-closed, half-blind in the glare, and then, out of it, blind again in the warm dark where the rock sweats. When I put a palm to the pillar beside me, it leaves a damp print. My fingers are long. They close on the stone and do not quite belong to the scraping mouth below me, though they work to the same rhythm at first, as if I had not yet decided what I am for.

I uncurl, and that is a decision.

My legs fold up under me, kneeling-fashion, low. My back is plated, grey-pink, segmented in a way that aches when I have been crouched too long under the low arch. Above me is roof: interwoven shed plates, mine and others', and haematite stones, and over that a thick felt of fungus that drips when the daily heat comes through the shafts and the condensation breaks loose. The felt is heavy and alive in the way this country is alive, not separately, but as a damp. Clingers are crawling over the outside of it in their tens of thousands, sucker feet clicking, a dry rain that never quite stops. When they ripple — a whole face shedding at once to drop a ledge and climb again — the sound runs through my own roof into my skull.

Under me, the floor bulges. A burrower passes, blind and pale and broad through the shoulders, and the hut tilts on its clay, and a crack parallel-walks the ground for ten metres and closes behind it. Spoil ridges rise and are colonized before the dust settles.

This is where I am: in a low domed hut, in a little clearing where the mat is kept cropped short. Inside a wall that is inside a chamber that is inside a valley under stone. Not a point. A layer, between the burrowers below and the fliers above and the herds passing through.

The herds come and the whole basin breathes.

I hear them before I see them, which is how everything here announces itself. Snuffling. Coughing. The scrape of kneeling legs and scraping mouths going sideways through the fungal velvet, chewing it down to mineral and leaving a clean wet path that shines in the light. They are dog-sized and armoured and pink-grey like me, and for a moment, in the noise, I might be one of them — a river on land flowing chamber to chamber through the broad arches, dust at their heels when they bolt. I stand at the edge of the river, not in it, working my own small mouth of stone: the knapped scraper hafted to bone, going sideways too, shaving fungus back from the clearing as they do, keeping the ground around the hut open. Where we have both worked, the felt regrows in days. Where we have not, it thickens.

My hands remember better than my eyes. Fingers find, in the dark under the felt roof, the bone needle polished almost glassy from use, and the cord made of sinew and hair, because there is no plant here to make cord of, no wood to burn save what the fungus gives us when it dries and curls at the edges like paper. I mend. That is mostly what I am.

I stitch a shed plate back into the wall where the last slab-fall cracked it. I re-lash the scraper. I re-haft, re-knap, re-tie. The place reclaims faster than I can finish — calcite is growing up through the fire-pit in a white mineral flower, brittle, and the wall-clingers have found the new seam in the roof, and the fungal threads are already sewing the crack in the clay where the burrower passed. Nothing I make is supposed to last. It is supposed to be mended and remended until the mending is the thing.

Water teaches me timing. After the heat sweeps through, condensation beads on the coolest faces and then, in an irregular pulse that travels the chamber like a wave, it rains for two minutes from stone and misses the patch I am standing in by a metre. Rivulets braid across the low ground, stained rust and ink and metallic orange by bacteria, then sink into travertine and come up foaming a hundred metres on. In the warm pools something swims through and the wake persists, and the eel-things haul themselves up the walls on fin-limbs, out of the water for hours, leaving glistening tracks on vertical stone to hunt the clingers on their own wallpaper. I wait by a rivulet until the pulse comes, lower my head, drink iron and ammonia and wet stone and mushroom. The smell is the country. It is 28 degrees and close and liquid, and it gets into my plates.

In the shafts, the air is textured. A vent sighs steam. The warm up-draught lifts the fliers — leathery-winged, sparrow to hawk-sized, screaming in the ceiling cracks — and they spin a little faster in the light. When they go up together it eclipses the sun-holes for a second: a vortex, a roar of tearing wings, then that sudden unnatural lessening, only differently loud. I feel the draught through my back plates like a hand.

And rarely, felt as a change in the air before any sound, the large ones.

I know it by the ripple. The wall-clingers ripple all at once. The fliers thunder. The herd bolts in one dust-raising surge so loud the stone seems to chew with them, and I am pulled — by my own knees, by the others breathing in the dark of the hut beside me, younger and smaller, whose plates have not yet shed — down and still and low under the felt roof while for thirty seconds the chamber is nothing but motion. After, the tracks are in the wet clay: three-toed, hand-sized, fresher than the rubble, older than the regrowth. It was never looking at us, particularly. We are a current it moves through, as it moves through us.

Later I coax the fire back to life in the pit, with curled dry felt and a little fat rendered from what the herd leaves, and the smoke is sulphur-yellow and thin and stupidly warm against a world that is already warm. A small incongruity, tended. The ash will wash into the rivulet by next week. The calcite will go on growing. I will mend around it.

If there is a sloping gallery somewhere that leads up and out, to air not breathed by this place, I have not taken it. I have watched my own boot-print in a sun-shaft half-fill with falling dust before I turned around, and fungal threads already crossing it, and thought: that is the correct speed for everything here. Formation and failure, eating and being eaten, herds crashing and walls flaring again. Nothing behaves as an individual for long, not the drip, not the stone, not me.

So you would find me here, if you came looking through the haze at the far wall where the arches dissolve: not standing apart and watching, not walking the valley like a visitor. You would find me crouched in the clearing, between the pass of the herd and the ripple of the wall, between the sigh of the vent and the pulse of the rain from stone, mending a hut that the burrowers are already tunnelling under, with a bone needle in a long-fingered hand, while the light moves, very slowly, over all of us and through all of us, and the country goes on being made, largely, by the animals running it — and, in my small way, by me.
