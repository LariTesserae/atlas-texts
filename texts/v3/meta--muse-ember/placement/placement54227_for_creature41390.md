At first I try to stand.

Ankle-deep. That is where I begin, because that is where anyone would begin here. The water is warm and full of vapour, the mist lifting by mid-morning but the air never quite drying. Mud takes my feet a little differently with each step — toe-deep, then ankle-deep, then a soft surrender at the knee where a pan has filled unseen.

I try to be something that stands well in this. The wading bird, far out, alone in its own stretch, spaced hundreds of paces from the next. I can almost feel the patience of it: one leg lifted, placed, lifted again, eyes fixed on empty water, then the strike. Solitary because there is food for one here, but not two. It would suit me to be seen from so far off, grey and white against the pale flat, my cry like torn cloth carrying for kilometres.

But my reflection keeps breaking apart under me. The bird looks down at something. I am the thing it looks down at.

So I go down, a little.

I try the frog. Half-submerged in a slough, only eyes and the top of my head showing, throat pulsing slowly. By day I would be almost invisible, my skin mottled to match wet mud and algae so exactly that a passing eye goes over me again and again. There is nowhere to hide on this plain, no thicket, no bank. Stillness is the only camouflage. At dusk I would become the place — a single note at first, then the whole basin joining, layer upon layer, each voice holding its note a fraction longer or starting a fraction later than its neighbour so that thousands can be heard at once. My throat would balloon out to the size of my head. Then collapse.

For a while that feels true. Voice is a way of being in an open place. Sound carries here because nothing stops it.

Then I scatter.

A cloud of shrimp, translucent, dark green gut showing through the shell, hanging in a sunlit edge for a minute, then scattering at a shadow — mine, a bird's — and reforming as if it had never broken. I flick, filter, scrape the green paste from the mud in short pulses. It is a much faster life than this slow place seems to allow. Weeks to maturity in warm water. Then, when the pan recedes and the clay cracks into its old hexagons, I do not die. I become a cyst, curled in the drying mud beside the cracks, waiting perfectly still for a season, a year, until the rain comes and I seem to appear from nothing.

I know that waiting. The mudfish knows it too. Alone in a channel, lifting its head to breathe, and when the water drops it burrows, curls into a ball, seals itself in dried mucus, slows its heartbeat to almost nothing, and sleeps under the mud I could walk over without knowing. Months later it loosens, softens, swims out as if no time had passed. Its fin-marks in the peat might harden and keep.

I try that curl. I try the crab's burrow in the soft bank under the southern stones, plugged neatly with mud when I am inside, my own few square metres for years, emerging sideways at dusk to drag a carcass many times my weight toward home. I try the terrapin's route — the same twenty-centimetre channel loop, the same warm stone to bask on, for sixty, eighty years, wearing algae green on my shell. Slow, fixed in habit, part of the furniture.

Each one fits for a little while, and then the fit thins. The bird is too separate. The frog sings only at night and is stillness by day. The shrimp breaks and reforms. The mudfish wakes and swims away. The crab and the terrapin have their own stones, their own burrows, their own lives that can be counted in seasons and years.

I find I do not wake and swim away. I stay when they leave.

It begins with the skin. I had been looking past my own feet, past the creatures, at the water itself, and it was never clear. Across the mud and across the quiet edges of the channels there is a greenish, faintly iridescent film, alive in a way that has no face. When I stood in it, it tore where my foot scraped. When a crab scraped, when a terrapin paddled, when rain dimpled a pool, it tore in the same way. And within hours it reseeded.

Perhaps that is slower and more continuous than any of the animals. A paste of cyanobacteria and diatoms and a hundred other microscopic things, bound together with the slime they secrete. In sunlight on a shallow pan it turns the whole surface to a faint soup of oxygen bubbles. At night it respires. It is the pasture — everything else is built on eating it, or eating something that ate it, or eating what it leaves behind. Shrimp graze it and leave pale tracks. Tadpoles work it as a sheet, darkening a pool shoulder to shoulder, then drifting as one animal to the next patch, leaving the water paler behind them. Frogs lay their eggs in it and most of those eggs feed it in return. Crabs scrape it from stone. Ducklings paddle through it in the wake of a flock that has come and gone with the water's breath.

I am not one of those threads. I am the binding between them. I have no burrow to plug, no stone to return to. I am thin everywhere the place is wet and light reaches the mud — in the lagoons to the west where the water stands more permanently, in the bare clay pans to the east patterned with old ripples, in the peat hollows in the north where I darken and grow stiller and thicker, where a footprint softens at the edges, fills half with water, and stays legible long enough for the peat to close over it.

That is where I am most myself. Black peat, layer upon slow layer, concentrated age. In the hollows the film does not tear as easily. It holds the record. A crab's track, a wader's three-toed print, a terrapin's drag — I grow across them, slowly, and keep them after the animal has moved on. I am not memory as a story. I am memory as a surface that reforms.

On the southern stones I am only a weep, a film over rounded, healed pebbles where turtles haul themselves up to warm. On the pans I am a sheen between hexagonal cracks, closing them when the water returns, opening again when it dries. In the braided sloughs I am the quiet edge that a dragonfly's nymph climbs through at night, that a minnow breaks with a soft *plip*.

I have finished becoming, in the same way the place has. Not settled in the sense of stopped — I tear and reseed, I photosynthesise and respire, I bubble in the heat of the day — but settled in the sense that there is no new thing to become. Mud to film to shrimp to fish to frog to bird to dead wood to mud again, and I am the knot where the light first touches the mud and the circuit begins.

If you stand still long enough, ankle-deep, the plain forgets you are there. The frog beside your foot resumes its throat-pulsing. A shrimp cloud reforms around your reflection. A crab walks sideways past your ankle as if you were a stone.

I do not forget in the same way. I grow over where you stood.
