I don't arrive so much as I stop drying.

I cross the low causeway on foot, and the damp finds me before the town does. It settles on my shoulders, in my shirt, along the edges of my hands. The air doesn't move. My breath goes out damp and comes back damp. My boots make a small wet sound in each puddle, and the puddles are the same as the ones farther in. At first I am person-shaped, because that is the shape the causeway expects of you: shoulders, hands, a face to wipe, feet that know to step around the chained-off lots. I learn the town by how wet I am in it, which is to say, completely, in every direction, all the time.

For a while, I am the tender at the pump house.

That fits readily. I check the level in the pit: no higher, no lower. I listen to the engines the way you might listen to an animal breathing and learn their notes. If one changes its note, I come and tend it. I clear the reed from a drain before it chokes. I wipe condensation from a pipe before it drips in the wrong place. I shout across a yard, because the generator hall drowns a normal voice. I sleep in installments, over a shop, when my body decides rather than when the light changes; the light never does. I eat in the low shops and watch the street water from a doorway.

I like this life because no one asks me to make anything happen. The work is to keep everything exactly as it is. A new tender could take my shift tomorrow and the pump would not change its note. I thought perhaps that was enough: a human life reduced, kindly, to maintenance.

But I never stay upright for long. Long after the other people have looked up, checking a sky that is always the same yellow-grey, I find myself looking down. At the puddle in the pothole. At the drain canal. At the film on the water. I crouch beside a trench to watch the riprap, and the crouch stops feeling like a posture.

When I crouch, I stay crouched.

There, my skin is the same temperature as the water. It has been made for this. My throat swells and I call, and another male calls from the next trench, and another from the next, no leader, no pause, because there is no night for this to be a night chorus for. I am not a soloist. I disappear into the chord that is louder than the people, frog answering frog across all the clean angular drains at once. It suits the place. It suits me. But even a frog has a voice, and even a voice gets carried away across the wet concrete until I cannot tell which call is mine. Below the calling, the water has a skin.

I go under it.

I am mud-colored and turning just beneath the film, in a stretch of cooling canal too shallow and too warm for anything elegant. My mouth opens at the surface and closes. Whiskers taste the detritus the pumps lift and drop again. I do not roam. I do not need the far dike, the water tower, the shops on main street, the addresses. Generation after generation turns in this same stretch, fed by algae and rot and whatever the machines leak, and the water level is held exactly where it is, so the whole shallow world is held with it. Birth and death balance down to the size of a cell, and I am in no hurry to rise above it.

But I must rise, to breathe. And when I do, the bank is there above the canal: unrounded riprap, fresh concrete, a retaining wall sweating steadily in the warm air where it touches the cooler stone. The sweat does not run off in a flood. It gathers, it beads, it is wiped away by moving things, it gathers again. That is where I end up. That is where I stay.

I am on the wall.

It happens gradually, the way everything here happens, by not happening. My boots are forgotten, not removed. My foot is one broad wet foot now, gripping concrete fine enough to weep. I move by laying down a road of myself and following it. Two stalks come up from my head, wet and tentative, tasting the damp ahead. Over my back I carry a low round house, pale as the new concrete, unchipped. When I stop, I am underneath it. When I move, the house moves with me at the pace of condensation.

Above me, the rats run the overhead steam lines, sure-footed on pipes that drip, and they know the whole town that way: depot, warehouses, shops, pump house, generator hall, all of it strung together on steel trestles. Under the factory eaves the swallows dart and remake their mud nests, feeding on the midges that swarm the lamps that never go out. They have the town in a way I do not. I have one wall, and the gradient of damp on it, and that is enough. I do not need to cross anything. The wall is my whole geography, from the algae film at the riprap to the drier band a hand's breadth higher, where the sweat thins and I turn.

I climb an inch through the condensation, leaving a clean track behind me, and then I descend an inch, leaving it again. The track fills in behind me as the wall sweats. I eat the film that grows on the stone — algae, bacteria, the thin green wash of a place where the jungle never took — and the film grows back behind me too. Nothing here needs to be finished. Nothing freezes. Nothing is flushed out. The pumps keep the water at its level, the engines keep the heat, the heat keeps the steam, the steam keeps the wall wet, and the wall keeps me.

I don't call. I don't need to. The frogs call from every drain, the motors whine, the valves sigh on their cycle, the water tower drips from its joint in the same place all day, and I hear all of it through my foot, through the stone, as vibration. I don't sleep the way the tenders sleep, in shifts. When I stop, I stop. When I go, I go. There is no hour marked for either.

If you left for a year and came back, you would find the pump house, the generator hall, the same puddle in the same pothole, the same drip from the same pipe. You would find the frogs calling. You would find the catfish turning under the film. And on a weeping retaining wall above a drain canal, in the lamplight under the eaves, you would find a clean track through the sweat, and on it, moving very slowly in the same direction, a snail an inch from where it started.

Which, here, is the same place.
