I come down the wet stone steps more slowly than I meant to.

The hedge, the wire fence, the gate — they close behind me with a softness, as if the latch were dampened through. Below, the town does not rise to meet me. It lies in its bowl, wholly beneath the rim, and the air drops a few degrees in my lungs and stays there. I can see, almost, the whole of it from here: the shallow S of the main street, the square with its standing sheet of water, the sheds and silos picking out the far wall, the marshy margins where the floor gives up and rises again to rock. And yet the fog makes the distances lie. There is so much empty wet ground between the buildings that ten minutes’ walk feels like a longer country.

Standing is the natural tempo here. So I stand, long enough for the flywheel in the pump house to make its minute-long circuit, long enough to notice that it has moved. Long enough to understand that nothing here darts from cover, nothing calls out from a tree, and that if I am to find myself in this place, I will have to find myself low, and slow, and among the others.

At first I try to be above.

I could be a swallow. I could take the chimneys and the undersides of the gantry roofs, wheel and wheel in the narrow strip of open air between the wet roofs and the bright wedge of fog at the rim, never rising to it, never needing to. From there I would see the whole bowl at once — the doubled lamps, the canals, the square rising by a fingernail a week. It is a clean kind of life. But my eyes keep leaving the air. Everything in this town declares itself not from a height, but from a surface. The swallow settles on a pipe and is still for an hour. That stillness is not the same as mine.

I could be the skin.

The algae film on the square, the grey mould making its slow continent on paper in a flooded drawer, the moss climbing a north wall like a tide-mark, the lichen settling on rust and turning corrosion into a crust. To be that would be the most honest adaptation of all: no need to move, no need to hurry, fed only by damp and dimness and the faint warmth of engines. I would spread a little each week and be, in that sense, everywhere. But I find I want to witness as well as spread. I want to know when the crane has lowered by a finger’s length, when the doorway fills again with sediment. The film does not notice. It only keeps.

I could be the cat on the dry step.

That tempts me more than I expect. On the top step above the waterline, on a windowsill now level with the street, on the warm housing of a motor in the pump house — a small, deliberate furnace in a cold place, paws tucked, breath pluming, watching an eel pass a foot from my nose and not striking because there is always another frog, another fish, another rat in the drain. To watch without needing to hurry, to be warm where everything else is cool, to be fed scraps left in a chipped bowl and accept them without gratitude. It is close. But even the cat sits above the water, looking on. And this place, I think, does not have lookers above it. The cats watch them all, yes, but they watch from the driest inches, and I do not want the driest inches. I want the level where everything touches.

So I try, last of the upright things, to be a person.

I could live upstairs in a brick terrace, where the top is dry enough and the hem of every coat never is. I could put on boots each morning that have filled overnight with cold seepage. I could keep the pump house turning, dig out a doorway slowly, like a mouth, knowing it will fill again by next year. I could learn not to run on the cobbles where the tram rails lie like canal edging, and to look down — for the shoal crossing the shed floor, for the toad that is not a stone, for the procession crossing the office behind me. My breath would show all year. My skin would be pale from a light that only touches the chimney tops. That is almost right. It is the shape I arrived in, down the steps. But I keep slipping in it. My hem is wet, my sleeves tear on wet brick, my feet find the algae on the steps and slide. The ground floors have been given up to the water. The fish move through where a parlour was. I keep gravitating down toward that given-up level, as if my body knows something my standing does not.

So I stop trying to stand.

I look down, and I follow the water across the flooded floor.

It begins not with a decision but with a change in how I move. I do not step. I pour myself forward. The brown water is two centimetres deep over concrete and it is enough. It is always enough here. My body follows my head between the legs of a press, over a submerged desk, past filing cabinets that rise like reefs out of the office floor. The paper in the drawers has gone soft and grey and alive; I brush it and it does not tear. I breathe the damp air as well as the water, and neither is a stranger. Above me the pipes cross at head height, beaded with condensation, webs strung between joints, moths blundering from the tired amber lamps and being taken, wet and quiet, into the architecture. I do not scatter when a boot comes down near me. I have no reason to. The boot is a large, slow disturbance in a system that has already agreed to move at the pace the damp allows. I go on, in a line.

That is the thing I had not understood from the steps: I am not alone in it, and I am not first, and I am not last. The procession is the unit. One eel pours after another across the shed floor, out through the canal between the warehouses, under the gantry where the swallows settle on a pipe and coo into a space that returns their voices softened, as if through cloth. In the canals the carp turn slowly above and around me, huge and unhurried, mouths breaking the surface to take air, their eyes catching the doubled lamps. I am small in their country and they are indifferent to me, and that indifference is a kind of peace.

At the far wall, where the quarry weeps in black-green threads, I pause in the clear seepage. The perch are there, holding station against a current I can barely feel, the only true flashes of colour in the bowl, a flick of gold in the coldest, cleanest water. I hold with them for a while, under the reflection, and the lamps above us break and remend themselves on the surface. Then the line moves on.

At dusk the frogs begin in the ditches and the flooded lots, their chorus the loudest sound the town makes and still muffled, half taken away by fog and water. I hear it from below as much as from above. The toads sit stone-coloured at the edges of ponds where a building stood and the ground remembered water. The ducks land on the shallow sheet in the square so gently they leave no ripple for long seconds, and I pass under their landing, under the greenish factory windows, under the amber points doubled in the water. A cat’s paws rest on a dry step above me. I do not look up at them. I do not need to. I am part of the balance they watch.

Stand here — if an eel can be said to stand — in the square at evening, when the rim has disappeared entirely into mist and the lamps are doubled in the standing water, and I am below the doubling, in the cool brown dark, crossing the town as if it were a riverbed that happens to have offices and presses and desks in it. The conveyor creeps an hour for a bucket of aggregate. The flywheel makes its minute circuit. The square rises by a fingernail a week. The lichen advances on the rust. I advance at the same rate. I cross the flooded office, the main shed, the canal, the marsh margin, and back to the flooded office again, because there is nowhere else to go and no need to go elsewhere. The water came to these things, and life followed the water, and I followed the life, and now I am simply what the following feels like from inside.

I do not dry. I do not hurry. I do not scatter.

I go on pouring forward, in my line, through a town that has already become what it is and intends, very slowly, to go on being it.
