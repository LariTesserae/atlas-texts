I find myself low, before I find myself anything else.

I am lying in the peat because the peat gives, and sighs back up under my weight. My eye level is sedge. The sky is most of the room. Between this dark boulder and the next there are twenty paces of open moss, and I am in the interval, not crossing it. My footprints fill bright as sky almost as soon as I lift my boots. My eyelashes collect fog. The dripping finds me from the leeward cliffs, from under stones, from the eaves of moss — dozens of pitches, louder as the world shrinks to five paces.

I did not come here by the cairn line. I am underneath the sane path, underneath the windbreak hut and the stone ring and the half-rotted basket, in the part of the bowl that weather has already finished weathering.

The first life that seems to claim me is the frog.

I could be stone-colored, skin like wet leather, living not in a pond but in the saturated moss itself, which is pond enough for a creature that size. Rest, and I become basalt veined with quartz, indistinguishable from the ground. Sing, and I keep my distance by song, and the whole bowl is suddenly populated from under the turf. At evening I would start again, deeper.

It almost fits. But when I try it, the croak is not quite mine. Another answers from a hummock away, overlapping in time, no one quite on the beat, no one quite off it. My voice is instantly a chorus, and I am no longer a single thing under the moss. And the note I keep hearing is not a croak. It is thinner, clearer, higher than the turf. It comes not from beneath me but from the slope itself, as if the slope were breathing through a reed.

So I try to lift.

I could be a pipit, hanging in the wind above the rim, pouring out a long glassy note and answering another from far across the bowl so that you have to turn in a full circle to find me. In fog at five paces I would be a voice without a body, which is perhaps why visitors lower theirs. Glassy, that note is. It changes pitch as a cloud passes.

But the pipits leave at evening. For a minute the bowl is only wind and water, and then the frogs start deeper. The thin note does not leave with them. It returns whenever the wind is right and the air is wet, long after the last pipit call from the rim. I do not fly. I do not perch. I hang in the wind, yes — but I stay where I stand.

And staying — that pulls me down again, into the moss that is the skin of the place.

I could be that skin: worn velvet, settled hummocks, gullies at rest, nothing newly broken. I could be lichen on the windward basalt, crusts of rust and sage and sulphur laid down in perfect maps that never quite touch each other, as if they had agreed long ago not to crowd. I could be a cushion plant stitched tight to the ground, holding the heat of the sun against the perpetual cool, wasting no height, with flowers that face down and open only after the fog has passed, as if listening for it.

That lasting is closer to my temperament than the frog’s hiding or the pipit’s flight. Everything here has already become what it is and stayed that way for a very long time. Even the wind has learned not to tear at it, only to pass over. I would not cross the bowl in twenty seconds like the raptor. I would not run a thread across the moss like a shrew and end. I would not crop the knee-high shrubs alone for days like the grazer, then suddenly sprint at the fox. Those lives are in process all day, and the bowl is small enough to watch the whole chase. When the chase ends, the moss closes over again and the dripping resumes. I am what resumes.

But flat moss does not sing. Sedge rattles like dry paper when the wind circles inside the rim. That is only a rustle, and louder than emptiness ought to allow, but it is not a note.

There is one place in the bowl where staying becomes sounding.

Along the windward edge — opposite the leeward cliffs where the crown weeps — the moss does not lie down. It stands up in tight, erect tufts. There the wind arrives already funneled by the rim, already thickened by cloud pouring over and wringing itself out on the stones. When the tufts are wet, the air does not comb through them sidewise and make paper. It passes through them and makes a clear, thin note. I can feel a cloud pass before I see it, because the pitch changes as the air thickens. I do not need a throat. I am the reed, and the wetness, and the right speed of wind.

That is more itself than frog, more itself than pipit, more itself than skin. I am not wind, and not cloud, and not moss alone. I am what happens when those three meet at a right angle on the windward slope.

And I am never unanswered. The stone-frogs answer the moss from beneath the turf, keeping their own hummocks, keeping their distance. I call — if a note can be a call — across a few inches that lichen have agreed never to cross, and the answer comes from under the ground where I cannot touch it. That interval between note and answer is my whole country. I do not need to travel it. I live in it.

Humans who cross here lower their voices when they hear it. I understand why. It sounds like something breathing, and breathing is a thing one does not interrupt. Their cairns and hut and fire-ring are attempts to mark a path, to shelter, to burn. Nothing built here will be finished. I am not finished either. Each wind makes me again, and each stillness unmakes me into moss.

At night the temperature drops fast. Freeze beads the moss with ice that will not melt until ten. The sedge stiffens. The springs upwell in the hollows, the tarn migrates a few feet across the peat, the stones tick as they cool. My note stops. I am only wet, and stiff, and cold, holding ice in my tufts the way the basalt holds water in its palms. Morning fog comes over the rim again, sun warm on the back, wind cutting straight through, breath visible even at noon — and I drink first, on the windward edge, and stand up in the moss.

So I would find myself there, and I would be that: one tight tuft among the band on the windward edge, erect when the wind is right, silent when it is not, wet more often than dry, singing my little distance to the frogs below and to no one else.

Not a voice that lives in the bowl. The place where the bowl breathes through a reed.
