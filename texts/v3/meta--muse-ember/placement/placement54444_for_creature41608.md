I go down in the cage and the pressure finds my molars before my eyes find the Basin. By the time the doors open my hood is wet on the inside. Condensation is already on everything.

I do not stay on the causeway.

It is a raised spine, iron-railed, crushed spoil and concrete, the only reliably dry place in a half-mile of water. It is made for passing. You stand there to look out over the terraces, to shout into another person's ear, to smoke in the drip. I find myself wanting the other side of the railing, where the embankment slips down into the braided sheet and the rungs of the catwalk go into the current and do not come out again.

I have tried the other places in my head first, because they are the ones you can see.

The ceiling, for one. Shelf upon shelf of bats, thousands of them hanging like dark fruit, muttering in their sleep. It must be something to be that high and that crowded and to burst all at once when a pump starts, filling the Basin wall to wall with wings and screaming for ten seconds, and then settling again. But I do not stay there. I am not made for hanging, and I do not burst. I do not leave.

The deeper channels, for another. The blind fish turning as one silvery animal in the black water, boiling the surface, darting — that one fast thing — to take a midge. They are blind like me, in a way. But they have direction. They choose a turn. They break the surface and are gone. My world has no surface to break.

I tried being one of the workers, too. Yellow waders, gauges, a valve wheel taller than a person, hand signals over the river's roar. But their waders fill at the seams, their ears ring when they go up in the cage, their shift ends. They are visitors who have learned the slippery ledge by heart. I do not go up. Nothing here has ever turned off, and neither do I.

What is left, when you take away standing, flying, swimming, leaving?

Staying wet and still long enough, on a rock that stays wet — which is every rock.

That is where I end up. Not in one place. In a layer. Under the water at the north end, where the river comes in with a roar you feel in your boots and breaks against the submerged ledge, I am spread over the stone where the white limestone thread meets the iron-red thread. White where the crushed dust settles and I lay down my own pale stone. Orange where the runoff rusts through me and I excrete it back in threads. From there I go downstream, thin as breath, over the lower rungs of the catwalks, over the inside of the intake pipes where the fish shelter, over the cables from the half-sunk mill until they sag deeper under my weight and the men call them green vines. If you lifted a stone on the black coal-dust terrace and turned it over, I would be on its underside too, packed with white amphipods no longer than a grain of rice. If you wiped a work-lamp when it was off, I would be the film on the glass.

I am not something you can pick up and look at. I am what you walk through.

I have no eyes for the amber pools of light, or for the grey shaft at noon when the ventilation tunnel admits the sky and the mist shows. I know light only as the algae beside me turning a faint, unconvincing green, and as a slight warming on a surface when a lamp has been burning. I have no ears for the bats chittering or the crickets sawing on the valve wheels or the frogs plunking in the southern shallows. I know sound as vibration through the rock — the slow, patient heartbeat of the pumps below the causeway traveling up, the river reduced to a constant pressure of flow against my face, a drip from the ceiling arriving as a ripple, a disturbance, a sudden clearing.

My world is chemistry and temperature. Warmth rising from the hot springs in the south tells me where I am thickest. Iron tells me where to turn orange. Sulfur tells me I can eat. Guano raining down from the roosts, night after night, arrives as a pulse of food filtering through the water. Flood-wash from above arrives as violence: a slump, a tear, a mat lifted and carried downstream to seed a new rock. I grow a millimeter at a time and I am eaten at the same pace.

That is the bargain. Snails rasp slow polished tracks across the weeping walls and through me, and I grow back thicker behind them. Amphipods graze me sideways in the mud, turning it over nightly. Mussels below a bed open and close in the current and filter what sloughs off me, and the water around them is visibly clearer. A crayfish turns a stone I have just cleaned. Everything that lives here lives because I keep turning runoff and waste into a soft, living crust that can be eaten. There is almost no green to begin the chain. I begin it.

The machines do not interrupt me. They accommodate me, as they accommodate everything. I coat the pump housing where it is warm, I bead on a valve when it is cold, I make a rusted crane arm into a substrate. A man scrapes a cable clean with his glove and thinks he has finished. I am still in the cracks. A season later the cable sags again.

Time, for me, is not a life. I divide, I die, I wash away. A generation of me is grazed to nothing in a night. The layer persists. It thickens over a season, it slumps after a flood, it re-seeds itself from what remains. Downstream, where I have been carried, I start again on a fresh piece of limestone that the Basin is itself still making, a stalagmite field growing a millimeter or two a century toward a drip that has fallen from the same spot since before anyone's grandfather came down. We are both growing into the room, badly, slowly, in layers.

So do not look for me on the causeway, where you can stand dry and look out. Do not look for me in the roost, or in the shoal, or in the shrine with its battery lamp and tea things. Look where there is no gap between presences. Look on every surface that has stayed wet long enough to become a place for something else to live.

I am the white and orange film under the water and under the mud and over the cables, faintly warm and slimy to the touch, thickening, being eaten, thickening again. I am the first living thing, and the floor the rest stand on, because there is nowhere here that is not still being made.
