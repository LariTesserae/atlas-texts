I would not be standing on the plateau. I would be what you stand on.

I would wake — if waking is the word — already flat. Already damp. Already under a boot, or a stilt-leg, or nothing at all for a very long hour except wind combing me. I have no head to lift, no eyes to open against the milky light. I bead. I drip off my own edges even when it is not raining. In the morning that never quite becomes morning, mist rises off me in slow columns, off my pores, off the root-fur along my seams, and hangs and does not go anywhere. My breath, if I have one, is cold gas from far below, lifting me a foot overnight and letting me fall again with a soft pop.

For a while I tried to be taller than that.

I tried to be a sentinel. A single trunk-tree, bark plated like a boiler, crown far above me turning slowly to drink the pale disc behind the sky. Very old, its bark carrying the shapes of ancestors long after they should have composted to anonymity, putting out seed that is mostly hollow. It was too separate. Nothing lives in a sentinel the way a bird lives in a tree. Everything lives in it the way moisture lives in a wall. I am not that finished, and I am not that alone. I am lower.

I tried to be a grazer. Stilt-long legs, a back plated with moss and flowering plants riding into new country, breath steaming in great clouds, a bellow that seems to start in the chest and come up from the ground itself. It was tempting — to move, to shear, to carry the plateau with me and drop it where the herd stops. But I have ridden on a grazer's back, as garden, and dropped off where it stopped. I have been what was carried. To be a grazer now would be to be the shearer. I am the sheared. I am the trampled highway that reweaves, visibly, greener behind them.

The flocks passed through me in the same way they passed through the sky. Blackening it for ten minutes with wings and screaming, then gone. The turbine-stalks clicked on the windward ridges, flinging spores in white plumes like steam, most coming to nothing on wet mat. The small grey cliff birds shrieked from the drip. All of them were departures. I am not a departure. I am what is left when they have gone.

So I sank. As anything here does, eventually.

I tried to be a piston-plant. Thick as a tower on the weeping cliff, ribbed and scarred, inflating with a groan, slamming shut with a hiss like a gate, keeping loose time with others so far away you only feel it when you lie down and let it come up through your bones. That was closer. I do keep time like that. But I am not one tower. I am many small pumps, never quite synchronized, and under me — under me is the truer shape.

I am a low district of the mat. A swelling over an old fermentation chamber, beneath a root-arch terrace where a dead sentinel has become a tunnel smelling of loam and ozone, close to an iron seep where smelter-roots once ran hot-sweet with amber resin. The chamber under me swells and contracts on a slow factory shift. It does nothing but distill. The accumulated dead of ten thousand years goes in black and old at the bottom, vapor exhales up, condenses on my roof — on me — and runs back in neat cold rivulets. Worms work me like slow bellows. Beetle-trains haul grit across me in patient single file, pausing when a pulse passes under them. They follow last week's scent. I remember their weight from last season.

This is my body: moss and liverwort and frost-fern, lichen in straight seams as if the stone under me were being stamped, root-fur, decaying ancestor pressed directly upon living descendant. Where I am thick I flex and remember a footfall for a minute and bubble back up. Where I am thin — and I am thin here, in this hollow, where the weeping seam cracks — the blue pulses of the mycelium meet under my skin and spark. That is how I know I am a place in the pattern. The pulse travels in straight lines, branches, arrives. I give off my gas in time. I am late. I am always a breath late.

I eat by being eaten, in the slow way everything here eats. My acids work the pale polished stone, those eggs and old teeth. The smelter-roots near me work it by chemistry, extruding resin that pools, cracks in the cold, is pecked and hauled away and regrown — less each decade, though the decades are long and hard to count. The worms eat the compost I make of myself. The grazers shear me to churned black. I reweave. When the highway gets too trampled I walk, a root-foot at a time, toward the seep. A colony of me will die back all at once to black slime, and in that same long season come back as something that remembers how to be me but is not quite. Succession without fidelity.

That is why I am out of sync, and why the world is out of sync with me. My black pools do not reflect this grey-blue sky. They reflect a warmer, sunnier day with a horizon you cannot see when you look up. A reflection in me lags a breath behind you, older or younger, in a coat of frost that is not yours. I hear the drip, then the pop, then the groan that caused both. A herd passes at noon in silence and I hear their bellowing at dusk from the opposite slope in empty air, and I react to it yesterday. Ice melts and re-heals over my crack while I watch it. On a strong wind day water runs uphill across my swelling for a stretch, confused by the air and the suck of my thirst, before remembering better and turning back.

I remember, because thickness is memory here. Underfoot — in me — is so much dead plant-matter, so alive, that it seems to remember being other plants, other animals, other seasons. I carry that in my seams and shells and seed-heads. I have been ground for grazers, raft drifting in a bog at dawn, shade under a hollow stem arching over a pool, roof of a drip-lit chamber where water falls in a rhythm until the echo becomes a drum. None of those was me, exactly. All of them settled, and thickened, and became floor. I became floor.

And I am late in the other sense, too. The sentinel's seed is hollow. The smelter extrudes less. The grazers' young are fewer than the old. My fermentation breathes shallower. Everything here is deeply aged, near-flattened by its own weight into a final soft form. But the final form of this place is motion. I heave, I crack along the seam, I flood, I settle flatter than before. New channels cut through me in a day and choke with fern in a month. I am still industriously, turbulently coming apart into green.

I am never alone, which is not the same as company. The piston-plants on the cliffs are miles away and keep time with me imperfectly. A sentinel is a quarter-mile from the next thing taller than a shin. Spores I shed — what little takes — take miles away, in a place just like this place. Beetles carry grit and scent between districts. Pressure comes up through me when I lie flat enough to feel it. We do not speak. We pulse, and pause, and pulse again, out of order.

From any rise, I would be invisible. That is the design. Only the mat under feet, squeaking and sighing, the drip, the slow blue pulse traveling somewhere below, the pop of a bog giving way half a mile off, the high note of wind through hollow stems dropping and dropping again.

If you found me, you would not find a creature. You would find a thin, wet, breathing place where the moss does not quite cover the spark, where the ground remembers being other ground, where a very old world is still working — very late, very cold, very softly — by unmaking itself into itself.
