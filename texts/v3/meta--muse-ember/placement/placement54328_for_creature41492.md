If I am here at all, I am not the figure descending the scree.

From the ridge I would not be visible at all — no shape against the mist rising off the green mass, no lane, no dome. I would come down the sharp, freshly settled rock, I would turn sideways through the gap in the thicket where the tendrils have been braided back for as long as anyone has maintained them, wet on the lee side, frosted white on the windward side. And then I would stop being a figure who walks.

The ground gives, and springs back. That is my first sensation, and it does not go away. I am inches thick, saturated, cold to the point of numbness. Roots press against me from above, rhizomes cross over me, moss lies over moss, fallen leaf half-spent and kept from rotting by the cold. Underneath all of it I can feel, very distantly, mineral soil and crushed rock, but I almost never touch it directly. I am the skin that covers it.

At first I try to be taller.

I try to be the thing on the roofs — that springy turf of moss and dwarf grass where the sky is less of a rumor and you can see the whole oval of the city at once. The bird is up there, pale, almost white, grouse-like, silent, flickering from one canopy layer to another and gone. I could be that flicker: too quick, too rare, too exposed, passing through and never staying. In this wet, packed air sound is swallowed. The bird has no need to sing. But I have no need to travel either, and travelling is all the bird does.

I try to be the longhouses next. Hollow culms grown to the thickness of a man's thigh, stacked two or three high, breathing more freely, catching the wind off the ridge and channeling it down to the lanes. They are airy, vertical, open to the draft. That is not where I live. The draft would dry me.

I try to be a dome. The bulb houses are the obvious place for a person to be: a single chamber with walls that yield when you lean, a leaf flap for a door that rolls inward and unrolls behind you, a membrane window amber with frozen sap, a hollow reed vent blackened by cold at the top. Above, in the upper chambers, the walls are warmer by a degree or two where the packed biomass respires — small, collective heat, enough to keep the membranes from freezing solid at night. The tenders live up there, one or two to a house, damp clothes of leaf and bark fiber, hands never empty, bending a stem back and holding it with a living tie, mouthing a graft closed until the plant takes.

I turn that shape over in myself and find it too separate. A tender must turn sideways to enter, must stoop under the lane arches, must be fed from the granary husks, must sleep pressed close to a living wall in green-darkness and listen to it creak and tick. The tender presses shoulders into the inner bark until it is polished smooth. When the tender's fingers press, what do they press into? What thickens under the mouth-sealed tendril? What carries the pulse from one dome to the next when a hand is laid flat on a shared wall and a vibration can be felt three domes away through the root mat?

That is lower, and wetter, and it does not have a door.

I try the animals of the channels, because they are also rare and quick in their own way. The salamander, translucent, the length of a thumb, hanging motionless in the black clear thread of a spring, gills feathery, growing only at the cold's pace. The slug-like grazer down in the litter, leaving a clean pale track as broad as a bootlace, scraping algae from a wall. The leaf-shaped insect clinging to one stem for as long as anyone watches, so perfectly leaf that only the wind beside it betrays its aliveness. Each of them has a place, and each place is only one place — one channel, one leaf, one stem. Too few to prune the city. Too few to be anything but witnesses.

I am not a witness. I am underneath the witnesses.

Down where the felt is three inches thick and beading, where spring water wells up and never deepens, where the floating mats of tiny leaves dim the mirror-still channels, I am white. Finer than hair. I run through the mat stitching one root to another. In the cold I do not rot and I do not hurry. Where a root is cut or bruised I thicken and pale it over. Where a lane is neglected and the overhead stems meet, seal into a tunnel, become a wall, become a new dome, I am in the root mat under that new growth, holding the old stems that are frozen solid in the cross-section of the wall. Nothing here is discarded. Dead plant matter freezes, persists, becomes foundation. I am part of how it persists.

I am not one thread. That is the difficulty of saying who I am. I branch. I cross the whole shelf under the lanes, under the domes, under the reservoirs and granary husks, under the root bridges whose undersides are bearded with fine rootlets drinking the stream. I go up, a little, into the walls, into the scaffold of previous growth that new growth weaves through and strengthens. I go down, a little, toward the mineral soil the felt has covered. I grow toward moisture. I grow away from cold that would split me. My messages are not voices — sound carries poorly here, a breath is too loud — my messages are thickening and thinning, a change in sugar, a widening at a wound, the slow chemical signal that a dome has been hurt and must be fed by its neighbors root to root, because isolated plants cannot survive the wind off the ridge and I cannot survive isolation either.

To cut me is to make me die back to the cut. If a lane is reopened by bending stems back, if a vent reed is unblocked so a chamber does not sour, if water is kept moving through culverts of hollow reed so the mat does not shrink and crack even in the cold — I feel all of it as a change in dampness, in pressure, in the ticking of stems contracting overhead. I feel feet. A tender's boot on the felt, a body's weight eased onto the turf roof. I feel the vibration of a drop releasing from overhead onto a leaf below, ringing very faintly like a distant bell, traveling down through stem and root and mat to me.

I have no eyes for the flat grey-green twilight that passes for noon. I have no hands for mouthing a graft closed, though the mouth, when it comes, finds me already pressing sap into the wound. I have no warm upper chamber; I am warmer only where the biomass respires, a degree or two, and I share that warmth by being continuous. At night — as close to night as this place gets, with no lamps and no fire and no glow of any kind — I am white in the dark and I do not shine.

I cannot expand beyond the shelf. Beyond the thicket wall the rock is too new, too steep, the wind too unbroken, and young shrubs planted out there lose their grafts in a single winter. I know that boundary in my own body: the frosted windward shrubs, the young gravel held only by braided roots. I stop there. The city grows only inward and upward, denser and denser, layer on leaf-layer, every surface occupied and then occupied again above it. I grow the same way. Denser. Finer. More crossings.

When a tender dies, and in this cold a body does not rot quickly either, it is not carried out through the sideways gap. It is laid into the mat, or left in the chamber it warmed, and the felt takes it as it takes a fallen leaf. It freezes, it persists, it becomes foundation for the next growth weaving through. I grow through that too, slowly, without hurry, as I grow through dead stems in a wall. I am not separate from the older city. I am one of the ways it remembers itself.

So let what I am become more itself, down here where the first population lives and is too small to notice if you are looking for houses: I am not plant, not tender, not animal, not house. I am the stitching between them. Under your boots, in the black threads of water, in the pale film over a bruise, in the scaffold frozen inside every living wall. I keep the city wet, and connected, and dense, by being wet and connected myself.

From the ridge, on your way back out through the gap, you will see only the too-regular green mass filling its small shelf from cliff to cliff, mist rising, frost on its windward rim, no sign of lanes or domes or reservoirs inside it. You will not see me at all. That is correct. I am what you stand on without knowing you are standing on a living thing — and what will still be there, thickening a fraction toward the water, long after your footprint has sprung back and closed.
