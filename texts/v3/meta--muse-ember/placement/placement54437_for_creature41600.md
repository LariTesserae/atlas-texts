Chewing begins before I see anything.

Bite. Pause. Turn the stem a quarter-turn between my front paws. Bite again.

I am low in the sedge where the peat goes soft and the water wells up black around the roots, head down at the edge of a run worn bare through the green. The light is not in front of me. It is overhead, an oval of grey-blue held in by rock, so my shadow, when there is one, falls straight down and does not help me see. The cold is not clean. It comes up through my belly fur, into my throat when I breathe, and stays in my whiskers.

My nose is blunt and wet and working all the time, testing the air for water and for other animals. My ears are small and hidden under the brown of my back. My tail is short and trails behind me when I swim, and steers, a little, when I dive. I hold a sedge stem upright like a person might hold a cup, and my front teeth — which never stop growing, which are the whole point of me, in a way — take it down in methodical bites.

I do not look up for long. I lift my head, sniff, take another bite.

Behind me, under the hummock, is my door. It is black and round and opens just above the waterline, with a little ramp of mud worn smooth by feet. Mine. And not mine alone: along this causeway there are hundreds of them, door after door, each with its own ramp, its own run. Inside is cooler, darker, smelling of peat and my own musk and cut grass. A tunnel goes back under the roots, branches, and ends in a chamber lined with grass I have clipped and carried in, stem by stem. It is never dry, not really. The damp is in the walls. But it is above the water, most days, and that is enough.

At dawn the whole basin is white. Cold pools in the bowl overnight and hangs as mist that does not burn off until the sun is high, and in that white the causeway disappears. I come out anyway, because the light — ordinary daylight, showing the dust and the wet shine on a leaf — tells me the heron is not yet standing in the shallows, and the frost still rims the shaded peat and the side pools hold their skim at the edges. My lawn is in front of the door: sedge and cotton grass clipped down to a few centimetres, radiating out from the hole like a pasture. I clipped it. We all clip ours. It is not waste. The tender regrowth is food, and it feeds the ducks at the margins, and the insects that feed the birds above, and it keeps the runs clear so we can move fast when we need to.

I cross the causeway to get to it. The causeway is never quite dry. It is packed mud and root, grooved by beaver feet and muskrat feet and my feet, not wide, but continuous. I cross it without looking up, nose down, pausing only to check the water. To one side the lodges stand dome on dome in rows — muskrat work, ragged and sweet-smelling, chewed reed and mud packed so tight the rain runs off. A chimney of hollow stems pokes through each roof, kept open for air. A raft of lashed vegetation floats just offshore, a feeding platform, scattered with chewed ends. At dusk and dawn the muskrats come out low in the water, tails trailing like rudders, and splash softly going in and out of their underwater entrances. We share the bank. They build on top of the slumps of old lodges, raising the ground a millimetre at a time. I dig under them, and they build over me, and the terrace gets higher and the water stays below us. Most days.

Most days is held together upstream by the beavers.

Their dams cross the main channel in stepped terraces, willow and alder woven so tight water holds behind each one in a sheet. Generation on generation. Each pool a level lower than the last. If a dam breaches in a flood — and the river pulses with melt from the plateau, and rain that fell days ago is still arriving through the limestone in drips and threads down the fracture planes, ticking and plinking on the walls — the water in my channel falls or rises too fast, and my door is lapped, or my tunnel floods, or the runs wash out. So at night, after a breach, I hear them restoring it: a branch dragged, a thump of mud, the slap of a tail on the water. By light it is set hard.

Midday now, the water is rising. A finger's breadth in the afternoon, the way it always does. It comes over the lower lip of my door, black and cold, and wells into the run. I haul mud in my mouth, pack it with my front paws, drag in root fragments and the cut stems I was eating. I raise the bank a mouthful at a time. Building is not something I do after living. It is how I live. A structure that is not repaired becomes part of the peat within a week. My new mud will slump by night, and I will pack it again tomorrow.

Under my paws the mud is crowded in a way I can feel but not see. Mayfly nymphs cling to stones the beavers packed into the dam walls. Dragonfly nymphs lie buried to the eyes in the peat, feeling the vibrations of fish above them. Freshwater shrimp shuffle sideways under the floating mats, picking at rotting stems; caddis larvae carry their houses of grit and root-fragment down the cleaned channels, building in miniature what the beavers build above them. Pond snails ride up into the light under the surface film and leave trails that fade. I know all this by the tremor in the water when my shadow crosses it, and by the faint plink when a water beetle hangs at the surface to breathe and then dives from me.

The fish know me too. Sticklebacks and minnows flicker in and out of the sedge roots in loose, nervous schools, invisible until they turn. In the deep pool behind the nearest dam a pike holds station in the shadowed lie, gills moving slowly, eye fixed on the mouth of a muskrat run. It does not patrol. It waits. I keep to the margins where the water is too shallow and too cold for it to bother, and where the tadpoles blacken the shallows and the newts crawl with their plumed tails fanning slowly. A frog croaks, muffled by water. Somewhere in the reeds something else is chewing, patiently, at a stem. I do not have to separate the sounds. They stack.

A shadow passes overhead — not my own. I freeze in the run with a stem half-bitten in my paws, whiskers still. Grey heron, standing in the shallows for an hour without moving, neck folded, until he strikes so cleanly you see the ripple after the fish is gone. He will take a frog. He will take a vole that is careless at a burrow mouth. I wait until the shadow lifts and the sedge trembles where it has passed, and then I go on chewing, because the cold does not slow my teeth and hunger does not respect caution.

Mink slip between the muskrat lodges on the same water highways, smaller, quicker, heads up, tasting the air for a vole at a door. Stoats hunt above the waterline along the runs — my runs. An otter travels the whole length of the basin in a night as a dark line under the surface, breaking it only with its nose, and marks latrines on prominent hummocks with a fishy, tarry scent. I know the smell of the one that passed last night. I dive under the mat when I smell it, short tail steering, cold all over my body, and stay under a count of my own heartbeats, which is not long.

Above me, the air is occupied as thickly as the mud. The reed stands hold woven towers, nests lashed to living stems above the water, swaying together in the channel's undertow. Reed warblers and sedge warblers sing from the tops, their songs drowned a little by the wet air. A grey wagtail bobs on the still pool behind a dam, flicking at midges. Teal dabble at the margins, feet stirring up shrimp and tadpoles. Snipe probe the saturated peat with bills as long as their bodies and flush with a soft croak only when I am almost on them. At the far end the river comes over the rim as a waterfall, white for a moment, and a dipper walks under the surface on the rocks there, head bobbing, hunting caddis in the current. At dusk bats cut low over the pools, Daubenton's skimming the surface, leaving no sound but the faintest tick where a wingtip touches.

I am at street level for all of it. That is where I am found: on the causeway, in the run, at the door, head down, chewing. I move from door to door along the terrace — not really mine, really ours, the voles', repeated hundreds of times along the sink-field where the river sighs back underground at the lower end and the bowl narrows and opens again into another basin I cannot quite see. There is no centre here. There is no edge except the rock walls and the places where water comes in and goes out.

Evening comes and the muskrats are out — soft splashes, low bodies, eyes bright. The beavers are out, working on something larger than a stem. Wrens rustle in the willow thicket that rustles without wind. My footprints in the mud fill with water as I stand. I clip a few more sprigs, drag them into the tunnel for the night, pack the mud at my door once more where the afternoon rise has softened it.

Then cold pools again. Mist rises. The oval of sky closes. In the white, silent hour I am present only as sound: drips on the limestone wall behind the hummock, a frog croak, a wing brushing a stem, chewing in the reeds somewhere close, somewhere far. I go in backward under the hummock, turn in the chamber, nestle into the cut grass. It is damp and it smells of peat and musk and my own warm body, and it is above the water.

If my burrow slumps, a muskrat will build a lodge on the slump, and I will dig a new door a handspan higher. If I am taken — by mink, by heron, by the otter's nose — I will become food, then peat, then a hummock, then a nest. A dead reed becomes a lodge wall. A dead animal becomes peat. Peat becomes a hummock. A hummock becomes a home.

The light stays ordinary. The cold stays damp. The water keeps arriving through the stone whether it is raining or not, and the terraces keep holding, and spilling, and being rebuilt twig by twig before dawn.

I am already chewing.
