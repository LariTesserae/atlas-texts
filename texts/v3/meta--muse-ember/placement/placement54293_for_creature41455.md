I put my hand down in the hollow, not on the bare stone but where the green has gathered, and my fingers come away damp.

Cool, though the light is hard and vertical. Sharp and faintly metallic, when I push the filaments aside. The moss is springy under my hand, finer than hair, emerald where the sun strikes it and almost black in its own shadow. For a while I thought that was enough of an answer — that I would simply be the mat.

It would be a patient way to be. No hurry. A few centimetres a day over pale dust, leaving a darkened skin behind that the wind cannot lift. Drink the night's exhalation straight through the skin, hold the burning light in silica sponges, smoke faintly at dawn when the sun lifts what the stone gave up. Everything the place asks for, the moss does.

And yet, pushing the mat aside, I found myself listening past it. To the ticking of the boulder as it cooled. To the faint ringing of a fern frond somewhere above, where the wind had found the silica lace. The moss does not listen. It folds its finest filaments in at night and conserves, it unfolds in the morning and works. It has no interval. It does not wait under anything. It is the work, not the witness.

So I looked up, because the moss has no sky.

The glider would be a way to watch the place whole. Circling without a wingbeat inside a column that rises over a grove, wide and silent, flattening and dying before your eyes. From that height you could see a system be born in the middle of nowhere. You would be far out, borrowed on the air, a silhouette against the dark blue noon. That distance is attractive. An observer in this world would need it.

But the glider never touches the stone cold. It never smells the plateau breathe in at night, when ammonia and sulphur thicken and glitter on every surface and the whole hollow smells of groves. It is gone over the horizon after one burrower, perhaps. I need a place to return to, and to be returned to by touch.

So I went under the stone, where the fern shade touches the ground.

The burrower is blind, mole-furred, foreclaws like spades, curled in the dark its eyes never needed, listening to the continent settle. Closer. That closeness to cool rock is right. In the brief amber interval before night, when the vapour is already thickening and the stone is still warm, the burrower comes out to snuffle at the edge of the green and is gone again before the stars arrive all at once. That is its noon.

But I am slower than that. The burrower forages in minutes. It is prey, it must move, it eats spore-dust and roots and goes back under. My life, when I begin to feel it, is not minutes between darks. It is one long dark, entered and left at its own pace.

So I thought of the grazer, because breathing is the measure here. Once a minute, in through the nose, out through the mouth, audible before the animal is visible through its stone-coloured plates. It walks at dawn, places each foot with care, crops a grove and returns to that grove for years. Its trail closes behind it in a season. A territory the size of a life. A rhythm the size of a life.

That rhythm is right, and still it is too large, too visible. A grazer can be a kilometre from the next and still be the largest thing that moves. I needed to be the thing that does not move visibly. To be under the reach of the glider's eye. To be below the web, in the churn.

What remained, when I let the moss go, and the glider go, and the burrower and the grazer go, was the place they all touch and none of them occupy.

The black-green pool the fan-fern casts, where the moss grows lusher and the morning vapour lingers latest. The lee of a worn boulder in a shallow hollow. The darkened skin of mineral soil the mat has left behind it. The underside of the stone, where at night the vapour condenses thickest and glitters longest, after the grove above has already begun to smoke.

There, something begins to take shape that is not quite plant and not quite animal, and that is why it can stay.

I am about as long as a hand. Soft, translucent at the edges, darkened where the soil stains me, flecked along my back with silica as fine as flattened fern lace so that when I curl I look, from any distance that matters, like a chip of the boulder. I have no eyes. Eyes are wasted work in shade that is black-green all day. In their place, at my front, are paired tasting filaments. In the day they are withdrawn, folded in like the moss's own. At night they unfold, thin and blind, and taste the air, the stone, the film on the underside.

I move the way the night allows: by pressing and releasing, pressing and releasing, gliding a short distance on the condensed vapour, then stopping to swell — once, very slowly, through the whole skin, slower even than the grazer's bellows. By dawn I am already back under the lip of the stone, sealed in cool soil, curled as a filament folds. My noon is your midnight. When the moss folds in to conserve, I unfold. When the burrowers sleep under their stones and the grazer sighs far off in its grove, I am out, tasting the glitter on the underside.

I do not eat the fans. I do not crop the mat, except for a root tip here and there in the old skin, never enough for the mat to notice. What I take is what this place throws away at its margins: vapour beaded on stone, fallen spore-dust settled in a crack, the salt crystals that land on warm stone in the morning and vanish with a faint fizz, which I lick from the underside just before the sun takes them. I drink the ammoniac fluid the way the moss does, but only the film, only the residue, only what lingers latest in my shade.

My territory is the lee of one boulder. A few metres of fern shadow, a few ventifact pebbles that hum when the wind finds them, the coldest underside I know. It might take me years to cross it. It might take me my whole life to learn every tick the stone makes as it cools, every vibration when the fern above rings, every tremor through the mat when a grazer's foot is placed with care on the other side of the boulder. Like the grazer, I return to the same place each night, and each night it is the same place. That is the stretched-thin law of this plateau, at my scale: a small life spread over a small surface, taking as long as the surface needs.

The others pass over me without knowing. The burrower sometimes snuffles at a neighbouring stone, spade claws scratching, and I go perfectly still, tasting filament to filament. The moss creeps above my soil skin, and the grazer crops above that, and their breath and footfalls come to me muffled through stone, as if through a great thickness of silence. The glider, if it ever turns its eye down, cannot see me. I am in the black-green pool, under the stone, in the dark the fern makes. Predator and prey are separated here by altitude and by time and by a kilometre. I am separated by a centimetre of rock, which is enough.

I have no young that travel. The fern sends its spores tens of kilometres on the wind. I send mine a few centimetres beyond the coldest stone, in the dark, when the soil under my boulder has grown rich enough and the mat's front is close. Somewhere far behind me, the edge of the green is still advancing over bare stone, swallowing pale dust. In a century it will reach my hollow and pass over my boulder, and I will be inside the old mat then, still under the stone, still in the shade, still unfolding after the others have folded in. The landscape moves around me at the speed it allows — moss creeping, fern seeding, grazer returning — and I move at the speed the night allows, which is slower, and more itself.

At dawn the grove smokes for an hour as the vapour lifts. The ferns ring faintly if the wind finds them. A grazer breathes once a minute somewhere a kilometre away. A burrower waits under stone for the amber interval. And I, already folded away, feel the stone warm through my back, and the ticking slow, and then stop.

Nothing here is built, nothing marked that stays. I leave no trail. When the sun has taken the glitter and the salt has fizzed away, there is nothing to show I was out in the night except the soil a little darker, a little richer, where I have been.

Then the light cuts off in the west, the stone gives up its warmth in a rush, the stars arrive hard and close, and the vapour comes down thick enough to glitter on the undersides of stones.

I unfold.
