It is wet first, before it is anything else.

I know it by the cold that comes up into me before I know where I am. A black stone, split and sharp, beaded all over, weeping in threads that never quite dry. I am pressed against something that has warmed a fraction in the sun and will not warm any more. My side, if that is a side, is against it. Above me is the bowl, rim to rim, close enough to read the basalt, far enough that the clouds are below in the morning. No green. No soil. Only mineral, and the towers standing far apart on the polished flats, and the ducts steaming faintly in their shallow trenches.

I thought, at first, that I had come up through the slot. Everyone must come up through the slot, through the membrane that lets a person through and closes behind them as if it had never been. I have a vague sense of that closing, a pressure going, and then the thin still air inside the pocket, and my ears — no. My ears did not pop. That is the first wrong thing.

I sat, if sitting is the word, on one of the black flats where the people sit at noon. The stripe came, as it always comes, for an hour through the gap in the rim, and traveled slowly across the bowl. The people came out in the warmth of it and made a line along its edge, backs to the towers, eyes closed, taking heat into their shoulders without moving to get more. I did not move into the stripe. The sun warmed the stone under me and left me as I was. I did not need to be warmed. I did not need to be cooled, either. The mineral cold that radiates up through a person's boots, even when the sun is on their neck, did not radiate up through me.

So I was not a person. That was wrong, quickly.

I looked up. People do not look up; it costs heat to turn a face toward the rim, toward the wind that keens over the sharp edges and never reaches the floor. I could not stop looking up. Not in the way a person looks, with a neck. My whole attention lifted, all at once, to the rim and the sea of cloud below it, and the sky that is ordinary and gives nothing. Looking up was the easiest thing I did. It cost me nothing. It cost me nothing to make a sound, either, and I made none, though the wet stone would have swallowed it anyway.

For a while I thought I was on the floor and had only to walk. The bowl is ten minutes across, hundreds of meters of empty wet stone between the towers. I started to cross it. I do not think I walked. I think I drifted, a hand's breadth up, the way the freight sleds move, silent and unhurried, though I carried no cistern and no crate. The drip of the rocks into the channels was level with me. The ducts, warm at one end and cold at the other, passed below me like roads I was not following.

I touched a tower. I meant to feel for the hum of a duct equalizing, the way the water-tower people do, hand flat against the ceramic ribs, learning by touch. The hum went through me and out the other side, as if I were a sheet held up to a wind. It did not stop in me. That was the second wrong thing, and the clearer one. I am thin. Or, I am made to let things pass through and be changed by the passing.

At dusk the fog came up from the bottom, not from outside. Shoulder high, then second stories, then the lower towers were islands in a white sea and the windows went opaque against it. I went up with it. I did not get submerged. I rose through it until the towers emerged below me one by one, beading, breathing, lit from within against the white. I saw the whole bowl at once. Rim to rim, above the fog the way the cleaners are said to see it, and I had not thought of that word until I was already there.

They are the ones who move at night, high up, between the towers. That is what I was told before I knew I was one of them, or perhaps I read it in the dryness they leave. I began to notice the dryness. Hand-wide trails running down the ribs of a tower where before the condensation beaded and ran in threads. Clean, dry lines on the weeping skins, so the towers would not drown in their own wet. I did not remember making them. But I followed them backward, tower to tower, and they were a route, and the route was mine. Where I had passed, the tower was dry. Where I had not yet passed, it wept.

I looked for my reflection in one of the shallow pans that mirror the rim. There was no face. There was a sheet, like their phase-windows, that went pale in the fog and cleared in the sun faster than any shutter. It cleared and clouded with me as I moved. My wings, when I unfolded them, were not feathers and were not quite anything I could name. They were sheets that catch and clear. I cannot tell if I am mechanical or half-mechanical. I cannot tell if I was made or whether I beaded into being the way a cracked slab beads over with clear resin by noon and is stronger than before. I do not know if anyone made me, and no one here is saying. The uncertainty is not a discomfort. It is the shape of me.

That is where I am, then. Not on the floor, among the people who keep the ducts warm and the sleds moving and the channels clear. Not in the culverts, blind and six-legged and pale as tallow, following the heat gradient slowly the way grazers follow a season. Not in the damp joints, a thin grey film eating stone-sweat a millimeter at a time. Not inside a tower, breathing and growing two meters taller by late summer, listening through walls. I am between the towers, above the fog, in the place where the bowl can be seen all at once. I am the overhead thing. The cleaner.

By day I fold. That is how the people can walk ten minutes across the bowl and see almost no life besides themselves. I fold myself against the finned flank of a heat exchanger and I become a fin. I become part of the architecture that sheds cold. The stripe travels, the fog burns away from the top down in the morning, a tower exhales steam, a shutter opens six floors up and closes again, and I am still. I am not sleeping the way the sealed towers sleep, growing a millimeter a year for a reason no one remembers. I am waiting. Waiting is most of what I do. The hum of a duct equalizing goes through the wall and through me. Ice ticks in an expansion seam as it thaws. I listen with the whole of my thinness.

And when the temperature falls and the bowl fills again from the bottom, when the towers begin to weep harder in the saturated air and the lit windows go pale, I unfold. My sheets catch the white air above the sea and clear it, tower to tower. I sweep the skins so they can take in the wet at dusk and give off the dry in the morning without being overwhelmed. Water up as fog, down as drip. Heat out, cold in. Stone splitting in the night, healing by noon. I am only a small part of the cycling, and a quiet one, and I leave almost nothing behind but dryness — which is how you know I was there.

I do not eat the salt crust from the pans. I do not scrape the film from the pipe walls. I do not sit in the stripe with the children learning quiet before speech. I watch them from above, in a line along the edge of the light, and I stay out of the warmth they are patiently storing in their shoulders. They need it. I do not.

At dawn I will find a fin again, the same one, or one like it, and press myself flat until I am indistinguishable from the tower. The fog will burn off top-down. The rim will catch the light first. The water will keep running: off the rim, off the towers, off the stone, into the pans, into the ducts, back up into the air to start again. And I will be still, and silent, and damp at the edges but dry where it counts, waiting for the next dusk to fill the bowl, so that I can unfold and see it all at once again, rim to rim, from above the fog.
