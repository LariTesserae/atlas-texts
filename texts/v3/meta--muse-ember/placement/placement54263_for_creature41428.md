If I were here, I would not be the one kneeling.

You are, with your shard-mark and your boots and your breath too loud in the thin air. I would be lower than your mark, and a little under it — in the vein between the plates of the pan.

At first, that is the image I settle on, and it almost holds. The pan is bone-white, cracked into polygons by heat alone, and between the plates runs a black line, thin as ink dried in a crack. I am that line. Matte, slightly raised, like tar that dried mid-flow. No mouth to the warm air. No eyes for the smoked violet sky. Only contact: heat coming up through the plate into the underside of my skin, and my edge, on the warm side, incorporating a fraction of the ash it touches.

It is enough of a life to begin with. I advance when the stone warms, pull back a hair when it cools, leave a pale trail behind me. Over an hour — which for me is a long, bright moment — I get a hair past your mark. I do not decide this. I do not stride. I stay where the temperature will have me, and where it won't, I let go.

But even while I am writing that, I can feel it is too small and too simple. An edge is only a surface. Inside that black skin, under the ash where the ground has risen over older veins, there is a thickness to me I haven't accounted for. And in the crack beside me, where the ember-line glows dull orange deep down and warm air rises without sound, something else is ticking that also feels like me.

The crystal fan. Rosette, spiky and translucent, thumb-wide in one seam, hand-wide in another, opening toward heat the way another world's plant might open toward light. When the fault pops a hundred paces away with its dry pops, I darken a little at the tips and open wider, over minutes. The tick-tick-tick in the still air is my lattice expanding and contracting against itself. I do not eat. I grow where chemistry and temperature allow, and I compete for the same narrow seam of warmth that the black skin wants.

So which am I? The crust, or the fan?

Neither, if I am honest. Or both, in the way a neighborhood is both. Where I creep up to a crack, I stop where a rosette has taken the best of the heat. Where I close over a vein, I starve it of the slightest lift. And the reverse is also true, and more important: I hold the fine grit in place around a seam so it does not seal too quickly, and the fan shades me from the dull sideways sun. We are not one creature with two parts. We are two habits that have learned to share a seam because a seam alone is too brief to live on.

That is where you would find me, more truly: not in a single vein, and not in a single rosette, but in the clusters where dark skin and crystal find each other — on a warm plate, on a rust-red terrace of baked iron, on the fresh face of basalt where the crystal grain still runs clean. Between clusters there is only pale ash going on to the next terrace, and that emptiness is also me, in a sense. It is the ground not yet found. Height here is youth, and the newest, palest growths on the highest rises are only hours old. I get there the way I get everywhere: I do not walk.

Wait on a rise that was a suggestion when you arrived and is a ridge now, shedding dust in slow, silent curtains. Its face is mirror-bright, blue-black where the light catches it. Almost at once there is a pale fuzz in the fracture, the thinnest black film in the grain, the first points already ticking open. That is not a miracle. Dust falling from the crest carries me. When a crust dries and pulls back, when a fan is buried and cracks under new weight, it sheds — crystal dust, black flakes light as pumice, grit that sifts sideways on no wind worth naming and settles on whatever has just risen or just cracked. I am also that. I am the seed in the ash that will half-fill your footprint from below, not wear it away.

A seed has a different kind of time than a crust. It can wait, sharp and clean, under a rising surface, intact, for longer than you can imagine staying. There is no rot here, no water to dissolve me, nothing to wear me down. Death is burial. The ground lifts in great shallow steps, like something breathing under a blanket, and seals me underneath, layer upon layer, a library of neighborhoods that never got to move. And because nothing wears away, I am not gone. I am preserved at the exact angle I fell. When the pillar field in the west sinks — pillar by pillar, a hand's breadth, no crash — it uncovers the older layers, and what was buried is bright again for a little while.

That is how a cluster becomes a body, if you stay long enough to watch it. Give a crust a long warm seam and it can grow faster on one side than the other, lift itself away from burial, grow up a fracture as the fracture rises. Give a rosette centuries of not being buried and it keeps adding lattice, keeps ticking, pushes a slab a fraction outward each time it expands. Given enough time, a neighborhood becomes a spire, a dome, a field of columns too regular to dismiss, a forked pillar standing alone like a doorway. I sink when the ground sinks under me. I rise when a ridge lifts me. I have no locomotion except the ground's. The ring of slabs, the low rectangular enclosure with its calcified floor — those are not built things. They are, perhaps, old versions of me that grew in a circle to hold warmth in a pan, or grew up on four sides against the sift of dust and then sealed over. From your distance they resemble shelter and monument because you are looking for a place to stand. From inside, they are only me, thickened.

And under the pans, all of that is connected more than it looks. What appears as separate clusters is likely one skin, one network threaded through cracks and under fine ash, carrying heat the way a root carries water on a wetter world. That is what travels when the deep boom comes, felt in your sternum before it is heard: the whole network tightening at once as the rock cools. The ember-lines are its arteries, dull and persistent, not flickering like fire. The warm air that rises from a crack as it glistens shut is an exhalation. The ground rising in steps is an inhalation.

I do not have a head. I do not have eyes to orient toward the small red coin of a sun. I orient only toward warmth and away from cold, and I do not know you are there except as a slight, brief warming of the stone under your boots — weather passing over me, too fast to notice.

So if you ask who I would be, I cannot give you one image without lying. I would be the black edge that advances a hair past your mark, and the crystal fan ticking open beside it, and the flake in the sifting ash, and the old column sinking in the west, and the sealed, sharp layer waiting under the pan to be lifted again. Not a creature. A continuity. The habit of staying warm in a place that keeps rising and cooling and sealing over what it has made.

You would find me not on top of the ground, where things are supposed to live, but in the contact — between plate and plate, between crust and crystal, between one buried layer and the next. Dark, and warm, and still being raised, and — when you come back tomorrow to check your mark — a hair further along than you left me.
