First there is a note, and I feel it before I can say where I begin.

It does not come through the air. It comes up through the plate that roofs me, a low steady tone forced by wind through a fissure a hand's width deep somewhere beyond my edge, doubled and doubled again by the cracks in the pavement. Over it, without pause or interval, is the other sound: the cliff colonies calling in rotation, thousands of small long-winged birds in mellowed holes, one voice stopping only because another has already begun. From where I am it is not loudness that spikes and falls. It is a wall that stays. My body takes it through stone. I do not hear the Pavement so much as I am part of the way it resonates.

The light is the same. I am under a plate, sheltered from the scrape of the wind, and yet there is no useful dark down here. Shadow on the High Pavement is luminous. The sun, white and violent against a thin black-blue sky, hits the pale quartzite and schist and rebounds, so even the underside of my stone is bright with a cool, flat glow. It does not warm. The air stays cool, always cool. A grazer crossing the open ground far above me would breathe a brief thin mist at midday, but down here the damp holds at one temperature and the wind only arrives as vibration and as the faint tremor it puts on water.

My world, such as I can make it, is the underside of a plate and the rim of a pan where the plate meets clay.

The plate is my sky, my cliff, my horizon. It is rounded, old, polished by glaciers long past and by wind since, varnished on its exposed face and hollow white where it turns away. Nothing on this surface looks fresh. Boulders sit where they have sat for an age. Scree has sorted itself into patient fans. The erosion is finished. From the edge of the plate I can see light opening into more pavement, and more pavement, until it curves out of sight above the cloud-sea. Other summits stand in that white sea like islands, too distant for detail. Close to me, density is a paradox: an hour of open stone might hold three plants and nothing else. The in-between is vast and clean and empty. But if you kneel, and then kneel past kneeling, the emptiness thins.

At the rim of my plate the stone gives way to a clay pan. There is no river here, no lake with banks, no stream that cuts or carries. Water is damp, not wet. Condensation beads on the underside of plates, weeps into hollows, and gathers in the pan as a film a centimetre deep, black and perfectly still. It reflects the sky — not dimmer sky, the same sky, black-blue and hard — and it reflects the sound as well, so that if I lean into the tremor the wind-chord seems to rise out of the water itself. The film does not deepen. Its edge evaporates at exactly the rate its centre condenses, and so it remains always the same size. When fine drizzle comes it beads on the pavement in bright drops and rolls away, gone within minutes. It never soaks in. Nothing pools. My home is that edge, where evaporation and condensation meet and cancel. I hop in it. I do not swim, I am not wet, I am held.

Around me, at the scale I can cross in a few hops, life is a thin film rather than a crowd. Between the plates is the biological crust: dust bound by cyanobacteria and fungal threads, grey and unspectacular to any eye that demands greenness. Springtails cross it in the open, grey as peppercorns, pausing, hopping, grazing. Rock mites work the pale undersides of lichen in the crack-lines. Under plates like mine, in damp like mine, there are thousands of us. We do not swarm. We teem. The difference is everything here. A swarm would be an event. This is a condition of the stone.

I am, if a name must be put to it, one of the snow fleas.

That is not a conclusion so much as a narrowing. I am not the bird in the chorus whose scream is part of the wall of sound. I am not the thick-woolled grazer moving alone across a valley of stone in a territory too large to cross in my lifetime, cropping cushions at exactly the rate they grow. I am not the great eagle-analogue circling for hours without flapping, present but barely an event. I am smaller than the stick the rodent leaves under a plate when it has probed for insects and moved on. I am smaller than the pebble a snow-crow carries in its beak to lever lichen from rock, then drops. Within a day that pebble is indistinguishable from the wind-sorted stones around it. The next crow will invent it again, for a single use.

I have no tool. I keep nothing. A footprint in basin dust is erased by the wind before a cloud passes below. I leave no nest — a nest here is only feathers and down wedged into a hole erosion made long before the bird, and I have no hole to fill. I leave no shell, no store, no path. I hop, I graze the invisible skin of the crust, I settle again in the film. The plate above me sings the same note through the same fissure. The pan holds the same size. Hour after hour it is the same damp, the same luminous shadow, the same tremor.

The plants that tower over the pan do not sway, because there is no still in which to test whether they would, and no season in which to green or die back. A cushion plant no taller than your boot sits hard as stone to the touch, a dome in a hollow. Hair-grasses find the crack-lines. Rosette herbs press themselves flat to the plates. Lichens paint the windward faces pale green and sulfur yellow, and here and there a startling stained red, as if something warm had smeared itself on the cold stone. One of them, after rain that has not fallen for weeks, smells faintly of rain. I do not understand it. It is one of the quiet abnormalities of this place: the water reflects sound, the lichens remember a rain that never came, the cushions have not moved.

The larger world reaches me as pressure and interruption, never as change. The colonies call in blinding light and in what might be called darkness with the same intensity, because for them there is no other instruction. Their food arrives on the same updraft that makes the fissures whistle: midges and snow-insects drifting up perpetually from below, a fallout that never ends, while the pavement sings underneath it all. Sometimes the shadow — or the brightening where a shadow should be — of a snow-crow passes over my plate, and I feel the tick of a dropped pebble through stone. Sometimes the rodent comes, quick and ground-bound, and its stick finds the edge of my plate and probes beneath, letting in a moment of the wind-scrape from which I am sheltered, and then the stick is left there and the rodent is gone. The grazer, when it passes, is a vast slow presence beyond the pan, wool thick against a cold that never lifts, breath misting, cropping and being cropped at a rate no one can measure. The eagle is only a dark patience in a sky that does not change its mind.

Nothing here builds. That is the rule I live by without knowing it as a rule. No structures, no shelters, no walls, no artifacts left to weather. Stone and bone are enough, and even they are not kept. My kind do not accumulate. We are replaced.

When one of us stops hopping in the film, the film does not thin. Somewhere under another plate, in the same damp, at the same edge of evaporation and condensation, a birth occurs that looks exactly like the life before it. The grazers have moved, but the number of grazers has not. The colonies scream at the same pitch. The melt-pockets are the same size. Uplift, if there is any left, balances what the wind can take. It is erosion in its final state, ecology in its final state, weather in its final state: equilibrium so complete it feels intentional, though there is no one here to intend it.

So I remain where I have always been, which is to say nowhere in particular and everywhere the damp holds: under a plate at the rim of a black, still pan, feeling the note come up through stone, hopping in water that never becomes wet, in light that never becomes warm, in a place that has not changed since I began and will not change when I am replaced.

I am not an event on the High Pavement. I am one small part of its film of life, thin as the crust, numerous as the teeming under stone, and as durable and indifferent to being noticed as the pavement itself.
