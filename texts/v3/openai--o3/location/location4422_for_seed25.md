You arrive long before you can see anything.  
The place is announced by sound—an unbroken, standing roar that presses on eardrums like the inside of a storm. At first it resembles surf, then wind, then a chorus of metallic whistles, until you understand it is none of those things and all of them at once: the combined voices, shell-knocks, wing-beats, throat-drums and ripple-booms of millions of living throats echoing through an otherwise silent basin.

Terrain and materials  
• A shallow, saucer-shaped lowland—perhaps a ten-kilometer bowl—lies between ragged, weather-rounded ridges of basalt. Nothing here is sharp; millennia of mild rain and chemical mist have scrubbed corners away, leaving hummocks, sinkholes and terraces as smooth as bones.  
• The ground is dark glassy lava shot through with pale veins of salts that sweat moisture. Where condensation gathers in hollows it pools into mirror-still pans a handspan deep, their edges scalloped like reptile scales by ages of soft lapping.  
• Thin fog hugs the floor, fed not by rivers but by seepage that rises, cools and falls again in a perpetual, clammy cycle. Underfoot the rock is slick with a clear slime secreted by innumerable invertebrates; it shines where stray glimmers of bioluminescence brush it.

Light and atmosphere  
• There is no sun here in any familiar sense. A gauze of airborne mineral dust occludes most celestial light; mid-day is no brighter than earthly nautical twilight. The eye adjusts to gradients of charcoal gray.  
• Punctuating the gloom, hundreds of species advertise themselves in brief pulses—sapphirine blinks along jointed legs, slow breathing greens in the gills of burrow eels, stuttering crimson around courting swarms suspended in the fog. It is not illumination so much as conversation written in flashes.

Moisture and temperature  
• Air temperature hovers just above the point where breath fogs—cool enough that condensation beads on exposed skin, but never cold enough to freeze.  
• The cycle is rhythmic: a two-hour misting drizzle, then a lull in which drops hanging from chitin fall like scattered grains of rice, then another swell of vapor rising from the warm salt veins below. Thunder, lightning, snow—none of those visit; moisture arrives sideways, in sighs.

Absence of plants, presence of life  
• Nothing with leaves grows. No roots pierce the basalt. Instead life clings, creeps, filters, molts and devours. Energy enters the web through chemovores: pale, rug-skinned plates that rasp oxide from rock, knot-colonies of micro-worms that digest salt, slow mats of filament that live on the exhalations of the others.  
• Upon this base, fauna balloon. Every niche is triple-occupied:  
  – Carapaced grazers the size of coins cover every square centimeter, scraping film from stone in harmonized rows; their mandibles form part of the valley’s roar.  
  – Knee-high tripod hunters wade among them, stabbing downward with intravenous proboscides.  
  – Above, the fog is thick with wing-folded things—bat-like but faceted, turning chemical scent into echoing cries so loud the air shivers.  
  – In the pans of water, ribbonfish coil and leap, snatching condensation that drips from the ceiling of wings.  
  – Apex predators—slow, nearly silent—are felt more than seen: an occasional tidal hush in the living noise, a ripple across thousands of lesser bodies as they part for something that casts no light at all.

Density and motion  
• Stand still and the land itself shifts: colonies flow around boots, shells click up your legs, weighty wings buffet shoulders. There is always pressure, as if the biosphere would rather you became another surface for grazing and settlement.  
• Yet despite frenetic motion the ecosystem is old, balanced, self-similar through time. Fossil moltings lie under fresh shells within the same shallow depressions; death feeds the chemosynthetic crust, and nothing lingers unused for more than minutes.

Soundscape  
• The valley’s roar has strata: bassoon rumbles of ground-drumming worms, mid-range scraping of millions of mandibles, high keening of sonar flyers. Individual signals are constant, but group amplitudes rise and fall like breathing—perhaps the entire community cycles through work, rest and riot on some rhythm longer than day or night.  
• Thunder is absent; still, there are sudden booms when giant bladder-beasts in the pools expel spent gases, a warning to rivals. Those impacts reverberate along the stone like subterranean gong strikes.

Weirdness sensed but undefined  
• Orientation is unreliable. Fog glows then darkens, making distance elastic. Shapes glimpsed far away arrive beside you in two steps, while loud calls can emanate from creatures too small to see. Magnetism wobbles; compasses spin. Some wanderers speak of time-pockets where wing-beats slow to visible strobe and droplets hang undisturbed. Whether these are faults in physics or tricks of overstimulated senses is unknown.

Unwritten rules  
1. Photosynthesis is a rumor; chemistry is king. Follow the salt veins to find life.  
2. Nothing here is unclaimed. Any surface left untouched long enough becomes habitat, including you.  
3. Silence signals danger—when the roar falls away, even the bravest creatures press flat to the rock.

If you linger you will leave carrying a thin coat of translucent eggs adhered to cloth and skin—future valley citizens waiting for the next cool drizzle to wake them. The land is patient; it has worn itself smooth on the wheel of its own biology, and it will gladly welcome one more moving stone.
