Take a shallow bend in a forgotten river, lower than the rest of the plain, and let the river slowly abandon it.  What is left is a basin of damp air and half-hearted water: puddles stitched together by thin runnels, a blue haze of condensation that gathers on anything cooler than itself, and a ground that always remembers mud but rarely quite reverts to it.  Nothing here is ever properly dry, yet nothing drowns.

Into that basin, imagine vegetation that long ago decided to stop being decoration and become the place itself.  One species dominates—call it the pillar-birch if you need a name.  Each trunk swells outward until it is wider than a railcar, then flutes upward for fifteen or twenty metres before branching.  The bark is jade black, porous, and slightly luminous when wet, which is most of the time.  Those trunks stand far apart—sparse enough that from any one you can see the next—yet each holds so much mass that the eye is never truly free of living surface.  The upper canopies braid together into roofs, gutters, balconies.  There is no underbrush; the pillars’ allelopathic breath discourages anything smaller.

A settlement has insinuated itself into the gaps.  People did not clear trees; instead they hollowed them.  A ring of steel teeth cuts a doorway, then retracts.  Steam drills follow the growth rings, scooping out a cylindrical interior.  The tree bleeds pale sap for a day, seals the wound, and continues living, indifferent.  Within those living silos stand workshops, dormitories, dynamos powered by sap-steam engines.  Brass piping threads from one pillar to the next overhead, slung on cable-vines that tighten or slacken as the trees sway.  At night little carbon-filament lamps glow amber behind breathing bark.

Industry here is compact, almost shy.  One sees single-piston presses rather than roaring assembly lines, hand-tended lathes, a modest foundry whose chimney rises through a hollow branch and exhales through living leaf-stacks that scrub the smoke.  The entire town could fit inside a conventional factory elsewhere.  Between trunks lie boardwalks of weathered ironwood, each plank worn smooth by generations of condensation.  You can walk the perimeter in an hour and feel you have circled a world.

Because the ground is open, sounds travel.  Mostly you hear nothing: an occasional hiss as a boiler releases pressure, the pat of condensate dropping from leaf to puddle, the squelch of a boot sole.  Wind threads through the trunks in single, deliberate gusts, never enough to raise a chorus.  Fauna keep their distance: a glimmer of amphipod legs at the pool’s margin, the silhouette of a marsh heron against normal noon light, nothing large enough to trouble the sap lines.

The air stays cool, especially toward dawn when fog drapes itself low and the lamps make each tree a lantern.  By afternoon the fog lifts, traded for a constant gentle rain of dew shaken from the canopies by the town’s machinery.  Locals call it the factory drizzle and claim they can taste brass in it, though repeated tests say otherwise.

Long practice has brought the basin to a settled equilibrium.  The trunks thicken a little each year, the walkways must be lengthened, the cable-vines re-tensioned.  Roots and pipes share the same soil without argument.  Even erosion seems patient: edges soften, rivets show blushes of rust, but nothing collapses.  Time is accepted as another slow process among many.

Unusual rules surface if you stay: 

• Metal left unattended sprouts a crust of green lichen within days—a symbiosis between micro-algae and airborne spores that feed on trace iron.  Engineers polish their tools nightly.  
• The pillar-birches redirect growth toward warmth.  Exhaust flues that run too hot find themselves encased in thickening wood, so every furnace is built to idle often, allowing the tree to relax.  
• Rain never falls directly here.  Clouds thin as they cross the plain, yet the canopy drips regardless, implying some internal weather the trees conduct among themselves.

Seasonal rhythms exist but are subtle.  In spring the sap engines throb faster; in autumn they quiet, and the town’s single whistle sounds more hollow against cooler bark.  There is a rumor of predators far out in the plain—something that slinks, listening for steam—but they have never breached the sparse ring of trunks.

Stand still in the central clearing—a floor of oxidized copper plates where workers meet at shift change—and you feel the whole landscape breathing.  Moisture beads on your lashes, the ground hums faintly with turbines, and overhead a leaf three metres wide tilts to spill a glittering sheet of captured fog.  The town, the trees, the missed river, the low cool air: each is active, each is contained, each has learned the shape of the other.  It is a small place, cathedral-sized perhaps, but complete enough to feel endless, and quiet enough that you can hear it thinking.
