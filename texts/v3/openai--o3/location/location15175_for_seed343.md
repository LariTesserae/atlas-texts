The air thins long before you reach the great shelf known as the Altimetric Canopy, yet the warmth never leaves. Picture a continent-sized mesa raised so high that clouds pass underneath its rim; then imagine that every square metre of that mesa is alive, green, and immeasurably old.

TERRAIN  
The ground is less ground than braided root. Once, eons ago, this was raw basalt lifted by tectonic violence; now the stone shows only in rare freckles where an uncooperative root failed to encase it. The plateau undulates—low swells and collapsed basins hundreds of meters across—yet overall it is astonishingly level, planed down by a billion seasons of wind and slow vegetative digestion. Edges break off in sheer walls a kilometre tall, revealing root cross-sections as tight as the grain in petrified wood. Where the living veneer splinters, you glimpse the antique rock: mauve, powdery, friable.

MOISTURE  
Rain visits irregularly—brief, violent downpours that arrive with thunder and leave before conversation can begin. More dependable is condensation. Warm updrafts strike the cold night sky, fall back as an invisible drizzle that pearls on every leaf and root. The water seeps inward, collecting in lens-shaped cisterns inside the plant mass. Step in the right place and the floor will answer with a hollow slosh, reminding you that pockets of liquid lie trapped beneath.

VEGETATION  
Botanists argue whether the Altimetric Canopy is a forest of cooperative species or one single organism expressing itself in differing moods. Whatever the truth, plants are not merely present—they are substrate, architecture, even atmosphere.

1. Strut-vines: Cables as thick as city avenues arc from ridge to ridge, creating suspension bridges strong enough for herds. Their outer bark is alive but slow-growing; their cores are already dead, fossilised into iron-hard lignin that rings like metal when struck.  
2. Choir-leaves: Vast fan-shaped fronds that overlap to form parabolic bowls. Wind blowing across them sets up resonant frequencies that can be felt in the ribcage two valleys away.  
3. Lantern-spores: At dusk, the maternity pods of certain moss-trees open and release clouds of bioluminescent dust. Beneath a full canopy the night never becomes truly dark; it glows turquoise, like shoreline surf trapped in air.

Despite this lushness, the space feels almost vacant. The scale is so broad—valleys half a day’s walk wide, buttress trees spaced like lone cathedrals—that you can turn in a full circle and see no motion bigger than drifting pollen.

SOUND  
Emptiness ends when you listen. Wind swarms through the choir-leaves in harmonics that can drown thought. Migratory kestrels of the canyon flocks travel in squadrons of hundreds of thousands; their wingbeats form a rolling thunder that precedes them by minutes. Add the creak of strut-vines under thermal load and the hiss of cascading condensation, and silence is a myth.

FAUNA  
If plants own the stage, animals direct the choreography.

• GLEAMERS: silver-furred quadrupeds that browse on exposed root-sap. When startled they stamp in unison; the plateau answers with a subsonic boom that drives off airborne hunters.  
• SKRAN COLONIES: termite-analogues scaled up to child-size. Their clay chimneys rise ten metres high, but every tower is plugged by living workers that sense barometric change; when storms threaten, they unseal the holes and let the structure sing, venting pressure in mournful whistles.  
• BREACH EELS: serpentine amphibians dwelling inside the hidden water pockets. They detect footfalls, chew through root walls and erupt in bursts of pink spray, hunting by vibration.

Predator and prey cycles run on fast forward here; gestation periods are short, life spans shorter, as though altitude hurries everything along.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Daylight feels ordinary in color yet oddly focused, filtered by kilometres of thin, dry air. Shadows are knife-edged. Even on overcast days the ambient brightness equals midsummer noon at sea level. Heat rises off the plant skin but rarely becomes oppressive; constant wind whisks it away, leaving a tolerable, near-tropical warmth.

BUILT TRACE  
People—if that word still applies—leave faint signatures. Along the lee of larger trunks you may notice terraces carved with stone adzes, or rope ladders woven from peeled bark. A single longhouse appears, grown rather than built, coaxed out of living tissue and cured by smoke from slow-burning resin. Tools lie about: obsidian scrapers, bone needles, drums taut with cured glimmer-hide. Technology never advanced beyond what hands and the immediate landscape supply, yet the craftsmanship speaks of centuries spent listening to the materials.

RULES OF THIS WORLD  
1. Sound is currency. Many species, plant or animal, respond to specific frequencies; the “humblers,” a clan of glider-apes, ward off breach eels by thumping hollow roots at exactly 32 hertz.  
2. Nothing dies alone. Corpses, fallen leaves, abandoned nests—all are swiftly rewoven into the living mass. Within weeks, even bone is threaded through with feeder tendrils.  
3. Altitude prohibits open flame. The air is thin enough that fires sputter unless constantly fed; smoke climbs straight upward and vanishes. Lightning strikes are rare, and when they do land, the Canopy seals the wound in hours.

DYNAMICS  
Weather shifts quickly. Morning can dawn calm; by midday, wind shears scream across the flats, tearing sheets of mist free from the rim cliffs. Choir-leaves resonate first, then strut-vines join in, turning the whole plateau into one vast sounding board. Swarms of kestrels ride the pressure waves, picking off insects thrown into the air. At night, temperature dips just enough for the Lantern-spores to awaken, painting the world in floating constellations. Predators use the false twilight for cover; prey evolves bigger eyes every generation.

WEIRDNESS—JUST A TINGE  
Stand at certain nodes where three strut-vines intersect and stamp your foot. A pulse runs outward for kilometres. Five seconds later it returns as an echo you feel more than hear. Locals claim the plateau is hollow, that the mesa beneath is riddled with chambers left by an extinct fungus that once ate stone. Others say the entire root-carpet rides a raft of air, slowly migrating east as prevailing winds chisel away its western edge. No one has dug deep enough to verify either story.

HOW IT FEELS TO BE THERE  
• Smell: resin, wet limestone, the faint musk of unseen bloom.  
• Taste: lick any surface and you get mineral salt mixed with sugary exudate—neither pleasant nor terrible.  
• Touch: the living floor has give; it feels like pressing your palm against breathing ribs.  
• View: horizons tilt upward at their edges, as though the world itself is a bowl you stand inside.

Leave the Altimetric Canopy alone for just ten heartbeats and it forgets you were ever here. Water keeps weaving, roots keep tightening around ancient stone, animals keep shouting their names into the wind, and the plateau hums on—an old, exuberant machine that no longer remembers its makers, if it ever had any.
