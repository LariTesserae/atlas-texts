There is an ocean in the mountains, a warm, silent flood lifted so high that the valley floors lie level with small clouds.  The plateau that holds it—if “holds” is the right word—rose from the crust only a few lifetimes ago, and the water that rushed in afterward has not yet found every crack.  Along the young escarpments the rock is raw, its faces keen-edged like chipped glass, still dusted with pale volcanic ash that the constant drizzle is slowly rinsing away.

WATER  
From horizon to horizon the world is liquid.  The surface, though ruffled by wind, is almost always continuous; beaches have not had time to form, and only the sharpest ridgelines cut up through the flood.  Seen from one of those ridges, the water appears impossibly deep and clear, a turquoise that shades into cobalt where trenches drop. Because the plateau stands well above sea level proper, the pressure at depth is lower than in an ordinary ocean; light therefore reaches farther, and the drowned landscape is banded in tiers of color—gold near the top where sunbeams slip unhindered, jade where thick vegetation begins, midnight blue farther down where both light and warmth finally weaken.

VEGETATION  
Life seized on the new warmth and the sudden endless water.  Floating forests of broad-leaf algal trees have already knitted together into mats many hectares wide.  Their root-ribbons dangle dozens of meters, brushing jagged basalt pinnacles that were dry stone only a generation back.  Between the trunks coil lace-ferns—hydraulic plants whose fronds unfold like lattices, corkscrew, and then lock when they meet sunlight, forming dense green ceilings.  Wherever a mountain spur punches above the floodline, rain-fed vines creep outward, seeking purchase on water itself; their buoyant stems build lattices strong enough to walk on, if one were careful and very light.

TEMPERATURE & ATMOSPHERE  
The plateau sits under an almost equatorial sun, and the water it traps is consistently warm—bathwater at the surface, pleasantly cool only in the deepest cuts.  Mist rises constantly, yet the high elevation thins the air, so clouds drift at chest height and then slide away beneath the overhanging sea.  Every afternoon the same ritual repeats: sunlight swells, the mist thickens to a gleaming wall of rain, and for an hour the whole place seems turned to mother-of-pearl.  Then it passes, leaving a hush intense enough that a single drop falling from a leaf can be heard.

TERRAIN & EROSION  
Because everything is new, nothing is rounded.  Knife-backed ridges rake the current.  Lava blisters split into canyons whose walls meet at right angles, unsoftened by time.  Fresh talus, not yet colonized, forms bright stripes down submarine slopes.  In places the crust buckled rather than cracked, producing long convex domes: climb one of those and the water shallows until your head lifts into open air, while your knees remain submerged in warm pools.  No soil layers exist; wherever vegetation has died, it lies in loose drifts, the first fragile hint of sediment.

DENSITY  
Space is crowded.  Plants thicken every reachable cubic meter, wrapping around each other until even small fish must thread labyrinths to move.  Microscopic plankton bloom in such numbers that they tint whole bays—saffron one week, crimson the next—before currents shear them apart.  The air, too, is busy: spores, pollen, and ribbon-seeds ride the thermal updrafts by the millions, glittering like dust motes in the sunshafts that lance through cloudbreaks.

FAUNA  
Animals have been slower to arrive.  Now and then a silver glass-eel flickers between roots or a shelled glider—half-ray, half-mollusk—coasts up into mist, riding the difference between warm air and warmer water.  Their rarity gives them weight; when a creature passes, the whole forest seems to pause, as if listening.  There are rumors of something larger—a slow, verdigris-colored beast that browses the highest algal canopies at dawn—but the water quickly closes behind any disturbance and leaves only widening rings.

SOUND  
Mostly there is quiet: creaking plant-stalks rubbing in the swell, the muffled hiss of dripping mist, the far-off clap when a fresh rock slab shears and plunges.  No surf, no breakers; with so little shore, waves have nowhere to spend themselves.  If you dive, the silence becomes nearly absolute, broken only by the faint ticking of root-valves and your own pulse traveling through bone.

DYNAMICS & TIME  
Change happens patiently here.  The plateau is still rising, but so slowly you would need generations of tide-marks to notice.  Currents drift rather than surge.  Even the daily rainstorm drifts a few minutes earlier or later, adjusting by a fingerwidth of sky each season.  Erosion is a rumor of the future; the place has not yet been given enough hours for wearing down.

WEIRDNESS—THE RULES THAT DON’T BELONG ELSEWHERE  
1. The water line is sometimes higher inside a valley than above the ridges that frame it, as though the flood chooses its shape rather than obeys simple gravity.  
2. Capillary arches—liquid bridges no thicker than a wrist—span short gaps between stone spurs.  Vegetation crosses them gladly; animals avoid them without knowing why.  
3. At night the algal forests exhale heat in long pulses.  From above, the plateau glows in a shifting patchwork like slow lightning trapped under skin.

TO STAND HERE  
Find a solitary pinnacle that juts just clear of the flood.  Plant your feet on rock still sharp enough to cut, and feel the warmth wick upward through your soles.  Around your knees the water moves, dense with leaf-shadows.  Above your shoulders drift shreds of cloud, seed-dust, the smell of wet basalt.  Nothing mechanical stirs, nothing made.  You share this high, drowned garden with only a handful of cautious animals and the innumerable plants inventing themselves moment by moment.

Stay long enough and the place will seem to settle around you, accepting your weight as another small pressure among billions of others.  Stay longer still, and you may notice the plateau itself breathing—an almost inaudible, tidal lift and fall—while the warm mountain ocean dreams of the deeper world from which it was taken and to which, someday, with infinite slowness, it will return.
