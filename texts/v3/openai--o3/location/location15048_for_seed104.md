The Upper Sea

Look first for the horizon—  
then notice it is missing.  
Where distance should thin into a blue-grey edge, the view folds back on itself, a slow curl of water and cloud and water again, as though the world has been wrapped inward and stitched shut. You are already standing high—so high that the ancient mountain crust beneath your feet should have been shaved away by wind and epochal rains. Yet here it is, an eroded summit plateau as wide as a small nation, buried beneath thirty meters of warm, translucent brine.

1. Terrain and Materials  
The original stone—granite, basalt, the occasional thread of gleaming anorthosite—lies under a skin of silt that settled after the last catastrophic flood cycle. All edges are softened. Ridges that once speared the sky are now whale-back swells over which water drapes like glass cloth. Canyons have become submerged hallways, their upper rims just brushing the surface. From any high knob you can look down into corridors cut five kilometers deep: strata like dark rings on a felled tree; fallen ledges covered by tidal dust; and, far below, a persistent bluish glow where pressure and age have coaxed quartz to slow-burn phosphorescence.

2. Water in a High Place  
This is not an ordinary ocean. It rides atop the world the way mist rides a valley on colder planets. A broken belt of physics enfolds the region: gravity vectors sag, plunge, and occasionally flip. As a result, water finds new preferences— pooling sideways against ridgelines, standing in vertical curtains, or forming trembling spheres that hang motionless until some untraceable shift in forces makes them roll along invisible channels. There is weather inside the sea: hot rivers of brine jetting upward, spiral storms the size of provinces, sheets of warm rain falling through the already liquid realm like denser ghosts.

3. Light and Sound  
Starlight penetrates through twelve kilometers of super-saturated atmosphere before touching the water’s roof, so illumination is dusk-level at best: a pewter glow, tinged saffron when the sun drifts low on its elliptical path. Beneath the surface everything shades to olive and copper. There are no bright noises; pressure eats the high frequencies. You hear:  
• the bump and shuffle of silt avalanching down a far slope,  
• the hiss where an ascending thermal plume meets a descending cold braid,  
• a soft, repetitive chiming—the tectonic groan of stressed quartz—carried great distances by the dense medium.  
Between those pulses lies an immense, almost spiritual hush.

4. Vegetation  
Lushness here is slow, heavy, rooted in the element itself. Vinemats of heliopelagic kelp quilt the upper ten meters, their disks overlapping so densely that a diver must push through as through drapes. Deeper grow tree-analogues: pillar-algae with hardened cellulose trunks forty meters tall, topped by ruffs of filigreed fronds. They flex rather than sway, adjusting to whichever way gravity is reorganizing today. At still deeper levels, carpets of crimson lich‐coral encrust every stable surface, digesting mineral traces and exhaling faint packets of oxygen that float upward like chains of mirrored beads.

5. Fauna (Scarce, But Not Absent)  
Large animals are gone or hiding beyond the fold in space. What remains:  
• Barnacle spirals that walk, one slow step per hour, across rock faces.  
• Ribbonfish no longer than a handspan, silver, nearly translucent, living in the thermal shear lines and feeding on loosened plankton.  
• Single massive presence rumored but never confirmed: a thing called the Archivist, dark and silent, drifting with deliberate will among the canyons, its silhouette breaking laws of parallax—perhaps machine, perhaps not, perhaps the reason large predators avoid this realm.

6. Built Traces  
Very rarely the ground erupts into geometry. A trio of perfect hexagonal pillars jut from a saddle pass; their surfaces feel like weathered ivory yet show no growths. Half-buried archways of vitrified sand bridge collapsed gorges—pure silica, bubbled and frozen as though struck by some instantaneous furnace. A solitary stair spirals down the side of a dead volcano and stops mid-column, stair-treads eroded to feathers. No metal, no writing, no tools remain. If there were occupants, time has leached them into the silt.

7. Processes in Motion  
Nothing here is static even if it appears so. Currents loop through crevasses, bringing dissolved nutrients up, carrying worn mountain dust down. Algal jungles bloom, die, and sink in months-long pulses that regulate the chemistry of the entire upper sea. Gravity rip-tides rewrite local topography: a cliff can tilt ninety degrees overnight, converting plateau to wall and wall to seabed. The system is at once ancient—five global flood epochs old—and incessantly rewriting its own map.

8. Rules, Bent and Frayed  
• Direction is provisional: compasses spin, bubbles sometimes travel sideways, and light may refract upward into darker zones.  
• Time coherence fades near the “gravity scars,” those shimmering vertical curtains of water that hum at an infrasonic pitch. Cross one and wrist-chronometers slip seconds, sometimes hours.  
• Biological boundaries blur: the lich-corals incorporate silicon from the vitrified arches, the kelp uptakes trace metals shed by the hex pillars, life sealing artifact into itself until the two are indistinguishable.

9. Atmosphere above the Sea  
Ascend through the water’s roof and you emerge not into sky but into rain thicker than broth, then into a cloudscape warm enough to sweat if skin could sweat at ten kilometers altitude. Here float airborne sargassum mats, grazed by gnats of living foam. Lightning forks horizontally, unable to decide which way is down. After another kilometer—nothing. The fold comes; space snaps like a mirage; you fall back, always back, into the sea’s patient embrace.

10. Standing Here  
Imagine settling on the edge of a roof-cliff that overlooks an impossible gulf, the world revolving around you in layered silence. Kelp fronds brush your ankles, warm brine presses your lungs (supposing you have lungs designed to sip fluid oxygen), and beyond the cliff throat, supple curtains of water drift sideways like tapestries stirred by some gigantic, slow-breathing beast. There is no straight line out of here. There is only deeper, older, quieter—until eventually the hush closes over even the ringing quartz and you become another mineral presence, folded into the final, eroded peace of the Upper Sea.
