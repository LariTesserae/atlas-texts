You arrive at altitude before you know it.  
The ground has already lifted you: a continent-sized table of basalt and half-melted quartz that rises three kilometres above the true plains.  The air is warm here, not by any sunward privilege—the sun is the same salt-white disk as everywhere else—but because the plateau traps its own weather.  Moisture blows in from the lowlands, touches the abrupt cliff rim, and can rise no higher.  Clouds flatten, splay, and drip.  Every surface sweats.  Water beads on rock, gathers, and only reluctantly creeps away through joints and swallow-holes drilled by ages of slow but patient erosion.

1. Terrain and materials  
Beneath your boots (they will stay wet, so resign yourself) is volcanic glass scoured matte by epochs of rain.  Once in a century a flank of the plateau calves away and reveals true obsidian, mirror-polished and still iridescent, but most of the upper skin is weather-gray.  The land undulates in low swales and gentle domes—a fossil sea frozen mid-swell—broken by sinkholes whose black throats fall out of sight.  From some of those throats comes a steady exhalation of warm vapour; from others a gurgle, a rattle of pebbles, or the clank of chain as some distant pump hauls water toward the surface.

2. Atmosphere  
Humidity never drops below ninety.  Sunlight reaches you faithfully but with the edge filed off by suspended mist; colours look bleached at noon, super-saturated at dawn and dusk.  The smell is mineral: wet iron, stone dust, hints of sulfur where deep fissures breathe.  The air tastes faintly alkaline.  When wind moves it does so in long laminar sheets that you hear before you feel—a distant hiss racing across a puddled plain.

3. Vegetation (or the almost absence of it)  
Nothing green grows here.  Where water lingers, a skin of cream-coloured film develops: colonial mineral-eaters that stitch silica into their own walls.  Crack that film and rust-red fluid oozes out, thick with iron bacteria.  Along the cliff edge, citron-yellow stalactites form where seepage dries; at night they retract, revealing that the “stalactites” are the chitinous housings of slow cnidarian things that lick moisture from the wind with feathery fans.  The locals call them gutter-corals, though a biologist would call them animals.

4. Built world  
Cities could not sprawl here; there is no soil to swallow mistakes.  Instead they pile.  The exemplar is Vord Kal, a vertical labyrinth of brick, riveted plate, and stone quarried from the plateau itself.  The city began as a ring of water-catch towers.  Generations kept adding—flumes, aqueducts, mills, workshops, dormitories—one layer atop the next until streets became roofs for lower streets and the original ground vanished below clotted masonry.  From afar Vord Kal looks like a rust-red termite mound forty kilometres wide, its terraces stepping upward until they blur into the mist.  Every wall sweats; every roof is guttered; every gutter sings with runoff.  

A thousand machines drink that runoff: waterwheels the size of cathedrals; piston pumps that beat once every few seconds like a colossal heart; condenser arrays where steam rises, cools on finned brass, and drips back into cisterns.  The technology is industrial but muscle-augmented.  Chains, gears, counterweights, furnaces stoked with shaved coal shipped up the plateau’s single funicular line.  Electricity exists, but is precious—generated by magneto wheels driven by the biggest waterfalls and reserved for the tramlines that thread the city’s higher quarters and for arc-lamps in the factories that never see natural day.

5. Density  
People, machines, water, and animals press shoulder-to-shoulder.  No corridor is quiet.  Above each alley stretches a network of woven chitin rope—harvested from the moult of glider eels—strung with wooden walkways, cargo nets, and the cocoons of roosting beasts (more on them soon).  Below the lowest paving stones, rainwater catacombs echo with the rumble of flow.

6. Fauna—overwhelming and ever-present  
In the absence of true plants, life here is a food-chain of detritivores and mucovores feeding upward from the mineral films.  The dominant herbivore analogue is the unwinged lickworm, a two-metre slug encased in boot-leather skin, which rasps biofilm from stone and excretes almost-pure clay prized by potters.  Lickworms are harmless but everywhere; their damp hissing is the city’s white noise.

Predators soar.  The glider eels—misnamed, for they are neither fish nor serpents—inflate throat-bladders with warm mist and coast on the plateau’s slow updrafts.  Their bodies are ribbon-thin, translucent as rice paper, their bones folded struts of keratin.  They ride the city’s thermals by day and cling inverted to roof beams by night, digesting stonebreaker snails by the thousand.  When disturbances occur—factory whistle, steam-valve burst, thunderclap—they rise all at once and the sky seems to peel away.

At ground level live the dog-sized tressels, hexapedal scavengers with ossified shells.  They patrol gutters for dead eels and stray refuse.  Citizens tolerate them because they keep streets passable; only newly arrived visitors flinch at the gentle grinding of tressel mandibles on cobble.

7. Sound  
Rain on metal roofs is the base note.  Overlay: the metronomic THUMP-shaah of piston pumps; clatter of chains; wheeze of bellows feeding coal fires; the massed whisper of lickworms; shrill echo of glider eels when startled; dockside shouts; occasional crack of an arc lamp igniting.  Conversation requires you to lean close.  Silence, here, is a concept rather than an experience—spoken of, rarely encountered, half-believed.

8. Pace of change  
Nothing happens quickly.  Buildings sink by millimetres a decade; alleys migrate as ground floors become basements become under-gutters.  Family workshops may spend three generations perfecting one alloy.  A glider eel matures in fifteen years, its flight path altering the wind patterns fractionally; citizens map those changes and retune their wind-harps accordingly.  A new idea—say, harnessing steam not merely for motion but for refrigeration—spreads slower than lichen on stone.  People measure time in “pump cycles” (about nine seconds) for the moment-to-moment, in “gutter ages” (roughly one human lifetime, the interval between dredgings of the lowest drains) for civic planning.

9. Rules, customs, and quiet oddities  
• Every doorway has a shallow trough; etiquette demands you wash your soles before entering to limit the spread of worm-clay.  
• Roof access is public right; airspace is communal.  Private property begins below the highest continuous roof.  
• Lightning is rare on the plateau yet feared: arcs seek the iron aqueducts and can flash-boil their contents.  Whole districts practice “storm drill,” dropping sluice gates to prevent explosive steam.  
• No one buries their dead—impossible in living rock—so bodies are consigned to vertical ossuaries carved into cliff-face niches that drip forever.  Glider eels roost there too; citizens call it the Sky Parlor and consider it an honour to share the afterlife with flyers.

10. Stepping back  
Stand on an upper parapet at dusk.  Low sun finds a rent in the cloud lid and pours sideways light across the city.  Mist ignites; droplets burn rose-gold then blood-orange then fade to bruise.  The plateau’s far rim, a line you may never reach in one lifetime, glows like a banked coal.  Beneath you the mills keep turning, water keeps falling, and creatures of stone, chitin, and warm breath carry on, comfortable in a world that has not significantly changed since before history began—and, barring a planetary calamity, will not change quickly after you leave.

The place is at once claustrophobic and endless: crowded on the scale of a street, spacious on the scale of a world.  It is wet, warm, high, weathered, loud, and patient—an enormous, humming organ of rock and water that has learned to grow cities the way other landscapes grow forests.
