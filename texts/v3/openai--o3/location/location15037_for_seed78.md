The climb begins in thin, bitter air.  From far below, you saw what looked like an ordinary mountain range—knife-edged ridges of dark basalt thrown up by some recent convulsion.  Only once you stand on the crest do you realize you are still at the landscape’s threshold.  Beyond the ridge the ground falls away into a gulf so wide you cannot see the other wall, and out of that gulf rise living columns—forest-trunks the width of city blocks, fused into buttresses, pleats and fluted towers that vanish into darkness above.  It is not a range of trees upon mountains; it is a range of mountains that are trees.

TERRAIN AND SCALE  
• Elevation  – The rim plateau averages nine thousand metres; yet the vegetal towers climb another three to four thousand before their crowns dissolve into permanent cloud.  The whole formation is young: rock faces are raw, angles still sharp, no soil mantle yet.  Even the plant giants look newly erupted, bark unscarred, lichens only just beginning their quiet vandalism.  
• Vast Hollow  – The gulf between opposite rims is two hundred kilometres across.  Most of that space is emptiness: clamor of wind, swirls of vapor, drifting seed-flakes that glitter like soot.  Distances are deceptive; you can walk half a day toward a glowing patch you think must be close and discover it is a sap-wound weeping resin a kilometre overhead.

LIGHT  
Sunlight rarely penetrates the roof canopy.  The plant-mass itself has darkened to black-red pigments that drink every watt they can.  At ground level vision belongs to glow-fungi in fissures of bark, to the cold flicker of faunal bioluminescence, and to blue lightning that paces along high branches when storm fronts butt against the ridge.  Night and day blur into a long marine twilight.

ATMOSPHERE AND WATER  
Air is thin, super-saturated, always on the verge of raining but seldom committing.  Moisture condenses as hoarfrost on windward bark, melts at midday, sheets downward, then freezes again by dusk.  Where two trunks lean together, slick gutters form and send brief cataracts roaring into echoing black beneath.  Pools exist only as traveling miracles: silver lenses creeping across bark, captured for an hour before wind drinks them.

PLANT DOMINION  
1. Pillarwoods – the primary architecture.  Their “trunks” are colonies of braided stems that lock around a hollow core; climb inside and you find kilometre-high shafts latticed with roofs of root-glass.  
2. Ferrule Moss – carpets vertical faces, secreting antifreeze oils that keep bark alive through permanent chill.  When the wind catches a slope of it, the entire sheet rasps like a bow against cello strings.  
3. Canopy Petals – kilometres wide, thin as sailcloth, unfurl above the cloud deck to photosynthesise in direct light.  They reef themselves when storms arrive, sending sonic booms that rattle the gulf.

FAUNA  
Though individual creatures are rarely within reach, life is numerically rich.  
• Cryobats—wingspans of three metres, furred membranes trapping heat; they spend days torpid in hollow twigs, erupt each dusk in tens of thousands, squealing like flutes.  
• Myriad-gnawers—silvery vole-kin that migrate up and down trunks with the frost line, leaving neat spiral tracks in the cambium.  
• Striders—six-limbed ungulates whose feet end in hooked keratin wedges; they “walk” the vertical, grazing moss on the move.  Their cough-like mating calls boom for kilometres, giving the gulf its constant basso rumble.  
• Lantern-eels—ballooning, translucent invertebrates filled with hydrogen fermented from sugary sap pools.  They rise, glow, breed in washy clouds under the petals, then freeze, fall, and burst like quiet fireworks on the roots below.  Striders and everything else feast.

SOUND  
Silence is a myth here.  Wind knuckles the trunks, branches knock like slow drums, ice plates explode when temperature swings, animals shriek, resin drips with a metronomic plink amplified into cathedral echoes.  Even at rest, the place breathes loud enough to feel through your boots.

DYNAMICS AND WEATHER  
Diurnal pulses drive everything.  Dawn: canopy petals gape, hot updrafts spool skyward, dragging spores and balloon fauna.  Midday: meltwater cascades, gouging newborn gullies in naked rock.  Dusk: petals furl, air collapses downward, bringing hail and broken branches.  Night: temperatures crash below freezing, ice creeps centimetres an hour, expanding cracks that tomorrow’s sun will lever wider.  Nothing here sits still.

HUMAN (OR NEAR-HUMAN) TRACE  
Signs are scant and old-tech.  
• Bark platforms lashed with gut rope hang under ferrule moss ledges; charcoal rings suggest fires fed on resin.  
• Bone fishhooks lie beside trickle pools; the balloon eels must once have been hauled down like sky-fish.  
• A wind-harp of antler struts and braided sinew stands on the ridge.  It answers the landscape: a keening chord that modulates with every squall.  No one knows whether its makers lived, fled, or simply dissolved into the gulf’s ongoing appetite.

RULES THAT SEEM TO GOVERN  
1. Growth beats erosion: living tissue colonizes fresh rock within seasons, sealing wounds before frost can shatter them.  
2. Height is safety: most herbivores climb, most predators glide or drop.  The floor—wherever one exists—is an ocean of splinters, melt pits, and bone dust.  
3. Light is a currency paid in motion.  Anything immobile for too long starves for photons or is eaten by something that still has the strength to move into brightness.

STAYING ALIVE, SHOULD YOU TRY  
Carry fire: not for warmth (resin burns hot, yes, but heat escapes in the wind) but for light and for scaring the cryobats that may mistake you for moss.  
Collect rime in gut bladders before sun touches it; once it melts it will plunge down cracks you can’t reach.  
Listen.  When the drums of branch-knock suddenly stop, it means the air has stilled; the downdraft that follows can rip the surest climber free.

EPILOGUE IMAGE  
In a rare shaft of brownish day you glimpse, far above, one canopy petal translucent against the sun.  Inside its vascular map small shapes swim—larval eels in liquid sugar, a sky-pond hanging kilometres overhead.  Wind bends the petal, sugar pours through a split seam, and golden rain begins its long, sparkling fall into darkness.  Every creature—strider, bat, moss-worm—raises head or pseudopod to drink the cold sweet that keeps this high, empty, thunderous kingdom turning.
