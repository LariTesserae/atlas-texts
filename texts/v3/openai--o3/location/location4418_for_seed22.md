From high above, the place looks almost simple: a low, humid plain opening outward for dozens of kilometers, broken here and there by ridges so newly hoisted from the crust that the stone still flashes glassy in their fracture-lines. Yet, as soon as you stand inside it, the plain rejects summary and begins constantly rewriting itself in motion and noise.

TERRAIN  
The land sits scarcely a handspan above a broad, subterranean sea. Heat from that hidden water rises through porous basalt, condenses, and bleeds out of the ground as drifts of warm mist. Because rain is intermittent and river channels are young, water collects only in sudden hollows—mirror-pans no wider than a city block, their edges abrupt, their clay bottoms the colour of bruised copper. The rest of the surface is firm, almost dry, and edged by narrow razorback ridges that look as if they were torn upward yesterday. Their slopes shed black shards that ring underfoot like broken crockery.

VEGETATION & LIGHT  
Every shard-field eventually yields to plants—though “plants” feels too small a word. Individual organisms rise tens of metres, fat with moisture, their trunks hollow and translucent like blown glass full of green fireflies. Nothing grows close beside them; each mega-flora keeps a personal radius of ten or twenty paces, so the scene is paradoxically both lush and empty. Their wide crowns knit into a high, vaulted canopy that permits only stray blades of violet daylight to reach the ground. Day never quite turns bright; even at noon the world is a cathedral lit by failed sunrise, the mist perpetually catching weak light and throwing it sideways into soft, moving shafts.

SOUND  
In that half-dark every sound is amplified. Moist air conducts vibration with ruthless efficiency, and the vegetation itself resonates. When wind presses through the canopy it plays the trunks like organ pipes: a chordal roar that can be felt in the bones from kilometres away. Layered atop the wind-music is the fauna—an abundance of winged, jointed, coiling lives whose calls are pitched for distance, not delicacy. Clicks, pulse-drums, and sub-bass hoots ricochet all day, merging into a near-continuous wall of sound. Long silence never exists here; at best one finds quieter interludes in which only a few hundred creatures are making themselves known.

FAUNA  
Most animals are adapted to a low-visibility world where echolocation beats eyesight. A moth the size of a frying pan throws rapid, metallic clicks; a six-legged grazer wears a bony diaphragm across its chest to beat thunder when alarmed; predators hunt by listening for reflections in layers of mist. Even the microorganisms, genetically coaxed centuries ago by human engineers, produce micro-sonic fields that keep them suspended in water pockets like vibrating snow.

THE CITY  
People arrived later and refused to compete by volume; instead they diverted it. They have built Horizon Span, a loose constellation of structures arranged across thirty kilometres, each separated by stretches of untouched ground. Buildings are low but broad, fabricated from self-healing alloys that drink the ambient resonance and convert it into power. From a distance they look like irregular dunes of matte silver, faintly trembling. Interior spaces remain almost silent: wall-skins absorb vibratory energy so completely that one’s own heartbeat sounds outrageous inside.

INFRASTRUCTURE & TECHNOLOGY  
Transport is handled by glide-rafts that hover a few fingerbreadths above the soil, kept aloft by controlled acoustic levitation. Ride one and you feel it humming underneath, tuned to a frequency that excludes all local animal calls so as not to mire the raft in biological chatter. Communication travels through modulated mist: microscopic, self-assembling filaments climb water droplets, rearranging to shape tight-beam lasers no wider than a hair. Data literally skims on fog.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Vision is a courtesy, not a guarantee. Anything crucial provides its own glow or echo.  
2. Nothing solid remains still; stone sings, metal drinks, trees breathe in octaves.  
3. Sound is currency—harvest it, bend it, but never waste it. The air remembers.  

DYNAMICS & WEATHER  
Afternoons bring the “falling hush,” a daily inversion when warm ground-air rises, dragging the sound layer with it. For twenty minutes the plain sinks into uncanny quiet, animals freeze, rafters slow to a crawl, and even the ridges stop ringing. Then the pressure breaks: condensing vapor drops in silver rain so sudden that lakes appear where dry ground had been. The water stays only hours before seeping back down, leaving fresh pathways for roots to invade the brittle rock.

WEIRD EDGES  
Walk far enough and you can find the places where engineers experimented with gravity lenses—ovals of space that weigh one-third the norm, installed to lighten construction loads. The lenses never fully shut down. Step into one and you rise on your toes, voice stretching into a doppler whine while the wind’s harmonic shifts three octaves higher. Wildlife has learned to nest at the margins of those ovals: eggs float there, tethered by saliva threads until hatching.

STAND STILL, LISTEN  
Pause beside a mist-pool at dusk and you may notice the pool returning your breathing to you, millisecond-delayed, laid over with alien harmonics. That is the land acknowledging your presence—mapping you, adding your outline to the great acoustic atlas that everything here shares. You are, for that moment, another note in the plain’s endless chord, a fleeting resonance in a world built from damp heat, dim light, sharp stone, and deafening life.
