The first sense that reaches you is brightness.  The air is thin and astonishingly clear; moisture gathers everywhere, yet the sky stays a crystalline blue so intense it almost hums.  You have come to a young highland—barely a few million years lifted from the sea—where ridges still wear the knife-edges gifted by their birth.  Nothing is rounded; every spur is a blade, every outcrop a tooth.  It is cool enough that your breath condenses, but the light is so strong that wherever it touches rock the stone steams faintly and the day feels warm to the skin.

1. Terrain  
   • Elevation 1.8/3 (high) meets Erosion 0.9/3 (young): imagine a mile-high plateau that has fractured into a labyrinth of narrow hanging valleys.  The flanks are fresh fractures of slate-grey basalt shot through with white quartz veining—straight, unweathered lines that glitter under the sun.  Gullies drop abruptly, some no wider than a street, some broad as a stadium floor, all ending in sudden cliffs where whole ledges have sheared away.  
   • Where the rock splinters, water seeps.  It is not a land of rivers; instead there are a thousand secret trickles, rain that arrives unexpectedly in short, hissed bursts and vanishes just as fast.  Pools gather on stone shelves and in hollow roots, bright as molten glass.  Mist forms mid-afternoon, clings to faces of rock, then drains away overnight.  Dampness hides, rather than dominates.

2. Vegetation & Density  
   The plateau is green to the point of claustrophobia.  Lianas braid from cliff to cliff like living suspension cables.  Ferns the height of a person crowd every horizontal surface, rooting in finger-deep moss.  High-altitude laurels—broad-leafed, silver-undersided—overlap their crowns so tightly that the canopy looks hammered from metal.  
   Down in the slots between ridges the plants grow stranger.  Lushness (2.5/3) becomes layering: orchids sprout from the bromeliads that sprout from the limbs of the laurels, three vertical tiers of life sharing the same shaft of sunlight.  Every leaf is slick; beads of water stay poised on fuzzed veins, refusing to fall, as though gravity here negotiates its contracts one droplet at a time.

3. Water & Sound  
   Despite the saturation, the landscape is nearly silent (0.0/3).  The thin atmosphere seems to drink vibration.  A fall of water only a few metres high is reduced to the faint patter of paper.  Your footsteps on loam are louder than the streams.  There are moments you remove your pack merely to confirm you still hear anything at all.

4. Fauna  
   Life moves, but rarely.  Now and then a black shape—part bat, part kite—glides from gorge to gorge, never flapping, vanishing behind ridges.  Something amphibian chirrs once at dusk; then the hush snaps shut again.  The plants are the loudest organisms here, their leaves creaking as temperature shifts, their seeds detonating with muffled pops that echo like distant fireworks.  Animals come in singles, never herds.  Predation relies on stillness and patience; with so little sound each motion announces itself like a beacon.

5. Built & Tech  
   Almost hidden among vines are industrial relics—scattered, intermittent, but unmistakable.  Narrow-gauge tracks appear on a ledge, run fifty metres, and stop at empty air.  Cast-iron cisterns, stamped with flaking serial numbers, sit beneath overhangs and continue to fill and overflow though no one attends them.  
   The most striking structures are the condensers.  They look like inverted cathedral spires made of copper fins and glass plates.  At dawn they glow rosy; by noon they drip steadily, funneling sky-water into porcelain culverts.  A chain of silent Pelton wheels, green with lichen, turns slowly in the down-flow, generating electricity for lights that are themselves derelict.  You pass switch-houses whose bulbs still shine—fed by the endless trickle—though their doors have rusted shut.  Industry lingers as function divorced from purpose: machines that work simply because no one has come to turn them off.

6. Atmosphere & Light  
   Light here is surgical—2.9/3 bright.  Every shadow is a pool of indigo ink; every sunlit surface flares with mirrored highlights.  At night the moon is viciously white, sky crowded with sharpened stars.  Because fauna are scarce, no wing or whisker crosses the beams.  The brightness, unsoftened by haze, gives every object an outlined precision that feels almost hallucinatory.

7. Weirdness  
   Stand long enough and you notice water sometimes crawls uphill, thin filaments drawn toward metal surfaces.  Leaves turn to follow not just the sun but the gleam of those condensers, as though photosynthesizing light reflected from copper tastes better than direct sky.  Quartz veins emit faint radio static on an old receiver; nobody knows why.  It is not a place of flamboyant impossibilities—only persistent, low-level violations, the sort that accrue like fine dust on certainty.

8. Time & Dynamics  
   Change happens, but on a glacial timetable (0.7/3).  Moss gains a finger’s breadth each year; cliffs shed a slab perhaps once in a decade.  Machines, fed by gravity and dew, will likely still be turning a century hence.  Colonies of algae glow turquoise in shaded pools each evening, fade by dawn—an ebb and flow too gradual to feel like motion while you watch.

Rules of the World  
   • Water is plentiful but never obeys: it appears, disappears, runs where it should not.  
   • Sound is rationed; any deliberate noise—a shout, a dropped tool—travels so far that conversation feels like trespass.  
   • All copper draws life: plant roots invade conduits, vines lace turbines, insects lay eggs on polished pipes.  
   • Nothing here rests horizontally for long; the land itself is tilted, impatient to slice itself into smaller pieces.

Standing on a ledge you can see the scale of it—valleys interlocking beyond sight, a regional maze of stone clefts and hanging gardens.  Somewhere out there, more condensers tick and drip.  Somewhere, maybe, a lone maintenance tram still creeps along the fractured tracks, its motor purring softly under the roar-that-is-not-a-roar of distant, miniature waterfalls.  But from where you are, suspended between too-bright sky and too-quiet earth, the landscape feels self-sufficient, content to keep rewriting the covenant between water, rock, and light at a pace no visitor will ever quite catch.
