The place is no bigger than a child’s play-marble, yet inside it unfolds an entire drowned world.

1. Envelope  
Everything here is water, a cool, velvety medium that presses from every direction. There is no free surface: the sphere is entirely flooded, the pressure identical at every point, so nothing drifts and nothing falls. Vision, filtered through near darkness, dissolves after a body-length; only soft phantoms of colour mark distance.

2. Terrain & Materials  
“Seafloor” is the wrong word, but there is structure. Across the inner skin of the sphere, freshly precipitated shelves of glass-clear aragonite jut like the first teeth of a newborn ridge. Edges are still razor-sharp; erosion has barely begun its patient filing. Veins of manganese give some ledges a soot-black glaze; others shine milk-white, and where the two mingle they grow pin-fine stalactites that point inward, meeting counter-stalactites that point outward, as though the world is undecided which way is up.

Because space folds in unstable ways, the inside seems larger than the outside. A swimmer who follows one ledge in a perfect loop can return behind herself, viewing the back of her own silhouette before she feels the shock of recognition. Geometry here is young and mischievous.

3. Vegetation & Ecology  
In the cracks and pockets where mineral meets mineral, a lush fuzz of algal fern crowds every millimetre. Each frond is translucent green and only a nail’s width long, but they knit so densely that they resemble rain-forest canopy in miniature. Their chlorophyll is useless; instead they glow by chemi-luminescence, exhaling faint violet motes that drift like plankton-fireflies. The total light those bodies emit is a breath above true night—and it never varies, because the algae feed not on photons but on the slow trickle of dissolved metals leaching from the newborn rock.

Fauna are few. Occasionally a single ribbon-worm—colourless, almost immaterial—threads between fronds, tasting, then retracts into some invisible fold. Once every long while, a tiny amphipod the size of an eyelash scoots across a shelf, stops as though listening, and abruptly vanishes, as if it remembered a different law of physics it prefers to obey.

4. Atmosphere of Quiet  
Sound moves reluctantly. One can clap stone to stone and hear nothing but a dull ceramic “tok” that dies an arm’s length away. Mostly the place hums with sub-audible tension: the barely perceptible hiss of calcite still solidifying, the microscopic clicking of algal vesicles dividing. Silence, here, is not the absence of noise but the presence of immense, waiting pressure.

5. Built Remnants  
Scattered between the sharp shelves lie artifacts that might once have belonged to someone: a doll-sized clay amphora with its neck snapped off; two bronze nails green with patina; a shard of vitreous tile painted with a sliver of cobalt sky—ironic, in a world that has never seen one. None are larger than a thumbnail, and all bear the same tell-tale curvature, as though they drifted in from a parent reality whose rules bent differently.

6. Density & Motion  
Everything is close. Vegetation knits stone, stone hems water, water fills distance. To extend a finger is to touch three things at once: mineral, leaf, and the cool thickness of fluid itself. Current is almost non-existent; change happens, but on the schedule of geologic dreaming. A ledge thickens by a grain each hour; a frond splits and becomes two overnight; every century the entire sphere expands perhaps a centimetre, pushing outward into whatever pocket of greater reality encloses it.

7. The Breakdown of Rules  
Light and space curve unpredictably; time sometimes stutters. A swimmer may feel themselves pause while everything else creeps forward a heartbeat, then be released to catch up. Directions don’t always commute: swim east then up returns you somewhere different from swimming up then east. Yet these inconsistencies are gentle, like the flex of an embryonic membrane, not the violence of tear or rupture.

8. Internal Logic  
•  Pressure is equal, so nothing falls.  
•  Chemiluminescence is constant, so no circadian rhythm exists.  
•  Distances fold; one travels by intent more than by trajectory.  
•  No complex tool endures. Anything intricate dissolves into artifact flakes, joining the scattered debris already here.  
•  Biology is opportunistic: anything that can live from mineral seepage thrives; everything else dwindles.

9. Standing in the Sphere  
Imagine placing both palms on a blade-thin ledge, cool and slick. Algal light pulses against the skin in slow cadences, violet to indigo and back. Tiny worms hover, vanish. Beyond, the curved interior repeats itself in mirrors of water and mineral until perception falters. There is the quiet conviction that if you could listen hard enough you would hear the world still making itself—stone crystallizing, life calculating how to use it, laws deciding which way tomorrow’s angles will point.

And always, around the entire globe of this pocket ocean, the dark weight of greater water presses, patient and unknowable, as though this intimate drowned garden were the pearl within some vast, unseen shell.
