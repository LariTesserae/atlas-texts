You arrive without ceremony at the mouth of a freshly-opened rift—a broad, slanting crack in the crust. Nothing here is quite finished yet. The stone underfoot is still grainy with the chalky dust of its own birth, and the walls are mottled where water has only begun to etch the first hesitant runnels. 

TERRAIN  
The rift widens as it descends, unfolding into a hollow the size of a modest valley laid on its side. Terraces step down in irregular ledges of pale shale and dark mudstone. The rock carries no memory of rivers or glaciers; it is raw, as though the planet simply pulled two plates apart last season. Here and there, entire sheets of ceiling hang by stone tabs, wanting only one more tremor before they fall and become new floors.

MATERIALS & MOISTURE  
Moisture clings to everything: dew beads on knife-sharp shale ridges, and thin curtains of condensation slide silently down the walls. Rain is an intermittent visitor; it seeps through hair-line skylights high above, gathers, then drops in brief silver threads that vanish as quickly as they arrive. Pools form in cupped ledges—mirror-smooth, no more than a handspan deep—before slowly bleeding away through porous seams. The air smells of wet dust and faint minerals, like a quarry just after a spring shower.

LIGHT  
Although you stand well below the surface, daylight reaches much of the hollow. The wide crack you entered acts as a light well, and smaller fissures lance in shafts of yellow-white. The effect is normal midday brightness, but localized—bright spotlights framed by shadow. Every few minutes a cloud passes overhead; the hollow dims, returns, dims again.

SCALE & DENSITY  
Space is paradoxical here. The chamber is open, easily large enough to hold several hill-side hamlets, yet every usable shelf, every shoulder of rock, is already spoken for. Low huts of stacked shale and puddled clay cluster on the terraces so tightly that doorways press against one another. Rope bridges, lashed from reed-fibers, zigzag between ledges like hurried stitching trying to hold a wound closed. From floor to ceiling, there is almost no unclaimed surface. You feel the saturation—no room between presences—yet the vista still sweeps far around you, its far walls veiled in mist.

THE PEOPLE & THEIR BUILT WORLD (primitive tech 1.4/3)  
Tools are stone and bone, hafted with sinew. Clay is fired in open charcoal pits; the hottest kilns are merely pits with bellows made of stretched hide. Ladders are whole saplings notched by hand-axe. Still, an organized mind is at work. Water gutters—simple half-logs split lengthwise—collect the constant drip, channeling it to communal cisterns. Sunlight is borrowed by polished sheets of mica set on angled poles, reflecting glancing beams deeper into recesses. Everything is functional, nothing ornamental yet; decoration will come when the place no longer trembles.

ATMOSPHERE & SOUND  
Sound hides. The stone swallows most of it, leaving a hush disturbed only by:

• the tap-tap of a drop finding a pool,  
• the soft rasp of woven sandals on dust,  
• an occasional muted clack as a mason trims a block.  

Conversation, when it happens, is low and brief; voices echo too easily in unfinished places.

ECOLOGY  
Vegetation is scarce—a smear of lichen luminous in shade, a few wiry ferns clinging where light lingers longest. Seedlings arrive as wind-borne chance through the skylights but rarely survive. Fauna is rarer still: pale crickets chirp once and fall silent, a thin snake slips between stones, and twice a day a handful of soot-colored bats spiral out through the main fissure to hunt aboveground before returning to roost in cracks the width of a wrist.

DYNAMICS—A PLACE STILL HAPPENING  
1. Geological: Small tremors pass weekly; each leaves new fractures that the inhabitants quickly explore and, if useful, annex with rope and torch.  
2. Hydrological: During heavy surface rain the fissures gush, turning ledges into brief cataracts; the people pull shutters of hide to deflect the sheets.  
3. Biological: Moss spreads where gutter-water overflows, attracting gnats, which in turn attract the first experimental swallows scouting for nesting sites. Life is auditioning.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
• Nothing here is stable; every structure is built with collapse in mind—walls kept low, roofs light, belongings portable.  
• Fresh rock is property; the moment a slab falls, neighbors negotiate possession before the dust has settled.  
• Daylight is currency. A spot in the splash of a sky-shaft can be traded for food, favors, or a place to sleep.  
• Silence is etiquette. Loud noise risks waking the stone.

STAND, LISTEN  
Hold still long enough and you can feel a subtle vibration in the soles of your feet, the pulse of the planet adjusting itself. The inhabitants do not fear it; they have chosen the rift precisely because it is new, because no one’s claim precedes their own.

And so the hollow grows: geology writing the lines, water punctuating them, and people—primitive, deliberate—moving quickly into every comma the earth creates, speaking softly while the ceiling decides whether it will stay or fall.
