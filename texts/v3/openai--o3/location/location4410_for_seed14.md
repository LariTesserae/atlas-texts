The place lies far below any surface you know—so deep that “below” and “within” begin to mean the same thing.  Pressure folds the water into a substance heavier than intuition allows, and the dark is so complete that even when your eyes are open you feel them closed.  Yet the water never rests.  Currents bite and shear, slide past one another in vertical rivers, then knot themselves into vortices that burst apart in clouds of silt.  It is a permanently storm-tossed abyss in which nothing is still, even though nothing seems to move.

Terrain  
Look for landmarks and you find only their eroded ghosts.  Ridges have slumped into broad, low swales; once-jagged buttresses have been planed smooth, their edges blurred by a hundred million years of slow-rolling slurry.  The ground is a hardpan of basaltic powder pressed into stone by its own weight.  Everywhere, thin sheets of sediment shift like dune fields caught in slow motion, crests collapsing, troughs filling.  Occasionally a line of tougher rock holds firm and rises a few body-heights—a ruin of an ancient cliff—before declining again into the featureless plain.

Materials  
Rock and water make up almost everything here, but neither is simple.  The rock is impregnated with metal oxides that glitter weakly when your lamp glances across them, hints of red and violet trapped in crystal grains.  The water carries a permanent storm of particulate: pollen-fine silts, flocculent skeins of decaying plankton, microscopic shells ground to dust.  Reach out and you touch a thickness, a weight, as though the sea were equal parts liquid and suspended earth.  No constructed thing intrudes; whatever once might have been built has either never arrived or long since dissolved into the sediment.

Atmosphere (if one can call it that)  
Temperature hovers just above the freezing point of brine.  The cold is not biting, it is suffocatingly calm—an abiding, matter-of-fact chill that seeps through every membrane, steals the heat of thought.  Pressure turns even small bubbles into silver pinpricks that snap shut the moment they are born.  Light is absent; only when crustal stresses crack open a vent does chemosynthetic fire briefly paint the silt from below in sulfurous green.  Most of the time, if you see anything at all, it is the faint blue chemi-luminescence of single cells drifting past like lost sparks.

Sound  
Silence here is a texture, not an absence.  You can sense, more than hear, the heavy hush of water rubbing against water.  Occasionally a deep thrumming arrives—distant seafloor quakes, or the groan of a buried plate adjusting its fit—but those noises are felt in the bones rather than heard in the ear.  When the silt storms rage, billions of grains collide with a hiss too high to register consciously, yet present enough to roughen the background quiet.

Vegetation and Ecology  
Life clings thinly.  On the rock plains spread bacterial mats the color of rusted tin, no thicker than frost on glass.  Filamentous algae rise a handspan into the current before surrendering to the shearing flow; they look like tattered banners frozen mid-flutter.  True plants are absent—there is no sun to feed them—and even these microbial growths appear only where vents leak trace nutrients.  

Fauna occur rarely, as suggestions more than presences.  
• A lone, translucent isopod the length of your forearm drifts by, legs paddling, then folds itself into the silt and vanishes.  
• A featureless eel—blind, ivory, slow as cooling wax—follows scent rather than sight, nuzzling the bacterial crust for edible patches.  
• Once in a great distance, a shadow larger than certainty passes overhead, ridge-finned and silent, and the water column itself seems to draw in breath until it has gone.  
These creatures are not numerous; each owes its survival to extraordinary patience and an energy budget pared to the bone.

Rules of the World  
1. Everything is old. Nothing young lasts unless it accepts the slow, grinding grammar of the deep.  
2. Movement is constant but destination is moot; currents rewrite the map faster than any traveler can memorize it.  
3. Light is an event.  Its arrival changes the neighborhood, drawing predators, scattering prey, re-writing behaviors that otherwise measure time only by chemistry.  
4. The pressure forgives no hollowness.  Any bubble, any pocket, any cavity is a flaw waiting to be erased.  Structures—natural or imagined—must be solid through and through, or resign themselves to collapse.  
5. Sounds travel as belonging, not as messages: everything is connected by the same vibrations, so secrecy is impossible, but interpretation is slow, dreamlike.  

Dynamics  
Despite the scarcity of visible life, the environment itself behaves like a bustling marketplace of forces.  Internal tides slam into escarpments and rebound upwards in plumes of mud.  Cold brines spill from fractures and roll across the seafloor like inverted waterfalls.  On a human timescale this feels restless, even chaotic; on geologic time it traces a looping choreography that is, paradoxically, the very emblem of permanence.  Nothing here is allowed to stand tall, and yet nothing is ever quite removed—everything is repurposed, kneaded, folded back.

Stand on—or rather, hover above—this ancient, drowned plain, and you may sense a crowdedness not of organisms but of presences: pressure, cold, weight, darkness, current, age, all interwoven so tightly that there is literally no room for anything more.  It is a saturation of the fundamental rather than the elaborate.  The longer you stay, the more you notice how complete the environment already is, how little it seeks or permits adornment.

In the end, what the place offers is an austere, turbulent stability: a world that has finished becoming what it is, and now rehearses itself endlessly in the dark.
