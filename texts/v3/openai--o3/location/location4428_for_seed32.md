You step into it as though into a box no larger than a cathedral nave, yet once inside there is almost no free air at all—every cubic span is occupied by something made, grown, fitted.  

1. Envelope and scale  
The place is a pocket-world carved into the flank of a greater geology, a chamber perhaps sixty paces long, thirty wide, ten high, sealed on every side. No corridors lead away; there is no sky to find. Its walls bow outward as if straining to keep something vaster at bay, but they never yield and they never move. Nothing in here has changed for longer than anyone remembers.

2. Substrate—wholly built, but low-tech  
There is no natural stone exposed. Instead the entire enclosure—floor, buttresses, ceiling coffers—is laid in an intricate tessellation of hand-sized bricks of compacted salt and pale feldspar. The bricks are mortared not with cement but with compressed dust and rootlets, a technique so simple it hardly counts as “technology,” yet so thorough that no seam breaks the pattern. Every face has been abraded by centuries of dry wind until it is satin-smooth and softly luminous, so the chamber glows white even where no direct beam falls.

3. Atmosphere, temperature, light  
The air is cool, almost chilly, and scoured of all moisture. Each breath carries the tang of mineral grit and the faint bite of alkali. Illumination pours from two sources: crystalline arches in the ceiling that catch distant solar glare forced in through fist-wide light wells, and the walls themselves, which scatter that glare into a uniform brilliance. Shadows exist only as suggestions underfoot. The brightness never waxes or wanes; here, time forgot to cycle.

4. Sound—loud in a place without life  
In such a small, roofed world you would expect a hush, but the opposite rules. A ceaseless roar fills the space, generated by wind that siphons through the same light wells that feed the chamber its radiance. The wells have been lined with rows of resonant slats—perhaps decorative, perhaps accidental—and the passage of air turns them into organ pipes. The tone is neither music nor noise, only a massive, unvarying chord that drowns thought and presses on bone. The bricks vibrate faintly; one can feel them hum through the soles of their boots.

5. Terrain and density  
Every horizontal surface has been claimed. Low shelves, terraces, and narrow ramps interlock so completely that the notion of a flat floor vanishes. Much of the chamber is filled waist-to-chest high with blocky furniture-like protrusions, so moving through it requires constant sidestepping, climbing, threading. It is impossible to see the whole interior at once; instead a warren of alcoves and blind courts crowds the eye. Despite the jumble, nothing is rubble—each protrusion sits deliberately where some nameless builder set it, belonging to an overall pattern that can be sensed but never quite grasped.

6. Vegetation—sparse, restrained  
Life clings here only as thin skeins of dull silver lichen that paper certain bricks, especially in hollows shielded from the wind. The lichen feeds not on water but on mineral vapor and faint light. Its fronds lie flat, unmoving, a biological veneer more than a plant. Now and then a patch releases a dry puff of spores that vanish in the drafts—no other motion breaks the stillness.

7. Erosion, but no change  
Though the fabric of the room is worn, it is worn into equilibrium: edges rounded, dust swept always to the same corners, bricks polished to the same degree. The roar of air scours every surface evenly; any mote entering is either ground into the walls or driven out again by the pressure hiss. As a result, nothing here proceeds toward either construction or decay. The place is finished, and permanence is its only activity.

8. Absence of water and fauna  
No drip, no damp trace, no memory of fluid remains. Salt rims every crack; if water once lived here it has long sublimated away. No insects navigate the brightness, no rodents scratch. The lichen alone keeps company with the wind.

9. Rules that emerge  
• Brightness is constant, denying circadian cues.  
• The wind never varies in pitch or volume; its source, speed, and direction are locked.  
• Any object introduced—tool, seed, flesh—will be abraded, bleached, immobilized until it conforms to the reigning palette of salt white and feldspar grey.  
• Sound swallows speech; visitors must communicate by touch or sign.  
• Because all surfaces are built, to remove even one brick feels sacrilegious, a wound in an organism that has otherwise completed its growth.

10. The feel of the place  
Standing amid the salt labyrinth you sense compression: space packed, light saturated, sound overwhelming, yet all committed to stillness. It is a desert folded into a room and then refined until nothing extraneous survived. The chamber is not dead, but finished—an art piece, a mausoleum, a lung for the planet maybe, where wind sings through salt to remind the larger world that stasis can be beautiful, and terrible, and deafeningly loud.
