You step into altitude so high the cloud-deck rests below your boots, yet everything around you is wet—streaming, dripping, spraying. It is not a mountain peak in any ordinary sense, but a titanic upland of living matter: a continent-wide mesa grown, not lifted, from the planet’s crust. Every square meter is plant.

1. Terrain and materials  
• The “ground” is a springy rind of lignin-rich tissue a hundred meters thick. Roots the width of rivers braid through it, knitting a surface the way scar tissue closes a wound. Nothing here has had time to break down into soil; erosion has barely begun. Fissures gape like the seams of a fresh loaf, still warm from the oven of tectonic upwelling. The rawness gives everything an unfinished sheen—edges too sharp, colors too saturated.  
• Where cracks open, cold vapors billow up from magma-warmed aquifers, condense, and fall again as silver rain. These cataracts sluice off cliffs of green flesh, carving embryo fjords whose walls continuously heal and re-knit. Geology and biology are one manufacturing line, caught in quality control before any piece leaves the factory.  

2. Water (2.1/3, wet)  
Liquid is everywhere but never still. Veins inside the substrate pulse with mineral-laden sap that doubles as river system. On the surface, sheet-floods sprint across the ridges, then dive into breathing stomata the size of wells. Nothing puddles for long; the living land slurps it back with thirsty peristaltic gulps. Mist hangs in perennial curtains, replenished by the warm guts of the plateau and chilled by the thin, high air.

3. Vegetation (2.6/3, total)  
Forget trunks and leaves. Imagine architecture grown from chlorophyll and cartilage: buttresses of photosynthetic bark arch over canyons; lacy membranes span kilometers, catching dim light and channeling it through fiber-optic phloem. There is no bare surface—every naked protrusion quickly sprouts scale-moss or lens-lichen that converts ultraviolet twilight into chemical charge. Parasite vines writhe like cables, not searching for sun but for the bioelectric hum inside larger hosts. Predation here is vascular: one plant taps another’s conduit, siphons sugars, then seals the incision with glossy resin before the victim even knows it has bled.

4. Light (1.3/3, dim)  
A perpetual dusk reigns, even at local noon. The photosynthetic infrastructure has adapted: chloroplasts line up like micro-mirrors, focusing sparse photons into reaction centers that glow a weak emerald. At night, bioluminescent calligraphy scrolls across leaves—long exposure photography made real as enzymes write instructions in light for neighboring tissues to read.

5. Temperature (1.0/3, cool)  
Air hovers just above freezing. Exhalations of warm fissure-steam occasionally swell the temperature, creating microclimates where frost cohabits with blooming iridescence. Each warmth-pulse is followed by a glassy rime that crackles as it forms, adding to the landscape’s clamor.

6. Sound (2.9/3, deafening)  
Silence is impossible. The entire biome is hydraulics and thunder:  
• Xylem-surges cannon through roots, a basso heartbeat you feel in your kneecaps.  
• Flexile canopies snap open and shut to pump spores into jetstreams, producing booms that echo like distant artillery.  
• Condensing fog falls as granular drizzle—millions of microscopic impacts that together hiss louder than rain on a tin roof.  
Earplugs quiver; even thoughts arrive with an aftertaste of resonance.

7. Elevation (1.5/3, high) & Scale (3.0/3, vast)  
You are on a shelf the size of Eurasia, so elevated the horizon curves down toward a sea of cloud. Local weather coils upon itself: downdrafts start, stall against the biomass, reverse into up-spirals that braid luminous pollen to mesosphere heights. Storms never leave; they shunt sideways along the plateau’s rim like cattle circling a fence.

8. Density (2.9/3, packed)  
There is no empty volume. Air is seeded with spore-flakes, water with ciliate seeds, every crevice pre-claimed by rootlets. Step off the prepared ledge (a natural flexing plate), and stems knit between your ankles before you land. Space is a suggestion the plants tolerate only momentarily.

9. Built environment (0.3/3, none)  
No tool, no ruin, no sculpture made by conscious hands appears. Yet you can’t help sensing intention: spiral staircases of branch, vaults of petiole lattice, domes that focus rain into central wells. Purpose without builder—biology as civil engineer.

10. Technology (2.8/3, beyond modern)  
The plateau’s genomic code is a library of engineering most physicists would label speculative. Inside certain fronds, quantum coherence is harnessed to tune nutrient transport; root tips emit weak gravitomagnetic ripples to fend off erosion before it starts; some leaf arrays lens neutrinos for reasons no visitor has decoded. It is “technology” only if you insist that problem-solving must be mechanical; here it is simply what life does.

11. Fauna (0.5/3, none)  
No heartbeat but the plant-pulse. No wingbeat but the flap of valve-leaves. Yet trophic layers exist: hunter-vines digest competitor tissue; sensor-pods intercept pheromonal radio; dueling forests deploy allelopathic fogs that rustle like speech. The drama of mouths without mouths plays on a continental stage.

12. Dynamic processes (1.9/3, active)  
Cycles run concurrently:  
• Dawn flood: rising thermals draw deep sap upward, bursting in flower-geysers that feed aerial plankton.  
• Noon freeze: radiative cooling drops temperature, encasing outer tissues in armor-ice.  
• Dusk molt: whole hectares shed cuticle sheets that drift like papery glaciers, to be reabsorbed downstream.  
Time here is not measured in years but in pulses—a metronome set by hydraulic surge and photosynthetic tide.

13. Rules of the world  
Gravity feels familiar yet slightly irregular, as though the land’s mass distribution flexes while you walk. Electromagnetic haze interferes with electronics; unshielded gear resets itself under the influence of bio-ionic discharges. You suspect the plateau could think, if “thinking” means sensing, integrating, choosing.

14. Atmosphere & scent  
The air tastes metallic, like rainwater on old batteries. Ozone tang competes with sweet terpene breath from yawning sap-wounds. Every inhalation feels medicinal and slightly narcotic, slowing the heart while sharpening peripheral vision—a pharmacological handshake between visitor and land.

15. Departure  
Somewhere beyond audible range—beyond even the plateau’s thunder—the planet turns. Up here, though, the biome’s own weather, chemistry, and pulse drown out celestial rhythms. You leave unsure whether you walked across a place, a single gargantuan organism, or both. Behind you, green fissures close, water hisses, and the thunder of growing things rolls on without need for witness.
