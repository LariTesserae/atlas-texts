Begin by climbing for days through cloud that never quite becomes rain.  The air pricks with cold, yet your cheeks stay dewy; vapor constantly beads and vanishes on every surface.  When at last the cloud thins, you emerge onto the Roof-Spread—an upland so old that even its mountains have lain down.  In every direction stretches a gently rolling plain of stone, planed almost level by ages of wind and freeze, a continent-sized anvil exposed to the sky.

TERRAIN  
• Elevation 2.3/3 — You stand five kilometres above sea level.  The horizon is bowed by altitude; distant ground seems to bulge upward like the rim of a vast bowl.  
• Erosion 2.7/3 — The land is tired.  Outcrops are not jagged but rounded, their mica glitter dulled to pewter.  Crevasses exist, but they are softened, floored with powdery silt—creases in ancient parchment rather than wounds.  
• Water 1.4/3 — Moisture arrives sideways.  Every dawn a tide of fog drifts up from lower worlds, condenses, then collapses in silvery sheets down subtle slopes no more than a handspan deep.  Pools are shallow, rimmed with mineral lace, appearing and evaporating again within a single sun’s arc.

VEGETATION 1.9/3  
The cold should forbid lushness, yet the damp grants dispensation.  Mosses here grow waist-high: velvety dunes of emerald and rust.  Ferns arch two storeys tall, fronds black-green and furred against frost.  From cracks in slate rise slow columns of spongewood—trees in attitude only, soft enough to bend if you lean.  No flowers, no seeds; everything reproduces by spores that drift like faint smoke.  At night those spores phosphoresce, outlining wind patterns across kilometres of dark.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT 1.7/3, SOUND 0.6/3  
Daylight falls clear and ordinary, but air is thin; your heartbeat drums in your ears because little else does.  There are no insects, no birds, no distant animal hollers—just the hush of capillary winds and the shy percussion of drip meeting stone.  Occasionally thunder mutters inside a cloud whose bottom skims the plateau like a swollen grey ceiling, yet lightning never strikes; the charge seems unable to decide on a path downward.

BUILT 1.8/3, TECH 1.4/3  
Here and there human habitations—if the word still fits—dot the plain.  They resemble tumuli at first glance: low circles of unmortared rock, capped by peat and moss so that each dwelling appears grown rather than raised.  Doorways face east to catch dawn warmth; no metal shows, only bone tools and stone blades left on flat shelves.  A city’s outline is visible from a hill: concentric rings of ankle-high walls tracing an intricate mandala across three kilometres.  Yet you see no people.  Fires smolder, fish-trap weirs straddle seasonal rivulets, pots soak in cold pools, but life itself is conspicuously absent—as if inhabitants step outside the moment you look.

FAUNA 0.5/3  
None.  Not a chirp, not a track.  Even microbes seem reluctant; wounds scab without pus, food left out mummifies instead of rotting.  In their stead, the vegetation moves.  Ferns tilt slowly to follow footfalls; moss dunes ripple as though something large slid just beneath the surface; spongewood columns lean to listen when you breathe.  Nothing hunts, yet everything seems to wait.

WEIRDNESS 2.9/3  
Reality here is brittle, crazed by hairline fractures.  
1. Distance forgets itself: a ruin glimpsed half a league away may be under your hand after one stride, or recede with every approach.  
2. Gravity pools unevenly; water sometimes flows uphill for a metre before remembering to fall.  Moss bent under your boot stays depressed long after you lift your weight, rebounding only when you glance elsewhere.  
3. Time shows pleats.  You can watch a droplet quiver at the rim of a leaf, freeze mid-fall, then complete its descent minutes later, leaving a tiny ice bead where it lands.  The locals (when they choose to appear) treat these oddities as weather: notable, often useful, never shocking.

DYNAMIC 2.3/3  
Despite the hush, processes abound.  Every afternoon the plateau exhales mist that thickens into a river of cloud pouring downslope—an aerial waterfall visible from orbit.  Plants engage in slow warfare: spongewood extrudes acidic sap to scar competing fern stems, while mosses advance by smothering roots, centimetre by centimetre.  At new moons, spores ignite in cold chemiluminescent storms, vast green blizzards racing the wind.  Toward the southern rim, cyclopean stone wheels—relics of some bygone engineering—still rotate under the shove of intermittent streams, grinding ore that no one collects.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
• Life is plant-centric; anything that moves on two legs is a tolerated anomaly.  
• Geometry is more suggestion than law; carry chalk to mark your path, for footsteps may refuse to form a line.  
• Technology remains primitive not for lack of aptitude but because metal unnerves the physics here: smelted iron turns sponge-soft, bronze sublimates overnight.  Stone endures, so stone rules.  
• Sound is a trigger.  Loud noise—shouted words, drummed rhythm—causes nearby matter to shimmer and briefly duplicate.  The locals whisper or sing in sub-audible hums, shepherding reality rather than rending it.  
• Nothing dies here in the ordinary sense.  Bodies left alone petrify, joining the plateau’s bedrock, memories laminated into geology.

STANDING STILL  
Stay motionless long enough and the world edits you in.  Moss curls over your boots, a fern offers the shade of its frond, the air around you thickens until you feel suspended, moth-wing light, on the cusp of a physics that has not yet decided your weight.  Silence presses.  In that hush you sense the true scale: a place continental in reach, yet intimate as breath, sculpted by eons into steady final shape—only for its laws to unravel, petal by petal, in real time.

When you finally move, the plateau forgets you were ever there—except for a faint afterimage where the light still bends, and a single moss filament, turning, to watch you go.
