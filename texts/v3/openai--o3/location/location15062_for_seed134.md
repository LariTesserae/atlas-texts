A place you can reach only by drifting downward through a hundred metres of silver-grey ocean, then turning sideways into a fissure whose walls almost meet above your head—the Gorge of Pistons, the locals would call it if there were locals.  

Terrain  
The gorge crowns the summit of a newly born seamount: raw volcanic glass, basalt daggers, unweathered ledges. Although the mountain’s peak rises nearly two kilometres above the abyssal plain, here at the crown you are still twenty metres below the surface; “high ground” is a relative term when the world is water. Edges have not yet learned to soften: every ridge is keen, every flank crisp. Cavernous alcoves unfold like organ pipes, each the size of a cathedral nave, yet the whole precinct would fit inside a modest town square if you could lift it into air.

Atmosphere (water-sphere)  
The sea is cool—eight, perhaps nine degrees—and startlingly clear. Normal daylight filters down through the gap in the roof, sheeting the stone in pale cobalt. Dust motes of plankton are almost absent; the water feels hollow, emptied of the usual soft haze of life. What fills that vacancy instead is pressure, a thick hush that grips your ribs, and a continuous concussion of sound.

Sound  
The place is loud in the way a factory floor is loud, but nothing here is built. The rock itself booms. Somewhere unseen, flexible plates in the cliff faces contract and release like giant bellows, resonating through the stone. Between pulses, smaller clacks and ratcheting ticks overlap: shell on shell, tooth on armour, valve on valve. The effect is a layered machinery with no engines—just life performing its errands.

Light  
Sunlight provides a faint top-wash, enough to give colour, but most of the illumination issues from the animals. Luminescent fins, turbid chem-clouds, bands of cold-white along serrated carapaces: their collective glow equals a cloudy noon. It is steady, not the flashing of ordinary deep-sea creatures; like everything here, change comes slowly.

Ecology  
No plants, no algae sheets on stone, no drifting plumes of kelp. Energy starts with the mountain itself. From razor cracks seep cool brine enriched with trace metals; where the brine touches oxygenated water, crystals bloom—chalcopyrite lace and iron-salt feathers. Micro-films of chemotrophic bacteria (closer to mineral than life) coat those crystals, and the animals eat the film.

The smallest are thumb-sized “riveters,” prismatic isopods whose forelegs end in opposed wedges. They traverse the walls, chiselling off bacterial scale. Their scrapings fall like filings and are sucked up by “sluicers,” metre-long eels with vacuum mouths that exhale sand-fine waste into neat dunes on the grotto floor.

Predators—“presses”—resemble vertical iron lozenges two metres tall. Each has paired shell-plates hinged at one side. It waits open until a current of riveters wanders between, then shuts with the report of a dropped anvil. The sound reverberates up the walls, feeding the gorge’s great pulse.

At the roof drift the largest things: “derricks,” six-metre medusoid forms rimmed with ring-gears of bone. They turn slowly, harvesting directional light with mirror-slick skins and bleeding the energy down tether-veins that anchor in the crystal seams. Their rotation is so deliberate that a full circle takes hours; their age is guessed in centuries.

Density & Arrangement  
Everything occupies the same cubic metres. Riveters carpet every surface; sluicers queue in serpentine layers along the chasms; juvenile presses cling like bolted plates to the adults’ shells. Nothing here keeps distance—contact is constant, near-touching, unavoidable. Yet the system is stable, each creature’s timing tuned to its neighbours’ sluggish cycles.

Weird Rules  
1. Nothing dies quickly. Wounds oxidise, plate over, and may remain open for seasons before sealing.  
2. Sound substitutes for sight in ruling behaviour. A silent animal is invisible, no matter how brightly it shines.  
3. The mountain is still growing. Once every several human lifetimes a new rift opens, emitting a hissing spool of crystal foam that hardens into more walls, new aisles for life to colonise.

Temporal Rhythm  
Across hours you might notice almost nothing shift—only the subtle migration of dunes as sluicers vent tailings. Yet over decades the geometry of the place rewrites: ridges extrude, caves filigree shut, the choir of presses tunes itself deeper as their shells thicken. It is industry at a geologic tempo.

Standing in the narrow mid-grotto you feel suspended inside a living engine, a cool oceanic heart whose beat counts epochs instead of seconds, whose gears are bone and shell, whose only fuel is the mineral breath of a mountain that has never broken the surface of its world-wide sea.
