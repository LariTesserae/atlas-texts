The first sensation is the heat—thick, bath-warm, surprisingly moist for a place that looks so bare. You stand at the bottom of a wide, shallow basin, scarcely twenty metres above the planet’s patchy sea. The land rolls almost flat to every horizon, broken only by low swells of scabbed rock and the raised backs of levees the colonists built generations ago to keep the tide at bay. Everything feels crouched close to sea-level, as if the world were trying to sink back under water.

TERRAIN AND MATERIALS  
The ground is old, worn smooth by ages of steaming rain and mineral-rich floods. Clay the colour of dried blood lies in peeled sheets, its surface crazed like parched skin even though it never stays dry for long. Between the clay plates, puddles sit in soft pockets of blue-black silt so fine it gleams like spilled ink. When you stir it with a boot the silt releases trapped gas in lazy silver ropes—evidence of the microorganisms that form the closest thing to vegetation here.

Outcrops of weeping basalt puncture the flats. Each rock drools condensation, the water beading on their warm surfaces before dripping away in steady ticks. The salt carried up from the brine aquifer blooms around them in powdery collars as white as bone. Wind, though weak, has had time to round every edge; nothing here keeps a sharp corner except the works of people.

HYDROLOGY AND ATMOSPHERE  
The water table practically grazes the surface. Dig half a metre and you find warm, metallic brine. Some nights, when the air cools by a precious few degrees, the ground exhales: mist wells from the cracks and hangs knee-high in slow, glowing sheets, scattering the pale light of the shrouded sky.

Sunlight is scarce—not because the star is faint but because the air is loaded with steam and industrial haze. Day is a coppery dimness, evening merely tilts the palette toward plum. The horizon always looks a little greasy, as if smeared with machine oil. Yet you can feel the sun behind it all, pumping heat downward like a forge bellows.

BUILT ENVIRONMENT  
Across the basin lies Carrow Mire—less a city than a loose archipelago of iron islands. Each district stands on pilings driven into the clay, linked by viaducts of riveted steel and weather-silvered wood. Squat brick boilers squat at the center of every platform, their smokestacks punching into the mist. Conveyor belts carry ore from open barges straight into roaring furnaces; reciprocating pumps thunk ceaselessly, lifting brine into tall condensation towers where rare fresh water dribbles out and the leftover salt is carted away in glowing wagons.

Despite the machinery, the built density feels thin. Whole blocks are separated by yawning wetlands or naked evaporation pans. Everything is spread enough that, when the factory whistles fall silent at shift change, you can hear water lap softly against pilings miles apart.

TECHNOLOGY AND POWER  
Industry runs on pressurized steam and coarse electric dynamos wound with brine-proof copper. At night the walkways glow with weak amber arc-lamps whose filaments hiss inside thick glass eggs. Cranes creak over the harbour, methodical and slow—it is too hot and too damp for combustion engines to behave, so the city relies on piston logic and heavy flywheels. The technology feels muscular, ironclad, but not yet delicate.

LACK OF VEGETATION  
There is nothing you would recognize as a plant. No stems, no leaves, no roots. The red clay occasionally grows a brittle crust—thin lacework domes no higher than a finger—that snaps like spun sugar underfoot. Up close it smells faintly of iodine; it is a colony of ferro-bacteria extruding iron salts for shelter. Along the levee walls, pale threads dangle like pulled candy: gypsum filaments spun by chemical gradients where seepage meets warm air. The city’s cooks rely on imported grain bricks; everything native to this place begins and ends in mineral form.

FAUNA—THE CROWD THAT NEVER STOPS MOVING  
Life, however, has not waited for plants. The wetlands churn with creatures built directly from the chemical broth. Wrist-sized crustaceans—called clickers for the sound of their knuckles—boil up from the muck in migrating carpets. Their shells are translucent, showing the dull glint of hematite plates growing inside. When they swarm they darken the causeways like moving shadow.

Feeding on the clickers are long-necked skimsails: rubbery, manta-shaped things that row through the shallows with fin-tips and gulp prey with vacuum-mouths. Their backs steam in the sun; the locals harvest the condensed fresh water that beads there, following the creatures in small punts.

And above, everywhere, the dronelines. These are thread-thin insects that live their entire lives aloft, weaving sticky cords between the condensers and the basalt stelae. Each dawn the cords vibrate like aeolian harps, a collective purr just beneath hearing. Workers spare them because dronelines snare the stinging midges that would otherwise make human life unbearable.

If you stand still, you notice the city always shivers—breathing with the pulse of its fauna. Pipes knock, boilers wheeze, but between the mechanical noises is an underlying animal rustle, as if the whole basin were a single swarm.

SOUND AND QUIET  
For a settlement this industrial, the quiet is remarkable. The thick air muffles clamor; steam escapes with a dignified sigh; even the tidal surf comes filtered through kilometers of reeds made of crystal filaments that hardly clack together. Shift whistles float softly then dissolve. When a locomobile hisses along the main trestle you hear it a long time coming, a lone heartbeat in a wide lung.

EROSION AND AGE  
Nothing feels new. The levee plates are pitted like fossil bone. Rail spikes wear collars of ferrous bloom. Even the people’s boots show a permanent patina of salt lacquer the color of old blood. Yet the city neither advances nor decays; it has reached a rough equilibrium with the warm, wet chemistry that birthed it.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Water is everywhere, but drinkable water is treasure—condensation is currency.  
2. Anything left unused will be colonized—by mineral, by microbe, by clicking shell. Ownership is maintenance.  
3. Noise invites predators; thus factories work in rhythmical shifts so the wilderness can anticipate them and stay away.  
4. Steel lasts, flesh persists, wood rots overnight. Every import must be either metal or already dead.  
5. Heat never truly leaves; night is only a slightly darker day.

DYNAMICS—WHAT MIGHT HAPPEN WHILE YOU WATCH  
Clouds gather inland each afternoon and march seaward. When they break, the rain falls almost hot, hissing when it hits colder mineral surfaces. Pools fill, crusts dissolve, clickers rush to release young before the water retreats. The city shuts its steam valves so as not to flash-boil passers-by.

Twice a year, distant tectonics burp plumes of sulphur that crawl across the basin like bruised fog. During these events the inhabitants seal their homes, and the skimsails erupt in mating frenzies, turning the air into low, gliding chaos.

EPILOGUE—STANDING AT THE EDGE  
You can walk the eastern levee at low tide and see the whole story: dark mud, faint lights strung like beads across the flats, steam stalking skyward in curling banners, and beyond it the humming skin of animal life that has replaced forests, meadows, and all green dreams. The place is hot, dim, and seemingly inhospitable, yet it thrums with organisms and with a stubborn human industry. It is, against expectation, very much alive—but in its own mineral, water-logged, salt-shelled vocabulary.
