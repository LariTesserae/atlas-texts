At first there is only weight and hush.  
The water here is not the racing swell of open sea but a patient, almost viscous darkness that hangs within stone the way sap hangs inside old wood.  Push a hand forward and it feels as though the water is thinking about the gesture before it lets you proceed.

TERRAIN & SHAPE  
Imagine the crown of a drowned mountain—an ancient peak whose summit chambers were once open to air and ceremony.  Now the ocean has climbed eleven vertical kilometers, and those summit rooms have become a flooded pocket high in the water-column, far above the buried lowlands.  The place is small, perhaps the footprint of a village church: a single nave, side passages no wider than a person, a stepped ambulatory that folds back on itself like the coils of a snail.  Every surface is carved—no seam of natural rock remains.  The builders quarried the very stone they stood on, then returned the cut blocks into arches, lintels, slow-spiraling ramps.  There are no right angles; corners are rounded from a million years of grain-by-grain erosion, until the architecture feels grown rather than assembled.

MATERIALS  
Black volcanic glass predominates, translucent enough that a lamp would send a wine-red glow several body-lengths out—if lamps still burned here.  In the dim you see mica gleams where ancient chisels struck, shallow sockets where wooden dowels once locked stone to stone, grooves that might have guided ropes or reeds.  Everything brittle has already surrendered; only the tongue-smooth obsidian remains.

LIGHT  
Nominally this is darkness: the sun that beats on the ocean’s surface never finds its way this deep.  Yet the water is a galaxy of faint points.  Minute crustaceans carry phosphor granules in the joints of their carapaces; eelpout with sightless eyes pulse a dull bronze from patches along the lateral line; every exhalation of a creature makes one more bead of milky green appear for a heartbeat before winking out.  It is enough to read forms by, never enough to name a color with certainty.

SOUND  
There is no drum of surf, no creak of timber, only a soft tick when a shell brushes stone or the distant hiss of a bubble navigating a ruined stairwell.  Most of the time the water is motionless; pressure, not current, keeps it pooled within these chambers.  Even your own heartbeat disperses into the density before it can echo back.

TEMPERATURE & CHEMISTRY  
The mountain root still remembers fire.  Thermal vents deep in its body leak warmth upward, ironing the water to a steady, bath-warm calm.  Minerals ride the heat plumes, precipitating in lacey shelves against the vaulted ceiling.  They look like frozen smoke, but tap one with a fingertip and it rings like porcelain.

ECOLOGY (WITHOUT VEGETATION)  
No plant clings to wall or floor; photosynthesis never reaches here.  Life survives by grazing on mineral fog and the slow rain of drifting carcasses from brighter layers above.

1. Glass Shrimp: jointed, nearly transparent save for flecks of opalescent bacteria cultured in grooves along their flanks.  They file in silent processions across the carved steps, scrubbing the stone of edible residue.
2. Chamber Lamps: fist-sized mollusks glued to the ceiling, beating cilia to waft hydrothermal nutrients into their mouths.  When disturbed they retract, their glow extinguishing instantly—the room feels as though someone snuffed a candle.
3. Hollow Fish: long, reed-thin vertebrates whose bones are latticed like scaffolding.  They drift vertically, heads down, sipping suspended iron from the vents the way swifts take insects on the wing.
4. Listener Crabs: flat, disk-shaped, pressed tight to walls.  Their carapace is tuned to resonate at very low frequencies; when the ground trembles, they shuffle toward the source in blind anticipation of fresh mineral spill.

DENSITY & MOVEMENT  
Space is thick with bodies.  Every archway is draped in curtains of life, each pocket of still water holds a micro-constellation.  Yet the scene is not frenetic.  Currents are weak; locomotion is measured.  A shrimp may require an hour to traverse a single rib of stone, a fish a full day to spiral once around the nave.  Change here is geological in tempo—bones accrue on ledges, become filigreed lime, are swallowed by the wall.

TECHNOLOGY & CULTURE OF THE MAKERS  
The marks left by hands are unmistakable, but the tools were simple: stone mauls, antler picks, lengths of fiber that once looped through those grooves.  One can stand in the center of the main chamber and imagine a scaffolding of poles laced together, masons waist-deep in cloud to chisel each block, no metal, no wheel, only patience against basalt.  Whoever they were, they preferred enclosure; even before the flood, light would have reached them only from torches or sunshafts cut in the roof.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Gravity is recognizably Earth-like, but nearly everything that matters happens in suspension.  Life adapts to float, cling, or swim slowly against pressure rather than against pull.  
2. Nothing grown survives: nutrition is mineral; architecture is memory; movement is negotiation with density, not distance.  
3. Erosion is the primary author.  The longer a surface exists, the smoother it becomes, until eventually object and water and creature share a common curve.  
4. Sound is spared, sight is rationed, touch is amplified.  A fingertip learns more than an eye.

FUTURE MOTION  
The climb of the ocean appears finished.  These rooms have been sealed in warm dark for epochs and will likely remain so until the mountain spine itself collapses, eons from now.  The biosphere within is complete—a closed cathedral of stone and slow blood where every death is substrate for another life, every errant bubble the murmur of a congregation that never rises from its pews.

Step—or rather, glide—through an arch and you will not disturb history.  You will simply inherit the same gentle pressure, the same distant ringing of mineral shelves, and join the uncounted bodies waiting here for the next minor miracle of change.
