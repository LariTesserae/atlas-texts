You come up on it from below, riding in the guttural throb of a lift-cage that was welded together generations ago. Through the lattice floor you see nothing but moving water: cataracts falling past you, side-ways sheets blown flat against the cliff face, vapor veils breathing outward, all of it silver in the ordinary midday light. The people who live here call the whole region the High Drown, for the simple reason that even at this height—nearly three vertical kilometres above the vanished paleo-seabed—you are still inside the ocean’s long, slow exhalation.  

TERRAIN  
• Skeleton-mountains: ancient, glacier-planed ridges so old that every hard corner has been rubbed into a whale-back curve. They rise like dark vertebrae in ranks kilometers long, then drop straight into flooded choke-valleys.  
• Flank-ledges: natural terraces where silt has settled for eons, wide enough for forests and for the occasional concrete pad of a forgotten pumping station.  
• Rift-channels: bottomless gutters the width of city streets, worn by meltwater and rain. They act as elevators for cold, plankton-rich seawater that foams steadily upward.  

MATERIALS & COLOR  
Rock: blue-black gneiss striped with pale quartz, slick as glass.  
Water: everywhere—falling, pooling, or hanging as mist. In the deeper cuts it goes midnight green and is clear for forty metres down.  
Soil: a dark, almost oily humus trapped behind natural stone lips; root-tangled, sponge-wet, fragrant with rot and cold flower sap.  

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Temperature hovers just above freezing. Sunlight—when the cloud lid parts—arrives in normal brightness but is instantly diffused by spray, so every beam blooms. At night the mist catches starlight and the cliffs form negative silhouettes, black on luminous silver. Humidity never drops below 95 %.  

SOUND  
The place speaks in three voices at once:  
1. Water: a continuous sub-ocean roar, punctuated by the rifle-cracks of rockfall.  
2. Wind: a higher register, moaning through caverns, fluting over ledge-edges, occasionally screaming in the rifts.  
3. Machine: low turbines, chain clanks, steam whistles—scattered but insistent, echoing for kilometres so they feel more numerous than they are. Conversation is done by hand-signal unless you lean in hard against an ear.  

VEGETATION  
Lush even in the chill. Everything drinks straight from the air.  
• Wavetop firs: brine-tolerant conifers with drooping fronds, standing right in the spray lines.  
• Grip-vines: rubbery, translucent creepers that braid together to net whole cliff faces; locals harvest them for rope.  
• Lily-safes: huge bowl-leaves that trap freshwater pockets inside their own curled edges—perfect watering holes for birds and for human hands.  
Below the rims, drowned forests of cobalt kelp sway in the updraft of current, entire canopies visible through the translucent green.  

FAUNA  
Abundant and loud.  
• Razor gulls wheel on cliff thermals, screaming over territory.  
• Lantern salmon run the vertical rivers, scales flashing like solder sparks.  
• Clamber-bears (half-otter, half-ape in attitude) patrol the ledges, living equally on fish and on the honeyed interiors of lily-safes.  
• Insects breed in such density that a resting boulder can look furred.  

BUILT PRESENCE & TECHNOLOGY  
Human marks are small, scattered, mostly harnessing the endless kinetic gift of falling water.  
• Pocket hydroplants: thumb-thick penstocks shoved into rift walls, feeding generators the size of wine barrels. Their dynamos power signal lanterns, winches, and the occasional hotplate.  
• Rust arches: the corroded ribs of an older, grander infrastructure—rail bridges that once spanned gulfs, intake towers now half-submerged, their red lights still pulsing when the current spins what’s left of the alternators.  
• Lift-cages: dangling on woven grip-vine cables, climbed as often as ridden—the only practical way to move loads up and down the cliff hierarchy.  

DYNAMICS  
Nothing holds still. Every freeze-thaw cycle starts new cracks. Water levels in the choke-valleys rise and fall metres within a single storm. Clouds tumble in, tear apart, reform. A ledge you hiked last week may have collapsed or been overgrown by this one. Locals read the thunder of distant rockfall the way sailors read surf.  

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Gravity is normal, but the air is so water-heavy that small objects dropped from a height sometimes flutter rather than plummet.  
2. Fire is a luxury. You keep it in iron-hooded pits fed by resinous driftwood and pay constant attention to stray spray.  
3. Rust is a universal solvent; anything ferrous must be painted, greased, or resigned to a short life.  
4. Travel is vertical first, horizontal second. Ladders, climber nets, and tension lines map the landscape more than any road.  

STAND AT THE EDGE  
Step onto a natural balcony of bare gneiss. Below, the rift-channel shines like a liquid obsidian scar. Beyond, ridge after ridge recedes into haze, each one fronted by veils of falling water, each veil back-lit so it glows. In the near middle distance a solitary smokestack leans out from a speck of masonry, coughing steam into a sky already saturated. A flock of razor gulls rides the up-draft, white undersides flickering like torn paper against storm cloud.  

Listen—every second is busy with motion, with erosion, with life insisting on itself in conditions that look, at first glance, like the world’s long quiet end. Yet the longer you remain, the more you feel the opposite: an ancient landscape still very much awake, humming, grinding, dripping, building itself anew out of its own slow ruin.
