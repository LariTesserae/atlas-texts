A half-submerged hall stretches before you, the size of a small village green yet roofed like a cavern. No wall or column is natural: every surface shows the patient chisel marks of people who worked with stone, clay, and wicker long before iron was dreamt of. Imagine an earthen cathedral that someone pushed down into a shallow basin and then let the water table rise to its ankles—that is the scale and posture of the place.

TERRAIN AND MATERIAL  
Floor: A patchwork of hand-fitted flagstones, each one slightly cupped in the middle from centuries of footfall and slow dissolution. Where a stone finally failed, its hollow filled with seepage and became a miniature pool. Some stones are replacements—crude, sun-dried bricks stamped with thumbprints and straw impressions.

Walls: Low retaining walls two or three body-lengths high trace an irregular rectangle. They lean, but do not topple—every few decades someone wedges new rush-ropes and wooden butterflies into the joints, arresting the collapse without ever quite correcting it. The upper courses are pierced by narrow slits. Rain blows in; mist exhales out.

Roof: Not a continuous plane but an archipelago of broad, overlapping lids of woven reeds daubed with clay. They sag on timber ribs like wet canvases. Between lids: fissures just wide enough for the grey daylight to seep and the night to drip its stars through. In a downpour the roof becomes a drum, scattering pebbles of water in great rolling waves of sound.

HYDROLOGY  
The whole hall rides a lens of groundwater. Water beads on stone, condenses on beams, and threads down gut-ropes into jars positioned to catch the slow tears of the ceiling. Gutters carved straight into the flagstones ferry overflow toward a single circular sump at the center. The sump is ringed by a knee-high parapet; peer in and you find a dark, echoing shaft only a few arm-spans across yet said to be bottomless. When wind elbows down the shaft it hoots like an animal caught between howl and song—one of the hall’s defining voices.

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
It is permanently chill. Breath hovers in front of mouths and then furls away, joining the milk-thin mist that perpetually hugs the lower half-meter of air. Light never climbs past gloaming; even at noon the radiance that falls through the roof breaks on the drifting vapour and lies in pale shards across the stones. Nights are darker still, though not silent—never silent.

SOUND  
Water patters, glugs, and slaps. The sump-wind keens. Somewhere, reeds braided into roof membranes rub when the rafters shift, issuing a rasp like a two-hand saw. And living throats add their own chorus.

FAUNA  
The hall is nearly empty of plants—only scant ferns claw cracks in the mortar—but life crawls, flaps, and skitters everywhere.

1. Gutter eels: Thin as boot-laces, translucent. They writhe through the floor-channels, feeding on mould and each other. They make a faint wet squeal when forced to turn sharp corners.

2. Clatterbats: Palm-sized insectivores roost behind the wall slits. At dusk they erupt in clouds, wings slapping the stone like thrown cards—thousands at once, creating the evening’s first great roar.

3. Basin crabs: Grey, fist-big. They inhabit the flagstone pools, lifting entire tiles sometimes to search for warmth that leaks from deeper stone. Their coarse claws drum as they tug the masonry—yet the pattern has a rhythm, suggesting communal work rather than chaos.

4. Lantern slugs: The strangest residents. Each carries a cold chemoluminescent pouch along its flank, glowing green enough to ghost the mist. They crawl the parapet of the sump at night, outlining the pit in a moving ring of dim fire.

DYNAMICS & CYCLES  
Every few hours a barometric pulse draws damp air down the shaft and shoves drier air back up. During the out-breath, spores and bat-dust plume into the rafters; during the in-breath, the roof membranes belly inward and tense the supporting ropes. These inhalations keep the entire structure from growing sodden enough to collapse—an accidental lung that preserves the hall.

The sump is said to rise and fall with lunar rhythm; when the water surface is close, clatterbats hunt low and crabs scurry higher. When it sinks, gutter eels slither into the shaft and disappear for days.

RULES & RUMORS  
• No loud singing while leaning over the parapet. Something deep sings back—on a delay equal to twice the time sound would need to reach a cavern a kilometre down.  
• Anyone who repairs a roof-tile must wet it with sump water first. Supposedly this binds the tile to the place.  
• A crab that removes three stones in one night is treated as an omen: residents harvest its shell and carve it into a whistle used to call the wind that cleans the hall.

ATMOSPHERE OF THE PLACE  
Though density of objects is low—wide breaths of empty floor between walls, roof and sump—the sensory field is thick. The hall feels conscious of its own age, resigned yet busy. Everything happens at a scale you can walk from end to end in a minute, but the layered sounds and drifting lights make it feel boundless. It is a made world that long ago escaped its makers and now rewrites itself stone by stone, claw by claw, drip by drip, in an unhurried but relentless choreography.

Stand still long enough and the building will breathe around you; mist will bead on your eyelashes; something small and many-legged will drum a message through the flagstones—inviting you, perhaps, to add one more mark of making to a place that has never stopped being made.
