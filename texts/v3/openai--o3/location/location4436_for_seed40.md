Walk north long enough through the thinning mountain air and the plateau appears underfoot without ceremony—one moment scree, the next a glazed white floor that goes on for days. From horizon to horizon the earth is hidden beneath tessellated slabs of ceramic the size of doors, each stamped with the same undeciphered seal. Someone—or something—laid the tiles recently enough that their edges are still knife-sharp; tilt one and you could shave with it. Whatever raw peak once stood here has been embalmed in workmanship.

Between the tiles run bronze ribs. They tick like cooling engines when dawn sun strikes them, and thin rivulets of condensation slide along their grooves. At nearly three thousand metres the plateau lives inside the clouds: mist is not weather here but furniture, wafting through arcades, pooling in hollows, forever beading into droplets. Water is intermittent, never absent; dry stretches end abruptly in mirrored basins a handspan deep where last night’s fog collected. The basins are rimmed with a thread of turquoise lichen—one of the few living greens you will see.

Look outward and the place feels open, almost empty. Look downward and the feeling flips: density presses from every side. The tiled surface is only the top layer of a hive. Half-height walls, buttresses and switchback ramps rise everywhere, so tightly nested that you can stretch both arms and touch architecture on either side. Multi-storey colonnades hide turbine halls whose flywheels turn so slowly a rotation takes a week. Pipes the width of your wrist form veiny meshes overhead, carrying cold water toward unseen condensers. The whole thing is built, layered, deliberate. No natural substrate shows, not even at the cliff edges where tiles run right to the drop and then form cantilevered balconies to keep the line straight.

Light is relentless. The air is thin, clouds ragged, the sun brilliant. Mirrors—some the size of orchard plots—glint from gantries and direct additional glare into shadowed courtyards as though photosynthesis were being coaxed from stone. Yet vegetation remains sparse: a fringe of slate-blue moss, a stringy violet grass that sprouts only where hot steam vents up through cracked joints, and the pale liverworts that colonise the undersides of rain gutters. Photosynthesis may not be the point; survival here is an opportunistic art.

Fauna, by contrast, is everywhere. Swallows with chromium irises weave among the beams, plucking condensation pearls right out of the air. Long-legged water striders skate across mirror pools as if auditioning for gravity’s dismissal. You may glimpse the plateau’s most common mammal—a soot-coloured mustelid whose fur alternates between solid and translucent in time with its heartbeat—vanishing into gear shaft openings. At night, lamp-eels surface in the reservoirs far below, threading their electroluminescent bodies through overflow grates so that the floor seems lit from inside.

Listen: despite so much life and machinery, quiet rules. The turbines hum at sub-audible frequencies you feel more than hear. Water drips, then is silent for minutes. Wind draws slow vowels through hollow pylons. Footsteps, yours or another’s, echo ahead of themselves, arriving seconds before the sound that produced them—one symptom of the place’s deeper strangeness.

Geometry here is negotiable. Climb a staircase that spirals four storeys up and you may emerge a level below where you began. Walk a straight hallway until the tiling pattern reverses and realise you’ve circled a tower you never actually turned around. Bypass valves open onto corridors that fit nowhere in remembered space. Locals—if such beings exist—might tell you the builders poured not only concrete but intention, and the intention has begun to warp under its own density. The plateau is still young in erosional terms; natural processes have hardly touched it. What weather could, when surfaces forget where they are? Yet the layout is already fraying in time.

Industry sleeps more than it works. Some smokestacks still breathe—exhaling only transparent heat—but most stand dormant, capped with nests of wire-weaving birds that tie lost jewellery into their twig lattice. Conveyor belts carry nothing, yet trudge on, hour by hour, in case something eventually wishes a ride. Every seventh sunrise, the entire grid flickers: lights lurch brighter, then exhaust themselves, and for thirty heartbeats the machinery is truly still. Afterward a new pattern of impossible corridors may exist and an old one may be gone.

Rules of living here, as understood by passing surveyors:

1. Never trust a reflected image; mirrors often show events an hour ahead.  
2. Water pooled in bronze basins is safe. Water running against gravity (you will see it) is not.  
3. When footsteps precede you, wait until the echo and the action realign before proceeding; the misalignment is how travelers vanish.  
4. The translucent mustelids can navigate the geometry without error. If one crosses your path twice in the same direction, follow it; you are walking in circles.

Days unfold slowly. A sheet of cloud will stall against a tower for half a morning, raining on one side, sunshine blazing on the other. Moss extends a frond, retracts it by sunset. Metal cools, warms, cools—centuries to fatigue. The place is patient, adjusting itself a millimetre here, a new paradox there.

And always the silence beneath the brightness, a hush so saturated it feels dense, as if sound itself were another building material waiting to be laid. Walk long enough and you may find the builders—whether machine, animal, or concept—still at work, raising shapes that shouldn’t fit but do, for as long as the laws of the plateau entertain them. Then the mist rolls in, the seams bead with water, and the whole improbable edifice gleams as though it has only just emerged into reality—because, in some slippery sense, it has.
