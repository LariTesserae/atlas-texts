The High Expanse of Lythrane

1. First impression  
The horizon is not a line but a wall, charcoal-red and miles high. You arrive through a slot in the cliff, feeling the air thin and warm, hearing almost nothing but the low rasp of wind on stone. An entire continent has been hoisted into the sky here: a plateau that begins where ordinary mountains would end and rises another eight vertical miles before it even considers leveling out. It is called the Expanse, though its natives joke that it is more height than width. Nothing green meets the eye; nothing wet touches the tongue. Yet something undeniably alive moves in the warm silence.

2. Terrain and materials  
Up close the plateau face is a cathedral of erosion. For thirty million years dry wind has milled the basalt into columns, bridges, hoodoos, grottoes and fluted alcoves. Layers of iron-rich ash alternate with bands of glassy rhyolite; each responds to the wind’s patient chiseling in a different way, so the wall is striped in texture as well as color. Where the cliff top finally levels it does so only momentarily; the surface undulates in cathedral-sized domes of vesicular lava, then drops again into labyrinthine canyons that fork 5000 ft deep. The ground is harsh but settled, the drama long-ago sculpted to a stable, almost ceremonial geometry.

Water is myth here. Minute traces hide as crystallized hydrates inside geodes or cling to microbial films in the shade, but none flows. Instead, capillary rivers of liquid tholins—thick, oily hydrocarbons condensed from the upper atmosphere—seep through micron cracks. They flash-evaporate in direct sunlight, leaving iridescent residue that confuses newcomers into thinking they see pools.

3. Air and light  
The planet’s lower atmosphere is heavy with nitrogen, argon and sulfurous haze, but at this elevation it thins to something Earthlike. Temperature is a steady 34 °C day and night, held constant by the greenhouse murk far below. Sunlight arrives filtered to a warm brass, strong enough for sharp shadows yet never bleaching out the colors of rock. At twilight the haze layer far beneath catches sunset and reflects it upward, so the underside of every overhang glows as if lit by hidden coals.

4. Soundscape  
It is mostly quiet, not because nothing happens, but because everything large learned long ago that noise wastes caloric vapor. Wind passes in sheets—silk, not roar. A pebble’s fall can be heard a mile away. Machinery in the cities murmurs behind layered baffles; even the factories speak in subdued consonants. The loudest events are the seasonal glassfalls, when a cliff-edge arch finally surrenders and ten thousand tons of obsidian descend in slow, breathtaking silence before the final baritone crack.

5. Life without plants  
No blade or leaf interrupts the stone, yet fauna is abundant. Life here learned to eat rock, sunlight, electrostatic charge—anything but photosynthetic sugars.

• Lantern mites: two-inch hexapods whose backs are plates of calcium-fluoride crystal. They farm chemiluminescent fungi in microscopic pits and flash in coordinated swarms across the night canyons, a moving aurora at ground level.

• Vent gliders: membranous serpents that ride the plateau’s constant updrafts. Their bodies store heat as phase-changing salts; at night the salts crystallize and the serpents sink, passively hunting whatever slept on the canyon floor.

• Cudgel beaks: dog-sized, keratin-armored birds that smash open geodes to lick at briny inclusions. Their tongues can taste chloride at parts-per-million; water here is the rarest spice.

• Echo worms: ivory ribbons twenty meters long that drill slowly—so slowly!—through rhyolite, leaving cathedral-quiet tunnels in which other organisms roost. Their pace is a handspan per year, fitting the world’s patient clock.

6. The built layer  
Towns cling to ledges, each perhaps four thousand people, separated by days of aerial rail strung from pylon to pylon across the abyss. Metal is plentiful; coal and water are not, so the culture skipped steam and went straight to magnetics and sealed-cycle motors. Factories run on wind-compressed gas: huge lung-chambers that inhale gusts by day, exhale through turbines by night. From a distance a settlement resembles a bronze moss, spreading low to stay below the wind shear, its roofs ribbed with intake vanes that swivel like ears.

Street level is limestone dust, basalt steps, networks of insulated conduits carrying tholin fuel. The grid hums at 23 Hz—barely within hearing—and citizens say the note keeps away the glass-eyed foxes. Civic architecture multiplies downward more than upward: lift-shafts, storage nets, cool galleries for the precious spoonfuls of water imported by airship from far, wetter hemispheres.

7. Industry’s symbiosis with the wild  
Because everything moves slowly, industry here is largely maintenance: replacing ablated turbine blades, coaxing electrolytic vats to refine copper without squandering water, trading crystal powders for animal byproducts (vent-glider salt sacs are coveted battery media). There is no clear border between ecology and economy; the towns plant scaffolds outside cliff nests so cudgel beaks will roost and drop guano rich in phosphorus, which fungi then mine for the citizens’ bioreactors.

8. Rules of the world  
Gravity feels fractionally light; locals claim it is exactly 0.92 g, though no traveler remembers the decimal. Combustion is poor in the thin, dry air; matches smolder uselessly. Sparks are precious. Corrosion happens electrically, not chemically: stray currents etch iron the way rain would elsewhere, leaving filigreed girders. Time itself seems to erode slowly; political boundaries ossify for centuries, then pivot overnight when a single natural arch collapses and severs an aerial rail.

9. Cycles and change  
Dynamic motions here run on the long dial. A canyon may widen a handspan in a century. A town council meets twice a week but may execute its decisions only after the next equinox, waiting for the predictable still-day when cranes can lift beams across the void. The people do not sleep exactly; they enter “interval,” a four-hour meditative drift synced to the plateau’s faint magnetic oscillations. Likewise the lantern mites flash on a 19-day period tied to the tidal tug of the moon you will never see through the haze below.

10. What you carry away  
When visitors leave, many report a phantom pressure behind the ears, as though the Expanse continues to rise inside them. They complain of ordinary rain sounding obscene, of leaves looking undisciplined. Some return with vials of rock dust that rings when poured, intent on proving the place real. Most fail, for the dust behaves like any other once removed from its warm, high hush.

Yet sometimes, rarely, a vent glider’s salt crystal arrives still molten, and under the correct brass light it ripples, remembering the updraft that lifted it upon a world where water is legend, stone is patient, and silence has breadth enough for whole cities to live inside it.
