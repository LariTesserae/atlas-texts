You reach it well before you see it: the place announces itself as a wall of sound. It fills the air like pressure, a continuous roar in which no single source can be separated from the rest—the wing-beat of insects larger than plates, the hole-whistle of vapour escaping through young stone, the drumming of warm rain that never quite becomes rain, and the throaty bellow of creatures answering one another across miles of shallow water.  Nothing in the choir pauses, nothing crescendos; the volume is simply there, as constant and unarguable as gravity.  The local people are born half-deaf and learn a swift, economical sign-language long before they can walk.

Light reaches the low basin only after passing twice through the sky.  First it must fight through a fat, copper-grey overcast that never parts.  Then it reflects from the pale sheet of water pooled across the valley floor, so that illumination here is indirect, scattering upward in bruised purples and greens and leaving the world in a perpetual, dreamlike dusk.  Shadows are soft, edges smudged.  Warmth, however, is uncomplicated: the air is thick, wet, and summer-warm even at midnight.

The ground underfoot is newborn.  Outcrops of basalt still show the cooling striations of the flows that laid them down; the surfaces are glassy, sharp-edged, unsusceptible yet to moss or lichen.  In many places the rock seems to sweat.  Water condenses on it the way dew forms on a drinking vessel, beads together, then weeps downward in slow, transparent curtains that empty into ankle-deep pans before evaporating upward again.  Erosion has hardly begun its long work.  Blocks break where the stone’s own heat still strains it, but they lie where they fall, corners intact, zero dust at their feet.

Vegetation in any familiar sense is absent.  What by distant sight might be mistaken for reed beds proves, at arm’s length, to be crowds of hollow mineral stalks: calcium tubes risen straight out of solution, brittle as chalk, chiming like flutes when wind from the vents passes through them.  On shadowed underfaces of rock hang pearly fungal fans—if “fungus” is even the correct kingdom for a lifeform whose tissues carry more silica than carbon and whose spores glow a dim seawater blue when the light around them fails.  Aside from those quiet exceptions, growth here belongs to muscle, not leaf.

Fauna is everywhere, packed so densely that empty space feels like an error.  Amphibious colosses ply the shallows: bodies like smooth boulders, six oar-legs, throats ballooning with every seismic call.  Schooling crustaceans climb one another’s backs to strip the condensate-films from rock; when disturbed they topple in an articulated avalanche.  High overhead, translucent kites—creatures of cartilage and gas bladders—spray each other with water vapor siphoned from the cloud ceiling, sustaining both moisture cycle and cacophony.  Predation is open and continuous, but surprisingly bloodless: kill sites are rinsed clean in moments by the omnipresent water sheet, leaving only polished bone and silence no one can distinguish within the din.

Among all this the city squats, primitive and improbably permanent.  Imagine a tessellation of basalt slabs lifted on basalt stilts, every platform nearly touching the next so that from above it resembles scale-mail laid across the floodplain.  Rampways of knotted leather (no workable fibers grow here, so leather serves for rope, for cordage, even for crude textile) link roof to roof.  On each slab families have raised waist-high walls of fitted stone—no mortar, only the newborn rock’s angular precision.  Hearths are rare; fire is stingy with oxygen in the close, damp air, and the people favor heatless lamps made from the luminous spore-fans, renewed nightly.

Technology stops not far beyond blade and bowl.  Adzes of obsidian, bowls of fired mud dredged from the valley’s single sluggish river, nets woven from the shedding plates of the crustaceans.  Yet the infrastructure is thick, layered: cisterns carved to catch the constant drip, gutters that route excess into municipal pools where amphibian fry are fattened for market, causeways whose every stone knows precisely how and where to shift when a seasonal swelling lifts the water by a handspan.  The settlement has the feel of something not so much planned as sedimented, each generation laying down a fresh, tight stratum atop the last.

Rules of the world, as the inhabitants recite them to visitors:

1. Nothing here changes.  (They mean: nothing changes fast enough to matter.  The sky never clears, the sound never stops, the waterline never varies by more than the breadth of a finger.  A person can grow old in the certainty that tomorrow will be acoustically, visually, meteorologically identical.)

2. Everything here is occupied.  (Move a stone, you displace a nest.  Dip a cup, a dozen mouths go thirsty.  The place is full beyond any frontier.)

3. What is full must add without replacing.  (So the city expands outward in successive rings of platforms rather than upward in towers; so the people farm fry and crustacean swarms but never hunt the great amphibians, whose calls they believe keep the sky bound in place.)

In the hush between thunderclaps—if you can find one—you might notice a subtle perfume, sweet as split aloe.  It comes from the mineral reeds when they fracture in the heat, and by morning the scent is gone, absorbed into the permanent wet of the air, just another layer in the valley’s saturation.

Visitors speak of claustrophobia in a landscape objectively wide.  They remember the soft, featureless horizon, the endless dusk, the way the noise beats against the ribs, but mostly they remember density: of bodies, of buildings, of water, of sound.  A place where the world is young yet already crowded, already sure of itself, and perfectly content never to become anything else.
