You enter a hollow that feels at once spacious and miniature, a cup-shaped depression only a few hundred paces across yet roofed by the planet’s own crust. The ceiling is a shell of sun-black basalt, cracked here and there so that ribbons of dull red radiance leak through. That ruddy, half-hearted glow is the only light the place ever sees; it is never truly night and never truly day, only a perpetual dim ember-time.

Terrain and materials  
• Floor: a crust of pale, lumpy salt shot through with veins of bright yellow sulfur. Footsteps crunch as though you were crossing a frozen pond of spice.  
• Rims: low ridges of vitrified sand that caught ancient lightning and melted into glassy pillows long ago; they reflect the red ceiling-light in oily smears.  
• Air: brutally dry, almost dessicating on contact, yet heavy with hot vapors—bromine, chlorine, and a sweet, metallic tang given off by the plants.

Vegetation (lush, but water-less)  
Life here learned to live without H₂O. The dominant organisms are “flare-trees,” columns of porous basalt grown by colonies of silicon-based lichen. They wick liquid sulfur up capillary channels and vent it as luminous gas through star-shaped pores; every few seconds each trunk exhales with a low hoot, contributing to the chamber’s restless roar. Between them sprawls a thick under-canopy of coppery fronds—wide, scale-like leaves that drink the ceiling’s heat directly, folding and unfolding like fans with every gust. Sporadic bursts of mineral pollen drift through the air, sparkling motes that settle on the salt and slowly crystallize into new sprouts. The chamber is green in no sense, yet extravagantly overgrown in reds, ambers and umbers.

Absence of fauna  
Nothing scuttles, flutters, or creeps. No eyes watch. Movement belongs solely to gases, dust, and plants that behave more like slow machines than living flesh. Now and then a frond decouples from its parent and sails upward on a thermal, burning to ash before it strikes the roof—vegetative self-pruning instead of animal death.

Hydro-acoustics and loudness  
Because the basin is shallow and covered, hot convection currents race from vent to vent, ricocheting off basalt walls. The result is a continuous, layered soundscape:  
• The deep, breathy pulse of flare-trees.  
• The mid-range hiss of migrating gases.  
• A brittle clatter whenever shards of salt shear off the ceiling lip and pinball across the floor.  
Silence never lasts more than a heartbeat, which makes the emptiness feel even larger.

The built, primitive city  
Toward the basin’s center rises a low, ring-shaped settlement—abandoned or merely unpeopled, for no builders remain. Its architecture is an exercise in immediacy:  
• Blocks of crystalline halite stacked without mortar, each block as white and translucent as a thumb-sized glacier.  
• Roofs woven from roasted copper fronds, dark, flexible, smelling faintly of mint and ozone.  
• Ladders lashed together from the hollow cores of flare-tree offshoots, light enough to carry, strong enough to last.  
Narrow lanes wander between the structures, gathering at a circular plaza around a broken spindle of obsidian. The spindle is bored through with hundreds of finger-thick channels—flute-pipes that once sang when thermal drafts passed them. Many are clogged by years of drifting dust; some still play, releasing a wavering chord whenever the room’s convection surges.

Technology and craft  
Nothing in the city required forging or compounded tools. Every artifact is cut, snapped, or grown: bowls of natural pumice, knives of cracked glass, cordage made from peeled fiber strips that never knew moisture. Whoever lived here knew how to coax the vegetation to grow into shapes, then harvest it before it petrified. Primitive, yes—but perfectly attuned to the materials at hand.

Dynamics and weather  
Every few hours the chamber experiences a “wind tide.” Hot gas wells up from vents along the perimeter, sweeps over the settlement, and escapes through the roof fissures. In its wake the temperature drops almost imperceptibly, and ultrafine salt falls like warm snow. Over centuries this repeating cycle has eroded sharp edges, smoothed stone, and buried half the outermost huts. Erosion is not ruin here; it is maintenance, sanding away the brittle so the new can find purchase.

Rules of the world  
1. Water is myth. Anything exposed to even a sip would flash to steam and be lost. Life solved the absence by adopting molten sulfur and liquid metal salts as blood.  
2. Sound is carried not just by air but by the salt crust itself; a shout against the ground will travel farther than one given into open space.  
3. Combustion, as you know it, cannot happen; oxygen is a minority gas, leeched out by glare-mosses that crave it. Firelight must come from chemistry or the planet’s interior heat.  
4. Death feeds nothing—no worms, no scavengers. Instead, dead plant matter sublimates, leaving fragile hollow casts which eventually shatter under their own negligible weight. The city likely filled its cemeteries with such ghost-husks.

Atmosphere, final sense  
Stand still and you feel the place breathing: slow, furnace-warm inhalations through the vents; quicker exhalations through the ceiling cracks. The salt underfoot pops faintly, realigning its crystals in the heat. One moment you believe you could map every corner of this pocket world; the next you notice that an entire wall of fronds has rotated, tilting toward a new fissure as though taking counsel from a lamp you cannot see.

There is no story here, only process—a self-contained bowl of gas, glass, salt and restless plants, endlessly loud, impeccably balanced, utterly devoid of animal stir.
