The wind is the first thing you notice—constant, cool, braided with damp, racing over a high shoulder of land that has been kneaded smooth by ages of rain.  It slips through a tight maze of green architecture, rattles reed-woven banners, and dives into echoing corridors that plants themselves have shaped.  Even before you open your eyes the place is noisy, a crowd of living voices layered one atop another: dripping water, wing-beats, throaty croaks, a thousand soft mandibles rasping on leaf, the clack of wooden tools against living trunks, the guttural syllables of the people who dwell here.

TERRAIN AND WATER  
Imagine a saddle-backed plateau no wider than the floor of a cathedral.  Its stone—blue-gray schist—rises only a few dozen metres above the wider highlands, yet the lip of the plateau catches every traveling cloud.  Moisture condenses in seconds, sheets down the rock as glittering veils, and gathers in channels already smoothed to a satin finish by centuries of overflow.  There are no true streams here; instead water seeps out of the stone everywhere at once, beading on every surface, dripping from every overhang, collecting in palm-sized pools before vanishing into mats of root and peat below.  Walk two paces and your boots squelch; walk three and they sink ankle-deep into substrate that is more living tissue than soil.

VEGETATION: THE CITY OF PLANT  
Root, stem, leaf, bark—plant matter is the dominant material, a continuous super-organism that has annealed itself to the rock.  From a distance the settlement looks like a tiered bonsai grove; from within, it is a labyrinth of halls and balconies grown, not built.  Load-bearing columns are enormous hollow reeds, their interiors honeycombed into rooms.  Staircases spiral up living trunks: each step is a tongue of hardwood that springs out when weight is applied, retracts when the tread is gone.  Walls breathe, exuding a faint resin that smells of cedar and rain.  Everything you lean on flexes slightly, as though the city is politely adjusting itself around you.

TEMPERATURE AND LIGHT  
Because the plateau sits so high and drinks so much cloud, daylight is bright but cool, filtered through drifting gauze of mist.  Sunbreaks—brief explosions of white light through ragged cloud—sparkle on wet leaf and blind you for heartbeats.  Nights slip just above freezing; the vegetation counters this by closing pores, thickening the air inside chambers, and glowing faintly amber where sap circulates fastest.

OLD EROSION, YOUNG MOTION  
Nothing here is sharp-edged.  The schist underfoot is worn round, the oldest trunks have grown to fit the rock’s contours like poured wax, and even fallen limbs decay in neat, plush terraces of moss.  Yet in spite of the aged, finished look, the whole site is restless.  Every hour a limb somewhere cracks free, dragged down by the weight of water; within a day creepers have lashed it into place as a new hand-rail or bridge.  Showers burst without warning, hammer the canopy, and are gone in minutes.  Gusts peel layers of fog away like curtains, reveal flocks of jewel-green birds wheeling, then slam the curtains shut again.

SCALE AND DENSITY  
The plateau is a single feature, bounded by cliffs draped in curtain-roots that dangle fifty metres and drink directly from the clouds.  Inside that tight boundary, life is so crowded it feels pressurized: fern tongues pry between cobbles, lichens enamel bare branches, moss climbs trees that are already clad in other mosses.  You could stretch out an arm and touch half a dozen species without stepping from the path.  Sounds have nowhere to escape; they bounce, overlap, amalgamate into a continuous, living roar.

FAUNA: THE CROWD  
If you hold still you will still be moving, because insects will explore you instantly—leaf-hoppers with translucent carapaces, beetles banded like agates, moths whose wings mimic the current weather-stippled leaves.  Small amphibians nest in knotholes that serve simultaneously as rain-spouts.  Tree-goats no heavier than a loaf of bread skitter upside-down beneath branches, lapping condensed moisture.  Predation exists but is quick, almost surgical: a flash of mottled fur, a snap, silence, and the chorus resumes at full volume.  Nothing large hunts here; there is no room for long chases, no line-of-sight for a stalking run.  Life favors the quick, the agile, the explosively brief.

THE PEOPLE AND THEIR TOOLS  
About two hundred humans make their home on the plateau.  They call themselves the Junara, though the word translates roughly to “Tenders of What Already Lives.”  Their technology is simple—flint knives, bone awls, woven fibre ropes—but they wield expertise that feels more like choreography than engineering.  To harvest, a Junara will tease a single stalk from a living wall, twist it three times, and the plant will obligingly release a precisely-portioned length.  Fire is taboo; cooking is done with heated river stones lowered into leaf-pouches of soup, or by letting fermentation do the work over days.  Waste never accumulates; anything discarded is claimed by a species evolved precisely for that chemical niche.

SOUNDSCAPE  
Morning begins with the gutteral pump of glider-frogs, soon overwritten by the percussion of dripping limbs and the sizzle of wind in reed chimes.  Midday is the high tide of insect strings, each species bowing its abdomen against leaves at slightly different frequencies until the whole grove seems to shimmer.  Human activity adds its own register: thuds of wooden mallets, the rasp of cane against cane, children shouting instructions as they race balancing circuits across flexing catwalks.  At night the plateau contracts into itself—stems creak closed, vines cinch tight—and the loudest voice becomes the deep, steady gurgle of water percolating through roots below.

RULES OF THE PLACE  
1. Nothing is ever still; if it looks static, wait a minute.  
2. Anything that falls will be repurposed by dawn.  
3. Fire is exile.  Flames threaten the whole; the whole will respond.  
4. Speak softly when the fog lifts—sound travels farther than sight, and you may startle something that does not miss twice.  
5. Take no more than your shadow’s weight; the plateau remembers.

ATMOSPHERE AND MOOD  
Despite the racket, there is a profound sense of enclosure, like being inside a living lung.  Air inlets and outlets dilate; pressure shifts against your eardrums.  The cool temperature keeps scents crisp: sap, wet mineral, the distant sweetness of fermenting fruit, human sweat, mushroom bloom.  Your clothes never fully dry.  Your skin finds a new equilibrium of damp that is not discomfort but constant reminder—you are inside an organism that does not end at its bark.

STANDING AT AN OVERLOOK  
Step onto a cantilevered bough that has thickened into a viewing stage.  Below, every surface is layered: silver water-film on green leaf on black loam on ancient stone.  A gust shreds the fog; for three heartbeats you glimpse the drop beyond the plateau’s rim, a web of root-bridges swinging, children laughing as they ride the sway.  Then the veil reknits.  You are alone with the damp wind again, heartbeat matched to the pulse of dripping water, wrapped in a thunderous choir that never, ever stops.

Here, high and cool and saturated, life has achieved something near total occupation of space—no vacancy left except the narrow corridors carved by wind and rainfall.  The Junara claim they do not live on the plateau; they live within it, as lungs live within a chest.  Stand here long enough and you may begin to feel the same: a borrowed organ in a larger, older body, humming with the pleasure of its own unstoppable growth.
