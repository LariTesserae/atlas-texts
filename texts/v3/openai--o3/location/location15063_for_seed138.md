You come in along a raised steel walkway. It is only five paces wide and the rail is hot to the touch, warm-blood hot, as if the metal itself has a pulse. Below the grate the landscape—small enough to take in at a glance, large enough to feel like a self-contained world—unfolds like the nave of an iron cathedral.

TERRAIN  
• Elevation: barely above sea level. The whole district is a shallow depression, the floor of an old river mouth that was dredged, boxed in with sheet-piles and then forgotten.  
• Shape: young, freshly cut. The dredgers left walls of black clay and sparkling basalt that stand almost vertical; the edges are sharp, their angles still crisp, unsoftened by time. Here and there a corner has cracked and fallen, leaving a tooth of rock that protrudes like a broken rib.  
• Water: a slick mirror lies ankle-deep across most of the floor. In some places it is only a skin of condensation; in others it gathers into slow brown pools that creep toward grated drains. A constant seepage trickles from the rock faces—“weeping stone,” the locals call it. Every twenty minutes a pump downstream coughs and the level drops half a finger before creeping up again.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
The air is thick, almost syrupy with humidity and heat; at 2.8 on the scale the warmth feels tropical, yet no direct sun reaches the place. The only illumination is a sulfur-yellow dusk that leaks through roof vents high above and the intermittent blue arc of welding lamps farther off. Visibility never quite reaches the far wall; everything is in half-shadow, outlines diluted by steam.

BUILT LAYER  
This bowl is an industrial cloister. The principal structure is the Pump Hall, a ring of brick and riveted iron set against the rock. A forest of smokestacks grows from its roof, but the stacks are short—so the fumes linger, helping keep the place dim. Conveyor belts as narrow as alleyways loop from balcony to balcony, carrying crates, pressurised canisters, lengths of rebar, then disappear into yawning doors.  
Footbridges, catwalks and pipe-runs lace the void overhead. None are elegant; all are functional, rivets big as thumbnails, ladders bolted on at whatever angle the crew found easiest. It gives the impression of a city sketched in rather than planned, yet the traffic patterns never change: the same carts squeal along the same rails at the same minute of every hour, the same pressure valves hiss out the same gout of vapor. The place feels frozen in a single heartbeat of activity.

SOUND  
Loud, constant, layered:  
1. A bass thrum—the master pumps, chugging at a steady 44 cycles per minute, too slow to be a vibration, more a slow heart.  
2. Mid-range clatter—chains on the conveyors, the whump of a boiler releasing pressure, steel drums rolled by unseen hands.  
3. Treble—water’s endless drip, insects skittering on sheet metal, the crackle of arc welders.  
The mix is so steady, so repetitive, that after a few minutes it becomes background music—unchanging, eternal.

VEGETATION  
Sparse at 0.8: only three kinds manage to hold on.  
• Rust-moss: a bottle-green film that colonises any horizontal metal kept perpetually damp.  
• Spindle reeds: sickly yellow stalks no thicker than matchsticks rising from cracks in the concrete, leaning toward whatever lamp is nearest.  
• Brine-ferns: fronds lilac-grey, hugging the weeping stone where mineral salts give them the taste they need.  
They add no real colour; they are incidental life, tolerated, not cultivated.

FAUNA  
Abundant despite the heat and din.  
• Pipe swifts—small bats with birdlike wings—nest inside steam vents, darting out to snap up condensation gnats.  
• Lantern crabs patrol the water film, glassy carapaces catching what little light exists. They click in platoons, scavenging molten beads of solder as if they were pearls.  
• Sump eels, blind and ribbon-thin, wriggle through the drainage channels and surface only when a valve opens—then drop back out of sight.  
• Iron rats, bigger than farm cats, with fur that looks singed even when dry; they know the rhythm of the place and cross conveyors between crates with gymnastic precision.

MATERIALS  
Stone: black clay, basalt, streaked with mica like tiny stars.  
Metal: mostly low-carbon steel, forever filmed with rust that never flakes because the air is too wet.  
Water: brown-green, giving off a faint mineral tang; hot enough that you see it breathe.  
Wood: scarce and therefore treasured—oak sleepers salvaged from an earlier era now serve as benches, door lintels, sacred objects almost.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Thermal equilibrium: nothing here cools below body temperature; liquids, solids, even the air all hover within a few degrees of one another, so condensation is constant.  
2. Temporal stasis: shifts change, but the pattern never alters. If you left for ten years you could return and meet the same eel surfacing at the same valve the instant it opens.  
3. Gravity favors water: every surface tilts imperceptibly toward the central sump. Engineers say it is intentional, but no original drawings survive.  
4. Silence is forbidden by physics: any lull is filled within seconds, as though noise must occupy every gap.

WHAT DAY-TO-DAY LIFE LOOKS LIKE  
Workers—perhaps thirty total—live in bunk pods tucked into alcoves around the ring. Their schedule fits the pumps: six hours on, six off, forever. They maintain the machines, harvest mineral sludge, mend catwalks. Recreation means sitting on a warm iron sill, feet dangling over the shimmer, listening to the layered roar. New generations are apprenticed in; old ones are buried in steel coffins sunk into the clay wall, their resting places marked by a single valve handle.

FEELING  
At first glance: oppressive, clanging, almost hellish.  
After an hour: mesmerizingly orderly, even comforting, like a giant engine room that accepts you as another cog provided you keep to rhythm.  
After a day: time has no edge; you cannot tell if the pumps have run for fifty years or five centuries.

STAND, LISTEN  
A droplet lands on your shoulder, warm as bathwater. A pipe swift whips past your ear and vanishes into a vent. Somewhere out of sight a whistle blows, sharp, exactly on the quarter-hour. The iron heart beats on. The water does not rise or fall more than a hand’s span, the machinery does not age, the landscape does not remember yesterday—because here, in this low, hot, weeping vault, the present moment is a complete and permanent country.
