First comes the light.  
It is so clean, so white-gold, that it seems to carry no color of its own—only whatever it strikes. Up here, three vertical miles above the planet’s median altitude, the atmosphere is thinned to a crystalline veil. Sunlight therefore loses almost nothing on its way down. Surfaces do not merely brighten; they ignite with detail, every flake, grain, and filament throwing knife-edged shadows across the plateau.  

The ground under that radiance is a fused crust of pale-lavender glass. Once it was basalt; millennia of micrometeorite sandblasting and ion storms have heat-welded the upper centimeters into a continuous sheet, crazed with wind cracks. It rings dully when trod upon, the sound swallowed at arm’s length by the rarefied air. Here and there the crust buckles into ribs and bowls—ancient impact ripples fossilized in place—so the land undulates like an ocean frozen mid-swell. Nothing liquid ever moves across it now; the planet’s last free water retreated to polar vaults in a previous epoch.  

Yet the place is green.  Not in patches or hesitant threads, but in rippling, velvet expanses that grip every concavity. The “plants,” if the word still fits, are columns of jointed jade no thicker than a finger and often ten meters tall. Each segment sports a crown of hair-fine fronds—hundreds per stalk—so the columns resemble chimney brushes frozen in a slow wind. They are called lamella forests by off-world surveyors, though no one built or surveys anything here anymore.  

Their biology is purely non-aqueous. In cross-section a stalk reveals no sap, no moisture at all, only a glittering lattice of hollow silica capillaries. Every dawn, ultraviolet photons crack carbon dioxide straight into solid carbon sheets that slide inward, accreting ring by ring like tree growth. By noon the lamellae flare their fronds and harvest airborne oxides of sulfur and iron, knitting them into micro-scale batteries. At dusk the whole stalk flexes, discharging stored power as faint green chemiluminescence; one may walk for hours under those cool trumpets of light without meeting another soul or hearing more than one’s own breathing.  

The plateau is enormous—continental, perhaps a third again the size of Earth’s Australia—and because it stands so high, it creates weather of its own. Thin air wicks heat away quickly, so temperatures remain cool, rarely above eight Celsius even under noon’s glare. Most afternoons the light wind—usually no more than a hush—rises into a slow, deliberate gale. It drags microscopic shards of glass across thousands of kilometers, sculpting everything it touches. Rock buttresses lean away from the prevailing flow; dunes of amethyst dust migrate a centimeter per season; lamella forests comb themselves into the wind, shedding older fronds that sail off like green snowflakes and shatter silently against distant outcrops.  

Erosion here is both artist and archivist. Out beyond the forests, on the broad bare swells, are mesas whose edges have been undercut until they balance as freestanding walls only a meter thick. Shadowless in the zenith light, they appear as doorways into blankness. Step through one and you’ll find no change—only more plateau, more ringing lavendar glass—but the intuition of passage remains, as though some rule of the place demands these thresholds.  

Fauna are rare, nearly mythic. An observer might spend a week and see nothing living but the plants—until, one twilight, a slate-colored kite glides overhead: two meters of membranous wing framing a tubular body. It neither flaps nor calls—the air is too thin for wasted effort—but simply rides the thermal shrug of day meeting night. Lamella pollen drifts upward in green motes; the kite opens a mouth like an iris aperture and sieves them, the only predator the forest knows. On rarer evenings a larger shape—matte white, limbless—passes silently against the stars, exhaling a trail of faint blue sparks: the plateau’s apex hunter, metabolizing stratospheric nitrogen into frigid plasma. No one has ever watched it feed. No one has ever found remains.  

Across all this immensity, silence is not merely absence of noise but the ground note of existence. The plateau is so wide that thunder from distant storm belts dies before arriving; so high that the planet’s magnetic storms shimmer in violet curtains overhead yet never emit more than a hiss too faint for unassisted ears. Sounds are local, brief, intimate: the brittle chime of one’s boot on glass; the papery sigh when a forest’s fronds bend in concert; the almost metallic click as a kite’s wingtip grazes a rock pinnacle. Such noises travel poorly; they belong to their specific square meters and no farther. Step away and each is instantly a private memory.  

Rules of the place:

1. Light is plentiful but warmth is rationed. Organisms invest in optics, not insulation.  
2. Waterless chemistry prevails; anything that depends on liquids is a transient guest at best.  
3. Height begets both isolation and scale—what looks empty up close aggregates into ecosystems measured in tens of millions of cubic kilometers.  
4. Wind is a sculptor with infinite patience; given aeons it will carve even star-forged glass into lace.  
5. Nothing shouts; survival favors the quiet and the light-armed.  

Were a traveler to pitch camp here, they would feel, more than anywhere, the planet’s rotation in the sidereal drift of shadows: noon’s flat radiance becoming dusk’s oblique silver, the lamella forests kindling emerald lanterns, the sky scroll of constellations wheeling unhurriedly above. Come morning, the world begins again—not as repetition, but as continuity, each day another layer in the crystalline skin of a realm whose true currency is time.
