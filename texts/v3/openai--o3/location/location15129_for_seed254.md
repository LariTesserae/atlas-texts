It does not begin with a single picture.  
It begins with a sound.

1. The Approach  
Even before you step through the bolted-iron hatch, you feel the place humming in your chest. The hatch is sunk half a storey below a fog-wet alley; warm condensate beads on its riveted surface and trickles along the floor-seam. When the wheel turns and the door sighs inward you meet a draught of air that smells of cut vines, machine oil, and the first minutes after rain.

2. Volume and Shape  
The vault beyond is no more than a modest nave—thirty, perhaps forty paces on its longest axis. Yet it seems crowded to the point of bursting. The ceiling is a parabolic arch of brick softened by time; lowest at the edges, it rises toward the centre and never clears five metres. Water has found every mortar joint, darkening the clay to bruise-purple. Filaments of calcium frost the drip lines in thin, permanent icicles.

3. Light  
Illumination is begrudging. Strings of pearl-cased bulbs run along iron conduits but only every third bulb glows, and each glow is the colour of old parchment. Light diffuses through vapour and leaf until nothing casts a sharp shadow. Near the floor, glow-boards—flat boxes of frosted glass—pulse with a sullen, industrial orange: the maintenance lights of forgotten machines still doing their duty.

4. Water  
The vault was built as a rain sump for the streets above. In an average hour a finger-thick drizzle falls from bleed valves set into the ceiling arch, pattering on metal catwalks and frond-packed gutters. The water never floods; it is drawn away by slow pumps hidden under the floorplates, their reciprocating pistons a dependable heartbeat. Now and then a louder exhalation rolls through as pressurised steam is vented: a white cloud that swirls, cools, and returns to the endless fall.

5. Vegetation  
No one planned the garden; it recruited itself from wind-borne spores and seeds dropped through storm drains.  
• Floor: a weave of root-thick moss the colour of oiled bronze. Pressure plates beneath sense weight and open drainage grilles. The moss has learned the rhythm, flexing upward just enough to drink before the iron lips part.  
• Columns: four cast-iron supports remain. Each bears a mature strangler-fig that split its way through the flaking paint, encasing the metal in living wood. Aerial roots hang in curtains, drinking directly from the ceiling drips.  
• Upper span: ivy, japonica, and some feral cultivar of bean have run cable-like along the electrical conduits, knitting a green quilt that dulls the hum of transformers. Pale nodules—leaf-thickened equivalents of fruit—bundle around warm junction boxes, siphoning residual heat.  
Every surface is either leaf, bark, or wet stone offered as temporary scaffold for something green.

6. Architecture & Industry  
Remember the parameter: built 2.3, tech 1.8. The vault is not raw cavern; it is infrastructure.  
• Catwalks: narrow, rust-freckled, three levels high. Originally meant for inspectors, they now host shoulder-high hedges trimmed by no hand—simply prevented from further spread by the steel mesh rails.  
• Machinery: centrifugal separators in the south alcove still spin, balanced on gyroscopic mounts. They extract rust particulates from runoff and feed them to a belt that once loaded maintenance carts. That belt broke decades ago; now pulverised iron simply piles in a cone where lichens the colour of drying blood thrive.  
• Control room: a box of wired glass perched half a level up. Inside, analog dials shudder, indicating sump depth, motor temperature, voltage. Auto-pens trace radiating spirographs on waxed paper. No operator has signed the log in years.

7. Atmosphere & Sound  
Three notes weave the vault’s constant music:  
a) The baritone churn of pumps—deep, slow, never hurried.  
b) Mid-range hiss of steam vents cycling every few minutes.  
c) The fickle, higher register of water falling on leaves, metal, water.  
The combined volume makes conversation difficult even at arm’s length; words must be leaned into someone’s ear or written on the damp slate tablets left by technicians long gone.

8. Life (and its near absence)  
Fauna rating is almost zero. In the warm dim there are no insects, no bats, no rodents. High metal concentrations and low oxygen pockets discourage them. Only the plants move, and they move slowly—leaf-faces relaxing and furling in a rhythm attuned to the vault’s artificial night-day cycle (twelve hours powered light, twelve hours near dark when only the glow-boards remain). A visitor who spends a day learns to mark time by the collective sigh of foliage settling at ‘dusk.’

9. Ecology, Closed Loop  
• Input: rainwater, trace nutrients leached from brick and iron.  
• Processing: plants absorb, pumps remove surplus, separators capture metals, plants reabsorb through root mats.  
• Output: almost none. The vault is a patient stomach digesting the city’s runoff into clean vapor that re-ascends through fractures in the ceiling. Aboveground weather sensors occasionally report inexplicable warm, plant-scented mists after storms—this is where they are born.

10. Rules & Rhythms  
• Growth is respected but restrained: when tendrils reach sensor lenses, a servo-arm snips them back with clinical precision. Sparks fly, the cut seals, nothing complains.  
• Nothing moves quickly. Even the steam vents have an unhurried courtesy, announcing release with a low click before they breathe.  
• Sound is never absent. Silence would be a sign of catastrophic failure and is therefore the one thing the system seems built to prevent.

11. Edges & Boundaries  
Every exit from the vault is gated. One corridor ends in a spiral stair leading deeper—locked by a mechanical clock that permits entry only for a single minute each week, presumably for service crews that no longer exist. Another corridor rises toward the surface but is choked by roots so densely braided that the passage might as well be welded shut.

12. What It Feels Like To Linger  
After a quarter hour your clothes are heavy with moisture. After an hour you taste copper on your tongue. After two, the constant roar begins to recede into the background and the slow pulse of the place threads with your own heartbeat. You start walking more deliberately, pausing so as not to miss the rare moment a bulb flares or a fig leaf tears under its own weight.

When you finally leave—spinning the hatch wheel back into place—the alley above seems thin, underwritten by silence. You may find yourself wondering if the city depends on its vault gardens more than anyone realises, and whether somewhere another identical chamber is breathing in the dark, leaf-lunged, pump-murmuring, patient, and perfectly content without a single animal to keep it company.
