First impressions  

Light—hard, white-gold, almost solid—pours down from somewhere overhead and strikes a shallow, pan-flat basin the size of a sports arena. The floor is bleached silica that has weathered into plates and hexagonal tiles; their edges curl upward like the lips of dried paint. No puddles, no stain of moisture: a place where water never belonged.  

Yet the basin is crowded with life.  

Terrain and materials  

The ground is low, only a few gentle undulations before it meets the curved wall that encloses the whole scene. That wall is not rock but fused salt-glass, banded and clouded, as if an ancient brine once flashed to vapor and froze in place. Millennia of silent micrometeor impacts have stippled it with dimples; despite its age it holds a glimmering polish and throws the incoming light back into the interior, doubling the brilliance.

Vegetation (lush, waterless)  

Rising from cracks in the plates are stalks that look at first like stands of bamboo—densely packed, jointed, 3 – 4 m tall. Coming closer you see they are hollow tubes of calcite and copper salts. They drink not liquid water but a constant trickle of ion-rich dust that falls from vents high in the wall. Capillary grooves wick the dust upward; inside each tube microorganisms precipitate sugars directly from mineral ions and sunlight. The result is a top canopy of fibrous banners—green only in the sense that oxidation has given the copper a turquoise blush. Under the glare these banners vibrate. Their edges are serrated like saws, and when the warm up-drafts blow they rasp against one another.

Sound (deafening)  

That rasp is not a whispering rustle but a grinding, metallic roar. Imagine thousands of whetstones dragged across one another, amplified by the concave basin so it swells into continuous thunder. Periodic gusts excite different harmonics; the whole vegetation layer becomes a living pipe organ that never quite repeats a note. Even the ground joins in: thin plates buzz against one another, adding a dry castanet clatter. Silence is unknown here; conversation happens in sign.

Atmosphere and dynamics  

The air is hot, 35–38 °C, and so bright you squint even with eyes closed. Thermal columns rise, strike the curved ceiling, then spill back as glittering falls of dust—an endless convection cell that keeps the chemistry of the plants alive. Once every few minutes a blue flash skitters across the ceiling: ion discharges triggered by the friction-laden air. They never reach the ground but their crackle folds another layer into the cacophony.

Built (urban, primitive)  

In the lee of the thickest stalk clusters stands a city—though a visitor might mistake it for the nests of termites. Each dwelling is a stacked hive of lozenges shaped from the very salt-glass plates underfoot. There is no mortar; slit holes allow the wind to whistle through, keeping the walls cool. Footbridges—woven from stripped banner filaments—span the canopy branches, so travel happens in three vertical tiers: root level, mid-stem, and the blinding roof of the forest. By day the streets look abandoned; inhabitants retreat inside or dive deep into the shade beneath the calcite tubes. At night the light never fades, so commerce and conversation bloom after the midday heat passes.

Technology (simple)  

Tools are stone, bone, and the occasional shard of conductive crystal harvested from the ceiling strikes. Metal is present only as patina on vegetation; smelting is impossible without combustible fuel. Instead the people have mastered cutting, pressure-flaking, and intricate lashing. Trumpet-like speaking tubes of rolled banner fiber channel messages from house to house, their ends stuffed with sponge-lichen to muffle the roar outside.

Fauna (rare)  

Few animals can survive the din. The most noticeable are moth-sized “skimmers”: silicon-bodied gliders that ride the thermal updrafts, their wings little more than translucent sails. They never land; instead they sip mineral fog midair and release microscopic eggs that drift until they find a banner edge to cling to. Predators are rarer still—hand-sized, gecko-shaped things that cling to stalks, mouths fringed with hair to sieve skimmer swarms from the air streams. The city’s children treat a glimpse of one as festival luck.

Scale (hidden, pocket)  

This entire world is small—roughly sixty meters across, thirty high—sealed behind salt-glass walls. No path leads farther; the glass curves inward overhead, and through its dim translucence you see nothing but a deeper darkness speckled with distant sparks. Scholars argue whether they live in a cavern inside a planetary crust, in the body cavity of something larger, or in a laboratory terrarium abandoned by forgotten makers.

Erosion and time  

Despite constant activity, nothing here feels young. Every stalk bears rings where banners grew and fell for centuries. The city foundations have subsided into the cracked plates, tilting doorways, rounding edges. When a banner finally splinters from age it topples, breaks into bright shards, and within a week a dozen sapling tubes sprout from its powdered fall, already scored by the same timeless wind.

Rules of the world  

1. Water is myth; life rides minerals and light.  
2. Sound is inescapable; silence is a superstition associated with death.  
3. Fire is forbidden—there is nothing to burn and too much that would melt.  
4. Each citizen must plant two stalk cuttings for every one they harvest, or risk the canopy thinning and the wind shifting.  
5. Newborns receive crystalline name-shards; when those shards weather to dust, a life is considered spent and celebrated in return to the plates.

Standing here you feel the brightness prick your skin, taste metal on your tongue, and feel the roar press against your eardrums like rising water. There is motion everywhere—dust falls, banners shiver, crystal flashes crackle overhead—even as the basin, unchanged for ages, rests in a balance so precise that removing a single plate might unseat the whole echoing garden.
