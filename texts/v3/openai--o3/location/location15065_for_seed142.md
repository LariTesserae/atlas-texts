From a distance the plateau appears to be a single slab of night suspended in higher night, as if someone had quarried a continent-sized sheet of obsidian, planed it smooth, and left it floating just beneath the stratosphere. Up close, its surface resolves into ancient wave forms—a fossilised ocean of glassy folds and troughs, rounded by so many aeons of wind that nothing on it possesses an edge any longer, only the suggestion that an edge once existed. The air is warm, dry enough to scratch the throat, and heavy with the faint metallic odour of oxidised silicates. No moisture beads on skin, no fog ever condenses. The world’s last hint of water quit this elevation long before the first furnaces were lit.

Across this dim, mineral sea sprawls a city: pale iron lattices, stepped foundries, and hollow towers rooted like stalagmites driven upward through the glass. Someone long ago decided that straight lines were a comfort, so every avenue is ruler-direct, every girder pinned to a right angle, but the earth beneath has shrugged for so long that the perfect geometry sits at odd tilts, giving the streets the melancholy air of blueprints gone to seed. Power comes from pressure engines that inhale the thin upper air and exhale it through turbine throats; night-sky chimneys pulse with slow, red sparks that briefly paint the surrounding architecture in copper ghosts. These moments of artificial lightning are the city’s only reliable illumination. Outside their fleeting halos lies almost perfect darkness—black enough that sound, not sight, becomes the primary map of space. When the pistons pause, the plateau feels endless.

Wind slides over the glass like a hush across a drumhead, carrying aromas of hot stone and distant lubricants. It is never wet, never cold, rarely stronger than a sigh, yet in a place so silent even a sigh acquires presence. Now and then the city’s engines cycle down for maintenance and, for a dozen heartbeats, nothing moves or speaks: a stable silence deep enough to be mistaken for the landscape’s native tongue. Then a belt jolts, a boiler coughs, and the floating hush breaks like brittle sugar.

Life is scarce, though the engineers swear that in the far peripheral sheds tiny chitin plates can be heard ticking against sheet-metal walls. They claim the creatures drink radiant heat rather than water, sequester trace metals into their wings, and breach the surface only once or twice a decade. No one has captured one alive; the machines work too smoothly for pursuit, and the dark erases even nearby shapes. Besides, no one starves here—nutrient pastes are distilled from vaporised minerals in vats the size of cathedrals, a cuisine of grit and chemistry that leaves the tongue forever convinced it is tasting the smell of rain that never falls.

The plateau bears other relics: crooked stone causeways whose original destinations are absent, their foundations worn to translucent fingers; crater-lakes emptied of anything liquid and now floored by vitrified ripples; the skeletons of cranes built in an age when the city believed in vertical ambition, all of them toppled and frozen mid-collapse as though gravity itself had wearied of rearranging the scenery. Everything here is in its final pose—the last frame of a film that can run no farther.

Above, the sky is not simply dark; it is a depth. Even the rare, weak stars are filtered by intervening dust, so they show themselves only as the faintest pinpricks, reminders that distance is a quantity, not a promise. Residents navigate by the city’s intermittent flashes, by memory, by the low percussion of impact hammers far across the works, by the slight temperature gradients where furnaces bleed warmth into the open. When newcomers arrive they often raise their hands, trying instinctively to swat away a blindness that is not on their eyes but in the air itself.

Nothing here corrodes quickly—there is not enough oxygen, not enough water, not enough time that has not already been spent eroding the plateau to its final, glossy monotony—so artefacts remain. A dropped wrench may lie on the glass for centuries, gathering only a thin film of red dust that granulates in engine vibrations and drifts off again. The city is a museum of its own momentum.

Rules here are simple and therefore absolute:

1. The ground never changes except by human hand; one can leave tools on it and find them again generations later.
2. Whatever light you kindle, you must bring with you. The sky will not provide.
3. Sound carries farther than sight; if you wish to meet someone, stand still and listen.
4. Water, being mythic, is spoken of only in metaphor. “A river of conveyor belts,” “a lake of slag,” “a rain of sparks.” The language has adapted itself to what exists.

And yet the citizens keep calendars, mark anniversaries, compose music meant to echo against the glass domes that cap their forges, plant steel pylons in ceremonial grids as though seed would one day sprout from them. Tradition may outlive sunlight.

On the outermost rim of the plateau, where the glass shears off into abyss, is a balustrade of riveted iron. Leaning over, one sees nothing—no bottom, no hint of vertical limit—only the thicker dark of a gorge without name. The engineers say the abyss is warm too, fed by the same subterranean fires that breathe heat into the plateau. No dust drifts up from it; no echo answers when a bolt is dropped. It feels less like a canyon and more like the mouth of the world, poised to whisper a secret so old it forgot itself.

There are nights—if “night” can be said to have meaning here—when the engines fall silent for longer than scheduled. During those intervals, some swear they hear a second city humming below the glass, its machinery deeper, slower, older by millennia. The plateau dwellers are not curious enough to dig; equilibrium is safety, motion a kind of blasphemy. So they listen, imagine shapes in perfect darkness, and carry on forging the only certainty they possess: that tomorrow will look, sound, and taste exactly like today, and that the night, being unbreakable, will cradle them forever.
