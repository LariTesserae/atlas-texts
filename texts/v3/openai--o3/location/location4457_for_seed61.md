The place is the size of a cathedral nave, yet it feels larger because every cubic span is used, populated, or criss-crossed by something. It lies two hundred stair-hewn steps below the daylight world, carved into a sedimentary slab as old as the first fish. Time has rubbed most edges smooth; centuries of seep and trickle have varnished the rock the color of damp iron, so that even the air seems to gleam.

Terrain and materials  
• Floor: A shallow, ankle-deep sheet of silver water braids through low channels. The current is so slow it looks frozen, but touch it and you feel the tug: patient, determined, forever the same. The limestone beneath has been planed flat by ages of silt—no sand remains, only a stony mirror scored with tool marks.  
• Walls: Terraced ledges rise like bookshelves, each ledge enlarged by hand with horn picks, each supporting something—storage pots, sleeping mats, votive stones. Between ledges, vertical slots act as down-spouts; water condenses on the ceiling, collects in hidden gutters, and hisses down these slots in perpetual sheets.  
• Ceiling: Twenty meters up at its peak, lost in haze, it’s ribbed with natural arches that drip steadily. In the highest vaults, skeins of glassy mineral—salt and quartz—hang like chandeliers, diffusing what little light there is into a permanent twilight.

Light  
There is no sun here. Instead:  
1. Coal-oiled clay saucers perched on every other ledge, each wick a blue-orange point no larger than a thumbnail.  
2. A pale, cold luminosity from the quartz veils overhead; they catch and lengthen any spark, so the whole chamber hovers at a dusk brightness, never more, never less.  
3. Once an hour, a shuttle of glow-gnats drifts in from cracks beyond; their phosphor abdomens bloom green for a count of thirty heartbeats, then wink out. Night, day, season—none apply.

Atmosphere  
Cool enough that breath fogs but never quite condenses. The odor is mineral: wet pennies, flint dust, faint sulfur from deeper vents. Humidity presses on the lungs; words come out as low croaks unless shouted.

Soundscape (loud, omnipresent)  
• The dominant note is water falling—a soft roar that fills the cavity yet never varies in pitch.  
• Under it, the clack of stone mallets as residents chip new alcoves. Even their smallest tap ricochets between ledges, multiplying itself into a drumroll.  
• Occasionally, the creak of rope winches mounted to ceiling hooks as baskets shuttle supplies from floor to mid-levels.  
Silence would be frightening here because it never arrives.

Built environment & technology  
This is a pocket city of perhaps eighty souls. Tools are antler, horn, and the rare meteoric iron nail traded from surface wanderers. Architecture is purely additive: every generation enlarges an existing shelf rather than dig farther out—growth by lamination. Ladders are split-timber, lashed with gut; bridges span three, four meters, planked with shale slabs. No wheel, no forge—heat is scarce because fire eats the oxygen, so metalworking stays impossible. All craft is cold-hammer, chip-flake, weave-knot.

Water management is their genius: reed-woven sluice gates (hauled from surface marshes) let them divert the ankle stream into cistern-pits for drinking, letting silt settle. Used water rejoins the main flow downstream through bone-plugged valves. Because nothing changes, the system never needs redesign, only patient maintenance.

Ecology  
Vegetation: None. Surfaces that would grow moss aboveground sprout thin sheets of crystalline salt instead, a habitat for microscopic brine films but nothing green.  
Fauna: Rare but memorable.  
1. One species of blind isopod, palm-long, lives in the runoff pools and is trapped for protein.  
2. A great moth the width of a bowl appears once a decade, banging the quartz ribs until it shreds itself into luminous dust. Its origin is legend; its dust is mixed with oil to extend lamplight.  
3. The dwellers themselves—short, barrel-lunged, skin bleached the gray of unlit wax—move little, speak less, and live thirty years, which here counts as full and satisfied.

Rules of the world  
• Nothing grows; everything accrues. Salt fur, mineral draperies, layers of habitation—they all thicken but never metamorphose.  
• Sound outranks sight: social status depends on one’s ability to navigate echoes, to predict when a shouted warning will return and from which wall.  
• The stream’s level is constant; elders claim it has neither risen nor fallen in memory. If it did, the city would have no answer. Stability is fate, not achievement.  
• Death customs require the body be set adrift downstream. No one knows where the water ultimately goes—only that no corpse ever returns.

Weirdness—fleeting hints  
Sometimes the quartz vault hums at a frequency too low to hear yet strong enough to ripple the water surface in concentric rings. No cause. No effect. The people pause, wait it out, resume. The city keeps its equilibrium.

Final image  
Stand on the lowest shelf, knee-deep in cool mirror water, lamp in hand. Above you, fifty stacked terraces vanish into violet haze, each lined with pots whose oil flames waver in the upward draft of falling water. A thousand droplets strike stone like distant applause. Nothing moves quickly and nothing, it seems, has ever moved differently. This is a world pocketed away from weather, season, and sun—a subterranean marsh-edge metropolis of salt glass, patient tools, and perpetual roar, balanced forever on the quiet insistence of a stream that never quite decides to flow.
