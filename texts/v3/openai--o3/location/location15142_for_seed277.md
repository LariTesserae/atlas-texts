High Plateau of the Night-Warm

1. First impressions  
You crest the last rise and find yourself on a continent-sized tableland, a broken sheet of dark basalt that stretches out of sight in every direction.  The air is warm—tropical, almost—but the world is nearly lightless.  A permanent soot bank, born of distant volcanoes and spread by upper-level winds, smothers the sky and turns noon into a bruise-colored dusk.  Only toward the horizon does the overcast glow faintly, where storm-light flickers inside the clouds like distant furnace doors opening and closing.

2. Terrain  
• Elevation:  Two kilometers up, give or take, but the plateau is so broad (three thousand kilometres across at its narrowest) that the eye reads it as an endless plain.  
• Rock:  Columnar basalt, its hexagonal joints rounded by aeons of warm rain and freeze-thaw.  Between the columns lie broad, shallow pans scoured by wind. The largest of these pans hold what passes for water here: ankle-deep mirror lakes fed not by streams but by nightly condensation.  
• Erosion:  Nothing is sharp.  Edges have been licked smooth, not worn away; gullies snake lazily, their walls scalloped and fluted like melted candle wax.  It is a landscape that has had time to forget its own making.

3. Water and weather  
Rain is rare but dramatic: when the soot-ceiling over-saturates, it cracks open in vertical curtains.  Pools collect, then vanish into cracks or the thirsty air within a few days.  More dependable is the daily “fall”—a silent precipitation of fog-dew after midnight.  It leaves beads on every surface, enough to keep moss alive and creatures hydrated, but never enough to green the land.

4. Vegetation (sparse, 1.4/3)  
• Yarrow-succulents: knee-high spires storing a month’s water under thick, waxy skin.  
• Iron-moss: a charcoal-black bryophyte that only colonizes the wind-scoured north faces of stones, giving them an almost metallic sheen.  
• Thread-vines: rootless filaments that drift on breeze, tangle on rock, absorb moisture directly from fog.

No plant stands close to another; each claims its own faint halo of resources.

5. Light (0.2/3)  
Starlight is useless under the soot.  Vision belongs to:  
• Bioluminescent gnats—minute green flashes that drift like embers.  
• Occasional sheet lightning in the overcast, bright enough to cast moving shadows—brief, cinematic moments where all the world is visible at once.  
For long stretches, though, the plateau exists in a monochrome of blues and charcoal greys.

6. Sound (0.9/3)  
Wind over flat stone whispers more than it howls.  You can hear individual droplets patter into pans, hear the soft clack of distant carapaces, the flutter of an unseen wing.  Long pauses stretch between these notes until even your heartbeat feels impolite.

7. Fauna (abundant, 2.1/3)  
Life here solves scarcity with mobility or metabolism.

a. Stokers  
Beetle-sized grazers whose gut flora ferment iron-moss into ethanol; at dusk they vent the excess as warm vapor, leaving tiny ghost-clouds that drift a meter before fading.

b. Boilbirds  
Oily-skimmed, long-winged flyers.  They roost in cracks during the fog-fall, absorbing water through their feet.  At dawn they pump that water through a vascular heat-exchanger in their breast, generating lift by flash-boiling it—wings hiss faintly with escaping steam.

c. Prowlbacks  
Cat-long quadrupeds armored in interlocking keratin plates the color of the rock.  Muscles and plates ratchet against one another with a sound like a slow, distant piston.  They hunt by heat scent, tracking the ethanol fires inside Stokers.

d. Rifold  
A serpent the diameter of your arm that spends most of its life inside basalt tubes, rasping out mineral shavings to ingest trace water.  It emerges only during heavy rains; every five, ten, fifty kilometres, a rifold head might rise from a drain crack like a periscope, taste the air, and retreat again.

8. “Industrial” without industry (tech 2.3/3, built 0.3/3)  
Nothing here wears a rivet or bears a nameplate, yet the whole ecology runs on principles that feel engineered.

• Thermo-biotics: Many animals carry miniature heat engines—chemistries that use ambient warmth to drive pistoning cartilage or magnetic sensory vanes.  
• Capillary towers: Certain succulents grow crystalline exosheaths of manganese dioxide, turning themselves into natural batteries that small creatures tap with chitin needles.  
• Vent gardens: Fault-line cracks exhale geothermal steam.  Over time, fallen iron-moss mats sinter into porous filters, directing steam through labyrinths; temperature gradients across these mats power living Stirling engines inside microbes, whose whirring protein rotors can be heard if you press an ear to the stone.

Thus the land feels “industrial” not because people built machines, but because life here discovered the same tricks—pressure differentials, redox gradients, reciprocating motion—and adopted them wholesale.

9. Dynamics (1.7/3)  
Every twenty-seven hours a pressure wave rolls across the plateau: soot-choked warm air rises, cooler edge air rushes inward, and the whole tableland breathes.  Clouds convulse, fog condenses, lakes appear, predators wake, then the system exhales back toward stillness.  Even on the quietest days you sense motion gathering, processes winding invisible springs.

10. Rules and oddities (weirdness 1.0/3)  
• Magnetic north is unreliable; basalt here is shot through with alternating polarity bands, so compasses spin.  Migrating Boilbirds instead follow infrared plumes from chain-lightning.  
• Night-warm organisms tolerate temperature swings of thirty degrees without metabolic slowdown; researchers speculate a protein sheath acting like antifreeze in reverse.  
• No fossils older than 400,000 years have been found here, though surrounding lowlands brim with ancient bones—as if the plateau periodically clears itself, wipes the slate, begins again.

11. Standing alone  
Space dominates.  Between any two visible living things lie football-field lengths of bare rock.  You could shout and hear your echo return unbroken; you could light a flare and watch nothing flicker in response for minutes.  Yet you are never truly alone—every surface crawls with micro-mechanical life, every fog bank hides wings, every pool reflects the hot eyes of something waiting.

12. Departure  
When you finally descend the escarpment the temperature drops, sunlight reappears, and ordinary green life crowds close again.  Looking back, the plateau shows no glint of civilization—only a dark, level horizon under a lid of smoke.  But you carry away the sense of an ecosystem that reverse-engineered the engine itself, a realm where biology and industry are synonyms and darkness is merely another resource to be processed.
