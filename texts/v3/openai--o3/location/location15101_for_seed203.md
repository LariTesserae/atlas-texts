The descent begins through a slit in the bedrock no wider than a creek. Twenty minutes of rope work later the fissure opens, suddenly, into an absence so large the mind slides off it. You are in the Green Hollow—though “green” is only the first impression of a light that roars across the stone like sunrise, bright enough to cast hard shadows yet sourced from no sun. The radiance comes from the plants themselves: sheets of pale-leafed thallus cling to every wall, trunk, and ceiling, seething with cold bioluminescence. They swallow the light that trickles down the entrance shaft during the three short summer weeks, store it chemically, then pour it back out for the remaining cycle. Down here, noon is a property of the flora.

1. Terrain and scale  
The chamber is continental in breadth, a single cathedral stretching for hundreds of kilometres beneath an ice-weighted crust. Its floor is raw, unsoftened—new stone still wearing the vertical scratches of the upheaval that created the void. Razor-edged ridges jut like saw teeth, separated by black gullies. Erosion has not yet had time to gentle anything; clambering across a single kilometre feels like navigating the vertebrae of a sleeping titan.

2. Moisture and temperature  
Air holds a constant chill just above freezing. Moisture breathes out of cracks, condenses on overhanging fronds, and falls in slow, silver drizzle. There are no rivers, only mirrored pools trapped in splintered basins; one step sends rings skidding across water so still it looks frozen. Yet the pools never quite ice over—heat seeps up from deep fault lines, enough to keep the liquid mobile, enough to feed the perpetual mist that threads the vault like low clouds.

3. Vegetation as world architecture  
Plants are everything here: ground, roof, scaffold, weather. Cable-roots as thick as subway trains drop two kilometres from the ceiling, touch stone, and instantly spread into buttress forests that lock whole sections of cliff in a living mesh. Membrane-leaves tens of metres wide stretch between stalagmite and stalactite, forming translucent terraces that flex when you walk on them, elastic and trustable like canvas. When one sheet dies or tears, others unfurl overnight to patch the breach; the habitat is self-maintaining, almost architectural in intent.

4. Atmosphere and sound  
Despite the turbulent growth cycles, the Hollow is mostly quiet. The loudest thing is the drip—fat, deliberate, arrhythmic. Occasionally a root-quake rips through: the plants, expanding, shove entire pillars of basalt a metre sideways with a report like artillery, and then silence closes in again. Your footsteps echo for a kilometre before the vastness swallows them.

5. Dynamics—cycles of creation and collapse  
Nothing stays static. Whole curtains of vegetation bright as winter auroras slough off when nutrient veins run dry, fluttering downward like sheets of burning newspaper, flaring brighter as they die. Within days, new growth has bridged the gap, colonising the fallen mat, digesting it, raising fresh stems. The landscape rebuilds itself in waves, a rolling front of birth and compost.

6. Fauna—rare, cautious, necessary  
Animals are few: pale spindle-insects that skate the pools, fist-sized gliders that leap from membrane to membrane, and, rarely, something larger—a six-legged browse-beast the colour of candle ash, grazing on new shoots. Every creature is quiet, low-metabolism, adapted to a diet of near-ice. They leave almost no sign except the occasional paired dents of tracks, already overgrown the next hour.

7. Primitive marks of builders  
Far out on one of the buttress plains stands a scatter of objects that are not quite plant: a fence woven from stripped root; a wind-drum made of membranous leaf stretched over rib-bone; a shallow hearth where slow fires smoulder on beds of dried sapwood. Whoever made them works in stone and fibre at best: chipped-flake knives, hammered nodules of meteoric iron used until their edges bloom away. You may never meet the makers—nobody lingers in one place long before the substrate decides to rearrange itself—but the artifacts confirm intelligent presence.

8. Rules of the Hollow  
• Light is plant-owned and may vanish if the thallus sleeps.  
• Growth pressure can change topography overnight. Camp on naked rock only.  
• Water collects in hollows but never accumulates above knee-depth; the chamber drinks its own rainfall.  
• Fire is possible but dangerous: burning vegetation triggers chemical brightening that attracts the few predators.  
• Sound travels far, but the chamber’s scale distorts direction; echo-location is unreliable.

9. Mood—the feel of the place  
Imagine standing on a gentle ridge made entirely of living leaf-lattice, under a ceiling so high it smokes with mist, while pale light bleeds from every surface. The air tastes faintly of menthol and mineral. Somewhere in the distance, a wall of foliage collapses with a hush like surf, and particles of luminous dust rise, swirl, and drift away on convective currents you cannot feel. It is at once a vast emptiness and an overwhelming intimacy: nothing here is inert, yet between those living structures yawns more emptiness than matter.

10. Departure  
Climbing out requires timing. Plant cables that were anchored yesterday may have shifted by dawn. Still, you go, rope scraping against cold bark, the Hollow’s glow shrinking beneath like a second sky. At the lip of the fissure you pause, listening; all you hear is drip and distant creak, a restless garden the size of a continent turning under its own slow storms. Then the surface wind hits—warmer than the breath below—and daylight seems suddenly thin after so much subterranean brightness.
