The place is scarcely larger than a cathedral nave—fifty   paces from end to end, half as wide, fifteen high—yet the sensation on entering is of stepping into something vaster than weather.  

1. Terrain and structure  
Everything you can touch is built. The floor is a grill of black iron, the walls layered shells of riveted plates, the ceiling a vaulted tangle of ducts and flues. You stand near the summit of a mountain-sized condenser, but the stone core is long entombed beneath successive generations of fabrication; not a finger-width of native rock remains exposed. What passes for “ground” is a mesh walkway sagging here and bulging there as though the metal were still cooling. Beneath the grate is a crawl of half-finished trusses, lumps of molten slag arrested mid-drip, and mirrored catch-basins filling eternally with run-off. The whole chamber feels embryonic—girders poke out like half-grown ribs—yet every bead of solder has set to diamond rigidity. It is an unfinished thing that will never again change.

2. Water and atmosphere  
The air is wet the way a lung is wet. High-altitude vapor is pulled in through louvered crowns overhead, shocked against cryo-panels, and precipitates out as a fine, freezing rain. Droplets condense on every surface, leap from overhangs in silver strings, hammer the basins below, then are sucked away through gutter-pipes the diameter of wrists. Mist hugs the floor at shin height, weaving through the grill like cautious smoke. Nothing smells of soil or algae; it smells of distilled water, electrolytic ozone, and cold sheet metal.

3. Temperature and light  
Despite the constant motion of steam and water, the thermometer hovers just above the freezing mark. Cold is not a whim of weather here but a requirement, maintained by silent coolant lines that web the walls like blue-veined glass. Illumination never rises beyond gloaming. A handful of sodium bulbs throb from cage fixtures, their light strobing as if trapped inside water. The rest is provided by status diodes—green, amber, rarely red—set into every panel, so that the chamber glows the way deep-sea creatures glow: faint, scattered, sufficient only for their own purposes.

4. Sound  
Silence does not exist. Two territories of sound overlap and do not vary:  
• The machinery: a throbbing 70-hertz heartbeat from distant turbines, overlaid with the hiss of condensers, the bass rumble of pumps, and a glassy ping each time super-cooled runoff meets warm iron.  
• The fauna: a blanket of high-pitched, overlapping calls—clicks, chirrs, stridulations—like one colossal cicada that forgot to breathe in. Because nothing cycles, the pattern never resolves into rhythm; it is a single, deafening now.

5. Fauna (overwhelming, strange, dense)  
They are not quite animals, not quite machines. Imagine fist-sized arthropods molded from translucent porcelain, jointed on brass hinges, their bellies glowing weakly with bioluminescent coolant. Thousands nest in the crannies of the half-finished scaffolding. They feed on trace lubricant films and the minute galvanic currents that skitter across fresh welds. Movement is perpetual yet strangely choreographed: each unit paces a hexagonal patrol route six body-lengths wide, antennae sweeping, mandibles tasting condensate. There is no leader, no evident goal. Once a week—though “week” is only inferred from the scarring on old shells—one will molt, leaving behind a lacework ghost that never decays. Pile enough of those husks together and they dampen footsteps like leaves in an autumn gutter.

Contact is harmless but intrusive: stand still and a dozen will climb your boots, probing for micro-voltage; brush them off and they continue elsewhere, unoffended, like rain rolling down glass.

6. Vegetation  
The only greenery is accidental. Where coolant lines sweat and the porcelain arthropods cannot reach, a pale moss the color of oxidized copper flowers in crusts no thicker than vellum. Its purpose seems archivist rather than ecological—it records each droplet’s passage in faint mineral rings, then goes dormant, never spreading far.

7. Rules of the world  
• Everything is manufactured, but the act of manufacturing has stopped at the moment just before completion.  
• Water cycles through but never leaves. It condenses, falls, is recaptured, chilled, and released again—an eternal piston of phase change.  
• Temperature, lighting, sound, even the arthropods’ patrol loops are locked in equilibrium. The system’s clock speed is zero; entropy is permitted only to the extent it can be immediately reversed.  
• Direction is mostly downward; above the ceiling are more ceilings. Eventually, below the lowest grate, pumps drop the runoff into deeper chambers rumored to be identical, like reflection beneath still water.

8. Standing here  
You taste metal on the tongue. Your breath comes out as a plume that lingers, edgeless, in the common mist. You must shout to hear your own voice, and even then the syllables feel temporary, peeled away by the unwavering din. Touch a wall panel and warmth spreads under your palm—the only warmth here—yet no process has changed, no gauge twitches. The chamber is a heart that has forgotten both birth and death, beating because nothing has told it to stop.

If you leave, every bead of condensation, every porcelain cicada, every sodium bulb will occupy the exact same coordinates an hour, a year, a century later. The place is not immortal; it is simply finished with time.
