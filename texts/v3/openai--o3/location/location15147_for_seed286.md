THE HIGH DROWNED TERRACE

1. First orientation  
You arrive already underwater.  The pressure on your suit gauges says “two hundred metres,” yet your position-fix pings report an altitude of five kilometres above planetary mean.  The terrace is the crown of an ancient, forgotten plateau, long since swallowed by a global ocean whose surface is still far, far overhead.  You are suspended in slow, cool, midday-bright water over a continent that never sees the sky.

2. Terrain – a living mesa  
What you took for seafloor turns out to be plant-stone: a single continental sheet of fused lignin, cellulose and shell-like calcium laid down by generations of gigantic reef-trees.  The sheet is flat, mildly undulant, and kilometres thick – strong enough to carry its own weather.  Rises and hollows the size of fjords break the tableland, but nothing here was ever “built”; growth alone shaped every buttress and overhang.

3. The vegetation that is everything  
• Trunk-ridges: dark-green cylinders thirty metres wide, braided like cables, run arrow-straight for dozens of kilometres before disappearing into the plate.  They are the vascular highways of the terrace, pumping nutrients with peristaltic waves you can see through translucent bark.  
• Sheet-leaves: broad as city blocks, thin as book pages, layer the open spaces in slow drifts, catching the modest downslope light.  Their surfaces are iridescent with iron-binding biofilms.  
• Factory-organs: massive, humming bulges the locals call “boilers.”  Inside, sugars ferment, releasing gases that keep the upper half-kilometre of terrace neutrally buoyant.  They rumble like slow steam engines.

In the terrace language, there is no distinction between “plant” and “ground.”  The ground photosynthesises; light is soil.

4. Water, light and colour  
Though deeply submerged, the high terrace sits in a lens of unusually clear water.  Sunlight, refracted through thousands of metres, arrives sifted but undimmed in hue, so midday is the pale turquoise of glacier melt and “night” is merely a gentler blue.  The water is a steady 8 °C – cool enough that nothing decays fast, cool enough to keep the gases locked in solution.  Suspended particles are few; visibility can reach a kilometre.

5. Sound – the permanent roar  
Silence is unknown here.  
• The trunk-ridges pulse: thub-THUMP, thub-THUMP.  
• The boilers vent: sha-hoooh, like surf inside a cathedral.  
• Farther off, glacier-sized leaf-sheets beat together: WRAWMMM…WRAWMMM… clock-regular, a heartbeat you feel in your sternum.  
The combined result is a low, metallic thunder that never ceases, never rises, never falls.  After a day you stop expecting change.

6. Fauna – abundant but dispersed  
Creatures treat the terrace as the rest of us treat a continent: too large to grasp, comfortable to inhabit.  Density is sparse—kilometres of emptiness between meetings—but life is unquestionably abundant.  
• Glass eels, three metres long, drift in slow figure-eights, mouths agape, straining bacterial clouds invisible to you.  
• Vault-whales, grey and leaf-thin, cruise just above the boilers, letting the up-drafts carry them like paper kites.  Every fifteen minutes they clack their cartilaginous jaws: a territorial gong.  
• Anklet crabs – copper-green rings of jointed bone – march endless pilgrim circuits around the trunk-ridges, harvesting shed bark and nada else.  They are the loudest individual animals: clicking like pickaxes in a mine.  
Predation exists but feels choreographed, part of the terrace’s unchanging equilibrium.  Nothing here is young, nothing truly dies; carcasses lodge in root-pockets where moss-kelp reworks them into new terrace.

7. Industrial without industry  
The techno-reading that confused your probes comes from the plants themselves.  The boilers run at fifty degrees, regulated by countercurrent tubes that recall blast-furnace jackets.  Some ridges plate their inner walls with magnetite and, in the slow slosh of currents, generate steady electrical fields: enough to light the halo of photoplankton that forever rings each trunk.  No hands forged these engines; evolution did, but the effect is unmistakably industrial.  Follow a ridge for long enough and you will pass through bio-made gantries, conduits, pressure-locks, all humming, all immaculate, all older than cities.

8. Erosion, or the lack of it  
Currents have scoured away every loose piece that ever grew, leaving only the terrace’s mature skeleton.  Its surfaces are rounded, polished, perfect; its edges balance the weight of ocean above with the gases within.  There is no sediment here, no rubble, just clean, living architecture in a state of permanent sufficiency.

9. Time and rules  
• Nothing here migrates seasonally; seasons do not reach this depth.  
• Growth and decay proceed, but at equal rates.  Biologists call it the Zero-Flux Eden.  
• Storms may rage up at the sunlit surface, continents may shift in the planet’s mantle, but pressure, sound, light and temperature on the high drowned terrace have not deviated in recorded centuries.  
If you visit again in a decade, your footprints in the soft bark will still be sharp, your exhaled bubbles still caught in the moss-kelp you brushed.

10. Departure notes  
Remember: there are no landmarks in equilibrium, only rhythms.  Choose one—say the thub-THUMP of the nearest ridge—and follow it home.  The terrace will not change; only you will, carrying its constancy out into the restless world above.
