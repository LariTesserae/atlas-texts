The Plateau of Quiet Furnaces

Terrain and elevation
Imagine approaching an uplifted shelf of the planet—an almost-continental mesa two kilometres above the surrounding crust. The escarpment’s rim is a fresh, ragged fracture that runs for hundreds of kilometres, its faces still sharp, uncrumbled, the bluish basalt showing glassy conchoidal shears. Little talus has had time to gather; whole walls stand as if snapped only yesterday. Up on the crown the ground undulates gently, like frozen swells on an ocean, but every profile remains crisp. No gullies, no meandering channels, no dust beds—erosion has barely begun its long work.

Atmosphere, light and sound
The air is thin but breathable, warm in the daylight (about 28 °C) and almost perfectly still. Sunlight comes unfiltered: a steady white that casts accurate, razor-edged shadows. There is no scent of water, only a faint metallic tang that stings the tongue. Sound scarcely travels; with neither wind nor animals, the plateau lies in a hush so deep that a sliding pebble seems theatrical. Sometimes—very rarely—a sublimating pocket in the bedrock releases a dry exhalation, a sigh like distant steam, and the hush folds closed again.

A land without water, yet lush
From a distance the plain looks bare, but step onto it and “forest” appears: pillars of vegetation spaced scores of metres apart, each rising ten to twenty metres, each shaped like a narrow cooling tower. Their skins are matte oxblood and flecked with micaceous green. These are lithophyllic furnaces—plants in the loosest sense. They photosynthesise with magnesium chlorides but, lacking liquid water, pump scavenged carbonyl vapours through internal reaction chambers. What a true tree does with sap, these organisms do with warm gases and powdered stone.

Approach one and its surface is warm—always exactly 47 °C. Minute pores around its base inhale the mineral-rich air that drifts just above the ground. Inside, baffles distribute the air across catalytic membranes; iron and nickel are plated into lattices the organism uses for slow, continuous growth. Every hour or so a vertical slit dilates, and an almost inaudible puff issues out, dust-fine flakes of excess chromium oxides. The plateau’s thin haze—the only “weather” here—is made of those motes glinting in sunlight, falling again in complete stillness.

Industrial echoes without industry
Nothing on this mesa was built, yet everything feels engineered. Look long enough and you will spot repeating geometries: regular ribs along a pillar’s flanks; heat-dissipation fins grown at precise angles; root fans that resemble radiators sunk shallowly under the crust. The whole ecosystem is a self-assembling, self-powered refinery, evolved instead of manufactured. It lacks any higher order planning, but to human eyes it borrows the language of smokestacks, vents, condenser coils—an entire industrial age fossilised into biology.

Materials underfoot
Soil, in the ordinary sense, is absent. The first centimetres of ground are a sintered sheet of basaltic glass, crazed by hexagonal fractures. In each joint, the earliest stages of erosion have only just produced a millimetre of dark granules. Wherever the vegetation’s dust exhaust has been falling longest, the crust hosts a glitter of chromium snow that crunches faintly underfoot and leaves tarnish streaks on your boots.

Ecology without animals
No insects explore the stems, no birds roost atop the towers, no burrowers disturb the crust. Energy flows vertically: sun to tower, tower to bedrock. Nothing moves horizontally except cast-off minerals. Decay is unknown here; when a tower exceeds its thermal tolerance—once every few centuries—it shuts its intake pores, collapses inward, vitrifies in its own heat and stands eternally as a hollow glass stack. They dot the plateau like silent chimneys, never reclaimed by scavengers because there are none.

Weird but stable
The environment is in near-perfect equilibrium. Temperature swings are minor, chemistry steady, population static. Each tower maintains precisely the metabolic rate that the sunlight allows, no more, no less. Geological uplift has paused; erosion has barely started; weather patterns bypass the high mesa altogether. If you returned in ten thousand years, you would find the scene almost unchanged—perhaps a few additional chimneys, a few millimetres more dust, but the same hush and the same slow exhalations of mineral breath.

Rules of the place
1. Water never appears in any phase; hydrogen compounds were boiled away in the planet’s formative eon. Life here cannot involve it.
2. Life harvests metals, not organics; carbon is present only as trace gas.
3. Energy flows are strictly vertical; without animals, nothing disturbs lateral continuity.

Standing alone on the Plateau of Quiet Furnaces, you feel inside an abandoned factory whose machines have not broken—they simply never needed operators. The bright sun, the perfect silence, the towering vents that are somehow alive: all give the impression that you have not arrived too late, but rather too early, catching a world at the beginning of its endless, patient work.
