First impressions  

You swim—or are conveyed—through a steel throat only a dozen metres long and suddenly arrive inside what the builders once called the Pump-Chapel. The room is no larger than the nave of a modest cathedral: forty metres end to end, twenty metres across, with a vaulted roof that curves like the belly of an overturned ship. There is no natural rock anywhere. Every surface is forged, cast or riveted: damp iron ribs, plates of pitted brass, viewing panels of thick green glass gone foggy with age. The entire chamber lies under more than a kilometre of ocean, yet itself is flooded; there is no contained air here, only cool, high-pressure seawater that moves in slow sheets from valve to valve.

Terrain and structure  

Floor, walls, and ceiling blur together, rounded by twenty centuries of soft abrasion. Where edges once met at right angles, they are now scalloped, almost organic; touch a corner and your glove slides along a continuous curve polished by endlessly circulating water. Ranks of gigantic pistons descend from the ceiling like sunken organ pipes, their once-sharp fins rendered smooth as candle wax. A central aisle of grating—loose in places, missing in others—descends a half-metre into a shallow trench where silt and rust flakes have gathered into low dunes. Nothing here is truly horizontal; everything sags fractionally toward that trench, as though the machine itself has tired and borne down under its own weight.

Atmosphere and light  

Light is scarce. A few sodium bulbs, powered by what remains of the station’s dynamos, still glow dully amber, but their housings are occluded by barnacles and mineral bloom. More reliable is the milk-blue radiance that seeps through longitudinal stress cracks in the roof; outside, bioluminescent plankton swirl against the hull in drifting clouds, turning the fissures into stained-glass windows whose patterns change from moment to moment. The combined glow is barely enough to see by, yet enough to render every moving creature a silhouette.

Soundscape  

Silence is impossible here. Three tones dominate. First, the continuous basso rumble of exterior currents pushing against miles of intake ducting—a sound like steady thunder, never ceasing. Second, the slower heartbeat thud of two piston pairs that somehow refuse to die; every eleven seconds they cycle, expelling a plume of bubbles that rush upward and shatter against the ceiling like distant applause. Last are the voices of living things: the clicking of jointed limbs on metal, the rasp of scaled flanks against corroded mesh, the occasional sharp crack when a hunting jaw closes on carapace.

Living things  

Vegetation is almost an afterthought: lace-fronds of translucent algae cling sparsely to warm housings, and mats of iron-eating bacteria tile the grating like soot-black moss. The animals, by contrast, are everywhere.  

• Gutter crabs the size of your palm scuttle through access slots, their shells coloured the same oxide red as the rust they graze upon.  
• Tubular glass eels coil around still-live electrical conduits, harvesting stray amperage; arcs flicker over their skins and flash silver like distant lightning.  
• Shoals of spindlefish circle the upper vault in slow parabolas, each fish keeping perfect distance from the next so the school resembles a net of moving points. Twice a day, a manta-squid six metres across glides through, venting spent steam collected from the roof; lesser creatures scatter until its shadow passes.

Processes in motion  

Nothing here is truly static. Warm effluent from the ancient boilers (no one remembers what they once powered) rises and meets frigid trench water, creating internal rain that falls in silver threads, replenishing a constant fine snowfall of mineral grit. Grates tremble under the weight of the piston blows. Funnels in the walls open and close, adjusting throughput according to some forgotten algorithm etched in cracked Bakelite relays. Predator and prey follow the hydraulic pulse of the Chapel’s machinery: when the pistons fire, rust flakes billow, microfauna feed, and everything else descends one trophic step to feed on them.

Rules of the place  

1. Pressure is absolute ruler; any cavity larger than a closed fist will sooner or later implode.  
2. Sound travels far and fast; a careless clang on metal will summon both scavengers and the curious echo-dolphins that probe the outer corridors.  
3. Corrosion is the only clock that matters. Touch any surface and a film of red will bloom on your suit within minutes.  
4. Light is life: whatever glows draws cleaners, which draw hunters, which draw the manta-squid. Darkness is temporary sanctuary.

Senses after lingering  

Stay an hour and the Pump-Chapel’s narrow scale begins to feel broad, because the eye is constantly pulled to motion rather than distance. The chamber’s far wall may be just metres away, yet currents, creatures, and particulate rain place veils between you and it, turning a simple crossing into a miniature expedition. The longer you remain, the more the room seems to inhale and exhale around you, not mechanical after all but something somnolent, half-biological, content to endure its slow erosion until the last piston stills and the last bulb fades and it is finally allowed to rest in perfect darkness beneath the weight of the sea.
