The mud-horizon begins everywhere at once.  

Stand knee-deep and you will not know where you entered, because the plain is almost perfectly level—an ancient seabed planed down by three billion years of soft rain, then hardened, then worn again until only the faintest undulation remains. Whatever mountains once guarded it are long ago dissolved and sifted into this gummy expanse. The highest point within a hundred kilometres is the thickness of your own hand.

1. Terrain and material  
• Ground: A colloid of silts and half-gelled clay called mirror-loam by the very few who have mapped it. Capillary water keeps it glistening; press a footprint and it fills from below, as though the planet itself exhales through the mark.  
• Patches of lithified ripple stone rise like broken dinner plates—fossilised storm-ripples from an ocean that vanished before chordates learned to wriggle. They are the only hard surfaces, and they ring when stepped on, singing like porcelain.  
• Hidden under the topmost millimetres: a pan of compacted sediment so old the grains have recrystallised. It is flat enough that distant thunder sets the sheet vibrating, so the land throbs imperceptibly, a continent-sized drumhead.

2. Water and atmosphere  
Rain does not so much fall here as remain. The air holds it in suspension; droplets bleed out of nothing, bead on your cheeks, crawl back skyward on the next updraft. At sunrise—though “sunrise” means only a bruised brightening of a pewter firmament—evaporation and condensation trade places every few seconds. Visibility flickers between hazy metres and hazy kilometres as curtains of mist unweave themselves.  
Runnels and filament-streams thread everywhere, none more than a handspan deep, all interconnected. Follow one and you eventually return to your starting point, like tracing lines on a convolution. Despite constant flow, the water is never silent.

3. Sound (the always-present roar)  
Wind at ground level is negligible; the plain is too low and too shielded by its own boundary layer. The racket comes from above, where stacked inversion sheets shear against each other. Picture a thousand rivers of air racing in different directions, colliding, shredding, re-knitting, every second. Their friction births rolling detonations that cascade downward: skyquakes. They break around you in sonic booms, fall subsonic, then infrasonic, then rise again, overlapping until no gap remains. Plug your ears and your bones still flutter. Fields of ripple stone amplify the resonance; each shard a tuning fork. Silence, here, is a theoretical construct.

4. Light  
The permanent overcast is thick with condensed aerosols that drink colour. Daylight arrives as a smudged sepia glow. At night the cloud deck becomes faintly phosphorescent—charged by the day’s solar wash and by piezo-luminescence from the endlessly flexing upper layers. Shadows exist, but they are soft like bruises on fruit.

5. Vegetation and sparse life  
Only two obvious plants endure the battering atmosphere.  
• Spindle-crypts: leafless columns of fibrous rind, as tall as a woman’s chest, root-capped like inverted mops. They drink water straight from vapor, scraping it with ionised hairs whose crackle can be heard a metre away. Inside, they farm green slurry for themselves; cut one open and a gallon of chlorophore leaks out.  
• Moor-glass: carpet patches of translucent blades, each an inflated cell. They lie in mats no thicker than a fingernail, turning the plain into occasional panes. Step on them and they burst with a ping, releasing metallic spores that drift until the next shear-front pounds them back to mud.  
Fauna is rarer still. The most common animal is unseen: the Creel Tone, a colonial membrane only microns thick that rides the inversion layers. When pressure shifts, it vents gas in staccato pulses, modulating the thunder—perhaps the closest thing the landscape has to birdsong. Once a decade, field xenologists claim, a single, blind, hip-high grazer lumbers out of the mist, feeds on spindle-crypt pulp, then dissolves—whether by rot or by choice none can say.

6. Built traces  
Across three million square kilometres archaeologists have catalogued ninety-one structures. They are never higher than a kneeling person, formed from the same mirror-loam but kiln-glazed by some forgotten art so that rain sluices off them. Their layouts are maddeningly tight: spirals whose turns leave no walking space, walls that touch yet do not merge, rooms too small to enter. No tools remain, no inscriptions, only the glaze’s faint iridescence, flashing like oil whenever a lightningless skyquake flickers light.

7. Weirdness—how the world refuses clean physics  
A compass here spins between pulses of thunder, not magnetism. Dropped objects sometimes hover a heartbeat before choosing gravity’s direction. Horizon lines bend; you can watch a traveller stroll away downslope yet vanish above your eye level. Researchers blame acoustic lensing: sound pressure gradients so steep they warp refractive index, which then tortures light and maybe more. Local folklore—if a land with almost no inhabitants can have folklore—insists the plain is the fossil of a god’s heartbeat, and the sound remembers.

8. Dynamics  
Though the terrain itself is nearly featureless, nothing stays still. Mist births and unbirths in seconds. The ground shivers under bass notes from on high. Spindle-crypt groves shed whole skins overnight and regrow by morning. Water migrates centimetres north, south, east and west in a ceaseless shuffle, tracing polygons that never repeat. Cartographers must stamp “valid for one week” on their maps, and even then the certainty is charitable.

9. Rules for the wanderer  
• Carry no metal plates wider than a handspan; they resonate to shattering in the pressure harmonics.  
• Memorise three distant objects because landmarks dissolve in fog and, more unsettlingly, sometimes migrate whole.  
• Wear non-slip gaiters—mirror-loam looks viscous but may act like glass if a skyquake’s node sweeps under you.  
• Speak only when necessary; your own voice can ricochet skyward, return magnified, and bruise organs.

10. Leaving  
Those who depart report a phantom thrum remaining in their chest for weeks, as though ribs remember the continent’s beat. They dream of flat, dark mirrors where rain walks backward into clouds. Most come to crave the quiet of ordinary storms, the colourful pain of sunlight, and the tiny, forgiving irregularities of land that still has mountains to lose.

The low, wet, dim plain is a paradox: ancient enough to be in its final geologic lull, yet frenetic at every other scale. It is silence’s antithesis, vision’s adversary, and—if you grow patient—an atlas of the subtle art by which planets keep singing after their voices should have failed.
