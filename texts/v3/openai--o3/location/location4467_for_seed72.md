You reach it by a freight lift that groans for a full minute, then stops with a sigh thirty metres under the canyon floor.  
When the lattice gate folds back, the air washes over you like the breath from a kiln—thick, mineral, almost greasy with condensed steam.

TERRAIN AND MATERIALS  
The place is a single cavern the size of an old cathedral nave—two hundred metres long, perhaps sixty wide, and nowhere high enough to lose sight of the soot–blackened roof ribs that engineers once bolted straight into the living basalt. The floor is unfinished rock. Tool–marks, cooling cracks and half–shattered drill lines zig-zag across it as though the bedrock is still deciding what shape it wants. No natural erosion yet: everything is too new, too sharp, as if geology only yesterday peeled its mould away.

A shallow grade runs from the lift toward the rear wall, and water—everywhere water—threads that decline. It seeps straight from pores in the basalt, trickling into channels cut with pickheads still leaning nearby. The rivulets meet in ankle-deep pans where iron skeletons of once–intended machinery wait, half drowned. The water is warm, metallic, faintly oily from the grease of gearboxes that were never given time to run.

ATMOSPHERE AND LIGHT  
Light is rationed. Sodium bulbs droop from armored cables and paint everything the colour of old parchment. Between bulbs, shadows reign, disturbed only by passing furnace-gnat clouds that glow where they catch the sodium. Now and again a pressure valve vents, backlighting the cavern with a sheet of white steam for the span of a breath before the dimness returns.

SOUNDS  
Even slow places can be loud. Here sound behaves like a lazy tide, rising until you hardly notice it and falling only when something in you wonders where the quiet went.

• Drip, drip, drip—a constant clatter on sheet-metal pans.  
• The wheeze of condensers trying to stay alive in the heat.  
• Chains clank when a distant conveyor stutters in its sleep.  
• A blanket murmur: thousands of living throats hidden in cracks and ducts.

VEGETATION  
Almost none. A grey filamentous algae tiles the water’s skin in places, exhaling a smell like wet limestone. On copper pipes near the ceiling, pale fern-analogues unfurl fronds no larger than fingerprints, sipping condensed vapor before the rivulets steal it away.

FAUNA—THE OVERRUN  
Animals have claimed the half-born city faster than the builders ever could.

1. Furnace swifts: soot-black birds that skim inches above the warm ponds, snatching up insects thrown off by the heat.  
2. Pipe eels: blind, rope-thin fish that migrated through drainage lines and now knot themselves around impellers, feeding on grease.  
3. Rat colonies: heat-adapted, slick-furred, occupying the abandoned dormitory mezzanine; their bodies thump like falling tools when dominance battles break out.  
4. Chitin lobsters: hand-sized crustaceans grazing the algae film; when startled they slap the water hard enough to sound like a dropped wrench.

Though they fill every niche, their lives pulse on a slow metronome: breeding seasons keyed to gradual temperature swings, migrations measured in months, not days. Nothing here is in a hurry.

BUILT ENVIRONMENT  
Steel catwalks grid the airspace below the cavern roof, connecting platforms where furnaces and presses were meant to sit. Many machines arrived, few were installed. Shipping crates lie splayed open, shipping straw fossilized by heat. A half-assembled piston press, taller than two men, juts at an angle as if frozen mid-rise. Power cables snake but lead nowhere; some dangle into ponds where eels braid themselves through copper strands.

Water-scooping wheels turn only because the seep keeps pushing them; they lift brackish cups that spill instantly, accomplishing nothing but rhythm.

Ecclesiastical graffiti—crew jokes, half-shed schedules—still chalk the uprights. One reads, “When they finish pouring the roof, the thunder will stop.” But the roof was poured; the thunder (echoes, condensation pops, the massed chatter of life) never stopped.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Heat is perpetual but not explosive—an oven left on low forever. Steel softens if you remain too long in direct contact.  
2. Water is inevitable. Block a seep and another drill-scar bleeds for it.  
3. Light must be made; darkness arrives uninvited and in abundance.  
4. Living things multiply in proportion to neglect.  
5. Change is glacial. Pipes rust grain by grain, algae inches outward, but the fundamental scene—half birth, half abandonment—clings like a superstition.

STANDING STILL  
Stay motionless on a catwalk and time becomes viscous. You notice how the sodium lamp by your boot pulses with an unseen grid’s heartbeat; how individual drops form stalactites on the girder before letting go; how the unhurried biology below seems content with this fragment of unfinished city, this humid hollow that humans declared useful once and have not yet remembered to reclaim.

Nothing here asks you to hurry. The place will become itself whether watched or not, one deliberate degree of rust, one generation of pipe eel at a time.
