Cold Submergence / Foundry V

1. First impression  
You enter through what was once a skylight: a ragged oval in a roof of riveted iron. Water pours down the opening in a slow, constant sheet, almost viscous in the near-freezing temperature. The hole admits a suggestion of dim grey daylight for perhaps two metres, then light and world both drown together. From that point onward everything is water, black, and close.

2. Scale and layout  
Foundry V is not large—no wider than a modest cathedral nave, no longer than two city blocks, its walls, floor, and ceiling all intact though ruptured in places. Think of a single room so crammed with machinery, walkways, vats, chimneys, and ductwork that, even full of water, it feels stuffed to bursting: a thousand overlapping iron ribs inside a drowned whale. The chamber sits at the low point of a coastal plain, long since claimed by the ocean. The outside tides now sluice through tears in the masonry; every six hours the pressure reverses and the whole interior breathes like a lung.

3. Terrain / structure  
• Floor: ankle-deep mud that never touches ankles, only gloved hands and magnetic boots; slag pebbles; the ghost of a railway spur.  
• Walls: layered boiler plate, knuckled by centuries of oxidation into red-black scales. In places the rust has flaked off to reveal the original makers’ marks: raised letters you can read by touch alone.  
• Ceiling: a puzzle of girders coated in salt frost. From them hang chains, hoists, conveyor belts—now limp, swaying slightly in the water’s faint currents like iron kelp.

4. Materials under pressure  
Steel has fermented into lace. Brass bearings have grown nacreous skins of carbonate. Ceramic insulators host colonies of pale filament algae; each filament tinged with a dull nickel sheen leached from the wiring it clings to. Everywhere, salt has insinuated itself, crystallising inside cracks, keeping the whole edifice paradoxically stable: break a girder and it may crumble, but leave it undisturbed and it seems petrified, eternal.

5. Light—or almost none  
Officially darkness is 0.5. In practice this becomes a smoulder rather than a glow. Here is where the weirdness begins. A handful of mercury-arc lamps, powered by tide-drawn turbines somewhere below, still ignite when the incoming surge reaches a certain pressure. They dim to invisibility after fifteen minutes, but while they burn their UV spill makes the algae threadlines shine a bruised violet. Elsewhere, chemical reactions in sealed vats occasionally burp out ribbons of green luminescence that drift upward like torn prayer flags, illuminating nothing beyond arm’s reach.

6. Soundscape  
Near-silence dominates: only your own respirator hiss, the tick of cooling metal, the faint clacking of a loosened sprocket glancing off a rail. Once every hour a deeper note rolls through the hall—a freight-train groan—when two warped plate walls flex toward each other and settle back. Fish scatter when it happens, though you may not see them—only feel the pressure ripple that follows.

7. Water character  
Temperature hovers just above the saltwater freezing point. Viscosity feels higher than open ocean: microscopic rust flakes thicken it, giving every motion a dreamlike drag. Visibility rarely exceeds four metres; lights appear as blurred moons, distances compress. Yet currents are active. Warm brine leaks from buried reaction chambers, rises in shimmering plumes, hits the ceiling, cools, and rains down again as needle-cold droplets even within the flood—water inside water.

8. Vegetation and other living things  
Vegetation is sparse and opportunistic. The filament algae mentioned earlier. Scalloped mats of chemosynthetic bacteria colonise junction boxes, exhaling faint oxygen bubbles that cling to bolts like silver beads. A single viney organism, nicknamed “wire-ivy,” threads itself through cracked conduit and only flowers (skeletal white polyps) when exposed to the arc lamps’ UV.

Fauna are rare, cautious.  
• Ghost shrimp: transparent, red-eyed, feeding on rust slurry.  
• Blind conger eels: one or two individuals longer than a man but thinner than his wrist, inhabiting the hollow boiler drums.  
• An introduced species: three mechanical inspection drones still roam their old patrol routes. Their filigreed housings now harbour barnacles; the drones navigate by sonar pings so soft you feel them more than hear them. When their batteries dip, they plug themselves into wall sockets that have somehow stayed live—proof the grid beneath still carries a charge.

9. Activity / processes  
The place is old but far from static.  
• Pressure cycles tug at every seam. Some doors only open on the ebb, others only on the flood.  
• A row of pistons near the south wall still moves, not driven by external power but by buoyancy: hollow rods fill with water, sink, purge, rise. They drive nothing anymore; they simply remember.  
• Mineral accretion forms stalactites of pure copper where leaking acid once dissolved wiring. A drop forms every six seconds, lands with a pop that resembles a distant spark.  
• There is an interior weather: warm flare, chill blanket, then thin snowfalls of oxidised dust spiralling down.

10. Rules of the world (as locals understand them)  
• Gravity is absolute only for solids. Liquids here show partial loyalty, hanging in canted sheets along magnetic gradients, then re-aligning unpredictably.  
• Iron holds memory: touch a certain panel and you will feel, as mild electric tingle, the last vibration it experienced before the flood.  
• Silence is fragile. A single hammer strike could bring the roof down; whispering is encouraged, welding forbidden.  
• Nothing combustible burns, but flash-rust replaces flame—strike a match and the phosphorus fire converts instantly to a burst of ochre dust, extinguished but still warm.

11. Atmosphere, in the emotional sense  
Foundry V feels densely peopled though hardly any living thing remains. You sense layered presences: workers who evacuated, inspectors who never left, machines that do not grasp the concept of departure. Every surface hums with long use, long erosion, long patience. The building is at equilibrium with its submersion; you are the disturbance.

12. Ways out, ways deeper  
Follow the conveyor deeper and you will reach the Furnace Mouth—a yawning rent in the floor where molten steel once poured. It now drops into a silt-choked sinkhole patrolled by the eels. Ascend instead toward the skylight and you breach into night air, a drizzle-slick iron roof, the distant white roar of the actual ocean only ten metres away. Either direction is a re-emergence: one into colder darkness, one into wetter wind.

13. Last image  
As you leave, the arc lamps flicker off. The bacteria vents sigh and close. In the renewed dark the drowned foundry seems to settle around itself, seamless again, waiting for the next slow surge of tide to animate its ribs. Silence folds back over the machinery like a blanket of silt, undisturbed until the roof splits wider or another curious body drops in from above.
