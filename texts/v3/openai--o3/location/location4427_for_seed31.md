The Plateau of Glass Ferns rises so high that roads climb through torn shreds of cloud before leveling onto an upland basin the size of a small valley. It is young land—its rim still broken and angular, as though the mountain’s crust split only a few lifetimes ago and the debris never had time to slump. Fresh-bladed ridges saw the horizon, and the cliff faces sparkle with fracture planes that have not yet dulled to weather.

Water is everywhere but rarely gathers into big lakes. Warm, moist air blown up from lower forests meets the cold breath of altitude, so vapor condenses on stone like dew on metal. Every vertical surface weeps: clear sheets slide down columnar joints, bead at ledge edges, then patter into runnels. Because this happens day and night, the ground is always dark with moisture. The soil, thin but rich, exhales a faint steam at dawn and smells of peppery humus.

Vegetation responds eagerly but without overcrowding. Wide spaces of bare basalt separate islands of green. Ferns are the sovereigns here—tree-ferns twenty spans tall, their trunks shaggy and black, their crowns unfurling in slow spirals that glint wetly. Between them: liver-bright moss lawns, ribbon-leaf sedges, shallow-rooted flowering herbs that hold raindrops like gemstone beads. Each clump seems to float in its own silence; nothing rustles, for there are no animals to stir the fronds and no insects to thrum in the still, mild air.

The quiet is almost complete. You hear only the constant hush of dripping, the soft crack when a young ridge flake breaks and skids down a slope, and, very occasionally, the distant roar when a newborn rill decides to fall all at once from the cliff rim as a short-lived waterfall. The sky is bright but not fierce: a regular temperate sun crosses cleanly; nights are clear and black, stars sharp because the air is thin and forever washed.

Across the basin lies a settlement—open, spacious, built as though its makers wished never to crowd the land. Low roundhouses of basalt flags are set far apart, each on its own slight mound encircled by a drainage ditch that doubles as a garden moat for water-mint and spindle-reed. Roofs are thatched with the fronds of dead ferns, their underlayer daubed with blue clay to keep drips out. Between the houses stretch long stone causeways raised two handspans above the perpetually damp turf. At nodal points where paths cross, the people have set upright slabs whose faces are chiselled with swirling grooves; water running down them is led into channels, then into cisterns sealed with wooden lids. All tools here are of polished stone, bone-white quartz, and the rare soft metals hammered from meteoric nodules hauled up from lower lands. The inhabitants do not smelt; they shape and braid and chip.

Because nothing moves but people and water, the place has rules of slowness. Wood smoke rises in almost perfect columns—fires are small, fed sparingly so the scents of resin and fern-oil do not overwhelm the pure upland air. Fields—if the rectangular terraces can be called fields—contain rows of edible tubers bred to live half-drowned; their broad leaves glisten like lacquer. Workers hoe in near-silence, speaking little because voices carry too far on the plateau, and privacy, like dryness, is precious.

There is an unspoken compact with the land: no structure may break the skyline. Watch-towers are built in dips; granaries hug cliffsides. Even on festival nights, when torches stud the paths, flames are hooded so their glow never rises above shoulder height. One senses that if the people shouted or built too tall, the still young mountain might notice and shrug them off.

Time here feels stalled. The drystone walls show no sag, the footpaths never rut, and the vegetation seems caught in an eternal first flush of growth. Perhaps uplift has stalled too; geologists from older continents suspect the whole massif is precariously balanced between forces, held unmoving by some deep mantle quirk. For now, nothing changes except the position of sunlight on wet rock, and even that shifts with a calm, metronomic grace.

Stand on the outer rim at midday: below lies a hallway of clouds, above an ocean of sky, around you the hush of ferns and the click of a single droplet leaping from stone to stone. No wingbeats, no chirrups—only the constant, patient pulse of water inventing rivers that will not fully form for millennia. It is a place not yet worn enough for echoes, where the future has not begun to erode the present, and everything—stone, plant, and human craft—lives in the long, bright pause before history starts to speak.
