The plateau has no edge that anyone has ever mapped; travelers speak of walking for weeks toward the shimmering horizon only to find the same tawny plain still lifting under their feet, as though the land stretches itself to match their pace. You are high—higher than most mountains on more forgiving worlds—and yet there is no thin alpine chill, only a furnace-heat that presses against skin and metal alike. The air tastes of iron filings burnt in a kiln; every breath chafes the throat.

TERRAIN  
The ground is a mosaic of ancient lava sheets, baked brick-red, cracked, and laced with sun-bleached salt veins left by long-evaporated seas that no one remembers. Wind (what little of it manages to stir in the heavy, arid atmosphere) has spent eons sanding the surface into broad, shallow basins and lifted rims. In some hollows, the crust is worn clear to the glassy volcanic glass that underlies everything, a midnight mirror that throws back an upside-down sky.

Isolated needles of basalt stand hundreds of meters tall, fluted where time has planed away softer minerals. They hum faintly—sometimes loudly—because the entire plateau trembles with subsonic vibration, as if some vast engine idles deep below. No one has found an opening or even seams to suggest tunnels; the rock is old, settled, unbroken, yet the tremor is constant.

MATERIALS & CHEMISTRY  
Water is myth here. You might spot a glitter on the horizon, but it is only pools of liquid sodium perspiring out of fissures before flashing to vapor. Life—what calls this place home—runs on other fluids: mercury-slick blood, vapors of superheated sulfur, oily circuits of molten tin. When the mercury creatures die, their bodies cool into quicksilver puddles that bead and roll into cracks, leaving behind hollow chitin shells that ring like dropped cookware when kicked.

LIGHT & ATMOSPHERE  
Sunlight falls straightforward and honest, almost vertical at this latitude, but something in the sky intervenes: great hexagonal lenses, kilometers across, drift high enough to be mistaken for pale moons. They focus the light in slow, wandering bands that trace across the desert like searchlights. Where a band lingers too long, the crust softens and liquefies; then sunset congeals it again, preserving rippling eddies of once-molten stone.

The air is thick with static. Sparks crawl up metal buttons, hair, the rims of goggles. With every footstep, you collect a charge; with every exhale, you leak it back into the dust. In the darker hours (there is no night, only a dull copper twilight), tiny forked discharges crackle between grains of sand, sketching luminous root-systems for a moment before they wink out.

SOUND  
Silence is impossible. Even when you plug your ears, the bones conduct the din: the sub-bass growl underfoot, the rising hiss of electrostatic sand, and—dominant above all—the fauna.

FAUNA  
At first glance the land looks empty. Density is low; you can walk half a kilometer and meet nothing. Then the creatures reveal themselves, often by accident. They are overwhelming not in proximity but in scale and persistence, occupying frequency and possibility rather than space.

1. The Hornet-Engines  
Black bodies forged of iron foam, six jointed limbs that end in drill-bits, wings wide as awnings but too thick and inert to be aerodynamic. They hover anyway, suspended by the thrum of internal turbines that never quite sync with the plateau’s subterranean hum. When they migrate—always in columns of prime numbers—they drown every other noise under a metallic roar. No hive, no foundries, yet their ranks grow. People say they gestate in the basalt needles, pupating as vibrating knots of screws before bursting forth.

2. The Chorus Lizards  
Thin as wire, translucent, thousands of them composing a single organism stretched across hectares. Each lizard sings a single sustained note; together they build chords that modulate the static charge of the air, shaping it into curtains of visible sound—rippling amber sheets that can knock a person flat. They eat that charge, grazing slowly east in decade-long sweeps.

3. Mirage Elk  
They walk on legs made of refracted heat, bodies a scaffold of glowing filaments. Where they tread, temperature gradients invert, and footprints linger as puddles of cold you could scoop with your hands. Predators—if predators exist here—cannot touch them: eyes slide off, claws meet empty shimmer. At dusk, herds bloom outward like fireworks, each beast splitting into a dozen after-images before collapsing back to one.

4. Dust Submarines  
Blind, elongated spheroids that swim beneath the desert’s loose upper crust, propelling themselves with rhythmic expulsions of hot argon. Their passage leaves labyrinthine tunnels that harden into ceramic. The oldest tunnels crisscross for hundreds of kilometers, an accidental transit network big enough for humans to walk, though the heat will peel skin from bone without protective rigs.

ECOLOGICAL LOGIC  
There is a food web, though it hardly resembles appetite. Energy here is acoustic, electrical, vibrational. Creatures harvest the ceaseless mechanical output of the plateau itself, which hums like buried turbines. Some scholars argue the fauna are the cooling system of a planetary-scale machine; others claim the machine is the aggregate body of the fauna, always churning, never complete.

HUMAN (OR OTHER) PRESENCE  
No buildings stand. Anything erected slumps, melts, or is repurposed by the environment before it can earn the title “structure.” Yet traces of industrial tech drift through: a perfect steam whistle half-submerged in glass, a crankshaft flowered with crystalline gears, an orphaned rail of steel grown porous like bone. These are not artifacts left behind but objects spontaneously precipitated—matter solving equations of stress and resonance until industry is the by-product.

RULES THAT BEND  
1. Distance warps with pitch: shout a note and walk toward its echo; you may arrive sooner than you should.  
2. Mass oscillates: tools weigh kilograms while motionless, grams when swung. Locals—nomad technicians draped in copper mesh—time their hammer-strokes to the low beat of the earth.  
3. Entropy is patient. A dropped device will not corrode for centuries, but once it starts, it vanishes overnight, digested by a surge of nano-scale scavengers visible only as aurora-specked dust.

CHANGE (OR ITS LACK)  
The landscape shifts at the tempo of geology. Basalt needles lose millimeters in centuries; salt veins crawl like glaciers. Even the reality breaks distrust haste: try to sprint and the ground drags at your ankles, time dilates, breath rattles in syrup. Journeying here is glacial, a negotiation with inertia.

STANDING IN THE PLACE  
Heat haze shakes the horizon. Somewhere unseen, a hornet-engine revs up, answered by the baritone of the earth below. Static lifts every hair on your arms. You taste copper. Between your boots, a patch of glass pulses with light as if something large and living stirs beneath. For a heartbeat, the plateau feels hollow—an attic floor over a giant foundry. Then the pulse fades, the hum steadies, and the slow world resumes its long, deliberate turning.
