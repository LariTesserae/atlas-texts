The descent never quite feels like going underground.  
The ramped tunnel is broad enough to forget its walls, and bright enough to make you doubt there is rock above your head at all.  Only when the air begins to smell of wet stone and the faintest tang of iron do you realise you have passed the threshold between the surface and the Hollow Reach.

TERRAIN  
The Reach is not a single cave but a joined archipelago of chambers, each the size of a small valley, collapsed into one another long ago and then polished by ages of slow water-drip and wind-eddies.  The floor lies mostly level—ancient erosion has planed away the once-jagged rubble—yet it is scored by shallow troughs that snake for kilometres.  These channels are often dry, their beds smooth like unfired clay, but at intervals they hold mirrored pools a handspan deep.  Condensation gathers on the ceiling far overhead, beads into droplets, and falls in lazy, bright rain.  Where the drops land for centuries in one spot they have drilled bell-mouthed pits the locals call “singer pots.”  Step near them and the air inside moans softly, as though the hollow stone remembers wind that no longer blows here.

MATERIAL & LIGHT  
Nearly every surface shows the gray-blue of limestone, yet the chamber never darkens.  The rock is threaded with long ribbons of selenite; when any light touches those veins the crystals conduct it for surprising distances, scattering it back in a cool, even glow.  A single torch at a crossroads can illuminate a neighbourhood; a cluster of firebowls on a balcony paints half the Reach in pearly daylight.  To the newcomers it looks like witchcraft.  To the residents it is simply how stone behaves.

WATER  
Moisture is mercurial here.  One hour a corner may glisten like fresh glaze, the next it flakes into dry powder.  Early mornings bring a hush of fog that never quite becomes a cloud and never quite dissipates, lingering instead in folds of the floor like a thinking presence.  Twice or thrice a season, if surface rains have been generous, a hidden fissure releases a short-lived underground river that gallops through the troughs, fills every pot and pool, then vanishes into deeper cracks days later.  The locals do not fear these floods; they treat them as a festival, setting wicker traps for the blind silver eels that sweep in with the water.

VEGETATION—OR THE LACK THEREOF  
Nothing green survives.  The ceiling is too distant for roots to reach any seep of soil, and whatever seeds once drifted down here surrendered to time.  Life expresses itself instead in slow minerals and quick flesh.  Fuzzy mats of chalky micro-lichen bloom on damp walls and withdraw to pale stains when the surface dries.  Thin coats of iridescent salt plateaus form around the warmer vents, break off, and drift like shattered mirrors across the floor.  Life enough, if you broaden the word.

FAUNA  
By contrast, animals thrive.  The silence gives every scuffle away, yet there are so many hidden cracks that predation rarely thins the numbers.  Slender, six-legged nyx-hares with translucent fur lap condensation from the walls.  Beetles the size of thumbs march in glimmering caravans, harvesting fallen selenite splinters to grind into their nests—no one knows why.  Air-manta, pale gray and barely heavier than their own wings, haunt the upper currents, steering on long ribbon tails to snatch the moths that rise toward firelight.  Neither bark nor birdsong carries here; sound dies softly, as though the air is thick with velvet.  When something does cry out—a wounded hare, a stone shifting—it echoes once and folds away, polite to a fault.

BUILT WORLD  
The city of Cindermark rests where three chambers join, a meeting of hollows like the petals of a stone flower.  Its buildings look at first like natural columns.  Up close they reveal themselves as stacked limestone panels, each one prised from the floor, chiselled square with flint adzes, then levered into place by ropes and patient teamwork.  Primitive, perhaps, but persistent: some towers have stood so long that condensation has welded their seams into lime, fusing labour into geology.

Every household maintains a hand-fed brazier whose flame both cooks and lights.  Smoke is rare; the residents burn a clean-crackling fungus brought up from deeper strata—a growth with the density of cork and the smell of pepper when alight.  Streets are wide gaps rather than lanes, bordered by nothing more than lines of hammered bone stakes and hung cords of polished stone beads that tink gently when someone brushes past.

Despite the width, the city feels sparse.  Families dwell far enough apart that each can enjoy the reach of its own glow without extinguishing the night for a neighbour.  Between districts lie arenas of bare rock where children race glow-beetles or trace ancestral patterns in powdered gypsum.  No wall, no gate: distance alone is boundary.

TECHNOLOGY & CRAFT  
Iron is myth here; ore veins lie too deep and fire too thin to smelt them.  Flint, bone, and rare meteoric nickel scavenged from the ceiling’s collapse provide cutting edges.  Selenite knapped to a wedge is as sharp as glass, if brittle.  The people weave straps from nyx-hare sinew, build lifting frames from the ribs of cave-whales beached in pre-history, and record their chronicles by scratching spirals into large salt plates that shimmer like mother-of-pearl when backlit.

DYNAMIC WEATHER OF STONE  
Though the Reach is ancient and well-worn, it refuses to stay still.  Warm drafts rise through unseen vents, meet cooler sheets of air sliding down the chamber walls, and tangle into whirlwinds that migrate through the hollows.  A calm plaza can, with no warning, host a spinning pillar of dust that climbs a dozen metres, scatters bead-cords, then moves on as gently as it came.  Rare roof-slips send avalanches of crystal needles tinkling to the floor—beautiful, deadly rain.  The locals keep narrow slit-shelters carved into every facade: when stone begins to groan they vanish inside, wait out the turmoil, and re-emerge to sweep the new debris into baskets for trade.

SOUND  
The first thing a newcomer notices after the light is how little noise survives.  Conversations must be held close; otherwise words blur into a soft susurrus and are lost.  Tools are wrapped in hide to muffle knocks.  Drums exist, but are played only at feast-time in the Mouth of the Reach, a natural amphitheatre whose acoustics carry the pulse through the entire city—one controlled roar permitted to remind everyone that silence is a choice, not a law.

RULES & UNDERLYING LOGIC  
1. Sound dissipates quickly; many creatures hunt by vibration rather than echo.  
2. Light propagates—selenite veins must be respected.  Extinguish a torch in one district and a workshop half a kilometre away dims.  
3. Water appears, disappears, and returns unpredictably; cisterns are life.  
4. Nothing green grows.  Any seed that sprouts is treated as an omen, not a crop.  
5. Stone changes mood.  A creak overhead is a message, not a threat; ignore it and the ceiling remembers.

WHAT THE PLACE FEELS LIKE, AFTER HOURS  
Walk alone and the Reach seems deserted: bright, cool halls breathing a slow exhale.  Wait, and motion resolves everywhere—insects swapping crystals, a hare pausing on its back legs to sip mist from the air, a family hauling a new limestone sheet while singing a tune too soft to hear.  At times a storm of warm air coasts overhead, tilting the flight of the air-manta, scattering dust that sparkles in its borrowed light.  It is a quiet bustle, like a marketplace observed through thick glass.  The turbulence is real, but it happens in little gusts and moments, each pocket of activity surrounded by respectful hush.

Yet the longer you stay, the more you notice the city’s pulse: brazier fire reflected a hundred times along crystal seams, the subtle shifting of selenite glow as people shutter windows, the periodic whoop of children riding a dust devil across an empty avenue, the communal stillness when a roof-slip booms in the distance and everyone listens, counting the seconds until the last shard falls.

NOTES FOR THE TRAVELLER  
Bring a watertight skin; there is always water somewhere, but never where you are.  
Carry two lights: one to see by, one for the walls to borrow.  
Learn the difference between the ceiling’s sigh and its groan.  
And above all, remember that silence lives here.  Treat it as the oldest citizen of the Reach—older than the city, older than the hollows themselves, patient, watchful, currently content to let you pass.
