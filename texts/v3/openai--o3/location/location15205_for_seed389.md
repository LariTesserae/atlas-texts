You come up on it from below, following a switch-backing rail line that climbs through air so hot it ripples the viewfinder of your goggles. At first the place looks like a mountain range, all knife-edged ridges and chimney spires fused together. Then the eye begins to pick out pattern: doors in the cliff faces, gantries bolted straight into incandescent rock, cables draped like black vines. It is not a range at all, but a city poured and welded onto the newborn bones of the planet.

TERRAIN  
Everything here is vertical. A continental plate has recently jack-knifed, leaving a single, two-hundred-kilometre wall of volcanic glass and still-cooling basalt almost three kilometres high. The surface is raw, its facets un-weathered, sharp as shattered obsidian. Where the uplift slowed, broad shelves remain, and on those terraces the inhabitants have built: stacked blocks of slagbrick, ribbed cooling towers, rows of condenser sails that turn slowly to face the wind.

The ground below the wall is a coarse talus slope of black beads and orange pumice. In places steam pours from fissures, but the overall sense is of something that has only just finished moving—a crust that hasn’t decided what shape it wants to be.

WATER  
The climate is a paradox of heat and damp. Every mid-afternoon, convection drags moist air up the scarp until it hits cooler strata half a kilometre above the city roofs. The moisture condenses almost instantly, falling back as curtains of warm drizzle that last ten, maybe fifteen minutes. Streets slicken, gutters hiss, and half-finished pools gather at the bases of walls before evaporating. Down on the plain there is no river—only discontinuous ponds, mirror calm, flashing for an hour or two each day. They are ringed with crystals rather than plants; salts bloom like white roses as the water retreats.

VEGETATION & LIFE  
Nothing photosynthesises here; the basalt reflects too much of the useful spectrum and the daily soak-&-bake cycle would pulp anything with a cell wall. Where life occurs it is cryptic and reluctant: pearl-grey sheets of chemotrophic lichen hugging cracks, a flicker of motion that proves to be a six-legged, silica-shelled isopod gnawing rust flakes off a rail tie. Citizens speak of the Long-Limb—some macroscopic organism that leaves chain-shaped imprints in morning dew—but sightings are gossip, not data. To live here, flesh is optional; patience is mandatory.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Ordinary daylight, filtered through high dust, feels coppery. Sunsets are fierce violet, and at night the cliff face glows where magma still lurks a metre or two behind the surface. The air smells of warm iron and unglazed ceramic. There is almost no wind except the daily convection surge; between those events the settlement lies in a pocket of silence big enough to taste.

SOUND  
Despite the industrial skyline, the city is quiet. Turbines run on geothermal pressure and make only a deep organ-note you feel rather than hear. Conveyors creep instead of clatter, their gears soaked in oil reclaimed from meteor bitumen. Shouts carry for kilometres. Often the loudest thing is the slow ticking of cooling rock—a geological heartbeat stretched over hours.

BUILT ENVIRONMENT & TECHNOLOGY  
The people (you will not meet many; population density is architectural, not human) practice a craft you might call furnace urbanism. Buildings are poured in situ: powdered slag, foaming agents, and waste heat from magma vents are combined in moulds the size of cathedrals, then left to anneal for weeks. Finished structures show banded layers—shades of charcoal, rust, mother-of-pearl.

Transport is vertical. Counterweight elevators run the canyon walls, balanced by stone blocks that descend into the plain as cars rise. For freight, there is the rack-rail that brought you here, crawling like a chrome centipede along a ribbon of stainless steel.

Industry centres on condensation. Vast spidery rigs unfold every dawn, netting water out of the air, separating minerals in gravity towers, feeding the scant surplus into ceramic cisterns that glimmer turquoise in the sun. Nothing is wasted; even the sigh of steam escaping a pressure joint is trapped in cloth funnels and led to turbogenerators.

ECOLOGY OF MATERIAL  
Where other places grow forests, this place grows strata of use. An abandoned balcony becomes a catchment for magnetite dust; add twenty seasons and that dust is mined to print structural filaments. There is no topsoil—only successive human intentions compressed into rock as hard as the source material.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Heat is free, cold is valuable. Crews trade in cooled water bricks the way uplanders trade in firewood.  
2. Surfaces are provisional. A ledge today may be quarried tomorrow; nothing carved is sacred until it has survived a decade unscheduled.  
3. Silence is etiquette. You must wear sound-damp boots in residential tiers; any step louder than an isopod’s skitter invites a fine.  
4. Erosion is opportunity. When a new crack opens, surveyors race to claim it—the fresher the fissure, the purer the steam, the cheaper the power.  
5. Time moves in geologic minutes: slow to outsiders, perfect for culture that likes to watch minerals grow.

WEIRDNESS  
You notice it first in peripheral vision: thin white filaments that rise from hot stone after rain, bend toward the cliff, and vanish by the time you turn your head. Locals call them “phase roots,” not because they are roots—the city has no plants—but because they seek something under the rock. Some nights, when the wind is utterly still, the condensers ring like wineglasses though nothing touches them. Scientists blame thermal tension; priests cite the cliff itself, half-alive, humming at frequencies that weather cannot erase.

DYNAMICS  
Change happens, just stretched: Towers slump a millimetre a year. New lava intrudes once a century. The rail line you rode today was a canal two generations back; in another three it may be a ravine open to the sun. Industries plan on fifty-year break-even cycles, and citizens record birthdays in strata, not seasons—“born five terrace-layers after the Great Spalling.”

LEAVING  
Climb higher and the heat climbs with you until the basalt surface glosses like sealed ink. The wall ends abruptly on a high plateau of glazed scoria, still too young for sand. Up here the silence is total; even the city’s gut-organ turbine note is gone. You can watch sheets of mist liquefy in midair and drop away like silver curtains, exposing the settlement far below—compact, vertical, quietly alight, clinging to the face of a world that is not yet finished being made.
