The place is born low, broad, and young, a freshly lifted tongue of land just a few breaths above a warm, tide-heavy sea. Its skin has not yet learned smoothness: ridgelets of jet-black basalt and raw, ocher clay rise in sudden seams and knife-edged dikes, their corners still sharp where the first rains have only begun to gnaw. Nothing here has had time to grow old—everything is still in the bright astonishment of first creation—yet the scene is already crowded far past comfort.

1. Light and air  
Daylight rarely reaches the ground intact. A triple canopy of foliage—wide, wax-skinned fronds below, towering palm-pillars above, and in between a loose web of liana and root-hung epiphytes—screens the sky until noon feels like the inside of a whale. What little brightness seeps through is tinted green and copper and eaten by steam; the rest comes from other, stranger sources. Fungal shelves embedded in bark exhale a bruised violet glow. Beetles carry lantern-bellies the color of molten brass. Damp air flashes with static pinpricks whenever one of the iron-rich ridges grinds against its nascent fault line. Vision here is a patchwork of lantern-spores, lightning smears, and narrow shafts of distant sun.

2. Water in fits and starts  
Moisture moves in pulses: a sudden vertical sheet of rain passes, hissing, and is gone; then nothing but dripping for an hour, the drops so large each is a separate syllable on leaf and stone. The ground is a maze of half-finished channels—knife-cut ravines a child could leap across, mirror-bright pools left behind by the last downpour, and narrow freshets that appear for ten minutes, carve a glossy wound in the clay, and vanish into steam. Between showers everything sweats: tree trunks bead, rocks weep, vines distill mist. The whole landscape gleams as though freshly varnished.

3. Terrain on the move  
Because the land is young, gravity has not yet had time to tidy. Slabs tilt unpredictably; boulders perch too high on soft mud; spires of unconsolidated ash stand like charcoal teeth until the next tremor brings them down. Every few hours the earth utters a muffled cough and a section of clifflet fractures with the crack of plate glass. The sound rolls outward, joins a thousand other collisions, and is swallowed by the greater uproar.

4. Noise, the ruling element  
Every living thing here is loud, and there are many living things. Amphibians the size of small barrels inflate throat sacs and detonate bass notes that vibrate in the ribs. In the midstory, swarms of thumb-long cicadas shred the air with metallic saw-songs. Overhead, fruit bats beat wings in dense clouds, each downstroke a bedsheet snap. Occasionally the booming of thermal surf from the unseen coast filters through the gaps, but even the ocean cannot dominate for long. Conversation is impossible; communication is percussion, gesture, fluorescence.

5. Overwhelming fauna  
• Riverback grazers: slick-skinned quadrupeds with double rows of bioluminescent dorsal nodules. They mow fern meadows in regimented herds, their chewing audible like rain on paper.  
• Sky-reefers: manta-shaped gliders that ride the updrafts from hot springs. They drop nets of sticky silk to entangle insects by the tens of thousands, then haul the bundle into their ventral maws.  
• Bone-tool primates: palm-high, six-fingered creatures that crack open snails with shard-flint but have never considered roofs or walls. They range in loose, squealing flocks, decorating themselves with iridescent beetle elytra.  
• Root-lurkers: eyeless serpents that detect footfalls through leaf litter and strike by prediction alone. Their skin is patterned like shifting shadow and they leave behind tunnels that steam in the warm air.

The press of life is so intense that empty space is a curiosity; nearly every surface heaves or creeps or blossoms.

6. Vegetation that holds the darkness  
Vines are thick as a person’s torso, braided around one another until they form living cables. Leaves overlap like roof tiles three and four layers deep, trapping the heat their own transpiration makes—a self-forged sauna. Flowers open only in the brief slits of dawn and dusk, spilling scents that tangle with one another: crushed mint, pepper resin, overripe melon, wet stone. Moss curtains hang from branches and wick moisture upward instead of letting it fall. At night the forest pulses with low-grade phosphorescence, enough to outline every trunk in ghostly lime.

7. Unusual rules and small oddities  
• Magnet-moss aligns its fronds toward basaltic outcrops; compass needles spin uselessly.  
• Fresh rock surfaces glow faintly for a week after fracture, storing and bleeding off mechanical stress as blue light.  
• Rain sometimes falls upward: steam vents burst onto a chilled canopy and condense into inverted showers glittering with salt.  
• Birds here do not sing; they drum hollow logs with beaks calibrated to particular frequencies, adding to the ceaseless din.

8. Turbulence as a way of being  
Nothing is ever finished. Pools fill and drain, banks cave, saplings topple under the sheer mass of epiphytes riding them. Storms assemble from sky-level perspiration in minutes, rage, and disperse. A path cleared by morning is swallowed by vines before nightfall. Even memory feels temporary; landmarks shift or are consumed, smells are replaced within hours, the soundscape rewrites itself every heartbeat.

9. Human scale (or the lack of it)  
There are no houses, no roads, no sign that permanence has ever been attempted. Anyone entering carries everything they need on their back and ties nothing down, for the forest will reclaim every peg. The only “technology” visible comes from the bone-tool primates: a cracked snail shell for a spoon, a splinter of obsidian for a knife—items so small they disappear into leaf litter as soon as dropped.

10. Standing in the middle  
Imagine crouching on a narrow bar of still-damp pumice, warm water licking at your ankles, darkness pressing from all directions. Your voice dies inches from your lips, shredded by insect static. The air tastes like iron and sap. Every breath arrives already half liquid. Something unseen thrums by overhead and the trees rattle a negative image of its passing. A moment later a cascade of broken branches rains down, followed by the blunt splash of an enormous body hitting a pool that did not exist yesterday. The concussion ripples through soil, water, and bone alike.

In this landscape, survival is improvisation, comprehension is fleeting, and stillness—when it can be found—is less a pause than the eye of an endlessly re-forming storm.
