The place is a basin the size of a small sea, only two or three hundred metres above whatever passes for ocean-level here. It is as old as anything that still remembers being land: every ridge has slumped into itself, every cliff has melted to a rounded kneeling shape, and the whole vale lies scraped flat like the floor of an emptied bowl.  

1. Terrain and materials  
• Ground texture: a quilt of fine, water-logged silt and ancient, glass-smooth cobbles. In cross-section the soil looks like layered parchment—thin laminations of ash-grey clay alternating with black mica that gleams when the twilight catches it.  
• Water: only a skin separates liquid from air. Runnels snake everywhere, some ankle-deep, some plunging into hidden sinkholes. When a wind rises, the water lifts in sheets and drifts like fog, refusing to decide whether it is liquid or aerosol. From low dolmens of stone, droplets condense perpetually and fall in slow motion, ringing like chimes against the pebbles.  
• Temperature: never above the freezing mark. Ice flowers form around every vibration, grow for minutes, then shiver apart and begin again. The cold does not bite so much as hum; everything is refrigerated, humming quietly, as though the whole landscape were an enormous condenser coil.  

2. Light and atmosphere  
• A star exists somewhere, but its contribution is more rumour than illumination. The basin lives under pewter dusk, bright enough only to sketch outlines. Colours hide; minerals show themselves instead in ultramuted pulses—violet in the mica, cyan in the pockets of trapped gas, amber where iron oxidises.  
• Now and then a flare of turquoise will ripple overhead, as though the aurora has dived to within arm’s length. These lights make local gravity flex: puddles bulge upward into anti-drops before splashing back. The air smells metallic and faintly sweet, like ozone left too long in a jar.  

3. Soundscape (loud; saturated)  
Silence never settles. The ground keeps up a soft percussion, the drip-rain of condensation striking resonant stone cups. Over that:  
• A persistent bass whirr—the rotation of something deep underground. No machinery was installed, yet something moves with the tempo of turbines, and the soil itself vibrates like the casing of a distant engine room.  
• Choruses of fauna (see below) grinding shells, clacking mandibles, expelling steam. Individual voices are indistinguishable; the aggregate is the sound of a vast factory floor run by no visible foreman.  
• Sporadic thunderclaps when patches of super-saturated fog suddenly freeze, release latent heat, and detonate.  

4. Vegetation (or the absence of it)  
There are no plants, no hint of chlorophyll. Instead, the eye catches occasional tuft-like structures—delicate needles of frozen brine that sprout, blossom, and sublimate in under an hour. Their “life cycle” is a physical reaction, not biology; still, they leave behind lattice ghosts, and travellers find themselves skirting around them as if they were real shrubs.  

5. Fauna (abundant, industrial, and entirely self-assembled)  
• Ore-Eaters: squat, six-legged hulks the size of engine blocks. Their exoskeletons are plates of magnetite hinged with flexing ice. They mine the basin by digesting stone, exhaling jets of super-cold steam that howl like sirens. When two meet, they weld into brief, sparking combat, trade minerals, then part.  
• Thread-Sirens: rope-thin serpents of woven aluminium crystals. They swim through the near-surface water layers, pulling cavitation bubbles that pop loud enough to stun smaller creatures. Their motion charges them with static; arcs of blue electricity tick across their backs.  
• Echo-Wolves: knots of sound more than matter. You hear them first—loops of your own footsteps, shifted half an octave. Then the loops condense into violet silhouettes that prowl the fog. A flash of aurora and they lose coherence, becoming mere echoes again. Yet something drips where they stood: a mix of brine and burnt smell.  
The population density is extreme; one cannot throw a stone without striking a shell, a filament, a knot of echo. Life packs every cubic metre as if afraid of empty space.  

6. Dynamics and cycles  
• Weather: miniature tempests hatch from the marsh every few hours. Ice crystals swirl upward, trade places with rain, then settle back. No drop of water is ever still for more than moments.  
• Feeding loops: Ore-Eaters expose fresh minerals; Thread-Sirens absorb the leached metals; Echo-Wolves prey on the Sirens’ electric discharges, collapsing them into energy they can “howl.”  
• Cataclysms of erosion: every few days, a whole sub-layer of clay liquefies and slides tens of metres sideways, like a conveyor belt resetting itself. Anything caught atop rides calmly; the ground itself is the mobile part.  

7. Rules of the world (reality breakdown, 3/3)  
• Causality is local, not global. A stone thrown may arc normally for the first metre, slow to a halt mid-air, then resume its trajectory ten minutes later when nobody watches.  
• Distance is negotiable. The basin looks five kilometres across, yet travellers have walked for days and found themselves still on the same shore, the horizon scrolling like an animation cel.  
• Sound acquires mass. Loud noises condense into black pebbles—“thunder-slag.” When cracked open they emit the trapped shout, then fall silent forever. Local fauna use them as eggs or currency; nobody is sure.  

8. Absence of built structures, presence of “industrial” processes  
No brick, no beam, not even a cairn—yet the whole landscape behaves like a foundry running on cold and vapor. The biome mines, refines, circulates, vents, polishes, recycles, and powers itself by turning erosion and condensation into mechanical work. Nature has stumbled into an industrial age without inventors: metabolism masquerading as machinery.  

9. How being here feels  
The air is damp enough to bead on eyelashes, cold enough to rim them in frost seconds later. Everything is too close—the fauna crowd the periphery of vision, the sound fills every gap. There is no true darkness or true silence, only the dim, the loud, and the unsteady. Your footprints fill with water, freeze, and erode before you have taken the next step.  

Some leave the basin claiming it is an after-image of another universe’s factory floor, abandoned but still humming: a place where physics is fraying like an over-used belt, and life—desperate not to vanish—has crammed itself into every loose cog and gear. Others say it is the future of marsh and industry alike, a vision of what happens when machinery outlives its makers and breeds with the landscape itself.  

Stand long enough, and the fog might coil into a pipe around you, hiss, and ask which version you prefer.
