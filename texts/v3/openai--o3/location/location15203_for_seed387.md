The place is not reached so much as overheard.  
Long before you find an opening, the roar tells you something enormous is awake in the dark. The water itself trembles with it, a metal-throated thunder that drowns every other sense and makes the bones behind your ears sing. Follow that vibration downward and you arrive—almost accidentally—inside the Pocket Yards.

TERRAIN / LAYOUT  
Imagine a single industrial block—factory floor, loading bay, switching station—sunk whole beneath a warm inland sea and then forgotten for two centuries. Erosion has done its patient work: concrete corners are rounded, steel joists feathered with salt blisters, glass panes frosted to translucence by endless hiss of silt. Yet the outline of streets, courtyards and gantry walks is still legible, like the fossil of a city compressed into one cramped chamber roughly the size of a sports arena. The roof is the original hull plate of the old drydock: warped inward, patched with riveted iron, pitted with fist-sized holes through which sheets of rain-bright water pour in thin silver walls. Nothing here is horizontal for long. Floors tilt, kink, subside into chasms, then climb again; the whole pocket feels as if it were caught mid-collapse and held there.

MATERIAL  
Everything solid is worked matter—no raw stone or soil remains. The ground is shredded rebar stitched by tarry barnacle-concrete. Rails snake through it, their wooden sleepers long dissolved; only orphan lengths of iron float in the murk like black reeds. Weld seams glow faintly green where leached copper salts have colonised the cracks. Loose flakes of rust drift constantly, so fine they behave almost like smoke, re-coating every surface in a cinnamon dust.

LIGHT & VISIBILITY  
Official illumination died decades ago. What survives is incidental: the phlegmy orange of crusted indicator bulbs still fed by buried batteries, the soft blue corona of arc-gaps that spark whenever a broken cable brushes a bulkhead, and above all the white strobe of the rain-walls whenever lightning forks through the sea overhead. Most hours you navigate by silhouette and memory; shapes are understood before they are seen.

HYDROLOGY & TEMPERATURE  
The Pocket Yards sit 40 metres below the original seabed, but only a thin skin of wreckage keeps the outside ocean from rushing all the way in. Warm brine seeps continuously through hairline seams, maintaining a temperature like bathwater. Pressure is mild—your ears fill and clear, nothing more—but currents rage. Venturi slits between collapsed wagons concentrate flow into knife-edge jets; elsewhere the water is still as a bell jar. The boundary between calm and fury moves from minute to minute, never repeating a path.

SOUND  
This is the landscape’s dominant weather. Three sources overlap until it is impossible to tease them apart:  
1. Wave impact on the hull plate overhead—a traveling drumroll that never quite ceases.  
2. The old turbine array: six-metre rotors jammed in shafts, freed by corrosion to spin whenever a surge finds them. Their blades beat irregularly, a giant’s percussion section.  
3. The harmonic cavities of the Yard itself. Wide boulevards focus sound into organ notes deep enough to rattle teeth; narrow stairwells amplify it into ultrasonic whines only mantis shrimp and headaches can fully perceive. Conversation is a luxury measured in mouth-to-ear distances.

VEGETATION / OTHER LIFE  
True plants never gained a foothold. What passes for growth is mineral: curtains of manganese oxide that dangle like kelp, rigid and bitter; quartz whiskers that sprout from crack mouths, fracturing to feed themselves new edges. Life more recognisable is sparse and opportunistic. Pale spool-eels (blind, ribbon-thin) thread their bodies through heat-leaking conduits to poach small crabs. The crabs in turn subsist on flake-rust and the gelatinous biofilm that coats every free surface—neither fully fungus nor bacterium, iridescent, smelling faintly of hot pennies.

BUILT INFRASTRUCTURE  
Despite ruin, the Yard remains legible as a city scaled to dolls:  
• A central plaza, once a materials exchange, now a bowl two stories deep where shredders dragged the floor down. Seven stainless columns still hold an overhead gantry—walkable if you trust century-old welds.  
• The Boilervanes: vertical stacks of cylindrical furnaces, each furnace open-mouthed like a terracotta jar. Once they burned coal; now they collect the trickling rain-walls and belch vapour rings that never reach a roof.  
• The Switchroom, a cube of thick glass bricks nested inside a steel cage. Inside it is paradoxically dry, air sealed since the flood. Rows of analog gauges twitch weakly, moved only by induction from currents outside. Entering requires flooding yourself with compressed air, a ritual that keeps the place almost sacred.

TECH & FUNCTION  
Most mechanisms sleep, some dream. Racks of conveyor motors still hold charge in warped armatures; thump one with a hammer and they spin for seconds, shedding flakes of starlight. A single reciprocating pump in the south pit runs in delirious starts, its valves cemented half-open, accomplishing nothing except rhythm. No governing intelligence is left—only ghost circuits completing pointless loops.

DYNAMICS & BEHAVIOUR OF THE POCKET  
The Yard is in stable ruin: nothing is repaired, nothing is allowed to quite fail. Whenever a roof plate finally snaps, the inrush of seawater finds a pressure-vent elsewhere that bursts and relieves it. Collapses become new corridors; jammed doors become dams that redirect flow. The system self-balances like a fevered but living organism. Walk its routes for a month and you will notice patterns: eddies migrate clockwise; sound levels peak at dawn; every tenth day the central plaza lies completely silent for exactly twelve breaths before the turbines catch again.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Mass equals memory. Objects too large to move keep the only reliable history—learn to read rust strata the way others read rings in wood.  
2. Vision lies; vibration tells the truth. Anything you don’t hear you probably imagined.  
3. Nothing here is new, but everything is restless. If you wish a hiding place, choose one that is already half-collapsing; the stable places are due their turn.

STANDING IN IT  
Suspend yourself mid-water, toes scuffing a rail, and the Yard feels enormous: a drowned metropolis murmuring inside its own echo. Swim twenty metres in any direction and you touch boundary wall; suddenly the metropolis is a toy trapped in a bottle. Beyond the hull plate lies open sea, richer perhaps, certainly quieter, but few make the climb. Down here, in the dim warm clangor, the city keeps its squat, stubborn pulse—and you, heartbeat syncing to its turbines, find yourself reluctant to leave.
