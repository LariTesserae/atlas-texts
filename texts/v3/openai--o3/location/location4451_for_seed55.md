The first sensation is temperature: a cold that is not vicious, just steady, the kind that invites a long-slow exhale and then stays exactly as it is for centuries. You are already under the water when you notice it, because in this place water is the only air. There was never a moment of entry; the medium is simply everywhere, a continuous world-ocean as far as distance itself can stretch.  

1. Terrain and scale  
The seabed—if the word “bed” still applies to something that covers a continent—is almost flat. Nothing here looks tectonic, nothing looks tossed or torn; the oldest forces have already finished their arguments. What remains is an endless mosaic of platforms, causeways, and shallow bowls arranged in a geometry so patient it can only be the work of hands that had nothing but time. From above (were there a vantage above) the layout would read as interlocking circles the size of small countries, each inset with concentric steps. The risers between the steps are no higher than the length of a human finger. Everything invites slow wandering, gradual descent.

2. Materials  
No natural outcrop breaks the plan. Every surface is too right-angled or too deliberately smoothed to be chance. The stone is a dark silt-basalt, quarried somewhere long forgotten and planed with simple chisels. Under fingertip it feels soft, almost waxy, the edges rounded by an erosion older than recorded light. Where blocks meet, you find swallow-tail joins, wooden pegs calcified to stone, dowels of antediluvian driftwood still locking slabs together. The craftsmanship is primitive, the ambition continental.

3. Light  
Light was never part of the design brief. Above, miles of cold water filter the sun to extinction; below, the builders provided no lamps. What illumination exists is exhaled by the vegetation itself—faint, irregular pulses of cyan along leaf margins, tiny sparks when spores launch. The place is readable, but only if you let your eyes idle, waiting for the next soft flare to outline the next dozen meters of hall or terrace. Between pulses you move by memory.

4. Vegetation  
Plants own the true architecture. Broad kelp-like fronds drape from the rims of every step, feather-moss quilts the gentle slopes, and root-networks the width of rivers stitch one platform to the next. The cold does not stunt them—these species favor it, crowding any square foot that light-pulse and mineral sediment can support. They grow not up but out, overlapping like pages of an unopened book until density approaches solidness. There are no animals to prune or crop them; nothing eats anything here. When a frond dies, it folds and sediment buries it, layer on layer, turning dead matter into new soil for the next generation. In the oldest basins, the floor is meters deeper than the original stone, cushioned entirely by compressed histories of their own leaves.  

Spore release is the nearest thing to weather. At intervals too slow to count—decades, perhaps—an entire species synchronizes, loosening opaline clouds that drift, settle, and germinate wherever geometry lets them rest. The spores are silent snow in water, the only fast thing you will ever see here.

5. Sound  
Silence here is not an absence but a texture: the hush of boundless mass. Occasionally a sheet of detached kelp skims across stone with a distant, papery rasp. In the great bowls you may hear the slow, glottal hiccup of methane finding a crack and sighing upward. Your heartbeat arrives to your ears almost before it happens in your chest.

6. Dynamics and erosion  
Almost nothing changes quickly, yet nothing is truly static. The water column drifts westward by fractions of a millimeter per second. The sweeping algae pads bend, unbend. Stone edges round a little more each century as plant-acids leach the surface. Entire terraces slide downward a millimeter a millennium under the weight of accumulated loam, locking tighter against their wooden pegs, becoming more themselves. This is erosion past its climactic moment: not the ferocity of carving but the hush that follows, when forms settle into their final outlines and begin to wait.

7. The built-yet-primitive paradox  
Every inch is constructed, but the tools that shaped it were no more than stone mauls, bone wedges, and braided-fiber hauling ropes—tools you could replicate by stepping outside your own epoch. Yet repetition made them mighty. Without metal, without draft animals, the makers levered whole mountains of basalt into perfect level. Their glory is scale, not finesse. Perhaps they began when sea level was lower, built the last course just as the first waves lapped their ankles, and then stayed as the water rose, planting gardens on their own rooftops until rooftops became the ground. Whatever intelligence drove them, it asked for endurance, not complexity, and got what it asked.

8. Rules of the world  
• Gravity feels a shade less than Earth-normal; a long glide of the fins carries you further than reason.  
• Pressure is constant: the water has had epochs to come into equilibrium with unseen vents below.  
• Life divides cleanly into photoluminescent vegetation and the stone it grows from; nothing hunts, and therefore nothing flees.  
• Nothing ever really lifts off the ground except spores; there are no bubbles, no schools of fish, no darting silhouettes. Movement belongs to root crawl and platform subsidence.

9. What it is like to stay  
Spend an hour here and you begin to count your breaths, matching them to distant creaks of timber locks deep in the stone. Spend a day and the place convinces you that motion is rude. After a week the water no longer feels cold; it feels correct. You may catch yourself listening for the next spore-pulse the way surface dwellers wait for rain. You may notice that pathways naturally spiral inward, toward bowls still deeper, darker, quieter, older—bowls so ancient the bioluminescence has migrated away from them, leaving only the absent glow of potential. At the very center, perhaps, there is a final step onto a floor that is not stone at all but the packed, black peat of a million preceding fronds: the true monument to builders who raised a continent and then ceded it, leaf by leaf, to time.

And if you leave—rare thought—you will carry with you two things: a ringing silence that lasts hours after the last cold droplet evaporates from your skin, and the half-certainty that somewhere, in one of those unlit concentric hollows, the original chisels still lie where their owners set them down, sponge-wrapped, mineral-dreaming, ready to resume should the ocean ever decide to give the world back its sky.
