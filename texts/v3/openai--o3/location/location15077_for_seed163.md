The first thing that claims the ear up here is not the wind but the water.  
It bellows.  It drubs every ledge, flumes through plant-made conduits, thunders under your boots in hidden galleries.  Only after a minute do you realise that the wind, too, is loud—yet its roar is simply woven into the larger, continuous hydraulic chord that rolls across the highland like an organ note.

TERRAIN  
You stand on a young plateau, newly heaved from older stone.  The rock is dark, shard-edged, still showing the dull sheen of minerals that have never known weather.  True soil is scarce; pockets of powdery grit sit in fractures like smoke caught in a crack.  Where the ground breaks into steps it does so reluctantly, as if undecided about its final shape—raw geology in mid-sentence.

ELEVATION & WATER  
Clouds travel socket-level with the ridges, so close that their trailing moisture condenses on anything it touches.  Every outcrop is a weeping wall; water slicks straight from the rock face as if the mountain itself were sweating.  Runnels converge into wide, roaring spillways that fall through the plateau’s soft underbelly and re-emerge miles downslope as curtains of spray.  Between cliffs, broad saddle-valleys hold marshland so saturated that stepping there is like walking on a soaked felt pad—resilient, springy, shining.

VEGETATION  
Almost everything visible is alive, but it is spaced apart, each form given its own quiet room.  
• The ground crust is a single, continuous botanical membrane—thick moss and shallow-rooted lichenoid plexus that acts as both carpet and dam, trapping a mirror of water only a finger-width below its surface.  
• Rising from this base are the pillar-trees: not boles with branches, but vertical, ropey bundles of vascular tissue three or four storeys high.  They grow in solitary clusters, their canopies flattening into umbrella dishes that harvest mist.  Water drips from their rims in steady, silvery beads, feeding the thunder.  
• Vines descend from those canopies, stitch to the moss-skin, and run in gentle arcs from pillar to pillar, drawing taut bronze-green catenaries through the air.  In bright sun the whole network blazes—leaf surfaces are pale, waxy, highly reflective—so the plateau can feel less like wilderness than like a field of enormous turned mirrors.

LIGHT & ATMOSPHERE  
The elevation lifts the land above most particulates.  Even on overcast days there is a backlighting brilliance, a milk-white solar wash that flattens shadows.  Water droplets hang in every cubic inch and serve as tiny, moving prisms; halos of colour chase your peripheral vision whenever you shift your head.

SOUND  
Waterfall plus wind equals permanence of sound.  No quiet corners.  Speech needs shouting.  The drone of distant machinery slots into the natural din so neatly that you notice it only when it stops.

BUILT & TECHNOLOGY  
Scattered across the plateau are compact industrial outposts—each little more than a steel crib of pumps, turbines, switchgear, and a cabin for technicians who rotate in by airship once a month.  
• Pumps raise seep water into elevated troughs where gravity then forces it through narrow pipes, turning turbine stacks that charge crystalline batteries.  
• Walkways of open grating run above the moss so technicians can avoid punching holes in the living carpet.  The metal is already going fern-green with sessile plants that treat alloy as just another substrate.  
• Outposts are self-contained; nobody has tried to connect them with roads.  From a distance they resemble fallen constellations—bright frames humming gently, half-swallowed by vines.

ECOLOGY & FAUNA  
Animals exist here, but almost as rumours.  You might glimpse:  
• The slate wader: a leggy, long-necked bird whose feathers shine basaltic blue.  Pairs patrol the marsh saddles in silence; when spooked they glide downslope, vanishing into cloud.  
• Glass frogs no larger than thumbnails, translucent save for tiny emerald hearts; they breed in the drip-pools cupped by pillar-tree leaves.  At night their synchronous throat clicks pass for the ticking of cooling engines.  
Predation is light, growth slow.  Life energy is drawn chiefly from water and light rather than fast metabolisms.  Organisms here seem built to last centuries, not seasons.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Water is the architect.  Every structure—rock, plant, or human machine—accommodates flow first, everything else second.  
2. Nothing hurries.  Change is measured in decades: cliffs slump grain by grain, vines thicken by millimetres per year, the industrial sites update on a maintenance cycle longer than a city lease.  
3. Silence is impossible.  Even sleep comes with percussion—rain on canopy drums, turbine basslines, restless wind harmonics.

MATERIALS & TEXTURE  
Touch the ground and your fingers return cold, slick, peppered with the faint taste of minerals.  The moss membrane tears reluctantly, its interior a lattice of almost clear filaments that spring back together.  Pillar-tree bark is leathery yet porous, exhaling faintly as it vents the moisture it cannot store.  Metal handrails collect a fuzz of tiny, bead-sized sprouts that make them feel velvet-rough rather than smooth.

SCENE—FROM DAWN TO MIDDAY  
Dawn: Thick mist blankets everything; sound arrives before shape.  The plateau feels limitless though you can see barely ten metres.  
Mid-morning: Sun punches through a rent in cloud.  Visibility opens to kilometres.  Pillar-trees throw ponderous shadows across the white moss, and every drip-strand between them glitters like wire.  
Noon: Brightness maxes out.  The surface becomes so radiant you have to squint or let goggles polarise the scene.  The roar of melt-runoff peaks; turbines in the nearest outpost kick up another gear and their low-frequency tremor travels through rock into the soles of your boots.

DYNAMIC, OR THE LONG STORY  
Year on year, the plateau inflates subtly as the living membrane lays down more tissue, thickening itself, smoothing hollows.  In another century the raw rock will be a rumour under metres of peat-like plant matter, the waterfalls will have retreated to newly bitten lips of stone, and the industrial frames—unless harvested—will stand ankle-deep in the slow, patient surf of vines, still humming, still shining, half museum, half organ stop in the endless hymn of water.
