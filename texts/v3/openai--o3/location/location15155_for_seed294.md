The place has no name that anyone remembers, so the travellers who still find it simply call it the High Flood.  

1. First sight  
You step out of a broken archway onto what you take for a cliff, only to realise it is the up-thrust rim of an immense artificial terrace. Below and above—there is little difference here—cold water hangs and moves at once: sheets the wind can fold like silk, globes that drift before bursting, cataracts that pour into mid-air with no river to feed them. In the thin, high air your breath smokes, then is taken apart by the breeze. Everything you can see was made—walls, ledges, columns, even the ground beneath the puddle at your feet—yet it has been scoured for so long that the chiselled joins have rounded into the softness of river-pebbles.

2. Terrain and materials  
The High Flood is a single, sprawling plateau of worked stone and fused glass that rises halfway to the stratosphere. Imagine a mesa the size of a small country, hollowed by courts, ducts and cisterns, its roof split by skylight canyons. Original colours—obsidian black, copper green, milk-white quartz—linger only in protected corners; most surfaces have been abraded to a uniform iron-grey polish.  

Broad troughs slope gently across the expanse. Some are dry, some brim to the lip, some fill and empty in minutes as weather tilts. Where the terrace has collapsed, knife-straight chasms drop into a night-coloured inner sea whose floor is too deep to guess. Water is everywhere, but everywhere contained by design: mill-wide basins rimmed with comb-toothed spillways, vertiginous flumes carved with wake-damping ribs, sluice-gate halls whose gates rotted away ages back.

3. Light and atmosphere  
A continual overcast of lilac cloud spreads overhead, lit faintly from a low sun that seldom breaks through. The light is mineral, diffuse, casting almost no shadow. At the plateau’s height the air is thin; move too fast and you taste iron in your lungs. The clouds leak more than they rain: slow beads that accumulate on their undersides and fall as perfect spheres, chiming when they strike stone.

4. Sound  
Most of the day the place is quiet enough that you can hear mist collect on your sleeves. Water murmurs in runnels, laps in hidden cisterns, ticks when a droplet shatters on cooled basalt. Silence lasts until something shifts—a corroded lintel surrendering its weight, a distant wall collapsing into a basin—and then the world booms like a drum, echoes fanning out through miles of galleries. Afterward the hush closes again.

5. Weather / dynamics  
Nothing here is stable for long. Turbulent cells propagate across the Flood like migrating dunes. Columns of super-cooled fog rise, spin, and fling off hail the size of coins. Whole reservoirs overflow, erode their retaining arcs in an afternoon, and carve new riverbeds through corridors that had been dry for a geological age. You may camp on bare stone at dusk and wake knee-deep in icy water under a sky clearing fast into hard stars.

6. Life (vegetation: none, fauna: plentiful)  
There is no plant, moss, nor lichen. Yet life walks, swims and glides by the thousands.

• Glass crabs: translucent, almost invisible, feeding on mineral film. They tap at seams for loosened flakes of silica.  
• Valve-swans: pale, flightless birds that paddle the upper basins, skimming bacteria from the water’s skin; their calls echo down flutes in the architecture.  
• Rift eels: ribbon-thin, reflective, living in the black trenches. When they surface they carry flickering motes of cold light—bioluminescent symbionts that lure smaller prey.  
• Gutter mice: warm-blooded, sleek, able to slow their pulses to survive sudden floods. They nest inside broken gear-housings, though the machines have not moved in epochs.  
• Echo bats: winged membranes of cartilage and ice; they drink fog, hibernate in dry acoustical pockets, and wake whenever a distant collapse booms.

Predation is brisk but oddly bloodless: bodies sink into grottoes where scavenger worms reduce them to lattices of bone and pearl-grey chitin within days.

7. On the absence of tools  
The entire plateau is engineered, yet no mechanism still functions. Bronze cog-beds stand in halls open to the sky, their teeth ground flat by eons of water carrying quartz dust. Power channels—magnetic, pneumatic, no one knows—are choked with stalactites of rust. Whatever civilisation shaped the Flood left no working artefact: only the shell remains, held together by mass and cold.

8. Erosion and age  
Every surface bears witness to endless weather. Lettering, if it ever existed, has been polished into unreadable grooves. Edges are dull, corners swollen, steps concave under the tread of centuries of water. In sheltered atria you sometimes find a single razor-sharp facet where a slab split yesterday; by tomorrow it will already have lost its shine.

9. Rules that seem to govern the place  
• Water is inexhaustible. Drain a basin and fountains open in the walls behind to replace it.  
• Gravity is constant but water occasionally ignores it, wavering in curtains that cross open air from no visible support; step through and you are merely wet, not crushed.  
• Storm cells are local and short-lived, yet they obey a larger rhythm: roughly every ten days a system the size of a county strides across the plateau, rearranging whole valleys.  
• No vegetation ever sprouts, even in sheltered mud. Seeds brought in rot untouched. The stone resists colonisation with quiet, absolute patience.

10. If you walk on  
Keep space around you. Walls shear without warning; pools flash-freeze, crack, and yaw open. Travel light, carry heat, and learn the foot-clatter of the glass crabs—when they all scuttle for crevices, a pressure shift big enough to matter is on its way. Seek the galleries three tiers below the roof: high enough to avoid the trench eels, low enough that the roof will not drop ice on you. You will be alone there only in the obvious sense; the Flood is never truly empty.

Stand at one of the western spillways at false-dawn. Under the mauve cloud-lid, the whole plateau tilts toward the buried sun. Water gathered through the night hurries westward in shining sheets, falls in a single, continent-wide curtain into the chasms, and for five heartbeats the dim light refracts through a moving wall a hundred kilometres long. The High Flood shows its bones in that brief wash of pale colour, and then pulls its veil of water back in place, ancient, turbulent, and waiting for the next small change that will, in time, change everything again.
