THE PLACE THEY CALL THE DRY GLADE

1. The first perimeter  
A low rim of handmade blocks – unfired brick and sun-baked salt – outlines the entire site in an irregular oval not much larger than a small village green. That rim is the only horizon you will ever see here; stand on it and your toes are still at the highest point. Everything inside lies perhaps half a metre lower, the ground deliberately scooped so nothing runs away, should anything ever flow. The blocks retain the crisp knife-marks of the masons who stacked them: no weather has had time to soften the edges. On some faces you can still read the pressed palms and thumbnail grooves of the builders, as though the wall forgot to finish curing.

2. Atmosphere  
Temperature hovers around the warmth of a comfortable greenhouse – 32 °C by most thermometers – yet the air is shockingly dry. You feel it first in the tongue: no moisture to taste, only a faint, back-of-throat bitterness like warm stone dust. Wind is absent; convection curls upward in slow, colourless ribbons but never gathers strength enough to rustle a leaf. Light comes from an ordinary sky overhead, though nobody remembers seeing a sun pass. The brightness is consistent, shadow-edged but never shifting, as if midday has been pinned here forever.

3. Substrate and architecture  
Nothing underneath is natural. Beneath a thin scattering of red glassy sand lies a tessellated pavement of plates the size of serving platters. Each plate is hand-thrown clay, sun-fired, laid in overlapping dragonscale that flexes slightly under weight. At joints, a tar-black resin (boiled from local plants) has been daubed to seal fissures. The builders had only chisels, adzes of sharpened bone, cords knotted for measure. They compensated for the coarseness of their tools with patience: every surface is deliberate, every curve exact to some vanished plan.

4. Vegetation (lush, yet water-less)  
Plants here carry no sap. They grow on oils, alcohols and liquid crystal, extracting the little hydrogen they need straight from the minerals of the tiles.

• Pillar-brooms: trunks a wrist thick, feathering into multitudes of copper-green needles that glitter like pressed mica. A touch leaves your fingers faintly greasy, never wet.  
• Wick-vines: braided cords that spiral around posts and each other, each strand hollow. Capillary tension draws volatile turpene vapours upward from the pores of the pavement; at the vine tips those vapours exude as sweet resin beads that harden into amber lanterns.  
• Skin-lilies: broad crimson sheets, membrane rather than leaf, stretched between rows of basalt stakes. They pulse once every few minutes, inflating gently to harvest warmth before deflating again in slow sighs.

All this growth is crammed so tightly that you can walk only in the narrow maintenance aisles the masons left, shoulder-width passages that zigzag like irrigation canals that never carried water.

5. Fauna (abundant, quiet)  
• Mote-eaters: fist-sized, six-legged arthropods with bodies of translucent horn. They park motionless on the undersides of plates, rasping infinitesimal flakes of mineral to free trapped organics. Every few hours one clicks—just once—like a pebble knocked against pottery.  
• Ribbon gliders: thin, lidless lizards that use the skin-lilies as launch ramps. They slither up, spread ribbed membranes and glide the two or three body-lengths to the next aisle, landing in absolute silence.  
• Amber gnats: clouds of hair-fine insects drawn to fresh resin. When they mass their whir becomes a distant, soft tambour, too low to quite locate. They are the loudest sound the place ever makes.

Because nothing drinks here, predation is mostly chemical: creatures metabolise oils into corrosives that etch through rivals’ shells, or exhale solvents to stun prey. Death leaves no rot, only brittle husks that crumble to dust and are swept into the seams.

6. Youth of the land  
Erosion has barely started its work. The pavilion columns (hand-packed salt crystal, turned on pole lathes) still bear the chalky tool ridges; the floor plates still shine where their potters’ nails scoured away slip. A single season of drought would ordinarily collapse such delicacy—yet the drought is permanent and so, paradoxically, nothing decays.

7. Boundaries of reality (weirdness 2.9)  
Step from one aisle to the next and you may notice the corridor repeating: the same chipped tile, the same knot in a resin seam, looping with the insistence of a skipped phonograph. Shuffle your feet and suddenly the aisle has three right angles in succession, still somehow returning you to your origin. Plants freeze mid-pulse when not observed; look away and you can catch their inflation out of the corner of your eye, the membrane forced to choose a state only when witnessed.

On rare occasions a plate lifts of its own accord, revealing a perfect clay underside with the sculptor’s fingerprint—a fingerprint that turns out to be yours when you trace it. People say the builders laid the tessellation atop time itself, not soil, and left it unfinished so chronology might leak through.

8. Processes (slow dynamics)  
The lily-skins grow by microns a month. Resin beads reach grape size in a decade before solidifying. Columns settle imperceptibly; a generation might notice a millimetre of tilt. Perceived loops in space unwind at a rate measured in human lifetimes, so a grandchild may escape a corridor a grandparent could not.

9. Soundscape  
Windless, waterless hush. Far away a resin bead drops—tick. A mote-eater chirks—tack. Between those single syllables lie minutes of silence that feel physically thick, as if the air itself has grain.

10. Unspoken laws  
• Introduce liquid water and the whole place fractures; clay explodes to steam, oils polymerise, life suffocates.  
• Metal resonates here. Even a bronze knife hums faintly, drawing geometric phantoms on the tiles. Most visitors therefore carry bone or stone.  
• Names anchor geometry. Speak what you see three times and it stays put, at least long enough to walk by.

Stand at the centre—there is always a centre, though its location wanders—and you sense the purpose: a handmade cradle for impossible biology, maintained with the simplest means, all of it so young that memory has not settled, yet old enough for logic to fray. It is warm, dense, richly alive, and utterly dry, a place that quietly rearranges itself each time you trust it to stay the same.
