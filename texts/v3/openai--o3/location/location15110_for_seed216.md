Out past the last ordinary sky, the plateau begins—a single, world-wide table of living matter raised so high that the air thins and goes a quiet, pearly gray. Nothing here is raw stone. The ground, the cliffs, the very horizons are the tissue of plants that long ago abandoned the idea of roots and chose architecture instead.

1. First impressions  
You arrive on what feels like a floor, but your feet slide slightly, as if on cured hide. It is bark, though the bark is the same gentle brown as a human palm. The surface bends under your weight by millimetres, then forgets you. Light is scarce: a firefly dusk that never quite lifts, cast by the afterglow of distant suns filtered through kilometres of overlapping leaves above. Shapes are enormous yet widely spaced, so distance is mostly emptiness—aisles between trunks, gulfs between platforms, warm wind pouring through.

2. Terrain and water  
The plateau is not level. It billows like slow surf, each swell a dome of vascular wood tens of kilometres wide. Where two domes meet, their seams form gullies that collect condensed rain. Water here is never still: it beads out of the air, seeps from pores in the “pavement,” threads itself into rills that trickle for a few kilometres, then vanish down breathing vents—spiracles that lead to hollow root-organs far below. Occasional “weeping cliffs” appear where the tissue mineralises; sheets of glassy cellulose mimic dripping limestone, forever wet, forever dissolving.

3. The built quality  
Everything carries the unmistakable decision of having been made, yet no tool marks remain. Each trunk is a column with repeating helical ridges, as if carved by a patient sculptor, but closer inspection shows growth rings inside the grooves. Bridges arch for a hundred metres with no supports except cords of living fibre that redouble themselves whenever the load increases. In some alcoves, the plants extrude flat, smooth bowls—tables? altars?—then stop, awaiting further instruction that will never come. Whoever first encouraged this growth has long since stepped away; what remains is their unfinished intention, still alive, still politely following blueprints whose purpose has been forgotten.

4. Scale and emptiness  
A single leaf can span a village. A single step can echo for minutes because the nearest trunk might be two kilometres off. Passages appear almost vacated, but look again and you will notice motion in the margins: a ripple along a vine, a glint of sap running down an interior wall. Density is low, but life is everywhere.

5. Atmosphere and sound  
Warmth seeps upward from the colossal body of the plateau itself, keeping the air comfortable even at this altitude. Breezes carry a faint sweetness, half nectar, half wet stone. Sounds travel poorly; fibre and moss soak them up. The world’s default mode is hush. You hear your pulse more than the wind. The loudest regular noise is a leisurely drip, drip, drip from condensing mists.

6. Fauna  
Creatures learned to move softly or be eaten by the structure that hosts them.  
• Glisten-gliders: transparent batlike things that ride thermals between roof and floor, sipping airborne moisture.  
• Petal cats: six-legged, leaf-pelted hunters that stalk by blending into flower clusters; their mews are ultrasonic, lost to human ears.  
• Lantern slugs: teal, fist-sized, bioluminescent; they chew dead fibre, leaving healed scars that glow for days.  
• Hulled men: sapients the height of a child, skin oyster-pale and pocked with nodules for exuding resin. Their technology never rose above stone tips and woven cord, yet they “build” by bargaining with the plants—trading sweat and song for a corridor extruded overnight.

Predator–prey cycles happen largely in slow motion. The plateau digests what lingers too long; vines drift like idle scarves until an animal brushes them, then cinch shut with terrible gentleness.

7. Water–vegetation interactions  
Because the substrate is alive, rainfall is not waited upon; it is manufactured. The upper canopy exhales water vapour by day, which cools and condenses through the night into a fine drizzle. The drizzle nourishes mosses that in turn exude a hormone prompting the canopy to exhale again. The process is self-refining, an internal weather.

8. Erosion and time  
An aeon of wind has rounded every edge, polished every filament. Where once the plateau pushed upward in violent folds, now it reclines, comfortable in its finished shape. No part looks young. Even new growth grows old in style, instantly matched to its veteran neighbours.

9. Dim light and odd optics  
Photons misbehave here. A lantern carried at eye level lights the path in front of you but leaves your own hands in shadow. Distant stems shimmer as though viewed across desert heat, yet the air is cool. Scholars (the few who reach this altitude) blame the plateau’s sap, rich in refractive oils that evaporate, hang in the air and bend light so that straight lines curve, distances double back, and some corridors appear longer from the inside than they are from the outside.

10. Reality’s frayed hem  
Move due north for a day and you will return from the east. Pitch a pebble into a ravine and you may later find it embedded inside a nearby leaf, polished, accepted. Dreams here borrow local colours—soft violet gloom, long silver drip lines—and when you wake those colours briefly linger in your peripheral vision. The place is not hostile, only creatively inattentive to causality.

11. Human (or almost-human) experience  
With only hand tools, travellers rely on what they can coax from the living matter. Peel a hygiene sheet off a trunk and it will harden into a bowl by nightfall. Pinch a vein and syrup bleeds out, fermenting by itself into a mild, smoky liquor. Yet nothing stays where you leave it; the plateau tidies, repurposes, adopts.

12. Dynamic moments  
• A mid-afternoon updraft rattles a mile of membranes high overhead, sounding like distant applause.  
• At twilight (a perpetual state here), lantern slugs rise on excreted bubbles, float, then burst, reseeding light.  
• Every ninth day, a “gravity glissade” ripples through: for ten breaths you weigh only half as much, vines slacken, gliders drop, and water arcs higher before it falls. No one knows why, only that it is regular.

13. Departing view  
Looking back from any edge you see what resembles a reef of woody coral jutting above cloud, mottled green and cinnamon. But step forward again and the reef becomes hallways, the hallways become organs, and the organs accept you into their slow, enormous breathing. The silence never feels empty; it is simply the plateau listening to itself, wondering—very leisurely—what it might try growing next.
