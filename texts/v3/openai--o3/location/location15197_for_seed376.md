The first thing you notice is how much of the world ahead is green-black and moving.  
You are standing on the rim of a high, frost-flaked plateau—2 000 m above the valleys, a place that should by every right be barren scree. Instead it is choked, layered, carpeted with living matter so dense that the rock only shows here and there like the worn bones of something buried long ago.

TERRAIN  
The ground was once a broad volcanic shelf. Millions of seasons of wind-plucking and freeze-thaw have rounded every edge; nothing is sharp, everything is softly gnarled. Shallow bowls, gullies and crevices—each a relic of slow, satisfied erosion—collect the scant water that falls. Thin lenses of ice glaze the shaded hollows, and when the sun climbs those films crack with a sound like distant china.

MOISTURE  
True rains are sporadic; instead the plateau survives on condensation. Clouds scrape the mesa walls at dawn, leaving beads of water that drip and run for an hour before flashing to vapor again. The vegetation has adapted: capillary leaves comb mist straight out of the air, and every few minutes a clear drop forms, falls, is lost inside a lattice of roots.

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
Cold rules here. Most days hover just above freezing, and nights fall ten or fifteen degrees below. Yet the light is normal—sun unfiltered by forest canopy, bright enough at midday to make the frost wink. The combination gives the place a strangely clean palette: silver rime on deep green plant-skin, coppery trunk-veins, black rock.

VEGETATION AS WORLD  
Plants are not just present; they are the substance of the plateau.

1. Floor-Weave  
   A living mat two hands thick covers nearly every surface. It is soft, springy, and faintly warm, exhaling a sour-sap smell. You can walk on it; your boots sink almost an inch.

2. Pump-Bolts  
   Rising three, sometimes four metres, these look at first like leafless trunks. Listen closely and you hear the slow cough-click of valves inside them. They are living pumps, cycling sugary antifreeze through the net of plants beneath your feet. About once an hour they vent waste heat as a plume of pale steam—nature’s own radiator towers.

3. Laminar Fans  
   Blade-leaves grow in overlapping discs that can spin when a crosswind kicks up. The movement keeps ice from glazing the leaf surfaces and, crucially, drives pollen through the air like soot from an engine stack.

In places these forms knit together, in others they hold apart, creating aisles, wells, even halls whose walls are braided vines the thickness of wrists. There is no architecture, yet the sense of enclosure is undeniable—plant-as-building by default.

SOUND  
Silence is the plateau’s keynote. Wind mutters but does not howl; the Fans spin without a whisper. Only the tiny fractures of thawing ice and the soft, metronomic drip of condensation break the hush. When a gust bends the mat you sometimes hear a low fibrillating rumble, like a distant factory heard through walls, but as soon as the air stills the sound goes out.

INDUSTRIAL WITHOUT INDUSTRY  
There are no made artifacts—no pipes laid by hands, no ruined watchtowers. But the biosphere itself behaves like an industrial complex:
•   Sap conduits mimic steam lines.  
•   Vertical Pump-Bolts fire in rhythm, their vents irising open and shut.  
•   When mist is thick, clusters of Laminar Fans lock and rotate together, forming what looks and feels like a turbine hall under an open sky.  
Stand in the middle of one of these events and you feel heat on your cheeks while frost still sparkles on every twig a metre away.

WATER IN MOTION  
After a condensation event the mat swells. Stored water pushes outward, and the plateau seems to breathe. Tiny rills thread downhill until they find a catchment bowl and pool. By late afternoon most of the water will have been reabsorbed, drawn back into stem and rhizome. The surface looks dry again but weighs twice what it did at dawn.

FAUNA  
Animal life is scarce. You might glimpse:  
•   A moth the size of a thumbnail, its scales mirror-smooth to shed ice, flitting between steam plumes.  
•   Once in a long while, a pale glider—four-limbed, membraned, silent—skating on updrafts. It lands only to lap dew, then launches again.  
No tracks, no calls, no predators. The plants have monopolised every calorie the sun grants.

DYNAMIC CHARACTER: PERPETUAL, QUIET TURBULENCE  
Although sound is muted, motion is constant. Roots contract and release. Fans cant toward any hint of breeze. Each dusk a wave passes across the mat as billions of leaf-pores close in sequence, a travelling darkening that runs from western edge to eastern cliff. At irregular intervals—perhaps every ten days—the plateau “retools”: a zone the size of a baseball field will suddenly slacken, its mat sinking thirty centimetres while new buds balloon. By next morning an entirely different arrangement occupies the space, as if a factory line had been reconfigured overnight.

RULES OF THE PLACE  
1. Warmth is earned. Step off the living substrate onto bare basalt and the cold bites instantly; step back and the mat’s residual heat seeps through your soles.  
2. Water never rests. If you want it, cup your hands under a Pump-Bolt vent or wait with a leaf-bowl during dawn fog. Pools are fleeting mirages.  
3. Stillness invites camouflage. Stand motionless for five minutes and tendrils begin to investigate your boots; ten minutes and they have forgotten you are not stone.  
4. Disturb nothing during a retooling surge. The biome’s energy spikes; any interference meets astonishing tensile force, like putting your hand into a hydraulic press.

ATMOSPHERE & SCENT  
Air at this altitude is thin, metallic on the tongue. It smells of green sap, crushed ice, and a faint sweetness—like sugar that has just begun to burn. In moonlight the exhaled steam halos every Pump-Bolt, turning the plateau into a silent forest of cold chimneys.

STANDING HERE  
You are alone on a cathedral floor of living tissue, under open sky, hemmed by distant cliffs wan in the haze. Nothing is manufactured, yet everything functions with machinic precision. Almost no animals move, yet the world refuses to sit still. Cold keeps muscle and breath tight, but every few steps a warm updraft strokes your knees.

Listen. Nothing but a drop breaking. Watch. The mat to the south ripples, and a new Fan disk unfurls, catching a breath of wind on its copper underside. The plant engines run, silent as mills in a snow globe, keeping their own counsel, ancient and entirely sufficient to themselves.

Here, life is factory, climate is fuel, and stone is scarcely more than the mold that once gave everything its first, long-forgotten shape.
