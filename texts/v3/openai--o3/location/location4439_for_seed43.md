At first you do not notice the absence of raw earth, because everything under your feet already feels so old and settled that it might as well be natural. Then you run a palm along the nearest wall and discover laid bricks—fat, hand-pressed lozenges of fired clay—each one fingerprinted, each one singing slightly when you tap it, as though someone once tuned the architecture like a drum.  

1. Terrain / spatial shape  
• Location: A shallow underground pocket, no wider than a small hamlet and never higher than a cathedral nave. You pass from chamber to chamber through narrowing throats, stairwells, and sudden rotundas; ceiling lines shift like cave vaults but every surface shows the touch of tools.  
• Floor: Interlaced terraces built of basalt cobble and sun-baked brick, stepped to follow a gentle tilt downward toward an invisible central sump. The stone has rounded with age—erosion by salt exhalations, by bare feet, by centuries of dripping water.  
• Watercourses: Only trickles at any moment, yet evidence of water everywhere. Condensate beads on vaulted ceilings and falls in rhythmic patter; skinny runnels are guided by chiseled gutters, converging into clay cisterns the size of barrels. In a rare larger hall you find a meandering ribbon of water just two fingers deep, its banks lined with wooden stakes hammered to hold the shape. The place is mostly dry between these pockets, but humidity clings to the skin.

2. Material palette  
• Brick, fired low and irregular, binds the bulk.  
• Mortar is charcoal gray, made from crushed shell and ash; decades of moisture have salted its surface white.  
• Support timbers: heavy acacia trunks blackened by resin smoke. They hold cross-beams where woven reed mats serve as platforms and nesting shelves.  
• Hardware is primitive: raw-copper pins, leather lashings, stone wedges. Nothing complex—just endless reiterations of basic joinery done many times over.  

3. Atmosphere & light  
The temperature rides warm—like evening air in late summer—and never swings much. Torch-niches sit every few paces, but the chambers borrow most of their light from a clever shaft system: vertical wells cut up to the surface where hinged mirrors of polished mica push daylight down in trembling slices. Dust and steam coil through those beams, turning the light buttery and alive.  

4. Soundscape (loud, dense)  
• Constant drip percussion, each chamber tuned by its own acoustics.  
• Wind throat: a rising and falling hiss caused by the stack-effect of warm interior air and cooler surface vents; every doorway thrums like a flute mouth.  
• Animal chorus: click of beetles, throat-buzz of cave swifts, the staccato croak of blind toads that lurk in cisterns. At feeding times an avalanche of bat wings drowns every other sound.  
• Human manufacture: stone mallets striking stone, rope creaking over clay pulleys, the slap of wet clay against molds. Nothing is automated, but nothing ever pauses either.  

5. Ecology (abundant fauna, sparse vegetation)  
Vegetation survives only where water lingers: curtains of pale moss on shaded brick, hair-thin roots sneaking through mortar seams, a few colorless ferns clinging to cistern rims. Fauna, by contrast, is everywhere.  
• Glimmerflits: tiny moth-like insects whose scales reflect torchlight; they pollinate the moss sporophytes.  
• Pipe toads: fist-sized, sightless, drawn to vibration. Their skin exudes a resin locals harvest to seal joints.  
• Dust swifts: birds that never leave the warren; their wings have shortened into scythe shapes for tight corridors.  
• Copper eels: finger-slim fish living in the main trench, on which both toads and humans prey.  
Predator-prey cycles are fast here; carcasses disappear beneath beetle tides in a night, and the nutrients run back into moss and mushroom.  

6. Inhabitants & practice (primitive tech in a total-built world)  
A lineage of craftspeople (the Brickborne) maintains the whole. Their rules:  
• Nothing raw touches skin; all surfaces must be amended or tiled lest “wild stone” steal your heat.  
• Tools may be no harder than copper or obsidian—an ancestral pact after an iron disaster on the surface.  
• All water is owed first to the nursery cisterns where eel fry are raised; only after the fry are fed may people drink.  
• To build is to bless: every new brick is kissed, a fragment of reed pressed into its face, then set while a chorus repeats a two-note chant that matches the drip tempo.  

7. Dynamics & cycles  
Morning: shafts admit light, animals stir, workers open vent-flaps to draw fresh air.  
Midday: humidity peaks; condensation rains hardest, a hundred little indoor showers. Children race with gourds to catch drops.  
Evening: torches strike; bats surge; food preparation begins—mostly eel broth thickened with crushed toad eggs.  
Night: vents close, drafts quiet, but forging fires (low bellows, charcoal) thump on. Even asleep, the place grumbles.  

8. Subtle weirdness  
Uneasy relationship with time: the Brickborne claim these halls can “heal,” that if a wall is broken and the shards stacked nearby, mortar slowly weeps from within the bricks to glue them again. Outsiders see only salt leaching and coincidence, yet the repairs—though crude—do appear without hands. Some say the endless chanting leaves resonance in the clay, like a memory the structure uses to remember its earlier shape.  

9. What it feels like to stand here  
Warm breath on your cheeks, grit under your nails, a low drum in your bones from the wind tunnels. You taste metal on the air and sense a ceiling overhead though it is lost in shadow. Everything is close, layered, incessant; nothing is still long enough to feel dead, yet nothing is new either. It is a place that has worn its artificiality so long that manufacture has become geology—an underground, hand-made savanna whose creatures roost in rafters instead of trees and whose rain falls from brick-laid skies.
