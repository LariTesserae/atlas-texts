The place is not a cavern so much as a single, sunken chamber the size of a modest cathedral—an architectural pocket hollowed into young volcanic glass only a few decades old. You descend to it by a steep elevator cage that rattles once, then stills; after that first shiver the environment settles into an almost reverent hush.

Terrain and materials
• Floor: The ground is a cracked, scalloped sheet of obsidian, sharp-edged where it meets the walls, as if the earth’s crust broke yesterday and never bothered to smooth itself.  
• Walls: Vertical planes of basalt laced with pale seams of anorthite; in those seams cling narrow ledges of greenish slime-lichen—the only visible life, starved and stringy, yet stubborn.  
• Overhead: The ceiling vaults twenty meters above you, a black bowl crusted with bronze condensers and copper drip-pans. From each pan, thin streams of water fall, forming hot, silver threads that strike the floor, hiss, and skitter away into grates.

Light and atmosphere
It is always dusk here. Illumination comes from sodium bulbs bolted to iron joists—every third bulb flickers, every fourth is dead. Their amber light never reaches the corners; anything more would cook the air. The temperature hovers just below scalding, pumped by geothermal conduits that run like arteries behind the walls. The air smells of wet metal and sulfur; breathe too deeply and you taste salt.

Water
Rain is manufactured, not meteorological: condensers extract vapor from the hot breath of the vents. Every few minutes a drip becomes a brief downpour over one quadrant of the floor, pooling in shallow basins before vanishing down a maze of pipes. The system is uneven—some sectors stay dusty, others gleam with standing warmth. Steam halos drift lazily toward the ceiling and are swallowed by unseen fans.

Built environment
Industrial density defines the space. Piers, catwalks, switchback stairways, and mesh bridges thread the chamber so tightly that any two walkways are almost within touching distance. Nothing is decorative; everything has a gauge, a valve, an access panel. The layout feels improvised—each new pipe forced the engineers to squeeze another path across it, layering the place like a three-dimensional circuit board.

Yet the scale remains intimate. You could pace the chamber’s longest axis in three hundred steps. From any spot you can touch a support girder with your palm if you climb or stoop just a little.

Sound
Mostly silence: the distant churn of pumps, the soft clack of an automatic breaker resetting, the hiss of a pressure line venting for two lazy seconds, then shutting off. Footsteps echo forever because nothing alive here muffles them.

Fauna
There are none. No bats, no insects, no cavefish in the catch basins. Only biofilms: slick emerald sheets that glow faintly where the light is weakest, feeding on minerals and condensation. They pulse microscopically, giving the impression that the walls themselves breathe.

Vegetation
Sparse is generous. Besides the lichen ladders and the biofilm mats, a single vine-like plant—pale, rootless—dangles from a crack near the ceiling, nourished entirely by drips. Its leaves are translucent and curl at the edges as though perpetually surprised to exist.

Dynamics
Despite the near-silence, nothing here is static. The water cycle runs on short, frantic loops. Valves tick open, sluice, slam shut. Thermal regulators swell, contract. Some days, a fresh fracture appears in the glassy floor, still warm to the touch, proof that the chamber’s parent volcano is restless. Engineers (they must exist; you rarely see them) respond by bolting another brace across the wound, threading another pipe to draw out more heat.

Rules of the world
1. No combustion—sparks are smothered instantly; methane pockets lurk.  
2. No loose fabric—vents can pull a scarf into gears faster than thought.  
3. Never step where lichen thrives—those ledges crumble under a full human weight.  
4. Everything drips; therefore, everything conducts. Touch metal only with gloves.  
5. When the ceiling goes quiet, leave: that means the condensers have stalled and the air will grow unlivable in minutes.

Weirdness
The unusual reveals itself at the edges. Occasionally a bulb fails and, before it is replaced, the biofilm directly under it brightens—self-adjusting phosphorescence keyed to human lux levels, as though the microbes prefer to keep faith with the engineering. In some dark recesses, abandoned instruments continue to tick, even disconnected, as if the chamber’s heartbeat has infected their mechanisms.

Standing in the middle walkway, you’re aware of a contradictory impression: a place small enough to memorize, yet dense beyond immediate comprehension; hot enough to boil thought, yet ultimately governed by water; devoid of animals, yet palpably alive. It is an engine-chapel, a greenhouse for minerals, a prologue to a city that might someday grow around it—if the dripping, the heat, and the silence don’t erode it back into raw, molten night first.
