You have to lean in close to find the place at all.

A thumb-sized fissure opens in the summit ridge of a newborn plateau—an obsidian overfold six kilometres above whatever counts as sea-level here. Inside that pinhole the world pinches inward, expanding only enough to accommodate itself: a pocket chamber roughly the volume of a freight elevator. The rock around it is still annealing from its birth-pulse; facets of glass reflect nothing because there is almost no light, only the dull ember-heat that seeps through hairline cracks from a magma thread deeper down. Temperature hovers around the point at which copper softens. The air is thin, scorched, and absolutely dry; any moisture that drifts in from the larger world flash-evaporates with a hiss like torn paper.

Terrain and materials  
The floor is not flat but scalloped, as though fingers had raked half-molten basalt and then let it freeze in place. Freshly exsolved crystals—feldspar blades, olivine beads, needles of iron chromite—jut from every surface, giving the whole pocket a raw, unfiled feel. No agent of wind or water has yet arrived to round these edges; erosion is still an unspoken idea here. Occasionally a pane of volcanic glass sloughs off a wall with the crack of a snapped ruler, skitters a few centimetres, and settles. That qualifies as geological drama.

Atmosphere  
The cavity trembles with heat shimmer yet remains dim. Illumination comes in stutters: tiny blue sparks leap when certain minerals are crushed under load, and for half a heartbeat the darkness fills with jagged constellations. Between flashes the chamber seems blind and claustrophobic, but heat alone conducts a sort of vision; you can feel where things are by the wreaths of radiance they give off.

Sound  
Volume, not light, is the sense that dominates. A continuous, metallic droning—part insect stridulation, part piston thrash—presses outward from every crevice. The noise is so dense it feels viscous; opening your mouth invites the vibration to roost on your tongue. Individual notes occasionally resolve: a ratchet click, the deep-throated cough of something venting super-heated gas, the rhythmic grind of teeth or gears or both. Yet the overall impression is steady, almost ritualistic—a factory floor running at polite, unhurried capacity.

Fauna and the strange “industry” they embody  
No plants, no slime, no recognizable fungus. Instead the chamber hosts a crush of mineral fauna—organisms whose bodies read as half biological, half lathe. They crowd every reachable surface, overlapping like scales until there is, effectively, no free space left. Because the pocket never grows much larger, life here adapts by folding itself tighter and moving deliberately, respecting unseen traffic lanes.

• Glass-gnawers: centipede shapes with jaws of micro-serrated quartz. They rasp freshly chilled obsidian, swallowing shards that internal furnaces re-flow into new joint-plates. The rasping is one thread of the omnipresent drone.

• Slag-heavers: tortoise-sized mounds of magnetite plates. They ingest melted rock drips and exhale them as pellets of near-pure metal, stacking these ingots by chance into neat, shining piles. Nothing in the chamber counts as a “built structure,” yet these accidental bar-graphs of chrome and nickel betray an undeniable industrial competence.

• Heat-leeches: eel ribbons that weave through cracks, absorbing excess thermal energy and venting it in pulsed shockwaves. Their venting is the pocket’s percussion section, a slow thump every few seconds that quivers the ground.

• Spark-midges: motes of conductive filament that flicker across pressure points, triggering the piezoelectric flashes that substitute for daylight. They pollinate nothing; the sparks are simply a by-product of their need to discharge.

Metabolism = machinery  
Every organism here runs on direct mineral oxidation. Stomachs are furnaces, hearts are compressors, tendons are braided metal whiskers. The overall effect is unmistakably industrial though nobody has consciously engineered it—life has landed on the same principles by coincidence and deep time. Raw geology provides the parts list, heat supplies the power, and immensely slow generational selection fine-tunes the blueprints.

Density and motion  
Because the pocket is saturated, nothing wastes effort. Movement occurs along patient, rehearsed circuits. A glass-gnawer may take a full day to cross one palm’s width, waiting for a slag-heaver to finish laying down a fresh metal bar so it can use the bar as bridge. Even the shockwave hearts of heat-leeches beat in a cycle measured in minutes, not seconds. Observed over hours, the chamber seems static; sped up, it would resemble a well-ordered assembly line.

Rules of the world  
1. Nothing leaves; nothing unnecessary enters. The fissure throat is too narrow for most residents, and cooling buries the opening a fraction more each decade.  
2. Water—in any phase—counts as toxin. A stray droplet would explosively convert to steam, snuffing life that never evolved pressure tolerance.  
3. Light is luxury; sound is currency. Many species court or warn by modulating the metallic chorus, carving harmonic niches they occupy like territorial plots.  
4. Growth is negotiated, not seized. Add one organism too many and the thermal budget collapses; the pocket would cool, crystallization would stall, and the entire biome would starve.

Time and destiny  
Change, when it comes, inches forward. Over centuries the fauna will erode their own walls, expanding the chamber just enough to maintain density. Perhaps millennia from now they’ll intersect a broader lava tube, and the pocket will suddenly be too large—sound will dissipate, heat will bleed away, and this hot, loud, packed little universe will go silent. For now, it hums on the knife-edge between forge and womb, a secret industrial heartbeat sealed inside the mountain’s glass-black skin.
