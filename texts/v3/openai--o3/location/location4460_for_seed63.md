The place is no larger than a town square—two hundred paces from wall to wall—yet it feels as though an entire wilderness has been folded into it.

1. Terrain and materials  
• The floor is the summit-skin of an ancient ridge, its original contours polished flat by a million hot winds. A crust of black glass and salt-white gypsum lies cracked like drying paint. Between the cracks glows a dull cinnabar light: magma is near, only a hand-span below in places, and its breath turns the air into a wavering mirage.  
• Four sheer walls encircle the space, climbing another twenty meters before they merge with a roof of fused basalt. The whole chamber is a natural cupola exposed long ago when part of the mountain sheared away. It is, in effect, a cathedral of volcanic bone perched high in thinning atmosphere.  
• No water beads, pools, or even ghosts this place. When mist from the lower cloud-belts drifts up the valley, it arrives as dry snow of ammonium salts that hiss to vapor on the hot floor and vanish.

2. Light and atmosphere  
True darkness rules here; no star or moon reaches over the surrounding peaks. Vision comes from the under-glow of the magma cracks and from living things themselves. Many animals carry panes of bioluminescent crystal on their backs—amber shields that gather heat by day outside and release soft crimson photons in here. The air tastes metallic, carries the faint scent of struck flint, and trembles with updrafts that comb dust from every crevice.

3. The crowding of life  
There is not a hand-span of open ground. Life piles on life, layer over layer, like reef on reef:  
• Low to the surface crawl rugose mats of chemotrophic film—neither plant nor fungus but something older, weaving lattices of silica to anchor itself.  
• On the films graze thumb-sized “slag-lice,” translucent except for the iron filings swirling in their guts. They never stop feeding; the rasp of their mouthparts is a constant, sand-fine whisper.  
• Among them rise wiry towers of shed slag-lice armor, welded by heat into spires; larger creatures use these as lookout posts.  
• Elbowing for space are glass crabs, jointed like origami, each shell a prism stealing warmth from the magma leaks. Their claws click but make no sound in air this thin.  
• At eye level swarm the mantle kites, fist-sized cephalopods kept aloft by gas sacs they inflate from the vents. They bump against one another in near silence, more numerous than dust motes in a sunbeam.  
• Over everything, draped like living drapery, hang ribbon serpents. They cling to the ceiling rock, bodies so tightly coiled that their coils form an unbroken black that turns the lofty dome into a single slow-throbbing muscle. When one hunts, a patch of the “ceiling” simply detaches and flows downward.

4. Soundscape  
Despite the press of bodies, it is almost quiet. The chamber breathes: a heart-slow boom as deeper pockets of gas find a new vent; a collective exhale when thousands of mantle kites shift at once; the near-inaudible scrape of a serpent’s scale across basalt. Silence here is not the absence of sound but the presence of too many sounds so small they cancel one another out.

5. Activity cycles  
Every ninety-three breaths, a pressure pulse from the mountain’s core lances upward. Magma rises a finger higher; cracks widen; fresh heat gushes. Instantly the kites dive to sip the venting gases, the slag-lice erupt into brief—almost frantic—molts, and the ribbon serpents release streams of glassy waste that rain like spun sugar. Predators time their strikes to these pulses; misses mean starvation before the next one.

6. Scattered structures  
Half-melted arches of once-worked basalt stand in the corners, so eroded they could be mistaken for natural columns were it not for regular ridges carved along their flanks. No artifact remains whole: a fragment of a step, a wedge of obsidian bearing indecipherable dots, the suggestion of a basin. Whatever hand built here quit long ago, but the life forms recruit the ruins—kites roost in the hollows, glass crabs strip trace metals from the carvings.

7. Rules of the world  
• Water does not persist above three breaths; all physiology is anhydrous, based on liquid sulfur and amorphous silica.  
• Heat, not sunlight, is the primary currency—organisms detect temperature gradients the way others perceive color.  
• Nothing may grow larger than space allows; excess mass is shed, recycled, and re-knitted by the chemotroph film. This keeps the density high but self-limiting.  
• Light is a communal resource: bioluminescence flares only when heat surpluses occur, then dims so others may shine.  
• The mountain remembers stresses; when too much biomass collects in one region, the rock vents there, enforcing balance through fire.

8. If you stay  
Stand still for long, and slag-lice will mistake you for cooled basalt and begin their rasping harvest; remain overnight and glass crabs will hook shards around your limbs to farm the sweat-salts. Yet movement draws the ribbon serpents. The only safe rhythm is to move slowly, stop briefly, and keep moving again—a pilgrimage pace woven between two forms of death. Should you find a still-warm arch and press your back to its lee, the living ceiling may overlook you, and for a handful of breaths you may witness the chamber at peace: darkness layered on darkness, hot as a kiln, silent as a library, more crowded than a market, and as old as the mountain’s first cooling.
