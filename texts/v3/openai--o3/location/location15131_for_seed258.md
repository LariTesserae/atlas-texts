The Plateau of Fabricated Green  

1. Arrival  
You come up through cloud instead of across soil. The up-drafts smell of warm rain caught in pollen, and when the vapour thins you discover that the “ground” under your feet is not rock at all but a braided, living mat of stems the width of rivers. This is the mean level of the plateau, four thousand metres above the surrounding world. There is no native stone here; every apparent ledge, span and plaza is a growth, patiently woven by the resident flora over uncounted cycles until a continent of architecture supplanted geology.  

2. Terrain  
Imagine a dish-shaped highland fifty kilometres wide and still rising out of the planet’s crust. From afar it looks unfinished—raw, green musculature pulled tight across a brand-new skeleton. Great vaulted “ribs” of lignin run radial from a central hub, leaving kilometres of open space between them. In those gaps the sky pours straight through, so horizon and abyss trade places whenever the light changes. The rim is crenellated with horn-shaped bastions—actually the swollen root-crowns of the plateau trees—out of which stilt-trunks descend, probing the air for still more foothold.  

Because erosion has scarcely begun its work, edges are crisp. There are no gentle slopes, only sudden steps: wall to chasm, mat to void, terrace to cloud. Water has yet to cut canyons, but it already seeps everywhere. The living floor perspires; clear sheets bead on glossy fibres, gather into braided gutters, then dive straight off into nothing with the roar of unbroken waterfalls.  

3. Materials and colour  
The dominant substance is a semi-translucent cellulose shot through with spiral capillaries of copper-green chlorophyll. Sunlight travels centimetres inside before diffusing, so the ground itself glows softly, like sunlit jade held to the eye. Where roots fatten to structural columns the surface hardens into something as tough as horn. Occasionally a root breaks under its own tension, and the fracture exposes wet, glassy threads that flash like crystal in the sun and then seal themselves minutes later.  

Sap does the work stone once did. It sets into lustrous panes to roof open courtyards or plates itself into scalloped eaves over dripping walkways. If you press your ear to a freshly glazed surface you hear a slow ticking—stomatal valves snapping shut and opening again in perfect, dreamless rhythm.  

4. Atmosphere  
Warmth pools here, trapped by the sheer mass of breathing tissue. Daytime air is thick, sweet and heavy; anything metallic left too long develops a patina of verdigris born not of oxidation but of enzymatic kiss. At dusk, the plateau exhales: a single collective sigh that lifts curtains of mist off every surface and sends them drifting upward, where they condense into fresh cloud only a few hundred metres overhead. The place manufactures its own weather and then slips back into windless stillness, a climate perpetually on the brink of rainfall yet rarely dropping more than a light, consoling drizzle.  

5. Sound  
Silence never quite lands. Beneath you the vascular highways hum with water pushed by osmotic pistons. The pitch is bass-low, a subaural engine felt in ribs more than heard. Layered over that is the continuous crackle of drip-lines: a hundred thousand threads of water striking leaf, span, pond, void. When gusts thread the open bays between ribs they strike the hollow trunks and the whole plateau sings—a chord changes by kilometres, a cathedral organ whose pipes sprout buds. During those crescendos the only other audible note is the far, unwavering thunder of the rim-falls, curtains of water diving straight to valley floor far below, never touching rock on the way.  

6. Ecology  
Fauna exist, but as punctuation. You might spot a rust-red membrane drifting from ridge to ridge: a glider-mammal whose limbs have perfected the art of not landing. In stiller corners translucent insects perch like living stained glass, siphoning sugars seeping through hairline cracks. Predators show up rarely—a pale, kite-shaped thing that hunts by silence breaks, swooping whenever the vascular hum stutters. Every animal here is adapted to distance and emptiness; the plateau’s real density lies in its single super-organism, a plant that forgot where its trunk ended and began writing itself across the sky.  

Decomposition is almost zero. When parts die, adjacent tissue repurposes them, sealing them under fresh growth so neatly that death never smells. Nothing rots; everything is re-threaded. Stable equilibrium reigns: nutrients circle, water cycles, form persists.  

7. Culture (if any)  
There are no tools, no carved lintels or shaped iron. Yet every surface bears the unmistakable deliberation of design. Leaves coil into spiral staircases that keep pitch perfect; root-tendons arrange themselves into long arched bridges whose keystones are living nodules that throb with colour when stepped upon, as though acknowledging weight. If there was an architect, it was chlorophyll and capillary pressure guided by some distributed intention pure biology can’t fully explain.  

8. Rules of the place  
• Growth never pauses, but it never wanders. The structure refines itself, thickening what already exists rather than exploring new frontiers.  
• Gravity is obeyed, yet challenged: mass extends horizontally farther than any mineral arch could support, held aloft by turgor and tensile strength alone.  
• Fire cannot live here. Moisture and living flesh smother sparks in seconds.  
• Time feels circular. A leaf forms, hardens into buttress, then pelts away in silent flakes that seed airborne nurseries, which drift only to land on the same plateau and graft themselves back into the lattice. Years pass, nothing fundamentally changes. Even the embryonic ravines left by water never deepen beyond a few centimetres; fresh growth heals them nightly.  

9. Standing alone a while  
Stay long enough and you begin to sense vast but patient conversation. Tremors you first took for wind resolve into coded pulses: root to rib, rib to crown, crown to rim and back again—perhaps instructions for how best to balance the newest load of cloud-rain, perhaps mere idle dreaming. No individual tree owns the plateau; the plateau is the individual.  

You can walk for hours and meet no wall. Space is generous, almost polite, assembling its vaulted chambers just far enough apart that you always glimpse the next horizon through apertures of luminous green. Loud though it is, the noise never crowds you. You stand in hollows between heartbeats of a single, colossal body and realise the emptiness was planned: a buffer so the music of water and wind has room to unfurl without knotting into chaos.  

10. Departure  
Eventually cloud thickens again, the rim-falls vanish behind a white curtain, and the organ-pipes hush as wind lulls. You step toward the edge—if “edge” can be named where living floor ends and blank sky begins. Down is immeasurable. Up is birthplace of more mist. Behind you, the plateau continues its unhurried knitting, secret and satisfied, a monumental artifact that required no hand but still speaks the grammar of architecture. Nothing here will be different tomorrow, or next decade, or the century after, yet every moment the green is infinitesimally revised, perfecting a form already perfect.  

The last thing you hear before cloud closes is water, laughing as it falls forever.
