The Hollow Grove

Terrain and shape  
Seen from above it would be easy to miss: a shallow oval no wider than a village green, sunken perhaps three meters below the surrounding plains of inert clay. The depression is almost perfectly level, a basin that has not changed its outline for epochs; water never stays long enough to carve channels and wind is too tired to bother with something already worn smooth. At its rim the ground folds into itself like a turned hem, revealing a thin seam of ancient seaside limestone—final proof that this place finished evolving long before vertebrates thought of spines.

Floor and materials  
Step inside and the clay gives way to plant. Not plants in the ordinary sense—there is no soil, no litter, no separate stems or competing shrubs. Instead a single organism, or perhaps a single interknit guild, has sheet-molded itself into the shape of a lacquered floor. It is pale green–white, semi-translucent. Underfoot it is resilient, flexing half a finger-width with each step; capillaries inside flash silver when the sun strikes them. Peering through the floor you glimpse faint plumbing—living xylem conduits as wide as wrists that shuttle sap in stop-start pulses. They reach outward toward the encircling walls.

Walls and architecture  
Those walls are roots turned vertical. They rise like the columns of an unfinished cathedral: twelve buttresses, evenly spaced, curving gently toward one another but never touching overhead. Their interior faces are ridged for strength and stippled with lenticels that exhale a cool, faintly resinous breath. Light entering from the open top pools against their inner bark, staining everything the color of late honey.

Atmosphere  
The air is cool—always a few degrees lower than the open plain, because the Grove exhales the previous evening’s stored chill. Moisture hangs but never precipitates into full rain; instead it condenses in predictable pulses. At roughly twenty-minute intervals a shiver passes through the canopy. Microscopic fissures open, release vapor, then reseal. Each pulse ends with a soft hiss, followed by a patter of droplets that fall straight down, never wind-blown, striking the flexible floor like handfuls of pebbles on drumskin. The droplets vanish almost immediately into the plant material, leaving only darker freckles as proof they were ever liquid.

Light  
Midday sunlight enters unobstructed from above—normal in intensity, but rendered theatrical by the architecture. The open roof is a rough oval that frames a square of sky; dust and mist make visible shafts thick enough to lean on. When clouds pass, the Grove dims but never darkens completely, as the floor itself phosphoresces a gentle mint green for several minutes after direct light is lost.

Soundscape—why it is loud  
Silence does not belong here. Deep inside each buttress a cambial engine beats: a three-stroke contraction cycle that hauls dilute sap up and pushes concentrated sap down. The rhythm sits in the low bass register, 40–60 Hz—almost felt more than heard—but the rounded cavity of the basin amplifies it into a rolling thunder that never varies in pitch or tempo. Overlaying that are the ancillary noises of life:  
• Sap-scarab swarms orbit the columns, their wing-cases ticking like riveters as they harvest sugary exudate.  
• Somewhere inside the walls a continuous grinding—roots rasping grains of limestone for calcium.  
• Every so often the hollow floor booms sonorously, not from footsteps but from internal pressure equalizing through unseen vents.

Water regime  
True rain visits only a few times a season. The Grove anticipates it. Moments after the first drops, the floor splits along hairline seams and bows downward, becoming a catchment bowl. Stored rain is shunted into central cysts and held indefinitely; afterward the floor resurfaces, grain by grain, until it regains perfect flatness. Between storms, all moisture is self-generated: vapor drawn nightly from the cooler air just above the plain, condensed inside the columns, and released in the regular hissing pulses.

Vegetation: total, yet sparse in volume  
Everything here is plant, but most of the space is hollow. Ninety percent of the grove’s volume is breathable air reverberating with pump-beats. The living mass forms only shell and floor, maybe a meter thick at its fattest. It is a paradox of abundance without crowding: an entire forest compressed into single, giant, all-inclusive tissue so that emptiness can persist.

Fauna  
• Sap-scarabs: beetles the size of thumbs, metallic navy, wings whirring at machinic pitches.  
• Choir crickets: perch along lenticel ridges, synchronize chirps to harmonize with the bass pumps, creating chords that seem intentional.  
• Blind drift-eels: slender amphibians that swim inside the plant’s fluid conduits; they surface through drinking pores at night to feed on dew-gnats.  
• One deliberate slow beast: a velvet sloth-analogue whose bifurcated tongue plugs into the floor’s capillaries like a fueling hose; it moves maybe twice per day, leaving square impressions that rebound once the weight departs.

Technology level?  
No forged metal stands here, yet the entire organism behaves like an antiquated factory. Valves, pumps, condensers, drip-trays—all executed in lignin and cellulose. If someone had invented Industry with only biochemistry at hand, it would look like this. The result is an industrial soundscape and throughput without a single artifact out of place. Nothing is built; everything is grown to specification and then remains, unchanged, doing its one perfected task forever.

Erosion, age, and stability  
Geologically the basin is finished; it has no remaining quarrel with time. The plant guild has reached its terminal design and holds it with obsessive fidelity. Seeds drop but never sprout—any intrusion is enzymatically dissolved into nutrient slurry. The only processes still running are maintenance cycles: minute patch repairs, sap recalibration, night-time chill harvesting. To an external observer the Grove may seem vibrantly alive, but on a deeper scale it is static art, a machine that has found its final state and now merely repeats.

Rules of the world  
1. Equilibrium is law. Any attempt to alter volume, temperature, or composition triggers compensatory action—valves open, pressures shift, fauna relocate—to produce exactly the prior conditions.  
2. Nothing rots inside; microbial decomposers are excluded by chemical barriers. Dead matter is denatured, reabsorbed, the mass conserved.  
3. Sound is privilege: outside noises enter but are damped; internal sounds are amplified, woven, made part of the pulse-score.  
4. Visitors who bring metal tools find them quietly corroded overnight into inert powders, then winnowed into seams between floor plates. The Grove accepts nothing it did not specify.

Standing in the center  
Linger a moment. Wind from the open world whispers across the rim but weakens immediately, as though deciding the interior climate is somebody else’s jurisdiction. The bass throb underfoot remains perfectly regular. A column exhales; mist ribbons up, catches sunlight, breaks into falling beads that tap your shoulders and vanish. Every sense tells you the same quiet contradiction: despite the volume of space you are surrounded, enfolded, enclosed by green intention. It has been this way since long before the nearest mountain finished eroding, and—barring calamity—it will be exactly the same when the plains outside have turned to dust and blown away.
