THE BASIN OF THIRTEEN THROATS

1. First sight
You crest the final knife-edge ridge and the world drops away into a gigantic stone bowl three valleys wide. The air is so clear it looks polished; sunlight pours in unimpeded, dazzling against pale rock. At the rim you already feel the sound—pressure on your lungs, a faint trembling in your bones—yet you can still hear your own boots crunch powder-dry grit. Then you step over the lip, and the place roars awake.

2. Terrain
Elevation: The basin’s floor lies just above the normal cloud roof, roughly five vertical kilometres up. Eye-level horizons curve subtly, hinting how thin the planet’s skin is here.

Shape: Erosion has planed the caldera walls into thirteen deep alcoves. Each alcove ends in a tall slit—narrow canyons that pierce the outer ridge and open to the western jet-streams. The prevailing wind pours through those slits, hits the concave inner walls, and detonates into sound. Locals call them Throats; you can see why.

Rock: Frost-shattered schist littered with long, balanced slabs. A veneer of glittering mica makes the ground look perpetually dusted in salt.

Soil: Where water pools, the “soil” is a black paste of decayed wind-blown algae, a centimetre thick at most, sitting atop ice-cemented gravel. Elsewhere there simply is no dirt—only naked stone and wind-polished pebbles.

3. Water
Precipitation is stingy: short sun-heated updrafts lift flecks of moist air that condense by afternoon into needle-thin sleet, then evaporate before midnight. Melt from an old glacier fragment hidden under talus provides the basin’s only persistent trickle. It divides and subdivides into rivulets that vanish in porous rock, reappearing hours later as fringe pools: mirror-flat pans no wider than a shield, colonised by greening films and iridescent insect larvae.

4. Light & sky
Daylight is ferociously bright; the high elevation leaves little atmosphere to diffuse the sun. Shadows are knife-edged, almost black. The sky feels overlarge, a nearly violet dome in day, crowded by cold, swollen stars at night. Weather changes little—clouds cruise far below like slow grey rivers.

5. Sound
Wind strikes the thirteen slits, turning each into a pipe organ. Notes overlap and beat against one another until the air inside the caldera becomes an ocean of vibration. The roar never dips below the volume of an angry waterfall; sometimes a single Throat booms in phase with its neighbours and the whole basin seems to shout one coherent chord that rattles teeth. Conversations are impossible; communication happens by gesture, drum, or vibrating staff pressed to another’s shoulder.

6. Climate & stability
Temperature hovers near 8 °C by day, falling just below freezing before dawn. It has done so, according to lichen rings, for millennia. Nothing dramatic happens here—no avalanches, no sudden thaws. Even the wind’s direction is a constant, locked by broad-scale atmospheric cells. Visitors often remark that time itself feels stalled, as if the valley were an ancient phonograph stuck in a single thundering groove.

7. Life

Vegetation (sparse):
• Pillow moss clings to sun-sheltered vertical cracks, forming thumb-sized clumps the colour of oxidised copper.
• Hanging on shaded overhangs are gray fungal sheets, dry as parchment until fog wets them.
• In pool rims grow joint-grass reeds only a handspan tall, their stems hollow so they resonate sympathetically with the basin’s roar, scattering their seeds in sound-driven pulses.

Fauna (abundant in numbers, not in mass):
• Whisper-hares: fist-sized lagomorphs with vestigial external ears, fur the exact colour of mica dust. They navigate by ground vibration, drumming hind feet to map echoes.
• Basin skimmers: dragonfly relatives whose wing membranes are riddled with lens-like spots that scatter the brutal light and keep them cool. They hunt in clouds over every puddle.
• Lamellorn: goat-sized ungulates sporting layered bony crests that act like acoustic fins; they face the wind and let the resonance purge parasites from their fur.
• Choir swifts: soot-black birds nesting in the Throats themselves. Their bones are thickened; the vibration that would liquefy other creatures’ organs only warms them. At dawn they wheel above the basin, tracing spirals that momentarily cancel certain harmonics, producing eerie drop-outs in the roar—a natural form of song.

Micro-life is everywhere: extremophile algae that grow transparent eyelid-films on rocks, bacteria that knit silica into fragile honeycomb sheets under standing water, mites that graze those sheets.

8. Materials & resources
Nothing here beckons a metalworker; ores lie too deep. But the constant vibration breaks rock into tong-shaped slivers perfect for chisel blades. Nomadic toolmakers visit seasonally, trading cured hare-meat for the basin’s “singing flakes,” prized because a struck edge rings like glass—useful for signaling across silent snowfields elsewhere.

9. Human presence (primitive tech, no permanent build)
Occasional highland bands camp behind buttresses that muffle the wind. Their shelters are hide cones held down by stone rings; no post, peg, or permanent mark remains when they depart. They hunt lamellorn with atlatls whose darts are fletched with choir-swift feathers, the only vane stable in this ceaseless gale.

10. Rules & impressions
• Gravity feels untrustworthy; bright air and endless roar create a mild, persistent vertigo.
• Fires burn blue and low; the oxygen is thin.
• Anything loose becomes an instrument. A canteen, a braid of hair, even your ribs will hum if you stand in the wrong place.
• Silence is the rarest substance: visitors instinctively hold their breath in the lee of a boulder just to taste it, then hurry back before the emptiness starts to ring in their skulls.

Every element in the Basin of Thirteen Throats expresses one thesis: movement without change. Wind moves, water trickles, swifts wheel, yet against the slow tape of centuries the scene has hardly shifted. It is a cathedral built by pressure and resonance rather than by hands—bright, high, and, above all, loud.
