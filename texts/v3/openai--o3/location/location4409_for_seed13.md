The first thing you notice is how sharply the land rises, shouldering its way out of the cloud-sea like the prow of a great stone ship. You are standing on a young plateau—only a few centuries since it heaved upward in some silent tectonic spasm—and almost nothing here has yet had time to round its edges. Basalt fins and obsidian ribs still gleam where the mountain’s flesh was split; shallow ravines follow fault lines as straight as a ruled page. The rock remembers its birth.

Terrain  
• A long, gently tilted tableland, about thirty kilometres from brink to brink, ringed by cliffs that drop sheer into vapor.  
• On the plateau’s crown a scatter of knife-back ridges march north–south, their flanks striped with tumbled scree. The ridges funnel the wind; in the lee of each you find narrow meadows where soil is only a finger thick.  
• Rain comes rarely but moisture is constant: every dawn the plateau condenses the passing clouds into beads that cling to grass and beard-moss. Runnels spider away from the ridge-shadows, merge, and vanish again into a single sinuous river whose stony bed is dry three days out of four. Dark pools hide under overhangs, perfectly still except for the silver flick of finger-sized fish.

Atmosphere & Light  
The air is thin, cool, and perfectly transparent; at noon objects fifty kilometres distant appear as crisp as things at arm’s length. Light behaves normally, but lingers: sunsets stretch for two hours because the western horizon is nothing but sky, so the sun can be watched sliding behind ranges so far below that the orange afterglow floods back upward and hangs there.

Sound  
Silence dominates. Wind roars only at the plateau rim; inland it drops to a papery whisper, hushed by the serrated rock. In that hush you hear the slow percolation of water through gravel, or the muffled stamp of an animal’s hoof. Twice a day a bronze gong sounds from the settlement, rolling outward like a pulse—then stillness knits itself again.

Vegetation & Fauna  
Plants grow where condensation lingers longest: pockets of dwarf willow, mats of mountain cranberry, columns of giant nettle with hollow stems thicker than a child’s wrist. Nothing forms a continuous carpet, yet the individual clumps are vigorous, green so dark it is almost black.

Animals are everywhere, though you rarely see more than a few at once.  
• Cliff-goats with ivory horns trace impossible lines along overhangs, licking salt from raw rock.  
• Spiral-shell tortoises, each scute furred with moss, bask on warm obsidian plates and retreat only after several minutes’ deliberation.  
• In the dusk, flicker-bats net insects that gather over pools; their wings tint the last light a smoky purple, an optical peculiarity no one here can explain.

Built World  
Halfway down a sheltered basin sits the city of Keirr, population perhaps four thousand. Its people work with what can be quarried or harvested by hand.

1. Foundations: slabs of columnar basalt levered free from nearby dikes. The blocks are stacked dry, no mortar, each fitted so precisely that you can slide a fingernail only partway into the joints.  
2. Superstructure: timber is too scarce to waste, so upper stories are wattle covered in lime-rich clay taken from the lone river’s banks, then washed in goat milk to harden.  
3. Streets: broad, loosely paved, flanked by runnels that catch every drop of condensed water and lead it to cisterns lined with fish-scale plaster.  
4. Public works: a hand-cranked funicular descends the cliff face to lower pastures; a sequence of lever-controlled gates releases stored water twice weekly for terraced gardens. Everything runs on muscle, rope, and leverage.

Technology stops just short of true metallurgy. The people can cold-hammer native copper beads into knives for delicate tasks, but main cutting tools are obsidian splinters hafted in bone. Every household keeps a polishing stone to resharpen these glass blades daily—obsidian chips fast, but so do the tasks repeat.

Social Rhythm  
By decree of the Cloud Calendar, nothing here is supposed to change. The plateau is revered as equilibrium made visible: no season brings storms, predators do not overhunt, humans must therefore match that stillness. Laws forbid permanent alteration of any watercourse; new buildings may be added only to existing foundations. The gong you heard enforces the daily pause: when it sounds, work stops, voices hush, and the settlement listens for imbalance. People say you can feel the plateau breathing then.

Unusual Traits (the quiet weirdness)  
• Chronoweathering: exposed metal—copper trinkets, bronze bells—does not tarnish. No patina, no green bloom, no rust elsewhere either. Geologists from below (when they can reach the rim ladders) claim the air lacks something, but what?  
• Mirror Pools: in eight scattered sinkholes water refuses to evaporate. Drop a stone: rings spread, reach the edge, and simply stay there, rigid silver circles hovering on the surface for minutes before relaxing—a glassy nod to the land’s enduring stillness.  
• The Plateau Line: migratory birds skim the rim, bank once, and leave. None have ever landed. The townsfolk shrug; “Why would a traveler stay where nothing moves?”

Rules of the World  
1. Conservation is law and tradition: disturb too little, or too much, and expulsion follows.  
2. Time feels unbraided—a year counts the same number of dawns, rains, gong-notes, but memories refuse to fade or to layer. Ask an elder about childhood and she will describe it exactly as it is this morning.  
3. Erosion paused with the land’s uplift; stone chips under direct tools, yet cliffs retain their birth-sharp shape as though the plateau’s clock was set at zero and never restarted.

Standing there you realise the quiet is not emptiness but tension, like a held breath. Everything—rock, water, goat, gong, cloud—occupies its ordained niche and neither advances nor retreats. And while the world below wheels through seasons, the high city of Keirr keeps its impossible accord: cool, damp, jagged, lightly green, humming with shy life, suspended forever between one heartbeat and the next.
