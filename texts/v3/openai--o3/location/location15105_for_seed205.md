The locals call it the Canopy Altiplano, though most who wander here never see the ground at all. Imagine a mesa the size of a small sea, already two kilometres above the lowlands, and on that mesa a second landscape rises: gargantuan vascular columns of living wood. Each trunk is the diameter of a town plaza, separated from its neighbours by half a kilometre of open air. Together they form a sparse but planet-spanning grid that disappears into the amber haze of distance. They are not trees in any familiar sense. Their bark is a puzzle-work of living plates that flex like muscle; their crowns lace together in an awning of jade membranes through which normal daylight filters, mottled, steady, warm.

1. Terrain and materials  
Between the pillars lies bare, sun-scalded stone—ancient, blunted, rust-red from a billion seasons of slow oxidation. Millennia of wind have milled the plateau into shallow bowls and scalloped ridges. In the bowls fine dust gathers, and in that dust root-hairs from the nearby columns roam like blind white worms. Each dusk a warm updraft slides across the plateau; moisture condenses on the cool stone, trickles along those root-hairs, and vanishes underground. There are no rivers, only erratic necklaces of temporary puddles that flash with starlight for an hour or two before the thirsty root-mat drinks them dry.

The vertical faces of the columns are alive with texture: shelf-like leaves the size of sails; blistered resin windows you could crawl through; naturally grown ladders of interlocked thorns. Where bark plates have fallen away, pale cambium glows faintly—plant tissue rich in bio-luminescent oils that leak and harden into translucent amber. Primitive people harvest the stuff with stone chisels, leaving tiny terraces halfway up the trunks like swallow nests.

2. Atmosphere and weather  
Hot, always: forty degrees at noon, rarely dropping below thirty even at midnight. Yet the air is thin from altitude; breathing feels like drinking warm water that never quite quenches thirst. Cloud often forms underneath the canopy rather than above it. When the canopy vibrates (it does so in wind; it does so in rage) the leaf-membranes shed vapour in glittering sheets—rainfall that never reaches the ground, caught and re-absorbed by lower layers of foliage. Thunderstorms unravel far below, hidden by the luminous green roof; you hear them but see only a diffuse pulse of light beneath your feet.

3. Soundscape  
The place is louder than any ocean. Picture a choir of a hundred million insects—thorax-drums, wing-violins, stridulating legs—each species keyed to a slightly different harmonic. Their combined voice resonates with the hollow interiors of the pillars, turns every trunk into an organ pipe. At sunrise the sonic pressure sits on your chest like a physical weight; conversation is reduced to gestures. Predators use silence the way desert cats use shade: a sudden pocket where the noise drops to nothing is warning enough to make every creature freeze.

The pillars sing too. Heated air rushes up their core vents, exits high in the canopy, and the living wood flexes to tune the note. Some tones linger for hours, others fracture every few seconds like distant gunshots. No one can map the pattern; people navigating by ear rely on gut instinct and a tolerance for vibrations in the bones.

4. Fauna  
Life flocks to structure. Every sparsely spaced column is its own skyscraper city, its own vertical prairie.

• The dominant herbivore is the sky-bison: six-limbed, gliding membranes between fore and hind legs, shaggy heat-reflective fur the colour of lichen. They live in the hollows excavated by roots, then climb the trunk using prehensile tongues to hook into bark seams. At dawn they launch, catch the thermal that rises from the hot stone, and drift to the next pillar, bawling like foghorns.  
• Between the sky-bison swirl dagger-beaked raptors whose wings are mostly feathered leaf—the camouflage so perfect that one often mistakes them for a drifting branch until the branch stoops and the screaming starts.  
• Lower down, in the dust basins, antelope-sized arthropods pick over fallen pollen cakes. Each step clicks; each joint gleams with the metallic sheen of copper oxide. When threatened they detonate stored resin sacs, producing a crack that rivals the columns’ own percussion.  
• And then the invertebrate majority: translucent cicada coils; centipedes that knit nets from silk-slime and suspend them between root-hairs; mites that farm algae on the living bark like tiny gardeners scaling a city wall.

5. Vegetation as architecture  
The columns are only the visible portion of a single organism—the Plateau Matriarch, according to travelling shamans. She flowers once each great year (roughly a century). The bloom begins at one pole of the continent and migrates column by column, a wave of crimson that travellers can measure in kilometres per hour. Pollen drifts like volcanic ash, thick enough to etch temporary dunes along the lee sides of ridges. Anything that breathes it directly hallucinates, not unpleasantly, for days; the indigenous tribes schedule their festivals to coincide.

Smaller vegetation is almost absent. Where a normal ecosystem would host shrubs, the Matriarch instead extrudes pillow-shaped photosynthetic cushions flush with the stone. These cushions double as water collectors. Primitive foragers slice them open to drink the sweet, slightly narcotic sap.

6. Human marks  
Stone rings—three or four in a lifetime of walking—dot the plateau. Each surrounds a spring where a fracture in the bedrock brings condensed water to the surface more regularly than the nightly dew. Huts stand half-collapsed: adobe mixed with powdered cambium, thatch woven from discarded sail-leaves. Toolkits are flint or bird-bone; resin knives hold an edge almost as sharp as obsidian but soften in the heat, so every hunter keeps them sheathed in damp moss until the moment of use.

Nothing is built high, because everything needed at height already grows there. Instead humans practice bark-shaping: they seed a ring of thorns into a wound on a young plate; within a month the thorns fuse into a basket, or a cradle, or the walls of a small dwelling. When the bark expands the structure rises with it like a living elevator. Some elders claim to have spent their entire middle age in slow ascent, house and all, watching the world diminish below.

7. Rules and rhythms  
• Water is not a surface feature but a cycle: heat → updraft → condensation → drip → uptake. Anyone who does not learn the drip schedule first will not live long.  
• Sound is territory. Animals carve acoustic niches the way reef fish claim hollows. Intrude on the wrong frequency for too long and a predator accustomed to that silence will investigate.  
• Fire is unknown here. The living wood is too wet; the air too thin; the nights too warm to encourage sparks. Stories of flame are treated like myths from some colder, crueler heaven.  
• Direction is felt through vibration more than sight; travellers anchor themselves by the deepest note in the forest of pillars, a bass that rolls like continuous thunder from the continental centre where, they believe, the Matriarch’s heartwood stands exposed.

8. Strange phenomena  
During the hottest weeks the plateau’s stone substrate expands audibly, a steady creak. That stress sometimes shears off entire lids of rock, exposing root umbilicals thick as subway tunnels. When torn, those umbilicals bleed clear gel that rapidly aerosolises—filling the nearby air with floating, refractive globes. Each globe is tougher than soap; they drift for days, bending light into rainbow haloes that move with the wind until finally they implode with a soft hiss heard only in the brief midnight lull.

Occasionally a column topples. No one hears it at first; the roar of the ecosystem masks all. But a smell—like crushed mint and hot copper—rolls across the plateau in a gust. Then comes the shadow: kilometres of canopy collapsing, driving a pressure wave that flattens dust basins and sends every creature scrambling for wind-hollows. In the wreckage new sprouts rise within hours, green spears forcing their way through splintered meat-wood, proving that the Matriarch wastes no fallen limb.

9. Standing on the stone  
Find a ridge at sunset. The light strikes low through breaches in the canopy, igniting motes of drifting pollen until the air itself seems forged of gold wire. You feel the ground vibrate: insect choir, wood organ, the breath of a hot high world that has lived a long time in its own balanced strangeness. Between pillars the sky is wide, empty, welcoming. Yet everywhere life presses inward—vegetal, animal, sonic—overwhelming but spacious, ancient but always in motion.

You are a temporary filament of attention on a continent that is also a single plant, listening to itself think in thunderous chords. Stay still long enough and the plateau will teach you its only real lesson: that even deafening life can coexist with great, deliberate spaces; that immensity need not be crowded to feel full.
