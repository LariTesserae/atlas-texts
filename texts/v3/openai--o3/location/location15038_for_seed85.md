The doorway-capsule releases you onto a ledge of polished stone no wider than a church balcony. From here the world is water—night-colored, moonless water that presses around you like a single, unblinking thought. A pale-blue phosphor line sewn into your suit’s visor tells you it is three and a half kilometres above the drowned planet’s notional “sea level.” Once, this had been the shoulder of a mountain; now it floats inside an oceanic sky.

Terrain and materials  
The peak is limestone shot through with newborn fractures: razor-straight, dust-free, edges still glittering where the planet-wide deluge scoured away weathered crust only a generation ago. Swirls of calcite catch the stray light of your torch, looking almost vegetal but remaining resolutely mineral—the nearest thing to a tree here is a column of stalagmite snapped in half and left gleaming like a broken tusk. Nothing has had time to round the corners; every ledge, spur and staircase feels as if it was chiseled yesterday and immediately sealed in cold brine.

The urban layer  
Someone—or something—met the flood with composure. A lattice of hexagonal arches hugs the mountainface, climbing and descending in even, deliberate rhythms. They are not masonry in the classical sense: swarms of self-assembling nano-clays fused into vitreous ribs, translucence shifting to opacity as you move. Where two ribs meet, you glimpse conduit-cores that carry faint pulses of silver charge: circuitry as architecture, city as slow, glittering machine. The plan is compact, about the footprint of an old Earth cathedral, yet it feels tall, vertical, knowing its real horizon is the water itself.

Atmosphere, or what passes for it  
There is no free air. A film only a centimetre thick separates liquid from the voids inside the arches: a membrane with surface tension tense enough to hold back tonnes of pressure. Step through a doorway and the water shears away with a sound like a silk sheet torn slowly. Inside: stillness, 6 °C, one-sixth Earth atmospheric pressure, nitrogen-rich, oxygen held in crystalline cages that dissolve the moment you breathe. The engineers who designed this place trusted molecular tricks more than pumps.

Light and sound  
Darkness is the default. Roof panels drink what few photons wander down from the sunless expanse above. Illumination appears only where your presence trips inductive gradients: a desk-smooth floor line glows, then fades behind you. Sound is equally reluctant. In water, distant groans of shifting stone travel for seconds, then are swallowed. Inside the dry vaults your footfalls are the loudest thing alive. The city does not hum; it waits.

Fauna, if that word is honest  
Now and then a silhouette drifts between buttresses outside: ovoid, no visible propulsion, skin a lattice of pores that leak cold light. They are catalogued as Filter Units—biomechanical collectors that devour free ions, nothing more. You never see two at once. Once, when your torch lingers on one, it pivots—no eyes, just an implied regard—then glides away like a thought you meant to remember. No gills, no mouths, simply purpose.

Ecology, or its absence  
With vegetation at absolute zero, life rewrites itself around mineral gradients and engineered reagents. The mountain leaks trace metals; the Filter Units harvest them and excrete packets that slot into sockets along the lattice arches. In turn the city’s walls glow fractionally brighter, powering the membrane that keeps its inner chambers dry. It is a circle so slight you could miss it; remove any piece and the equilibrium would crumble, but while each participant performs its single task, nothing changes.

Rules of the world  
1. Time is held at arm’s length: the flood is considered a finished event, not a process.  
2. Erosion pauses at the brink of rounding; crystalline edges must remain young.  
3. Light is a currency spent only when observed.  
4. The city’s machinery knows more physics than its builders left written. You may examine but never fully parse.  
5. Nothing here hurries; persistence, not growth, is the highest form of life.

What it feels like  
Stand on an outer balcony, inside a bubble of silence. Above, kilometers of black water pile into a weight you can’t sense; below, the slope falls away to unkown trenches. If you listen carefully you may catch the cathedral-deep resonance of a stone slab shifting a fraction of a millimeter—a tectonic heartbeat magnified by water. Between those heartbeats is a hush so complete you suspect sound itself has frozen.

What remains to discover  
A library pod sealed behind a triple membrane, books printed on sheets of aerogel so thin they drift inside like trapped smoke. A shaft descending to a chamber still full of water where statues—perhaps citizens who chose stasis—stand robed in silky bacterial films. A beacon panel etched with equations that treat water not as liquid but as programmable matter, awaiting a gesture no one remembers.

The landscape resists story. It is a cathedral of postponement, a high, drowned refuge that has already survived its catastrophe and now rehearses the same, careful moment forever. Put your palm to the membrane doorway once more; feel the tension hold. Outside, the black sea keeps its flawless dark. Inside, a single corridor line fades behind you, erasing proof that anyone was here at all.
