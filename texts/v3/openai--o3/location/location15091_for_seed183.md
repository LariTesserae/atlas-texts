The climb begins in silence, and then the place decides it will no longer be silent.

Terrain
You enter a country of verticality: ribs of pale stone thrust three kilometres and more into a sky so brilliantly white-blue it hurts to stare. The mountains are old—every ridge planed, every ledge cupped and polished—yet they remain savage, pitched at impossible angles. Between the spires run trenches rather than valleys, narrow and blind, so that you walk in a maze with walls of glittering limestone on either side. The rock sweats. Thin films of water ooze straight from its pores, join, and fall away in narrow ribbons that sheet the cliffs like transparent curtains.

Water
Rain never quite stops here; it just changes phase. Vapor condenses in sudden blooms, becomes silver drizzle, then mist again, all in the space of a few heartbeats. Little freshwater lenses perch like mirrors in stone bowls, their surfaces trembling from the continuous drizzle of cliff-tears. In places the walls leak faster, and vertical rivers roar, vanish into porous gravel at the trench floor, then reappear farther downslope as fountains that seem to erupt against gravity before flopping earthward in surrender.

Temperature & Light
The air is cold enough that your breath shows, yet the world blazes with light. Sun—or something sun-like—finds you no matter how deep the chasms. It reflects off every wet face; it fractures inside clear beads clinging to rock grain; it ricochets through drifting curtains of mist. You have the disconcerting sense of walking inside a gemstone cut to trap and multiply radiance.

Materials & “Vegetation”
Nothing green grows. Where a temperate mountain elsewhere would harbor mosses, only mineral blooms appear: wafer-thin discs of copper-blue and sulfur-yellow, arrayed like scales; branching dendrites of manganese that resemble ferns painted onto the cliffs; tiny quartz whiskers that pierce the air like translucent thorns. They are not alive, yet they do respond—brush a hand across them and they realign, as iron filings do near a magnet, then slowly relax when you move on.

Sound
There is no single soundscape; there is chorus and counter-chorus. Waterfall roar overlaps a constant, hollow, wind-born moan funneled through slot canyons. Every so often the mountains cough: slabs fracture with a crack that seems to ring on long after you think it should fade, the echo looping back on itself, louder each pass until it collapses into a bass thud that vibrates in your chest. Standing stones—pale cylinders balanced on narrow pedestals—hum audibly. Put your ear to one and you hear a multilayered chord, as though the strata are singing to themselves.

Built Traces & Tech
Scattered among the trenches stand low rings of slate, roofless, no more than shoulder-height. Their interiors are smoothed to a sheen, as though something once sat or knelt there for centuries. Beside them lie tools: hafts of fossilized wood bound with sinew-like strips of mica, blades chipped from volcanic glass. All are sized for hands roughly your own. None show rust or rot, only a patina of continual washing. A few rings are half-completed; crude hammers and stone wedges remain where the builder paused—yesterday? a thousand years ago? No one is here now.

Fauna
No wing breaks the light. No paw print scars the wet gravel. Even microorganisms seem absent; wounds refuse infection, and any stray crumb of food you drop is scoured clean by the constant drip before mold can take hold.

Dynamic Processes
Weather is quicksilver. A trench fills with fog that churns like water just brought to boil, then empties in a sudden gust, revealing rock that was never visible moments earlier. Ledges slough pebbles in slow, continuous slides. Mineral filigrees grow fast enough to watch: a bead of brine evaporates, leaves a new ring of crystals two millimeters wider than before.

Rules of the World & Weirdness
Reality here bends, though never to the same shape twice. Distances stretch or compress: a ledge ten strides away telescopes into a kilometre of effort; a far summit appears beneath your outstretched hand. Gravitational direction is advisory rather than compulsory—see water that climbs, humming pillars that lean forward past their center of mass but do not fall. Sound sometimes arrives before its source: the ring of a splitting slab is heard a heartbeat before the stone parts. Your own shadow splits too, duplicating on opposing walls, crossing itself like hands in a mirror.

The locals—if those ancient ring-builders still exist—may have learned not to fight these inconsistencies but to choreograph them. A half-complete slate ring always lies where resonance is strongest, where echoes fold into standing waves that erode rock faster than any chisel. Build the ring, tame the note; leave the mountain to finish the sculpture for you.

Atmosphere
The air tastes metallic—iron after biting your lip—and faintly sweet, as though laced with distant thunder. It is heavy with negative ions, prickling skin. In twilight (should you stay that long) the entire ravine ignites in slow stroboscopic flashes of sheet lightning, silent but blinding, illuminating the water paths carved into every wall like veins in glass.

If you remain through one full day, the landscape reorders itself. Paths you walked are gone; new terraces stand where mist once hung. Only the oldest, most eroded ridges stay fixed, as though proud of their antiquity and unwilling to join the younger stone in its restless dance.

Departure
Climbing out is easier than descending—the mountain, perversely, wants you to leave. It eases gravity’s grip, widens steps, presses warm updrafts against your back. Behind you the soundscape retunes, waterfalls slacken, mist collapses into stillness, as if the country of vertical light is inhaling, waiting for new footsteps to set it ringing again.
