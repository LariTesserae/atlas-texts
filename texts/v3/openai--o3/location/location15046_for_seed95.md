From a distance the highland looks almost featureless—a tawny, sun-bleached tableland floating above a haze of its own making—but the moment you step onto it the ground reveals endless texture. The plateau is ancient, so ancient that every harder seam of stone has already surrendered to wind, frost, and ten thousand seasons of warm rain. What remains is the final, lace-fine version of the landscape: a maze of low ridges, shallow basins, and narrow fins of rock standing only as tall as a person yet stretching for kilometres, like the ribs of some eroded colossus.

Terrain and materials  
• Bedrock: primarily pale dolomitic limestone riddled with solution pockets, shot through with veins of ochre iron that rust in streaks wherever moisture lingers.  
• Soil: almost none. Where dust gathers—behind a ridge, inside a hollow—it forms just a fingernail of powder laced with mica, flashing in the ordinary yellow light.  
• Water: visible only in brief, intimate encounters. Each dawn, warm air rises from the lower valleys, condenses against the chilled plateau, and beads on the stone. By mid-morning that condensation has trickled into fingertip-wide runnels, feeding pools that shine for an afternoon before sinking away through fissures. After the infrequent cloudburst, whole fields of limestone breathe steam for hours, but by the following sunset they look dry again.

Atmosphere  
Elevation keeps the air thin enough that breathing feels like a deliberate act. Despite the altitude the temperature remains warm; the sun’s heat is trapped by a slate-gray band of moisture that rarely forms clouds but always lies heavy above the rock. Scents travel poorly here—wind is light, almost embarrassed—yet the air is not bland. It tastes of mineral dust, faintly salty, and of something musky and animal that never quite resolves into a known smell.

Vegetation  
Life in rootless fragments.  
• Frill-lichens: parchment rosettes glued flat to stone, cinnamon and sage in colour, curling at the edges when dew evaporates.  
• Mortar-shrubs: knee-high mounds growing straight out of cracks, their stems so woody they clack against one another in the breeze. Each shrub may stand metres apart from the next; together they never manage more than a thin dotted line across the horizon.  
• Spiracle moss: lurks inside hollows that stay damp for weeks after rain; from above you see only tiny pinholes—the moss breathes through them, releasing cool vapor curls at sunrise.

Fauna (overwhelming, though rarely loud)  
Nothing here lives in small numbers. Scarcity of plant matter forces animals into dense, competitive niches around every droplet.  
• Quill-mites: finger-sized arthropods resembling walking pine cones. They blanket damp basins thousands at a time, combing the condensate with capillary bristles. When disturbed, the entire carpet rolls inward like a quivering tide.  
• Lantern ropers: translucent worms that hang from ridge-crests on strands of dried saliva, absorbing light all day and releasing it as amber bioluminescence at night to lure diurnal moths trying to sleep.  
• Ridge scalders: cat-sized, warm-blooded reptiles with skin patterned like the limestone. They secrete a mild acid through their feet, etching spoon-shaped hollows where they curl to rest; over geologic time they have contributed visibly to the plateau’s moon-cheese texture.  
• Cloud shepherds: the only large vertebrate. Antelope-tall but crane-thin, these ungulates travel in compact herds—thirty or forty pressed shoulder to shoulder—so they may share body moisture. Their hollow horns capture morning fog and channel it down grooves in the skull to the tongue. A passing herd leaves behind sharply printed hoof-rings that persist for years in the slow-moving dust.

Soundscape  
Silence is the plateau’s default note. Wind scrapes but seldom whistles; water seldom splashes. What you hear instead are momentary swells: the dry clatter of mortar-shrubs in a single gust, the brittle crackle of quill-mites rearranging themselves, the basso shudder of a cloud-shepherd chorus calling through their resonant horns. Each is brief; each falls back into hush.

Built traces  
Stone markers appear where logic says no hand could bother to leave them: waist-high pillars mortared with the same silicate crust that binds the cliffs, small corbelled chambers only big enough to crawl inside, a single lintel balanced across two standing slabs and engraved with spirals too shallow to catch dust. None are younger than the last phase of heavy erosion, which means they have watched at least half a million warm seasons pass. Whatever society placed them here built sparingly, confident the world would not change enough to erase the gesture—and they were right.

Weirdness that feels natural  
1. The plateau runs on capillary time. Moisture, heat, even animal movement travel by incremental soak rather than by flow. Walk fifteen kilometres and the temperature, the pattern of sunlight, the density of mites will all be nearly identical, as though you never moved. Only when you stay still for days do you notice change; the landscape edits itself when unobserved, not while crossed.  
2. Life here obsesses over surface tension. Every organism seems built to harvest, store, or manipulate thin skins of liquid. Lantern ropers spin films of spit so strong you can lean your weight on them before they fail; cloud-shepherd horns condense water out of air too dry for breath. It is as though evolution skipped the liquid-water phase and went straight from vapor to dew.  
3. Gravity has a curious local texture. Throw a pebble and you might see it fall in a shallow curve, pause mid-air when crossing an unseen boundary, then continue. No one believes it until they juggle their own water bottle and watch droplets hang like glass beads before obediently descending.

Dynamics—slow motion everywhere  
Stone creeps; pools evaporate; herds migrate on a circuit so gradual that a newborn will not return to its birthplace until it is too old to walk. Travellers camping for a week find their footprints still crisp months later because dust settles grain by grain. The loudest event in a decade is the collapse of a limestone arch, and even that begins with years of quiet groaning.

Rules to move by  
• Carry shade rather than water; condensation will refill any vessel overnight.  
• Never camp beneath a lantern-roper colony unless you enjoy being peppered with stunned insects dawn to dusk.  
• If a cloud-shepherd herd surrounds you, stand still and exhale through your nose. They will sample your humidity and decide you are competition or company.  
• Respect the pillars. Local animals cut wide arcs around them, and the ground nearby vibrates at a frequency too low for human ears. People who lean on the stones complain later of dreams in which the plateau speaks—slowly, syllables minutes apart.

Standing on one of the higher fins at sunset you look west across thirty kilometres of near-level beige stone, its surface crazed with gullies that read like braille under the sliding light. Pools left by yesterday’s rain glitter inside every hollow, each no bigger than a dinner plate yet, taken together, creating the illusion of an inland sea made of mirrors. Out of that reflected sky rise the silhouettes of cloud-shepherds, breath steaming despite the warmth, horns tilting to drink the last drops of colour. Around your ankles quill-mites rearrange, ready to harvest tonight’s dew. Everything else waits—slowly, densely, quietly—for darkness, which changes nothing quickly but eventually changes it all.
