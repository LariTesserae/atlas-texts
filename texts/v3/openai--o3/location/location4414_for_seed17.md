You descend through pale, near-freezing water that seems to go on forever, yet the pressure never rises.  It takes a moment to realise why: the ocean itself is thin.  Molecule for molecule it is water, but the water is laced with hollows—great vacuoles of almost perfect vacuum that drift like bubbles large as cities.  The planet’s gravity is weak, held in check by its vast size, so the liquid never quite sorts itself out.  Down here “depth” and “height” mean only distance from the nearest empty pocket.

A plateau of brand-new stone hangs inside this diluted sea.  Picture a continental shelf chiselled yesterday: edges knife-sharp, corners un-crumbled, glass-black basalt still bearing the cooling fractures that spidered across it the hour it froze.  Ridges climb another kilometre, cut by canyons so young they have no silt at their feet; the walls flake obsidian like pages from an unopened book.

There is no plant life.  Surfaces are naked mineral, salt-dusted, rimed with a lace of ice where strands of colder vacuum brush through.  Yet the place is not lifeless.  The ecology here is animal from the bottom up.  

1. Micro-engines: centimetre-long crusts of living metal cling to rock faces.  They grind stone to ions, run those ions through bio-turbines, and hiss out a trickle of heat.  
2. Grazer flocks—gelatinous manta shapes—cruise slowly through the hollows, sieving these micro-engines the way baleen whales sieve krill.  
3. Between them move the long hunters: silver cords two hundred metres nose to tail, idle as cables until a grazer blunders too near and the cord coils shut like a sprung tape.

No sound reaches you at first; the water is too thin to carry it.  Then a pressure field swings past and the world explodes with noise.  Every ten seconds the plateau shudders under a standing roar: industrial compressors anchored to its flanks drink the rarefied water, smash it into dense packets, and vent the packets toward distant foundries.  The process never pauses.  Centuries of operation have driven the resonance up until the whole shelf sings—a single basso note deeper than most ears can register but loud enough to feel in bone and tooth.  The creatures are born into it; it sets their pulse.  To newcomers it is deafening.

Scattered among the rocks are the machines themselves.  They are not ruins—nothing here corrodes or weathers—but they look abandoned simply because no operators are visible.  Iron caissons the size of cathedrals stand on articulated feet.  Their sides bear placards: black chevrons, serial numbers, polite warnings in dead alphabets.  Intake trumpets yawn toward vacuole walls; outlet chimneys glow dull orange where work heats their cores.  Conveyor rails run nowhere, half-laid and shining.  Once in a while a maintenance drone—boxy, propeller-legged—puts out from a hatch, swaps a filter, and sinks back inside, job done.  Nothing else about the complex has changed in recorded memory.  The world orders it to continue, and so it continues.

Light arrives by engineering as well.  Sodium arcs writhe in cables stretched between spires, dumping daylight into the water.  Where a vacuole drifts across a lamp the beam refracts, grazing canyon walls in fat, wandering bands of yellow.  Shadows pivot lazily; time seems frozen because the pattern of bands has shifted only a few degrees since the first observers came.

Rules of the place, as they reveal themselves:

•  Gravity is low but exact, allowing slabs of vacuum and slabs of water to coexist without mixing.  
•  All metabolism is mechanical: chemistry feeds pistons, pistons feed chemistry, the line between organism and engine erased.  
•  Sound travels only in the denser strata, so silence and thunder alternate like weather.  
•  Nothing corrodes; there is too little oxygen.  Sharp edges stay sharp.  Footprints you leave in silt will last geologic spans unless something deliberately removes them.  
•  Currents are weak; temperature gradients almost flat.  Change happens, but at a pace so uniform it feels like stasis.

Stand on the basalt rim and look outward.  The plateau falls away into a gulf lit by its own far-off machinery.  Grazer flocks drift below like slow constellations.  A maintenance drone purrs by, unaware of you, trailing a cable that could power a city.  Above, a moon-sized vacuole glides across the lamps, and for half a minute the light fails; absence as loud as the compressors’ song.

Then the arc flares again, the basso note swells, and the cold, wide, half-empty sea resumes the immense, meticulous act of remaining exactly as it is.
