Climb high enough through the thin gray air and the world levels out onto the Plateau of Green Silence—though “silence” is a misnomer uttered only by distant cartographers. Up here, three kilometers above the vanished oceans, the ground is neither soil nor stone but a living mat of photosynthetic wood-flesh, ancient beyond counting, braided so densely that it has become the continent’s bedrock. The plant-mass is the planet’s crust; step on it and it flexes, groaning like timber hulls in a restless dock.  

WATER  
There is no liquid water. Centuries of ionic winds stripped the last vapors from the sky, leaving an atmosphere too cold and too dry to permit the old chemistry. Life went elsewhere for its solvent: sugars flow here the way rivers once did, viscous, amber, and slow, seeping along capillary grooves in the vegetal surface. Every droplet is fiercely hoarded; when the sugary fluid meets open air it crystallizes into diamond-hard stalactites of caramel glass. Travelers chip them off for drinking, crunching sweetness instead of sipping.  

LIGHT & AIR  
The sun is a tired ember smeared behind perpetual dust haze—dim, copper, and too distant to glare. Photosynthesis has adapted: leaves are midnight purple, black, and ultraviolet glossy, optimized to wring photons out of near darkness. The air is thin and cool (barely five degrees on most days) but never still. Every dusk, gravity waves roll down from extinct mountain ridges and set the entire plateau humming.  

TERRAIN & AGE  
Erosion long ago polished the highlands flat, but the plant crust remembers the vanished relief. Where volcanoes once stood, there are now bonsai calderas of petrified bark—broad circular sinkholes whose walls reveal two billion years of annual rings. Elsewhere, the crust has cracked, sagging into hexagonal tiles a kilometer across. Each tile tilts minutely in response to the wind, like scales on some geologic reptile, making the horizon flicker as you walk.  

SOUND  
Nothing here is quiet. The plateau is cavernous from within; the photosynthetic substrate is laced with hollow tubes and resonance chambers. Wind entering one fissure emerges from another as organ-pipe chords that can be heard for twenty kilometers. At night the fauna add to the din: membranous things beating rhythmic patterns on hollow stalks, predator drones clicking in ultrasonic ranges, the distant bass of a root-born heartbeat as mega-rhizomes pump syrup through continental arteries.  

VEGETATION—THE TOTAL CITY  
Plant equals world, and civilization works entirely in that medium. There is a city called Vires Regnum that you may not notice until you are inside it: no skyline marks its edge because every tower, bridge, and boulevard is coaxed growth. Architects here are arbor-smiths; they splice command genes into the local cambium so that trunks branch in icosahedral lattices, leaves fuse into membrane roofs, and fruit ripens pre-hollowed for habitation. From a distance the metropolis is only a region where the canopy becomes fractal and purposefully sparse, providing lofted plazas open to the sky. At street level you discover balustrades of living vine glowing with bioluminescent runes, lift-shafts grown as spiraling vascular bundles, and avenues that widen or narrow on command, pulsing like throats to aid pedestrian flow.  

TECH—BEYOND MODERN  
The people—whether human descendants or something that merely remembers humanity—employ a plant physics that current textbooks would dismiss as myth. They write software into RNA knots, route computation through plasmodesmata, and transmit power as standing acoustic waves through the continental xylem. Their transportation is the stridulation rail: you sit inside a seedpod, the city plucks a structural root, and the resonant shockwave launches you down a guide vine at near-supersonic speed. No combustion, no wheels—just music loud enough to shove matter.  

FAUNA  
Despite the scarcity of water, life is exuberant. Many animals are half-plant themselves: chlorofauna crusted with pantry leaves, sap-drinking leapers whose tendons double as phloem. Dart-wasps of translucent lignin drill egg chambers deep into the living pavement. Larger forms stalk the cracks—six-legged grazer-knots that shear off surface tissue, followed by flocks of cleaner mites. Apex predators are sound hunters: blind, sail-backed lizards that triangulate on heartbeat echoes and strike with tongues barbed in glass. No creature wastes moisture; waste is exhaled as dry aromatic smoke that perfumes the wind.  

DYNAMICS & WEATHER  
There is “rain,” but it is mineral: at unpredictable intervals the upper atmosphere cools enough for potassium salts to precipitate in shimmering curtains. The crystalline dust whistles as it falls, a glittering storm that scours exposed surfaces and sharpens every edge. In response, the city folds—branches re-knot, plazas seal, residents retreat into interior sap-caverns where temperature and pressure remain constant. Hours later the walls unfurl again; new structures sprout, seeded by the very salts that tried to erode them.  

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Conservation of Sweetness: every gram of sugar solvent is tracked; spill it and you pay a fine in days of service grooming the root-pumps.  
2. Silence Is Suspicious: the society trusts noise—life hums, creaks, sings. Anything quiet is likely dead or predatory.  
3. Build Only Living Things: dead matter cracks under salt rain. Tools, dwellings, even sculpture must possess metabolism to self-repair.  
4. Do Not Harm the Resonance: fracture a wind organ and you risk disrupting the stridulation network; traffic will stall, and the city itself may detain you.  

STANDING THERE  
So you walk the plateau trail—violet lichen crunching underfoot, thin wind vibrating bass notes through miles of hollow timber. Dim rust light paints gargantuan fern-spires whose fronds interlock like gothic arches. Above, dart-wasps trace calligraphy in the sky; below, sugar rivers glow faintly, their warmth the only real heat in this cool, high, eroded eternity. In the distance the living city shimmers, towers flexing, sending out rhythmic thunder that doubles as both welcome and warning.  

The place feels final, settled into the long quiet after water, after stone, after ordinary biology—a late chapter of a planet writing itself in chlorophyll and sound. Yet it is restless, alive, always making more of itself. The plateau will be here at the universe’s last dusk, humming its impossible music into the cooling dark.
