The place lies one level down from daylight—neither a deep abyss nor a token basement, but a broad, under-skin hollow the size of a string of small valleys laid end to end. It could once have been a dry graben between plates of basalt; the rock is still young enough that its faces meet in knife-edges, ridges crisp as unground glass. No rounded corners yet, no velvet of settled dust.  

Cold hangs everywhere, a dry-ice cold that smokes the breath and makes iron ooze frost. The ceiling—thirty, forty metres above—never quite disappears, but it is lost in a gauze of condensation that plays dim tricks with the industrial lamps spaced along catwalks. Those lamps offer the only steady light: a low amber, barely a streetlamp’s glow, smothered by steam. Daylight filters down through abandoned ventilation shafts in colourless shafts that seem to stall halfway. The result is a half-lit dusk in which every object has two shadows—one faint, one inky.  

Water is present more as a mood than as a body. It beads on every surface, threads itself into mossy films, drips at intervals long enough that each impact can be counted. Here and there shallow basins have formed where the rock has fractured; they hold platter-sized mirrors of near-frozen water, skin constantly rehealing after each drop. In freshets after rain at the surface the basins overflow and send brief rivulets searching the floor, but for most of the year moisture lives as sweat and breath.  

Vegetation has colonised almost everything that is not strictly vertical. What looks at first like a floor of stone proves to be a mat of intertwined stems, a living felt a handspan thick. The plants are not small things rooted to soil but the soil itself: pale, ropey tissues that knit into load-bearing masses, climb pylons, braid around stair rails. They have no leaves—the light is too poor—but develop long translucent blades that lie flat to catch any photic crumb and shiver when machinery sets the air moving. Some masses have swelled into domes the height of cottages. One can walk for hundreds of metres on this green-grey quilt before rock shows through. Despite that, the cavern still feels spacious; the living carpet is low, the pillars of stone high and far apart, and sightlines run unbroken for half a kilometre.  

Sound dominates. Forty years ago an exploratory mining outfit drove a line of ducted turbines along the cavern’s central corridor to pull warm surface air downward and feed prospecting furnaces. Most of the furnaces are gone, but the power train never stopped. Twelve axial fans, each big enough to swallow a truck, still turn under their own inertia. They spin slowly—three, maybe four revolutions a minute—but their blades bite the close air and turn it into a continuous bellow that drowns conversation and vibrates bone. The note never changes; it has become part of the cavern’s geology, a permanent low A, felt more than heard.  

Because the fans turn slowly, the atmosphere circulates in patient tides. A traveller can feel a faint pull northward for twenty metres, then an eddy that drifts back south. Spores ride these loops, and thus the plants have spread in ringed, fingerprint patterns that repeat for kilometres. The noise also masks nearly every other sound; life here has adapted by going silent or by ignoring the din. Fauna is scarce: a colourless amphibian that navigates by touch, a black hopper the size of a fingernail, two species of blind moth that rest with wings pressed to the vibrating duct walls. Anything larger would need more energy than the dim ecosystem can spare.  

Built things appear sparsely, almost shyly. A single rail cart left on a spur of track; a boiler shell rusting beneath a cascade of roots; a corrugated hut whose door still bears the company stencil, though the characters are blurred by lichen. None of it seems abandoned so much as quietly ceded to time. Now and then, one meets a bank of gauges still live, powered by trickle-current from a line that somewhere far above still feeds the grid. Amber needles twitch, measuring pressures no one reads.  

Rules and rhythms  
1. Nothing happens quickly. Water takes days to gather, hours to fall, minutes to strike the pans. Plants grow centimetres a season. Even the great roar of the turbines is a slow roar, a glacier of sound.  
2. Light defines territory. Where a lamp fails, plants bleach to ivory and lose their blade-forms; where two lamps overlap, knotty buttresses rise. Crews once shifted lamps on trolleys, and the vegetation still traces those long-forgotten arcs.  
3. Metal sweats. Any exposed steel will film over, then gem with stalactite droplets of brine. The plants favour such spots; salts are precious here.  
4. Silence is dangerous. If the fans ever truly stopped, the place would become so still one could hear capillary water move. Then the cold would sharpen, condensation would freeze, and the living carpet—unable to shed ice—would suffocate. The few caretakers that surface records mention were tasked with keeping just enough spin in the rotors to keep time itself from halting.  

Standing on a grated catwalk you feel the floor quiver under the bass note. Your breath comes out as visible ribbons, shredded by the vibration. Below, the living ground flexes—a faint, slow surge like tideless surf—as air pressure rises and falls with the turning of the blades. In the dim you can watch a thread of water release from the ceiling, fall forty metres, and burst on a plate of root tissue, flinging up cold mist that shines for an instant in lamp-glare before the dark reclaims it. There is no mystery here, only patience, and the ceaseless drone that fills all available space.
