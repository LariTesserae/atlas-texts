The place is known by the high-plain nomads as the Frost Nave, though it is neither fully frost nor fully nave, but something wedged between.  

TERRAIN  
Imagine a mountain ridge still in the act of being born: slabs of slate and milk-white granite tilted upright as if a giant had tried to stack books in a hurry. The ridge rises so quickly and so steeply that true summits have not yet had time to crumble; edges are young, knife-clean, unrounded by age. Then, halfway up, a single transverse fracture opens—a gash only half a kilometre long and scarcely fifty paces wide. Standing on the brink, you look into a vertical‐walled chamber, cathedral-scaled, its floor hidden by blown mist. Light pours down through the slot all day; at noon it is almost painful, reflected from every ice-rimmed ledge until nothing has a shadow. Thin air tingles in the lungs, and every exhalation smokes. The Nave is small compared with the whole massif yet immense compared with a person, a hollow large enough to swallow a hamlet and still feel empty.

WATER  
Moisture here is neither plentiful nor absent. The upper walls gleam with film-thin sheets of meltwater that bead, run six metres, and instantly freeze again. In niches where the rock flares just enough to catch a handful of snow, tiny amphitheatres of moss collect runoff and hold it like sponge-green goblets. When clouds scrape the ridge, needles of cold rain drift inward, never a downpour, always a passing rinse. You hear individual droplets land.

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
Air temperature stays just below freezing. Yet the brightness is almost tropical, a paradox of altitude: sunlight, unfiltered and thin, scatters in crystals of suspended ice until the very cold seems to glow. Every surface is back-lit; even shadows are a pale slate-blue rather than black.

VEGETATION  
Against expectation, the fracture is lush—though “lush” here means density, not height. Mats of cobalt moss, chain-lichens, and tufts of fat-leafed alpine sedge drape the walls like tapestries. Because soil is scarce, roots weave directly into stone fissures; you can peel a plant free and find no dirt at all, only hairline cracks. Twice each day, as direct sun angles in, the vegetation opens minute pores and exhales a sharp, green menthol scent, steaming visibly in the chill.

FAUNA  
Life beats its own, brisk rhythm.  
• Cloud-gnats drift in thousands, feeding on airborne pollen; in direct sun their wings diffract light in prismatic sparks.  
• Horned skimmers—dragonflies the size of a child’s hand—hunt the gnats, their wingbeats the loudest sound you’ll hear most hours.  
• Cliff-conies, white and three-legged, leap the ledges, browsing sedge and vanishing with a squeak if a pebble shifts.  
• Lace owls, nearly silent even in flight, work the dusk: they bank through the slot, snag a cony, and coast back out before you have registered movement.  
Predation is swift, recycling nutrients that would otherwise wash out with meltwater. Carcasses vanish in minutes under beetles and biting snow-flies. Nothing lingers long enough to rot.

SOUND  
Wind seldom howls here; the fracture lies across prevailing flow and creates a lull. Sounds resolve as miniatures: a single drip finds a puddle like a struck bell; the twitch of a cony stirs gravel loudly; wing-shear murmurs overhead. Long moments pass in which the landscape seems muted, waiting.

DYNAMICS  
Despite the hush, the Nave is restless. Plates of rock part fractions of a millimetre each season; sometimes, without warning, a whole slab the size of a cottage groans, pitches forward, and drops a metre in slow motion before locking again. Ice curtains fracture, fall, and burst into diamond fog. Predators time their hunts to these avalanches—startled prey makes clumsy choices.

BUILT PRESENCE & TECHNOLOGY  
Stippled among the uppermost ledges stand cairns of bluish slate, each no taller than a person, capped with antler prongs. They are works of the Sky-Dwellers, a people still in the bone-and-basalt era. Their camps never persist—only circles of flattened sedge, a hearth of cracked stone, and crude rope bridges dangling from pitons of carved horn. The bridges look ephemeral yet can bear the weight of a laden goat; they span gaps shorter than ten metres, because that is the longest length raw yak sinew can hold before fraying. Now and then you spot a drill mark, a shallow hole pecked by hand-held flint awaiting a future anchor. It is as if the builders leave promises rather than structures, reminders that humans also pass through.

MATERIALS & COLORS  
Rock runs from pewter to ivory, flecked with micaceous glitter; fresh fractures flash silver for one blue-white instant before frost dulls them. Ice is never just clear; long exposures to sun tint it turquoise at depth, while new growth forms matte white scales like fish skin. Vegetation adds the only strong hues: saturated greens, blues so dark they are nearly black, and in high summer, brief dots of crimson where blood-sedge flowers open for seven days.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Gravity rules but grudgingly—air is thin, and updrafts can prolong a seed’s flight for hours.  
2. Time of day matters more than season: at noon photosynthesis and hunting explode; by early afternoon surfaces freeze again and everything slows.  
3. Nothing decomposes in place; scavengers or slides carry it away.  
4. Any tool you drop will bounce once, stick in a crevice, and be claimed by lichens within the decade.

WEIRDNESS—SUBTLE  
When mist thickens, light refracts inside the slot, projecting duplicates of anyone standing on the rim onto opposing walls. These images—“mirror walkers” to the Sky-Dwellers—move a heartbeat out of sync with their owners. Locals claim the duplicates remember the real person’s last misstep and show how not to repeat it, a gift from the mountain itself.

ATMOSPHERE, THE FEEL OF IT  
Cold burns the nostrils but also clarifies every smell: resin, wet stone, the faint metallic tang of fresh ice. Your bones register altitude before your mind does; each breath is work, yet you keep inhaling simply because the air feels clean enough to drink. Light, everywhere, all the time, until closing your eyes leaves green ghosts dancing.

And always the sense of suspension—between peak and cloud, between bright life and immediate hazard, between what the mountain has already given shape and what it is still inventing. The Frost Nave is a landform in mid-sentence, a clause not yet closed. Anyone entering steps into that unfinished grammar, becomes for a while part of the ongoing edit, and emerges changed—etched a little sharper, a little younger, much like the place itself.
