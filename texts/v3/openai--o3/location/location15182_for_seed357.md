You climb through cloud long before you expect to find ground, and then the ground is green.

Not the scattered moss-and-lichen green familiar to most heights, but a continuous pelt—a living, photosynthetic floor that flexes a little underfoot, pillowed by warm water moving just beneath its skin. From horizon to horizon this plateau is a single organism, or perhaps a braided league of them, stitched together into a continent of tissue. The notion of “bedrock” feels remote; the only stone here stands exposed in blade-like fins where the land has thrust up recently, still glass-sharp and unweathered. Everywhere else, plant matter has already seized the newborn crags, coating them in root-gauze and broad, sun-drunk fronds.

Water is constant, but rarely still. Veils of condensation skim sideways on hot winds, snagging on tall structures that are equal parts tree and tower. The locals call them “weepers,” though there are no locals—just the habit words leave behind. Each weeper is hollow, ribbed inside like a giant reed; warm air races up through the column, gathers moisture, and finally bursts from the crown in a hissing spray. From afar the plateau seems to breathe—great exhalations of silver vapor that rise, condense, and fall again as quick rain. Nothing of that water ever quite leaves the system. It lands, sinks a finger-width into the sponge ground, and is wicked back toward the towers to be lofted once more.

Because everything alive here is also architecture, the land looks built—and in truth it is—but no tool or deliberate hand shaped it. Growth is the sole mason. Alcoves open where the skin of the plateau thins into translucent membranes; they roof warm, dripping chambers whose walls pulse gently as sap moves. Bridges appear where vines fuse in mid-air, thickening into suspended walkways that sway under passing gusts. Whole audiences of leaf-panels fold and refold, adjusting angle to sun and wind like restless seats in an empty stadium.

The heat is heavy, though the altitude is high enough that conventional wisdom would promise chill. Warmth wells from the plateau itself, a metabolic by-product: sugars ferment in deep, oxygen-poor pockets, and the released energy keeps the skin above human-body temperature. Touch the ground too long and sweat beads instantly. At night the temperature barely drops; instead, the surface throbs with an infra-red glow you feel on your lashes.

There is no animal life. Nothing darts, hunts, or sings. Yet the place is not silent—far from it. Sound here is botanical. Stalks knock together like bamboo chimes. Vast, petal-shaped vanes flick open with a floorboard crack, then snap shut when a buffeting thermal tries to tear them loose. Deep beneath, immense root-cables grind slowly against the still-sharp rock blades, producing a basso scrape you feel in bone more than ear. Thunder is common, but real lightning is rare; almost every rumble issuing from the clouds turns out to be the land itself, splitting, knitting, tearing, mending.

Because the plateau is young, its topography is raw: ridges meet at knife points; valleys dive suddenly, then end in vertical pouched pits whose walls sweat so hard they resemble waterfalls made of glass beads. Erosion has had no time to soften any of it, but growth has, in places, already hidden the severity behind curtains of leaves. Now and then you glimpse the truth—an unclad spar of volcanic glass protruding like the keel of an overturned ship—before the windblown vapor conceals it again.

Despite the omnipresence of life, the space between living masses is generous. Whole hectares lie empty but for a low quilt of photosynthetic film, as though the plateau periodically smooths itself to bare canvas, waiting for the next outburst of structures. The emptiness amplifies motion: when a new tower begins to bud, it does so in public, furling up from nothing in hours, spiring to fifty meters before dusk.

Rules here, so far as anyone has bothered to write them:

1. Growth follows stress. Bend a branch and, days later, a corridor thickens along the force line, ready for weight it has never felt before.
2. Water never pools unless invited. Any cavity unattended for more than a day is sealed shut by a quick-knitting scab of cellulose to keep the system’s water aloft and cycling.
3. Decay equals seedbed. Fallen structures rot to sludge within a week; from that slurry, templated buds emerge carrying the memory of whatever fell.
4. Nothing is permanent. Windstorms—common at this altitude—can shear half the visible world away in a night. By the following week, the plateau will already have mistaken the loss for an opportunity and filled the gap with new plan.

Stand still for an hour and the land will begin to draft around you: a ring of rootlets sniffs at your boots, gauging whether you are friend, mulch, or future foundation. Wait long enough and you may find a low wall swelling behind your back, inviting you to stay—or encouraging you not to leave, opinions differ.

Yet for all its turbulence and clamor, the plateau carries an odd serenity. Nothing hunts you. Nothing watches with eyes. The only motive here is expansion, repair, the endless rearrangement of green intentions. You, brief visitor, are noise within that larger noise—soon to be forgotten, perhaps to be incorporated, certainly to be outlived.
