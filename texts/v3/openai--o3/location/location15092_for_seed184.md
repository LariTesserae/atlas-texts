In the charts of the benthic surveyors the site is coded HS-17, but the divers who first slipped through its arched gate call it the Brine Cloister.

TERRAIN
Picture a stone bowl the size of a cathedral nave, sunk forty metres beneath a rain-shattered inland sea. Basalt walls rise like dark choir stalls, their surfaces eased into long, fluted curves by centuries of lazy currents. Nothing here feels jagged or new; every edge has been stroked smooth, as though the whole chamber were carved from wax left too close to a candle.

FLOOR & MATERIALS
The basin’s floor is almost level, paved in interlocking iron plates laid five, maybe six human generations ago. Each plate bears stamped serial numbers and rust-flecked maker’s marks—now illegible under carpets of emerald eelgrass and puffed moss-corals. Where plates have buckled, silt has gathered in shallow hollows, forming mirror-still ponds within the greater water, their surfaces broken only by the rise of a slow, silver exhalation from buried vents.

BUILT PRESENCE
Packed into this single pocket is an industrial hamlet—half-factory, half-cloister—so dense that walls share walls and roofs lean on adjacent roofs, leaving almost no true open space. Cylindrical boiler-halls butt against buttresses of a drowned chapel; pipe gantries web overhead like iron ivy; staircases curl between levels of catwalks in layers three and four deep. Everything is designed for humans who no longer visit, yet all remains upright, orderly, waiting.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT
The water is warm—as warm as a tepid bath—its heat welling from slow, unseen geothermal seams. Light filters from the storming surface high above but wanes to a perpetual dusk before it reaches the basin. Illumination is mostly the hazed glow of planktonic threads, pale green and pulse-timed; their light passes through particulate silt like sun through stained glass. Lanternfish are almost never seen, yet the walls gleam softly where colonies of photophilic algae coat the old basalt in sheathes of veiled jade.

VEGETATION
Despite the iron and stone, the Cloister is lush. Kelp with coin-shaped fronds clings to every rail. Feather-ferns sprout from bolt holes. On the chapel’s collapsed rood screen, crimson cup-plants fold themselves like yawning carnations, releasing slow torrents of oxygen bubbles that drift upward, gather under overhangs, then slide away, bell-clear and perfect. Roots explore seam-welds, crack rivets, and hang in curtains across corridor mouths, turning aisles into watery hedge-mazes.

SOUNDS
Quiet reigns. There is no crash of surf here, only three kinds of noise:
1. A faint, rhythmic thrum rising from the factory core—ancient pumps still drawing brine through titanic filters.
2. The occasional tick of metal contracting as temperature slides a fraction of a degree.
3. The hissless bloom of bubbles when a plant cusp opens, as brief and polite as a sigh in a library.

FAUNA
Life moves sparingly. A single pair of glasshead groupers claim the upper vault, circling the same slow ellipses year after year. Pale shrimp graze the algae by the thousands, but they are almost invisible until a torchbeam passes. Once a day—never on a schedule the divers can predict—a great manta-shape of living mercury drifts through, absorbing planktonic light, giving none of its own. Nothing hunts openly; energy is too precious for haste.

RULES & WEIRDNESS
1. Corrosion stalls here. Leave a blade overnight and it shows less rust in a week than topside steel gains in a day. Some speculate the pumps saturate the water with trace inhibitors; others mutter about the basilica’s original purpose—an electro-chemical sanctuary whose rituals still echo.
2. Sound refuses to carry gossip. Shout, and your words crumble five metres from your lips, no matter how strong your lungs or amplifier. Yet the factory’s heart-beat can be heard throughout the basin, unmistakable and omnipresent.
3. Growth is proportional to stillness: the longer an object remains unmoved, the more luxuriant the vegetation that grips it. Disturb a ladder and its frond-coating withers within hours, only to begin regrowing the moment you swim away.

DYNAMIC
Change, here, is an affair of generations. Pumps slow by imperceptible margins; plates sag by millimetres per decade. Entire mechanical halls have settled on mud cushions so gradually that the gantries crossing them remain level, like instruments on a ship whose hull is becoming the sea. If a wall is going to fall, it will take its time—sending a prophetic rattle once every few months so the patient ecosystem can sidle clear before collapse.

WHAT THE PLACE OFFERS
• For engineers: valves big as bell-towers, still greased, still willing to turn if coaxed.
• For botanists: species found nowhere else—warm-water kelp that photosynthesises using the dimmest green.
• For hermits: silence deeper than solitude, a hush in which thoughts conduct themselves at the same unhurried pace as the tides.
• For poets: a living paradox—an industrial garden, a mechanical monastery, a drowned room that nevertheless breathes.

Stand mid-chamber, hand on a rail grown thick with lace-moss, and wait. Soon the plankton lights will flare, the pumps will exhale once, and the slow bubbles will shoulder past. Nothing more dramatic than that will happen today, or tomorrow, or next year—and that enduring, deliberate calm is the Brine Cloister’s most faithful law.
