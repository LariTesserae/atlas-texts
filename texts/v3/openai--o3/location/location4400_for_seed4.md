Just inside the bark-wall of an old, hollow trunk—no wider than a modest hall and no higher than a two-storey cottage—an entire city breathes, dripping and dark.

TERRAIN  
The “ground” is not ground at all but a tangle of living, pallid roots that have thrust themselves into a shallow pool of iron-cold seepage. The pool is never more than ankle-deep, yet it rings the chamber like a moat. Patches of slick cambium rise from it as islands: sharp little shelves only days or weeks old, their edges still bright and uncured where the parent tree split itself apart. Because everything here is so new—freshly torn, freshly grown—lines stay crisp; even the smallest fracture has a blade-keen edge that will cut skin if brushed.

WATER  
Moisture weeps constantly from the ceiling of wood overhead. Every heartbeat, another droplet forms, grows gravid, and falls with a muffled plink into the pool. Mist lingers close to the surface; the air is nearly saturated, and exhalations of warm bodies flash into miniature clouds before fading.

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
Despite the enclosing bark the place is cold, a still 5 °C at most. There is no outside sun. What illumination exists comes from slow, greenish pulses along vascular fibers in the roots and from the eyes of animals. Most of the time the chamber is black, shapes implied only by smell and texture. When light does appear it is momentary and local, casting living silhouettes that scurry away again.

VEGETATION  
The tree is the architecture. Huts, platforms, even causeways are coaxed rather than built: thin cambium is scored with stone flakes so it thickens where desired, branches are tied while supple so they knit into latticed walkways, and leaf clusters are folded into roofs still attached to their petioles, so they keep growing, repairing every tear. Nothing here is dead wood; if left untended it heals, swells, and slowly devours any rope, hide, or bone that rests against it.

BUILT—URBAN, PRIMITIVE  
Along the inner circumference a ring of thirty or so dwellings clings to the bark: oval doors half submerged, smokeholes that never vent more than a ghost of warm breath, narrow ledges where tools dry. The craft is simple—woven reed mats, knapped-stone adzes, cords of braided gut—but it is systematic, repeated, a recognized skyline in miniature. A single footbridge—grown, not lashed—arches across the pool; at its apex hangs a basket that serves as communal meeting place, an airy town square suspended in murk.

FAUNA—OVERWHELMING  
Life moves everywhere the eye fails. Waterfilm slugs browse the slick roots, leaving shining trails. Hand-sized amphipods jerk in the shallows, feeding on the perpetual rain of bark-lice that fling themselves from higher niches. Above, the walls squirm with rope-long millipedes whose boots click faintly in the hush. Predators appear as sudden rearrangements of darkness: the snap of a fern-mantis folding its leaves around something squealing, or the vibration of membrane wings from eyeless moth-bats that drink sap straight from the ceiling veins. Nothing is large by outside standards, but in the confines of the hollow they dominate, their numbers endless, their presence felt as a crawling pressure on the senses.

SOUND  
For all that life, the soundscape is subdued: drip, drip, drip; the soft grate of chitin; the occasional slap of something breaching the pool’s skin. Speech, when it happens, is a brief throat-clicking almost lost in the damp.

DYNAMICS  
The whole micro-world is in slow motion yet never still. Roots thicken overnight, forcing huts to shift. New fissures open without warning and must be bridged by dawn or risk isolating a family. Predator cycles are felt weekly—three nights of silence when the fern-mantises hunt, followed by frantic daytimes of breeding bark-lice. Weather, in a place without sky, is a matter of internal pressure: warm exhalations from the tree’s deeper sapwood sometimes fog the chamber so completely that vision ends at one’s own wrist.

RULES & WEIRDNESS  
1. Wood heals. Any cut surface will close within an hour unless wedged apart.  
2. Water cannot be boiled; the ambient cold and constant influx wick heat away quicker than crude hearths can supply it. Soups are fermented instead of cooked.  
3. The tree listens. Rhythmic pounding or repeated chants change the pattern of sap pulses, coaxing certain walls to bulge, others to hollow. No one knows whether this is sentience or reflex, but every family has its own cadence, its own architectural song.  
4. Fire is taboo, partly by custom, partly because sparks wake the deeper fauna: pale, many-jawed things that drum inside the trunk when disturbed. The last time flames were kindled, four generations ago, one entire quarter of the settlement vanished into a crack that had not existed the night before.

STANDING HERE  
Kneel on a sweating cambium shelf; you will feel it tremble with the heartwood’s beat. Dip a finger in the pool and it comes back tasting of minerals and leaf-bitter tannin. Breathe: resin, cold rot, the metallic hint of arthropod blood. Listen: a single droplet falls somewhere behind you, and hundreds of soft bodies freeze to hear where it lands. Then, as though a signal passed, they all resume their crawl, and the chamber is alive again—hidden, wet, frozen, and immensely, intimately awake.
