At first you hear almost nothing—only the hush of warm water coursing around you, the way blood murmurs in the ear—but when your senses widen a little you notice a faint, distant hiss, as though an endless sheet of rain is falling somewhere just outside your field of vision.  In a way, it is.

TERRAIN AND WATER  
The place is a vast, high-topped massif that has only lately shouldered itself out of the planet’s mantle.  It stands so tall that its summit floats within thick, light-eating cloud, yet the summit itself is not dry land at all; it is a young interior sea, an embryonic ocean caught in the still-forming crown of the mountain.  Picture a crater tens of kilometers across, brimful with warm water that never quite spills because the rim keeps climbing, millimeter by millimeter, with the heat of slow subterranean convection.  The sea feels almost suspended in the sky.  Along the inner walls of the cirque, half-melted terraces of basalt show raw tool-marks where stone has been prised away for dwellings; much of the rock is still glassy, newly cooled, tasting faintly of salt and iron when you press a finger to it.

ATMOSPHERE AND LIGHT  
Light is hard to come by.  The cloud ceiling overhead is so thick—sodden with the water that evaporates from the sea below and immediately condenses again—that noon looks like a sunless dawn.  A dull silver glow filters down in shafts that wobble with every eddy of the upper wind.  Underwater, visibility is better: the surface acts like a pale mirror, and whatever light does pierce the clouds is scattered through the shallows, turning the first two or three meters into a luminous milk where outlines blur and distance is guesswork.

SOUND  
Because the air is dense with humidity and there are no strong winds this high, sound carries poorly.  You can shout from the rim and a friend paddling at the center of the crater might never hear.  Close by, though, every action feels amplified: the creak of a vine-rope, the slosh of a paddle, the single drip of water from leaf to leaf.  Farther off all noises dissolve into that omnipresent sigh of rainfall—rain that is not falling from above but rising from below and descending again in an unbroken loop.

VEGETATION  
Life has wasted no time exploiting the new basin.  Lush vines the width of an arm dangle from ledges into the water, each vine sprouting sheets of cupped leaves that drink directly from the warm sea.  Spongy mats of Burgundy-colored moss quilt the cave mouths, expanding so fast you can almost watch them creep.  Giant bladder-ferns float free like rafts, their undersides veined with filaments that filter minerals from the juvenile ocean.  In shaded coves, luminous algae paint the walls with teal constellations, giving every night voyage the feeling of rowing through inverted starlight.

FAUNA  
Animals are scarce, cautious, often silent.  The commonest sight is the glass-wing ray, a cartilaginous creature that has learned to “fly” half out of the water, flapping into the saturated air then gliding back with barely a splash.  Deeper down, pale spiral eels haunt the newborn thermal vents, each eel coiled around its own personal plume of warmth.  On land—the precious little of it that exists—quick, dark shapes the size of cats slip through the viney curtain, leaving dew-wet footprints shaped like upside-down hearts.  No one has named them yet; encounters are too rare.

HUMANS AND BUILT SPACE  
A people live here, though calling their settlement a city would be generous.  They occupy the narrow rock cornices and a flotilla of interlinked reed barges tethered to basalt moorings.  Their tools are of obsidian, shell, and the occasional hammered meteor iron scavenged from high ridges.  Houses are bulbous baskets of plaited vine, half submerged so that doorways open directly onto water.  The oldest communal hall—barely ten years old—is a ring of standing stones jammed into still-plastic lava; when the mountain heaves at night, the stones tilt a fraction more, and each morning the townsfolk debate whether to prop them up again or let them lean where they will.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Gravity wavers near the thermal vents: warm columns of air and vapor create buoyant corridors where stone tools can be lifted with one hand though they weigh thirty kilos elsewhere.  Children play at tossing pumice through these corridors to see whose stone floats longest before falling.  
2. Fire is almost impossible in the open—the air is too damp—so all heat comes from in-ground vents or from chemical reactions coaxed out of certain algae fats.  
3. Written words decay faster than memory.  Palm-leaf scrolls mildew within months, bark slates delaminate in weeks.  Oral history is therefore law; the elders’ nightly recitations under a net of glow-moss dictate where canoes may travel, which cliffs may be quarried, whom one may marry.  
4. Time moves slowly on the mountain.  Erosion has barely begun its work; a dropped hammer will still lie where it fell a year later, cockpit-clean.  Yet the whole massif is rising so gradually that only elders with long memories know the waterline used to be lower by the span of a wrist.

DYNAMICS AND LONG VIEW  
Imagine returning after an age.  The crater-sea will have widened, perhaps broken through a low saddle to birth waterfalls that tumble into the cloud-wreathed valleys below.  The reed barges may calcify into petrified rafts, forming the nucleus of coral-stone towers.  The glass-wing rays might evolve into true fliers, haunting the dim air the way gulls haunt coasts elsewhere.  But for now the world is paused at the dawn of its own story: warm, dim, water-drowned, and teeming with possibility, every surface a palimpsest on which the first light scratchings of civilization have just begun to show.
