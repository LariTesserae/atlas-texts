The place is neither cave nor forest nor city, yet it borrows rules from all three.

1. Arrival  
You descend through a split in the sediment, knees brushing spongy root-walls, heat drifting upward as if the planet exhales. Five metres below the daylight the crack widens. Suddenly you stand inside an under-dim grotto the size of a cathedral nave: thirty paces long, fifteen wide, twelve high. The roof is a vault of living green, and every surface that should have been stone has been replaced—slowly, over centuries—by patient, photosynthetic matter.

2. Terrain and material  
• Floor: an undulating plain of lignified moss that feels like cork underfoot. Here and there it opens into shallow basins where condensation has collected, mirror-pools no wider than a dinner table.  
• Walls: braided trunks the width of train rails, fused into flying buttresses. Their bark is translucent jade, and inside you glimpse fiber channels pumping sap in lazy pulses.  
• Ceiling: sheets of leaf-glass—broad, thin fronds hardened by mineral uptake. They diffuse the only light: a weak, lime-colored glow generated by symbiotic algae threaded through the veins.

Erosion has long finished its argument with these structures. Nothing crumbles; everything has found its equilibrium between growth and decay.

3. Atmosphere, water, temperature  
Warm, like the inside of a greenhouse at dusk. The air holds permanent humidity but rarely free water; moisture beads on plant surfaces, then slides into the basins where it lingers before evaporating. A faint metallic tang rides the humidity—iron shavings, machine oil, something combusted but faint.

4. The built layer  
Toward the rear of the chamber the vegetation rearranges itself into purpose: a low, circular platform grown from root-wood, ringed by iron rails that are clearly not a plant’s doing. Pneumatic tubes, bolted to living columns, hiss as they shuttle canisters deeper into unseen levels. A pulley crane, its lattice grown partly from hardened vine, partly from riveted steel, creaks overhead. The city above abandoned these works generations ago; the plants did not. They learned the gear ratios, blanketed the belts with chlorophyll, kept everything running in slow, dreaming obedience.

5. Light  
No shafts of sun reach here. Instead, bioluminescent moths—dozens at any moment, thousands overall—wander circulating thermals, their wings scribbling pale gold arcs. More constant are the umber pilot-lights of fungus bulbs embedded in the walls, each the size of a thumbnail. The combined glow is enough to navigate but not enough to make color trustworthy.

6. Sound  
The place is loud in layers.  
First layer: a waterfall of insect chatter—clicks, rasping wings, drumming legs on hollow bark.  
Second: the vegetal hydraulics—a peristaltic thump each time the great trunks shift nutrient solution.  
Third: machinery—belt joints clacking, a steam relief valve exhaling every forty seconds, the low rotational growl of a dynamo somewhere beneath the floor. The sounds never synchronize; the chamber’s rhythm is polyrhythmic, restless.

7. Fauna—overwhelming  
Everything that moves is multiple. Vine-rats with fern tails scurry in visible armies, always in search of the next algal crust. Three-eyed perch live in the condensation pools, feeding on the constant rain of insect carapace. But the space truly belongs to the Flock: bat-sized locust heirs whose wings are transparent except for a lattice that resembles printed circuit boards. They come in storms, hanging from the ceiling fronds until a pressure only they feel triggers liftoff; then the air becomes a living grinder, audible first as a wind, felt next as a thousand wing-beats against the skin. The plants do not mind; the Flock trim excessive growth, pollinate, carry spores outward through vent shafts.

8. Rules and strangeness  
• Green replaces stone. Anything abandoned long enough is digested, its blueprint half-remembered in cellulose.  
• Muscle and engine merge. Roots wrap gear-teeth, sap coagulates into bearing grease, xylem becomes conduit.  
• Predation is contractual. Nothing here hunts to extinction; everything feeds in passes, pruning rather than erasing.  
• Light is a privilege you barter for noise. When the chamber quiets, luminescence dims; when the great chorus swells, every bulb brightens as though applauding the sound.

9. Dynamics—what happens if you stay  
Within an hour the Flock will pass twice, siphoning stray salt from your sweat as they go. Moss underfoot will sample your pressure pattern and begin knitting matching ridges, eager to replicate what it finds useful. If you touch a trunk, its surface will ripple, extruding a feeler the texture of soft kelp; it will taste the air you displaced and decide whether the local plumbing needs recalibration. Should you open a canister on the rail, the platform will rotate—slow, unstoppable—presenting a downward spiral staircase of intertwined roots and iron. Wind from that shaft smells of coal smoke and blooming jasmine. Down there, rumor says, are larger halls where entire locomotives have been assimilated, running perpetual loops to grind minerals for the canopy farms.

10. Departure  
The exit, when you want it, is always at the far end from where you stand, because the chamber edits itself to delay goodbyes. But it does not imprison. After sufficient wandering you reach a root-ramp curling upward, its surface stippled with bioluminescent lichen arrows. Climbing, you notice the hum of machinery fading, replaced by the chirr of surface cicadas and, finally, the pale daylight above soil. When you look back, the ramp’s mouth has sealed into an unremarkable couch of moss. Only the faint metallic hiss of unseen pistons reminds you the under-city is still working, still growing, still listening.
