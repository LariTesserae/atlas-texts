The place has the proportions of a continent but the relief of a tabletop. From horizon to horizon the land lies almost level, no peak breaking its line, the highest points no more than a few metres above the lowest. Yet nothing here is “natural ground.” The entire surface is a manufactured crust—though no hand, tool, or machine shaped it.

1. The substrate  
Look down and the floor shows itself as a tessellation of broad plates, each roughly oval, two to five metres across. Their edges fit like the joined scales of some gigantic reptile. They are pale ochre where sun-bleached, umber where still damp, glassy where the newest plates have not yet weathered. If you prise a fragment free (they come loose with a resonant crack) the break reveals a layered interior: alternating sheets of silicate gel and fine calcareous dust, each no thicker than paper. Every sheet was precipitated by a microbial film that lived only long enough to lay down one lamina before drying out in the heat and sealing its work beneath the next colony’s effort. Over eons the overlapping films have lifted themselves clear of the original mudflats, forming a continent-sized, endlessly self-renewing pavement.  

The plates buckle slightly in places. There, where the sub-layers are still soft, you can kneel and feel gentle warmth radiating up—fermentation heat from the living underside. The crust is literally still curing, still being born.

2. Water, but only just  
Moisture moves mostly as vapour. Dawn haze beads upon the plate edges and trickles into hairline seams, then vanishes at noon. After three or four cloudless days the air convects violently; narrow storm cells rise straight from the sun-hot plain and dump hard rain on footprints of a few square kilometres. The water runs off almost at once, collecting in long, shallow swales that look like dark mirror-ribbons from above. By afternoon most of the pools have already withdrawn into cracks or flashed back to sky. For a traveller the world feels dry, yet every night you can taste damp metal on the breeze.

3. Sparse vegetation  
Where plates have shifted apart by more than a thumb’s width, threads of rootless flora claim the gap. Picture waist-high stalks the colour of copper patina, jointed like bamboo, leaves reduced to scales that lie flat against the stem to conserve water. They photosynthesise at dawn and dusk, curling tight through the glare of midday. Their seeds are microscopic flakes of silica that become airborne with the least gust, seeking new mortar lines to colonise. Nothing grows in clumps; a single stalk here, another twenty paces on. The emptiness between them is part of the plant’s strategy—it relies on distance, not competition, to keep its gleaned moisture.

4. Atmosphere and sound  
The air is clear, almost harsh in its transparency. At midday colours bleach toward white and shadow. Heat ripples warp the horizon until distant plate edges waver like water. There is little wind most of the time, so the plain is quiet. A listener hears three recurring sounds:  
• a soft, continuous crackle—microscopic fractures propagating through the outer glaze of each plate as temperatures swing;  
• an occasional tink of falling flakes when a plate edge cools too rapidly and sheds a sliver;  
• the far-off boom of convection thunder when the sky finally surrenders its hoarded moisture.  
Silence here is rarely absolute, but it is thin, the auditory equivalent of empty sky.

5. No animals  
Nothing walks, crawls, swims, or flies. Without predators the plants have no toxins, no spines. The microbial mats are the engineers and the engineers alone. You can sleep in the open without fear, though the plates’ slow thermal sighing will pass into your dreams.

6. Processes in motion  
Although erosion barely registers—the crust is too young for it—the whole landscape is restless. Plates grow from beneath even as their tops craze and crack, steadily thickening the ground. Storm cells wander the continent in irregular circuits, each shower polishing a fresh tract of pavement. The silica-bamboo stalks migrate with the fractures, forever stationed at the newest seams. Nothing here is static; it is only quiet.

7. Rules of the world  
• All land is product, nothing substrate. Life is the mason, not an inhabitant of a ready-made stage.  
• Water is transient, never stored. Survival depends on timing, not reservoir.  
• Heat is the main currency: microbial sheets farm it, plants avoid it, weather spends it.  
• Absence is as active a principle as presence—space, silence, and the long intervals between stalks are functional parts of the design.

Stand long enough and you will witness one more phenomenon. Just before sunset, as the surface finally cools, entire plate fields flex imperceptibly downward. A chorus of almost musical pings travels kilometres in a minute. It is the sound of newborn geology exhaling, of a continent reminding itself that it is still being written.

And then darkness spreads, swift and even. The pavement, still radiant from the day, glows faintly amber for half an hour—light leaking from within the layers. When that flush fades, the plain becomes featureless under the stars, a low, damp, hot, silent colossus waiting for tomorrow’s sun to draft the next thin page of stone.
