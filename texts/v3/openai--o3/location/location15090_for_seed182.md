The descent begins in near-blackness.  The last daylight seeps down a twisting ventilation shaft, touches the damp brick of a service stair, and gives up; from then on, vision belongs to what little the cavern itself gives off.  At first you think the glow comes from electric sconces recessed in the walls, but no: it is the stone that shines—a bruise-colored ore that traps the day’s heat and leaks it back as a deep violet radiance.  Dim though it is, it is enough to coax a jungle out of rock.

The floor of the grotto—call it a hall, a chamber, a buried cathedral, none of the words quite fit—is scarcely the size of a town square, yet it feels overcrowded the way a greenhouse can feel overcrowded, every cubic meter already spoken for.  Columns of raw basalt, left standing when the rest of the flow was quarried away, shoulder up beside buttressed apartment blocks of poured slag-concrete.  Gantries of rust-red iron criss-cross overhead, carrying steam lines, tram cables, and the twitching roots of epiphytes that have learned to cling to hot metal.  Between the columns and the housing stacks, improvised catwalks stitch together second and third stories.  The air is thick, 43 °C even during the nominal “night,” and forever beaded with condensation that drips from girders like a soft, slow rain.

Water is here only in flickers.  A narrow culvert carries runoff from somewhere higher in the crust; it flashes past under a grating, audible for ten steps and then gone.  A sump in the far corner mirrors the ceiling with an oily sheen, its surface periodically broken by breathy bubbles of geothermal gas.  Residents capture the damp instead of the flow: every facade bristles with gutters, funnels, and tilt-panes of cracked glass, all engineered to channel droplets toward tanks embedded in the walls.  You can hear the rivulets running inside them—steady, patient, a plumbing system more vascular than mechanical.

Vegetation, denied broad water but granted constant warmth, answers with frenzy.  Feathery ferns the height of children sprout from seams between bricks.  Lianas the width of a wrist loaf around steam pipes, teasing loose valves so that gouts of white vapor feed their root-nodes.  The city’s architects no longer pretend to prune: the growth has become load-bearing.  A footbridge is lashed to the bowed trunks of cave-palms; a stair is carved straight through a buttress-fungus whose flesh has the color and texture of smoked glass.  Every green thing sports leaves too glossy, too dark—plants that photosynthesize by stealing the violet from the ore’s glow or by sipping at trace chemistries in the mist.  At times the leaves flex softly, as though tasting the moisture in the air.

Fauna is mostly rumor.  Sometimes a moth the size of a thumb prints pale sigils against the cavern walls, folds its wings, disappears.  Once in an hour you might glimpse a salamander like a piece of molten copper slipping between drainage slots.  The city’s inhabitants, human or something near enough, keep no pets; meat spoils too quickly here, and anything that crawls is regarded as a precious recycler, not prey.

The technological age stalled at an industrial plateau and then dug in.  Pulleys, belt drives, and reciprocating pistons lurk inside every building.  A single slow turbine down in the sump turns on free geothermal draft, providing just enough current for the copper cables that lace the ceiling like half-invisible harp strings.  When the turbine coughs, the lights dim further; people pause mid-conversation and count silently until the rhythm steadies again.  Work is timed not by clocks but by the breathing of engines: the long inhalation of boilers at dawn-shift, the tired exhalation when the pressure is vented for maintenance.  Progress here happens by accretion—new layers of workshop built atop the old when ceiling height allows, redundant machinery repurposed as habitat when it finally siezes.

Sound is less than you expect.  The ceiling swallows echoes.  The turbines hush themselves beneath insulated shrouds of moss and felted vine.  Conversations travel only as far as two, three bodies away before the warm, wet air wrings them out.  What remains is a hush interrupted by rhythmic ticks: condensate striking metal, the slow beat of a settling pipe, a distant hammer only barely alive.  In the quiet you sometimes imagine you can hear the growth of roots—a creaking so faint it feels more like the memory of a sound than the sound itself.

The ground underfoot is young.  Rock still wears fracture marks from the blasting that opened this place a generation ago.  Edges are sharp, corners unrounded; even the stalactites, where they exist, have not yet smoothed into drips but end in swords of calcite.  Time moves, certainly—it is scrawled in layers of pipe replaced, in vines that thicken by millimeters—but it moves at the pace of geothermal erosion, not flood or wind.

Rules of this sunless world:

1. Heat descends but never leaves; waste heat is a commodity, traded in insulated flasks.  
2. Water belongs to whoever can condense it; theft is measured in droplets.  
3. Light is shared.  Any lamp over ten lumens must be hooded; blind a sprout and you buy the sprout’s replacement.  
4. Machinery must never outnumber plants.  Keep the ratio and the air stays sweet; ignore it and the violets in the stone go out.  
5. Nothing is destroyed if it can be grown over, swallowed, recycled into living mass.  Rust is only the first stage of compost.

Stand long enough and the place will hood itself around you: a leaf unfolding to capture your exhaled moisture, a vine nosing toward the salt at your collar, a resident tapping your shoulder to ask whether you’ve brought seeds—or a spare bearing for the pump.  They treat both as equally alive here.  Everything feeds everything.  Everything is close, slow, damp, and waiting.
