First impression  
You step out of the elevator cage and the world drops away beneath your boots. The wind is warm, tasted of iron filings and wet leaves. All around, to every horizon you can see, the landscape is a single, interlocking work of masonry, alloy and cultivated greenery—a continent-sized hanging garden built against the sky.

1. Terrain and structure  
• The ground you stand on is not ground at all but the crown of a tiered platform, one of thousands that step upward and outward like petrified surf. Each tier is ten to thirty metres high, faced in weather-softened brick and riveted steel. Together they form vertical peninsulas and finger-ridges that climb for kilometres before dissolving into the haze.

• Extreme elevation defines everything. The lowest terraces begin near sea level, but the highest balconies butt against the stratosphere. Canted suspension bridges, freight trams and stair-cables traverse gulfs whose depths you can barely discern through the mist.

• Erosion is visible but resigned: corners rounded, iron plates patinaed green, rain-slick gutters colonised by moss. It is an old, confident artifice that long ago achieved its final shape and has since merely mellowed.

2. Water (damp, intermittent)  
• No rivers carve these heights, yet water is ever present as a thin, moving skin. Dawn fog condenses on condenser-reeds—long brass quills that line every parapet—dripping into cisterns that feed hydroponic plots. At midday you hear the hiss of vapour bleeding from thermal pipes, and once or twice a week a broad-backed cloud smacks into the terraces and bursts, delivering a fast, drumming rain that sluices along stone gutters.

• Here and there the terraces dip into bowl-shaped catchments; within them sit mirror-still ponds a few arm-spans across—sky-wells where dragonflies patrol and engine grease swirls in oily crescents.

3. Vegetation (lush, dense)  
• Every level is planted. Fruit canes crawl up guy wires; banyan analogues punch roots through expansion joints and dangle aerial curtains that cows chew as they pass. Ivy fingers stitch the brick seams. Humid grottoes under staircases host cymbidium orchids so numerous their scent—smoke and honey—becomes the default smell of the city-range.

• Gardeners are everywhere, though you rarely see them exerting themselves. Most of the work is done by gravity irrigation and slow, deliberate pruning automatons—brass-shouldered machines that move no faster than a strolling child.

4. Climate and light  
• Temperature hovers in the warm band: 25–30 °C on most days, cooler only where altitude thins the air. Brilliant sunlight floods the upper façades. Where natural daylight would be shadowed by the next terrace, great heliostat mirrors tilt and shimmer, pouring reflected beams onto crop squares and power collectors. The total effect is brightness with few true shadows—an ever present golden reveal.

5. Soundscape (loud, industrial)  
• Nothing here is silent. From below rises the guttural pulse of low-rpm turbines pumping water and air. Cargo hoists clatter, rack-and-pinion trams rasp along elevated rails, whistle valves exhale. Add to that the biological choir—parakeets skirling through archways, cicadas test-buzzing rivet seams, caprid bells clonking as animals wander balcony pastures. The noise never peaks into frenzy, but it never rests; it is a constant texture, like surf heard inland.

6. Technology (industrial tier)  
• The machinery is bulky, riveted, tangible. Steam conduits share space with early electric cabling bundled in tarred cloth. Power is decentralized: every second terrace bears a heat-sink tower that vents white plumes. Workshops turn out brass sprockets, rubber seals, porcelain insulators—parts meant to be repaired a hundred times rather than replaced once.

• Yet for all the engines, the settlement lacks hurry. Conveyor belts ambulate at walking speed, cranes move with measured inevitability. The civilisation’s motto seems to be: Plenty of time; the stones aren’t going anywhere.

7. Fauna (abundant)  
• Life has embraced the scaffold. Swifts have learned to nest inside exhaust cowls warmed by waste steam. Broad-shell tortoises graze rooftop algae films. A species of cliff-ibex descends ladders as confidently as natural rock. Rodent analogues travel the pipe maze so effectively that plumbers hang bells on any conduit they intend to enter. Predatory hawks perch on lightning rods, their white droppings tracing hieroglyphs down the steel.

8. Rules of the world  
• Gravity and weather obey familiar physics, but culture has chosen permanence and verticality over sprawl. “Natural ground” is mythic; children are told bedtime stories of dirt. Everything that grows does so by invitation, every animal that lives here has discovered the niches left by builders.

• Change happens, but very, very slowly—an accretionary mindset. A new terrace might be proposed, surveyed, funded and raised over the span of three generations. Conversely, nothing is ever demolished; pieces are cannibalised, facades are over-plated, voids become vaults or cisterns. The city-range is a palimpsest of its own history.

9. Subtle weirdness (low)  
• The only truly uncanny note is the absence of any geological substrate in living memory. Archivists claim that a mountain once stood beneath the first tier, quarried away to build the structure that replaced it. Whether stone still lurks under the deepest vaults or whether the whole edifice hangs, cantilevered from an impossible root, no one can confirm. The question troubles philosophers more than engineers.

10. Standing still for a moment  
Lean against a lichen-mottled rail and watch. Overhead a freight dirigible puffs along the wind-corridor, trailing its own shadow over terrace after terrace before the light swallows it. Vines shiver as a family of white-bellied martens skitters past. Somewhere far below, a klaxon sounds the slow-hour shift change—three notes, unhurried, echoing up the stone groves like the bell of some titanic cathedral.

Everything here is made; everything here is alive. Stone, steel, sap and steam occupy the same ecological parchment, crowded, balanced, slowly weathering under a bright, generous sun.
