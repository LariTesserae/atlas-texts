The ridge is a long, bent finger of rock, lifted well above the valley clouds yet still below the higher ice peaks that shine farther south. Here the air is cool but not cruel, the sort that draws a faint vapour from one’s mouth at dawn and leaves a delicate wetness on stone at dusk. In the notch of the ridge, occupying scarcely a square kilometre, lies the settlement of Cyclion—too small to be called a city, too deliberately wrought to be a mere hamlet.

TERRAIN  
The ground is an old plateau of slate-blue schist, its surface worn satin-smooth by winds that have had millennia to explore every edge. Between broad sheets of bedrock run shallow gutters, natural fractures widened a finger’s breadth by frost and time. They lead, always, toward the cliff lip where trickles of captured rain vanish in silver threads. Most of the plateau is bare, though occasional bowls in the stone hold a skin of moss or a puddle whose depth never exceeds a thumb.

ELEVATION & WATER  
Moisture is precious but strangely dependable. Mornings summon a travelling fog from the lower valleys; it drifts up the cliffs, meets the abrupt chill of altitude, and condenses instantly. Cyclion’s inhabitants understood this long ago. They have laid out ranks of lean-to frames—simple ribs of cedar lashed with flax rope—on every roof and unclaimed ledge. Overnight, beads of water gather on stretched linen, run down taut cords, and drip into cisterns cut directly into the bedrock. The trickling is the loudest regular sound in the settlement, a thin, glassy patter you can follow through half-sleep.

BUILT FORM  
Structures are low, squared, and widely spaced, as though the designers wanted the plateau itself to remain visible at all times. Walls rise from the natural stone rather than atop it: rough-hewn blocks of the same blue schist, quarried just paces away, stacked without mortar but aligned so precisely that hairline cracks alone betray the joins. Roofs are flat, engineered less as shelter and more as water-gathering terraces. Ladders of seasoned pine rise to each roof; their rungs are burnished by decades of bare feet. At the heart of Cyclion stands a circular open space—forty paces across—its rim engraved with increments of shadow. When the sun is highest, its light falls cleanly down the central shaft of the plaza’s single standing stone, telling noon to anyone who looks.

TECHNOLOGY & CRAFT  
Tools are iron at best, bronze more often, but their makers are deft. Every door latch is a cunning arrangement of bone pins and wooden wedges; every drainage channel has been chiselled with an artisan’s patience. No wheel rolls here—too little flat ground—so sledges, levers, and human strength move the world instead. Night fires burn spruce and juniper in shallow clay braziers; smoke climbs straight up, showing the tranquillity of the air.

VEGETATION & FAUNA  
Plant life stays low: clumps of mountain clove, bottle-green, smelling faintly of pepper; wind-shaved junipers hunched like cats; a few pale birches whose bark peels in curling tongues. Animals are rarer still. Ravens ride the thermals that start late morning, coasting without any need to flap; sometimes they drop snail shells onto rock, then spiral down to feed. A white goat may appear on the far ridge once in a season, its hooves knocking loose a shower of gravel. Otherwise, the settlement’s chickens—quiet, slate-feathered, bred for calm—are the only daily companions.

ATMOSPHERE & SOUND  
Quiet dominates. Conversations carry effortlessly across the plaza, so people speak in a near whisper. The wind is seldom more than a hush, a padded paw passing over the plateau. You notice negatives: the absence of insects buzzing, the absence of rustling leaves, the absence of rushing streams. What remains is the irregular metronome of water drops, the clack of a chisel against stone, and, on festival nights, the low animal drone of a bull-horn flute.

EROSION & TIME  
Everything here feels negotiated: stone tested and allowed to stay, wood tolerated until it yields. Walls show shallow scallops where rain and grit have abraded softer bands of schist; inscriptions are half-readable, their original, sharper faces sloughed away like old skin. Yet nothing looks in danger of collapsing. The plateau itself seems to have reached an agreement with gravity, a long, slow bow that will take eons to complete.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Water is owned by no person; every cistern is public, and guards are posted solely to prevent contamination.  
2. No new building may hide more than a third of bedrock from the open sky; the ground must always be able to “breathe,” as elders phrase it.  
3. Sound travels farther than sight—if you hear someone working, you are required to make yourself known so they are not startled.  
4. Nothing changes quickly. Elections occur once every forty seasons; terraces are widened by a hand’s width per generation; even the children seem to mature at a measured pace.

DYNAMICS  
The clouds form, the linen nets drink, the cisterns fill, the evening fires glow, and the cycle restarts. Storms rarely climb this high, and when they do, their violence is spent before reaching the ridge. Outsiders sometimes wander up the mule path, stay a week, grow restless at the stillness, and leave before learning the unspoken fifth rule: patience is the true currency here.

If you stand at the edge of the plateau at twilight, the valley below darkens into a bowl of shadow while the peaks above keep their last pink light. Cyclion hovers exactly between them—neither summit nor lowland, neither wild nor mastered—perched forever in a cool, damp equilibrium, quiet as held breath.
