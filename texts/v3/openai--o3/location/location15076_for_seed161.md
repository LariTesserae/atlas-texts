You enter it from below.

A stair of wind-planed shelves climbs a cliff so tall that its summit is hidden inside high, pewter haze. The rock is old beyond reckoning—granite stripped of everything loose, its faces rounded like river pebbles though no river has flowed here for a hundred million seasons. The entire massif was lifted once, then eroded almost flat, then hoisted again by some forgotten convulsion; what remains is a last, stubborn spine of continent. It feels less like a mountain than the fossil of a mountain.

WATER  
There is none to see. Every crevice is dry, white-rimmed with salts. Yet at dawn the air itself beads and vanishes before the first touch of sun. Life here survives on that handful of droplets and on vapor pried from clouds by chemical tricks older than leaves.

LIGHT & AIR  
The sun is screened by altitude and a permanent veil of dust lifted from distant deserts. Daylight is never brighter than late twilight; shadows are soft and multiple, as though several faded lamps are hung in different corners of the sky. The air is thin, warm, and restless. Wind claws the stone constantly, moaning in tongues that change by the ledge.

TERRAIN  
The plateau’s crest is no broader than a temple courtyard, but its edge is a broken crown of minarets and buttresses. Caverns have collapsed, leaving needle-slender fins that interlock like the ribs of some giant carcass. Between them lie slot corridors only an armspan wide, floors polished glassy by dust and time. The verticality is overwhelming: the ground is never merely underfoot but also beside you, above you, behind you.

VEGETATION  
Despite the thirst, greenery crowds every niche. It is not the green of meadows but a dusk-colored jungle pressed flat against stone:  
• Ladder-lichens form quilted mats, each cell a cupped mirror that snares the morning condensation.  
• Rootless “hammock vines” cling by resin disks, hanging thick curtains of flexible, hollow strands that store both water and sugars; cut one and it bleeds a syrupy sap with the smell of ginger and iron.  
• Plate cacti less than a finger thick overlap like shingles, their skins waxy black to drink the weak light.  
All grow slowly—centuries pass before a vine reaches the next ledge—but in this cramped space they overlap so tightly that rock is visible only where creatures or humans have kept paths clear.

FAUNA  
The plants feed a surprising multitude. Broad-winged gliders—skin-winged, beakless—slip from pinnacle to pinnacle, screaming territorial arias that echo loud as cathedral bells. In the gloom under the vines, fist-sized gastropods rasp the stone for lichen dust; their shells are polished by constant crowding against one another. Burrowing mice with translucent fur share tunnels with black-eyed crickets; both harvest the nightly dew, storing beads of water in cheek or gullet. Predation is brief and brutal when it happens, but most creatures live by theft of moisture rather than flesh.

SOUND  
Wind is the bass note, continuous. Over it rise the gliders’ metallic cries, the clatter of slug-shells, the dry hiss of cacti venting excess heat at noon. During the hour of dawn the whole plateau rings: thousands of plants snap open their dew-traps in unison, a brittle crackling like distant fire.

THE SETTLEMENT  
On the south face lies Khar Teles, “the Stacked Place,” home to perhaps three hundred people. Their architecture follows the cliff’s logic. Rooms are chiseled inward, fronted by balconies no wider than a bedroll. Each household keeps a ladder of twisted vine-rope; walkways overlap in a spiderweb whose strands quiver underfoot. Tools are bone, obsidian, or hammered copper scavenged from ores exposed by erosion. Every surface is used twice: roofs are drying racks for salt-meat, floors are cisterns chiseled to catch dew that drips from ceiling nets of woven fiber. Nothing here rusts—there is too little water—so the oldest copper blades still carry a mirror finish that remembers centuries of sunset.

TECH & CULTURE  
The people are master carvers of the soft outer granite rind; with stone hammers and serpentine chisels they fashion spiraled gutters that guide the nightly condensation into communal basins. They fire clay in wind-fed kilns built into natural chimneys, producing dark, lightweight ceramic traded to the lowlands far below. A code of ledge etiquette governs movement: you must sing—a low, two-note whistle—before stepping onto any shared platform, or risk collision in the dimness.

DYNAMICS & TIME  
Almost nothing changes quickly here. A new fruiting lip on a cactus is news; the collapse of a stone blade, when it happens once in a generation, is the stuff of epic. Even the wind’s song shifts only with the slow widening of slots by grain-by-grain attrition. Life endures by accepting the pace: patient vines, slow metabolisms, long memories.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Water is sacred because it never stays. Any wasted drop must be repaid with a day of silence.  
2. Nothing living touches bare stone without purpose; every surface is either harvest ground or foothold.  
3. Heights are given, not conquered: to fall is to exit the world, for no return path exists from the depths below.

STANDING STILL  
Pause on the highest balcony of Khar Teles. Feel warm, dry wind stream past like invisible sand. Hear the plateau breathe—deep, slow, older than your species. Between one heartbeat and the next a glider knifes through the dimness, its shadow larger than itself. The stone beneath your feet has not shifted since before rivers learned to curve, and yet it is eroding, one silenced grain at a time. This is the paradox of the Stacked Place: everything is crowded, everything is loud, and still the years crawl like lichen across stone, patient and inexorable, in a fortress of dryness balanced a mile above the phantom sea that left long ago.
