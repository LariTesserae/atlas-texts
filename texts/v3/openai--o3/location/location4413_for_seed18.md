The place is called the Throat-Workshop, though no one agrees whose throat it is.

TERRAIN, OR WHAT PASSES FOR IT  
Nothing here is geologic. The “ground” is a circular cat-walk of iron lattice a hundred metres across, cantilevered into empty space halfway up a manufactured escarpment that climbs out of sight above the cloud line and plunges into haze below. The wall behind the cat-walk looks like rust-red cliff, but when the wind peels mist away you see rivets, access hatches, and maintenance rails: it is cliff-shaped machinery, a mile-high condenser-forge built centuries ago and now weathered into its own kind of landscape.  

Beyond the rail of the walkway there is only void: a shaft three kilometres wide, square in cross-section and perfectly vertical, the interior of a titanic tower whose roof was never put on. Every surface—floor plates, corbelled buttresses, hanging ducts—has been eroded by ages of mist, oil-rain, and lichen until it mimics badlands topography. The whole thing feels paradoxically ancient and freshly manufactured, like a machine that decided to age the way mountains do.

WATER  
Moisture is intermittent. Eight times a day the condenser-forge vents steam that cools against the higher struts and falls back as warm drizzle. Run-off crawls along bronze gutters, drools through spigots, pauses in shallow trays of hammered copper where tadpole-things wriggle, then disappears through slots in the deck. Outside vent-cycles the air is merely damp enough to bead on steel and keep moss glossy.

VEGETATION  
Every seam leaks life. Thick ropes of jade-dark vines thread the lattice, their leaves flat and wide to drink condensation. Orchids the colour of welding arcs root in crevices of caked lubricant. Because the structure is hollow, sunlight pours from above for most of the day; photosynthetic mats sprawl even on undersides of girders, bending toward reflected light that bounces up from the shaft’s bronze floor far below. The greenery feels opportunistic but also curated, as though the original engineers planted it to keep the metal cool.

TEMPERATURE  
The perpetual updraft from factories deeper in the shaft keeps the air at a sauna-warm 30 °C. Touching the wall is like pressing your palm to the flank of a living animal: comforting, but faintly throbbing with heat.

ELEVATION  
You are already two kilometres above the foothold platforms; another kilometre of wall arcs upward before losing itself in mist. Wind shear howls: warm air rushing upward inside the shaft meets cooler downdrafts that fall along the outer skin of the tower. Wings, if you had them, would tear.

EROSION  
Centuries of this wind and steam have rounded the once-sharp geometry. Lettering on plaques is sanded illegible. Edges of beams have become river-stone smooth. Yet nothing looks ruined; rather, the place seems to have reached equilibrium—machines and elements negotiating a truce.

SCALE & DENSITY  
Though the tower is colossal, the inhabited part—the Throat-Workshop proper—is intimate: the cat-walk, a few inset chambers, a dangling elevator cage. From any given spot you can see from railing to wall in a single glance. Between you and the next human presence stretches echoing emptiness. The void feels vast, the community tiny: six boilermancers, twelve gardeners, a flock of companion corvids, and the machines themselves.

BUILT & TECH  
Every atom was milled, cast, or printed. Even the “soil” in planters is recycled carbon-foam. Overhead, ducted turbines the size of train cars churn, powered by furnaces lower down. Bronze capstans creak, turning belts that lift cargo. Cables hum with direct current; porcelain insulators bloom fungus-green lichen. A slow, ceaseless industrial heartbeat underlies everything.

LIGHT  
Normal daylight descends through the open roof, diffuse and gold, caught in drifting steam. At night, sodium lamps buried in the wall silhouette vines in amber halos.

FAUNA  
Life loves scaffolding. Swifts nest in bolt-holes; iridescent beetles graze algae rinds; long-tailed pollinator bats sleep behind pressure-gauges. Most charismatic are the corridor crabs—palm-sized brass-shell arthropods originally released as cleaners. They still scour grime from coils but now also tend moss gardens, clipping browned leaves with surgical claws.

SOUND  
Deafening. The condenser vents roar like surf. Chains clank. Steam whistles through relief valves. Wind moans in organ-pipe ducts. During a vent-cycle conversation is conducted by semaphore paddles or not at all. When the cycle ends, the sudden hush feels like ears plunging underwater.

WEIRDNESS  
The tower’s interior space warps perception: because every surface is slightly curved and nothing vertical meets true horizontal, depth slips. Drop a nut from the railing; it seems to fall crooked, spiralling with the updraft for minutes before it clinks on a beam far below. The resident corvids know routes where rising thermals hold them motionless in mid-air. Occasionally tools left overnight acquire faint patinas of unknown alloy, as though the wall itself rewrites metallurgy in slow, dreaming edits.

DYNAMIC  
Change is glacial. Girders corrode a millimetre per decade. Vines measure time in slow sweeps toward sunlight. People serve five-year maintenance rotations and see almost no difference between their arrival and departure photos—until they line them up century-archive fashion and notice the whole cat-walk has sagged a handspan, and an entire new eco-niche has layered itself over the old.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Gravity is steadfast but whimsical airflow can counterfeit weightlessness.  
2. Metal is not inert; it forgets nothing and occasionally remembers new shapes.  
3. Life, once invited, never checks out.  
4. Anything dropped is a gift to floors you will never reach.  
5. Silence is brief and precious; when it comes, everyone stops to hear the place catching its breath.

STAND A MOMENT  
Lean on the warm railing. Mist beads in your hair; turbines bellow like storm gods arguing theology. A swift knifes past chasing a beetle; a corridor crab drags a fallen orchid petal toward a drainage grill. Up along the towering wall, a single maintenance lamp flickers, then steadies, illuminating a bloom of violet moss that was not there yesterday and may be ancient tomorrow.
