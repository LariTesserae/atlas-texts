The place is a basin hardly higher than the surrounding seafloor, yet it lies on land—a shallow, slate-grey saucer ten kilometres wide that breathes water the way other landscapes breathe air.

1. Terrain and Materials  
• Rim. The valley’s lip is a jagged collar of basalt shards, freshly cracked and knife-thin. Nothing has rounded them yet; each sheet still glints where it split last season.  
• Floor. Inside the collar the ground looks smooth from afar, but up close it is a quilt of overlapping plates—mica, obsidian, nacreous gypsum—tilted like loose roof tiles. Gaps between plates open into narrow runnels a handspan deep. Water wicks up through these seams and overflows, spilling across the plates in thin, shining skins.  
• Substrate. Dig down half a metre and the ground turns viscous: a slurry of mineral silt and half-formed clay that sloshes as if the earth has not quite decided to harden into stone.

2. Water (wet 1.6)  
The basin is never dry, though it seldom rains in the ordinary sense. Each dawn a milky fog condenses a few metres above the ground. Beads form, merge, then fall like strings of glass beads onto the plates. Where the droplets strike certain crystals they ring—pure tones, long-lived, overlapping. Nothing moves quickly; the water collects, seeps, and gathers in saucer-sized pools whose surfaces tremble but do not spill, held in place by an invisible skin of surface salts.

3. Light (dim 0.7)  
The sun is a copper coin behind a permanent ash-blue overcast. Daylight never exceeds the glow of dusk; colours compress to greys, silvers, the occasional oil-slick green where light refracts in water film. At night the plates phosphoresce faintly, as though remembering the pressure of the day’s light.

4. Atmosphere and Sound (loud 2.3)  
Sound is the most abundant thing here.  
• Drip-notes: Every minute hundreds of water drops strike crystal plates, each drop a different note. The valley is a giant, slow percussion instrument.  
• Plate chorus: When a cold breeze snakes through the rim shards the plates vibrate, humming at sub-bass pitches you feel in ribs before you hear.  
• Distant roll: Beyond the rim the unseen ocean booms against hidden caverns, sending heart-slow pulses through the ground.  

Stand still and the air is a layered roar, yet every component has space; no sound masks another. Silence would feel wrong, an absence of a necessary pressure.

5. Temperature (cool 1.4)  
Even at midday breath ghosts white. Water is too cold for ordinary life to thrive; touch stays numb for minutes.

6. Vegetation (0.1)  
No plants grow. What lives is mineral or nearly so.  
• Glassmoss: a film of silicon needles one molecule thick creeps across shaded plates, nourished by trace metals and photonic residue.  
• Rainshard mycelia: thread-thin networks inside cavities knit crystals together, feeding on chemical gradients rather than carbon. They glow violet where plates crack.

7. Fauna (rare 1.2)  
Living creatures appear seldom and singly.  
• Skimfish: eel-long, ribbon-flat animals of cartilage and membrane that ride the thin water sheets, gulping trace nutrients. Seen only at dawn for minutes.  
• Condorclams: fist-sized bivalves that inflate gas bladders, drift on fog, then deflate and clamp to rock. A valley the size of a child’s bedroom may hold two, and they never congregate.

8. Built (scattered 1.2, tech 0.5)  
Across the basin stand maybe thirty stacks of five to seven plates each, balanced edge-on like playing cards. No tool marks, no binding agent. The lower plates are older than the cracks around them, meaning the stacks pre-date the valley’s most recent upheaval. Their arrangement—the only geometry here—seems placed to tune the drip-notes; when a stack falls, a chord of the valley’s music vanishes.

9. Erosion and Youth (1.4)  
Every surface looks newborn. Edges are unblunted, colours unoxidised. Yet change is patient. The collar erodes microns a year; a century later it will still be recognisably itself, only its overtones slightly shifted.

10. Density (2.4)  
Wherever you stand, something touches your perimeter of perception: a plate edge at shin, a hanging bead at brow, a subsonic tremor in gut, overlapping mists at fingertips. Space is never empty; it is saturated with form, liquid, vibration.

11. Dynamics (slow 0.6)  
Processes here have the tempo of mineral growth.  
• A droplet takes twenty minutes to form and fall.  
• A pool takes eighty years to merge with its neighbour.  
• A rim shard, once broken, will lie exactly where it lands until a quake in some future epoch tips it one plate length farther downslope.

12. Rules of the World  
• Resonance predicates structure: crystals align to amplify tone; tone guides the condensation of fog; fog drip sculpts new crystal faces.  
• The living ride the mineral processes, using resonance the way forest life uses sunlight.  
• Nothing that relies on speed can survive; the whole valley is a sanctuary for the slow.

You can walk the entire basin in a day, yet people who attempt it speak of forgetting whether they ever began. The steady, layered music remaps their sense of time to the pulse of plate and drop. They step out again at dusk, clothing damp, mind ringing, convinced not that they visited a landscape but that the landscape briefly dreamt them into being so it could hear its own harmonies through alien ears.
