The air is dry enough to rasp the back of your throat, yet it feels cool, the way stone feels cool after sunset. You stand—if “stand” is the right word here—on a ledge halfway up a wall of jagged cliffs. Above you, the range rises another three vertical kilometres, its peaks tapering into points so thin they vanish into the violet haze. Below, the drop is longer still. The world has always been tall long before it was wide.

Terrain and materials  
Everything you see has been abraded for eons by a wind that has no scent of water. The rock is a pale, brittle salt-glass––almost translucent, laced with pans of iron that glow dully when the half-hidden sun slides behind the ridge. Fissures snake across the surfaces like frozen lightning, evidence of countless freeze-thaw cycles where nothing ever truly melts. Here and there the cliffs crumble into plate-sized flakes that drift outward, hang in the thin air for a breath, then plummet so slowly that you can watch them tumble for minutes before they disappear into the dusk below.

Atmosphere and light  
The sky is a deep indigo washed by perpetual twilight. A star—perhaps the system’s sun, though it is too small and too red to resemble anything so familiar—hovers low on the horizon, offering just enough radiance to reveal outlines, never colors. The dimness is constant; no true night, no true day, only a sullen half-light. Dust motes, charged with static, float rather than settle, forming slow currents that twist like ghost rivers.

Elevation and emptiness  
Distances feel both stretched and compressed. A plateau that should take hours to cross shrinks at unexpected angles, while a nearby tower can recede for days. Gravity, though weak, is fickle; sometimes you must lean into it, sometimes you must remind your boots to stay attached to the ledge. Voices, if there were any, would fail to carry: the air is too thin, the pressure too variable. Silence dominates, broken only by the staccato crack of distant stone shattering under its own tension.

The built world  
Yet an entire city clings to these cliffs. No mortar binds its structures. Each edifice is a honeycomb of mineral cells—hollow prisms that have grown right out of the host rock. Imagine crystal cathedrals, but eroded, roofs opened to the sky, walls fluted by wind until they resemble the ribcage of an enormous fossil. Sky-bridges eight metres wide arc from spire to spire, each bridge splintered into threads barely thicker than fingers. Somehow they hold. There are streets—terraces, really—carved in perfect switchbacks, and amphitheaters that descend in concentric squares rather than circles, as though questioning Euclid with every tier. No tool marks exist; the city appears exhaled by the mountain rather than built upon it.

Technology and absence  
There is no machinery, no leftover furniture, no scorch marks of power sources. Doorways gape, but hinges never existed. Everything looks prepared for inhabitants who never quite arrived or left before they could disturb a single grain of dust. You can search for hours and find not one shard of worked metal, not one discarded utensil. If culture happened here, it shed no artifacts.

Vegetation and other life  
Life clings in narrower forms. Filaments the width of hair sprout from shaded cracks, branching into fractal nets that glitter like frost. They are neither plant nor fungus; touch one and it retracts so swiftly the air pops. A kilometer away, you might glimpse a winged shape coasting on updrafts—long membranes, hollow bones; perhaps it lives off the filaments, perhaps it only dreams. Sightings are rare, never repeated of the same organism. Tracks appear, three-toed and light, but lead nowhere before fading. Whatever dwells here does so sparsely, as though population were an affront to the architecture.

Dynamic processes  
Weather is the ruling organism. Each dawnless morning, a cold front slides along the verticality, generating spiral mists that fall upward, pouring into the sky like inverted cataracts. At irregular intervals the salt-glass mountain exhales a deep, resonant boom—an internal fracture propagating through kilometers of crystalline strata. When that happens, bridges realign, ledges migrate. A path you mapped yesterday may have rotated ninety degrees, or the gravity well beside it may have weakened so much that pebbles float waist-high for hours before resettling.

Sound  
The place is almost perfectly quiet, but not inert. A distant, slow breathing hum persists, so low you feel it more than hear it. It originates from no single point; rather, the entire massif resonates, as if the stone remembers an ocean and is trying to sing it back into being.

Rules of the world—where weirdness seeps in  
1. Direction is negotiable: climb long enough and you may arrive where you began.  
2. Time dilates in narrow canyons; your pulse feels sluggish, while the dust spirals accelerate.  
3. Material certainty wavers: set down a stone, blink, and it may become translucent or slide half-embedded into the ledge.  
4. Density is inversely tied to attention: the harder you focus on a structure, the more hollows you discover inside it, labyrinth spawning within labyrinth.  
5. Nothing falls exactly as physics predicts. Objects choose their descent, curving like dowsing rods toward unseen attractors.

Standing here you realize the city is less a ruin than a physical hypothesis—a scenario the planet once tested and never retracted. Erosion has not destroyed it; erosion is part of its grammar. The mineral honeycomb, the patient wind-sculpting, the shy quasi-flora, the gliders, the inconsistent gravity: each completes an equation of presence and near-absence.

To stay is to participate. Eventually you will find that your own shadow detaches during certain twilight pulses, fanning out behind you like another sky-bridge, testing its weightless feet on the precipice. Whether it returns is one more matter the mountain has left open for debate.
