When you first arrive you are unsure whether you are indoors or outdoors.  
The place is the size of a forgotten cloister—two or three hundred paces across at most—yet nothing around it hints at walls or roof.  Overhead hangs a ceiling-dark sky so choked with charcoal-brown dust that daylight cannot push through.  It is the long, unbroken twilight of a world whose sun still burns but never quite reaches the ground.

Terrain  
The floor is nearly flat, only a shallow bowl whose edges lose themselves in the murk.  It is built of plates of compacted siltstone, each plate a hand-span thick, the edges curling upward like old parchment where eons of mild, patient wind have gnawed it.  The rock is warm to the touch—geothermal seepage leaks upward through hairline cracks—but it is bone-dry.  No true sand lies here; every fragment is too fine, baked into a smooth powder that will not drift unless you deliberately disturb it.  Kneeling, you can smell a faint bitterness, the scent of desiccated salts.

Water  
There has been none for longer than the stone remembers.  The place sits well below the planet’s vanished sea level, a lost pan where ancient brine once evaporated.  Under each footfall a dull crunch whispers: crystal films of sodium-rich salts fracture like thin ice.

Vegetation  
Life is present only as scattered colonies of pigment-black lichens, each no larger than a fist.  They cling to upright shards of siltstone where trace warmth rises.  From a distance they might be mistaken for soot stains.  Up close you see they are corrugated, breathing very slowly, opening minute fissures at the warmest part of every long day–night cycle to sip gaseous moisture from the air.

Atmosphere  
The air is still, almost heavy, heated from below until it sits at a perpetual late-summer warmth.  Because the sky blanket never parts, nothing ever cools.  There is no scent but salt and dust.  Humidity hovers just above zero; exhaled breath ghosts outward and vanishes without a trace.

Light  
True darkness reigns until your eyes adjust.  Then a near-infrared gloom outlines things as gradients of warm and warmer.  A traveler carrying primitive fire—knapped-stone striker, dry lichen filament, a twist of resinous root—would create the only visible color here: a soft orange bubble in otherwise endless charcoal.

Erosion & Time  
Everything feels long-settled.  No gusts, no floods, no seasonal frost to crack the ground.  Only the slow sublimation of salts and the constant sift of fallout from the sky rearrange surfaces grain by grain.  Set a pebble on the plain and, a millennium from now, it will still be where you left it, only dulled, half-buried, and faintly warm.

Sound  
Silence dominates.  You become aware of your heartbeat, the brush of your clothing.  Occasionally—perhaps once an hour—a sheet of surface salt will ping softly as internal tension snaps it.  The sound is like a needle dropped on glass and dies at arm’s length.

Ecology  
There are no animals, no insects, nothing that moves of its own accord.  The lichen colonies share the plain with filamentous mats hidden just beneath the upper dust: chemotrophs that sip heat and mineral ions.  They grow so incrementally that the plain’s appearance will not change in a human lifetime.

Rules of the World  
1. Heat comes from below, not above; dawn and dusk mean nothing here.  
2. Anything moist brought into the basin—skin, wood, water, meat—desiccates in hours, leached by capillary salt and warm vacuum-dry air.  
3. Fire is possible but precious; without larger vegetation the only fuels are the lichens themselves and yellowish nodules of ancient tar that occasionally work loose from the stone.  
4. Orientation is difficult.  The land is low and featureless, the sky unmarked.  Travelers navigate by the slight downhill pull toward the center of the bowl or by memorizing the scattered pattern of heat-vent cracks.  

Standing in that hush you realize the place is neither hostile nor inviting; it is simply indifferent, a settled equation of heat, salt, and darkness that has not changed since the ocean left.  It will outlast any brief curiosity you or your tools can muster, continuing its slow, warm, silent equilibrium long after the sky clears—if it ever does.
