No road approaches the place; there is only the slow aerial lift that glides in over a ragged skyline of stone. From the gondola’s grated floor you see nothing that resembles an ordinary valley. Instead, the land is a sunken labyrinth of tablet-flat mesas and cylindrical sinkholes, as though a planet-sized hand once pressed its knuckles into an ancient mountain range and left a pattern of wells and ridges. All of it lies very high—more than three thousand metres above the lowland plains far beyond the horizon—yet the summits have been planed and rounded by incomprehensible ages of wind, frost, and the slow crawl of vanished glaciers. What remains are titanic walls of grey-green limestone and pillared schist so old that the stone looks tired, its strata collapsed into broad, sagging folds.

1. Terrain and water  
The surface up here is cool and mostly dry to the touch, but every depression hides dampness. Night clouds scrape the rim of the plateau, exhale, and leave beads of condensation that creep, bead, then tumble downward. Runnels no wider than a finger thread the cliffs, joining just long enough to become film-thin waterfalls that vanish before they ever reach a true river. The only standing water clings to the bottoms of the sinkholes—emerald pools whose edges are scalloped like gigantic ammonite shells. From above they look tranquil. Up close they whisper, fed by drip-feeds from hundreds of unseen cracks.

2. Light and atmosphere  
Because the plateau punches directly into the region’s perpetual cloud ceiling, daylight here is always bruised, more suggestion than illumination. Mornings bloom a pewter grey; at noon the sun is a faint disc; evening arrives without ceremony. Even on clear days (perhaps three or four a year) the high walls throw long shadows that knot together, so that vision is forever adjusting to half-darkness. Luminous mosses have adapted: a slow green gleam fills many of the overhangs after dusk, and every resident carries lenses tinted against their faint phosphorescence.

3. Vegetation and density  
Whatever can root in a crack does so fanatically. Ferns overlap ferns, their fronds combing each other like crowded umbrellas. Dwarf pines cling horizontally from sheer faces, trunks twisted into permanent parentheses. Where a ledge widens to more than a metre, gardens happen almost spontaneously—tiers of vine-fruit and black-leafed cabbage drooping over the abyss. The density is overwhelming in places: step off the carved walkways and you vanish waist-deep in coils of vegetation that breathe faint, cold steam.

4. The built world  
Inside the cliffs runs the city of Evigrad, grown layer by layer like barnacles on a whale. First came abandoned mining adits from a lost imperial age, then the brick galleries of the torrent-mills, then the steel viaducts, then the glass-roofed manufactories, each generation fastening itself to whatever surface remained. The result is a vertical palimpsest: soot-scarred smokestacks that never rise more than twenty metres before butting against an overhang; freight elevators that shuttle laterally as often as they rise; bridges whose footings are bolted to the ceilings of other bridges. Everything is close: one façade is seldom more than four metres from its opposite face, so voices travel downward in echoes that last half a minute.

5. Technology  
Evigrad’s industry is tuned to scarcity and opportunity. Water is unreliable, so every drip is caught in ribbed cisterns; when a cloudburst arrives the entire city groans as thousands of counterweights reset at once. Small dynamos line the galleries: the citizens harvest the slow, constant draft that sighs along the sinkholes. Most power, however, comes from the Resonant Lines—steel cables strung across chasms that vibrate in the wind at infrasonic frequencies. Magneto-coils drink that vibration and feed direct current into a warren of ceramic-sheathed conduits. The gentle, unending hum is the city’s heartbeat.

6. Soundscape  
Despite machinery, the place is mostly quiet. Cogs are muffled, turbines cased in felt, aloud voices considered impolite after the evening gong. Instead one notices micro-sounds: drip-tap of condensation, the creak of a root slowly lever-prying a stone block, the breathy roar of air plunging through a vertical street, the wind-harp thrum of the Resonant Lines. A stranger who speaks above a murmur finds their words swallowed by architecture engineered for hush.

7. Fauna  
Life moves sparsely but decisively. Once or twice an hour a shadow crosses the upper daylight: cliff-skates, leathery hex-winged grazers that browse on airborne algal threads. Inside the crevices live ash-white salamanders whose eyes have grown mirror-bright and nearly useless. People keep small goat-analogues called triskel—three-horned, sure-footed, and prized for milk that already tastes faintly of salt and stone. Larger animals simply do not occur; there is not enough contiguous ground for a true grazer to gather speed.

8. Weather and dynamics  
The ecosystem is in constant, patient motion. Moisture migrates sideways more than down; vines creep centimetres per week across bare stone, sewing new ledges for seeds. Twice each year, temperature swings trigger a phenomenon the locals call the Fall of Breath: warm air rushes upward from lowlands, meets super-cooled rock, and condenses instantly into silver fog that pours over every rim at once. For twelve hours the city floats inside an inland sea of cloud; its inhabitants lay out linen traps to collect pure water and tea-moss spores.

9. Subtle strangeness  
Older generations swear that the plateau is not entirely inert. Hammer on certain walls and they reply in perfect fifths. Workers claim machinery sometimes starts itself after moonless midnight, driven by sympathetic resonance with stone they describe as “still dreaming of its own making.” Geological surveys detect pockets where gravity relaxes by nearly three percent, enough that ladders must be built crooked or the climber feels the wrong sort of fatigue.

10. Rules that emerge  
• Everything is vertical first, horizontal second.  
• Water is currency; silence is courtesy; moss-light is common law.  
• Buildings are never demolished—only re-purposed or enveloped by newer skin.  
• If you hear a tone that harmonizes with your own breath, you must pause and match it; superstition insists that disharmony invites rockfall.

Stand long enough on one of Evigrad’s narrow balconies and the landscape begins to clarify its intentions: to rise high, to wear itself low, to drink clouds one silver drop at a time, and to cradle a civilization that answers ancient erosion with patient construction. Everything here is both final and in motion, a paradox of stone and vapor, dim light and green urgency, waiting for the next drift of mist to redraw its edges.
