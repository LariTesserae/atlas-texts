The place is no larger than a village green—an oval of rock perhaps three hundred paces wide—yet it sits halfway to the sky on the back of a weather-chewed massif.  One reaches it by a single ribbon of wind-planed stone that coils up through broken buttresses, then opens without warning onto a balcony of quiet land surrounded by endless drop.  Cloud-banks brood below; a faded, amber sky smolders above.  No true sun is visible—only a swollen, rust-colored glow that passes for daylight on this world—so the plateau is held in a continual dusk.

Terrain and materials
• Floor: vesicular basalt polished by ages of salt-laden gale.  Cavernous pockets in the rock still whisper where trapped gases burst free eons ago.  
• Rim: ragged parapets of columnar lava form natural cloisters and windbreaks.  Between columns, air whistles like organ pipes, turning every gust into chord and counter-chord.  
• Soil: a powder of mauve and cream, dry as ash, rich in nitrides and metal salts but devoid of moisture.  When footsteps disturb it, grains snap and spark, lighting brief blue pinpoints—static discharge is the local equivalent of dew.

Atmosphere and weather
Warm air (perhaps the exhaust of a deeper planetary furnace) climbs the escarpment, meeting the colder, thin air of altitude and detonating into constant turbulence.  Downdrafts roar over the rim, scoop up purple dust, and fling it skyward in helical columns that never quite settle.  Each hour or two, charged vortices crack open in sudden lightning, white inside, violet at the edges.  Rain has never been recorded; water would boil away in seconds and poison the native biochemistry besides.

Vegetation—lush, but waterless
Plants here do not drink; they mine.  Root-spires of silicate and carbon lace the basalt, wicking metals instead of moisture.  Because there is room to breathe, stands are widely spaced, giving the whole patch a sparse orchard feel.

1. Ironwillows: jointed trunks of hematite and living polymer plate, ten metres tall, each joint vibrating in wind to harvest charge.  In the dimness their leaf-analogues glow a chalky green—bioluminescence fed by current, not by chlorophyll.

2. Scaleferns: low rosettes whose overlapping fronds are pale zinc.  Touching one leaves a metallic taste in the mouth and a tingling in the teeth.

3. Pollen-towers: soft, coral-pink tubes that erupt glittering spores whenever thunder rolls.  The spores are microcapacitors—living flashbulbs—that drift until they find new crevices of charged stone.

Fauna—abundant and loud
Nothing here is quiet.

• Skirl-moths: kite-shaped, transparent, vibrating wing-membranes that sing in complex chords.  Flocks of hundreds maneuver like shoals of fish, mapping electric fields the way bats map echoes.

• Rock-jacks: fist-sized crustaceans with quartz claws tuned to smash ironwillow bark.  They clatter incessantly, adding sharp percussion beneath the wind-organ tones.

• Shardback grazer: the plateau’s largest resident, a loping tripod the height of a pony, its carapace made of overlapping vitreous panes.  It breaks scaleferns into slurry, somehow converting metal into tissue that flexes without cracking.  When startled it flares every plate at once, producing a thunderclap you can feel in your sternum.

Soundscape
The symphony never pauses: wind roaring through columns, ironwillow joints humming, skirl-moths piping, rock-jacks clacking, lightning cracking like snapped flagstones, and, underneath it all, the distant groan of the mountain itself as hot gases worry their way upward.  Speech must be hollered or conveyed by drum; local life has evolved to broadcast on every possible frequency.

Light
Dim, indirect, never more than late-twilight brightness.  Illumination comes from bioluminescence, corona discharges, and the white-hot interior of lightning bolts.  Shapes drift in and out of view as your eyes adjust; depth perception fails beyond thirty metres, making the grove feel simultaneously cramped and boundless.

Built traces and technology
Tucked into the largest lava cloister stands a cluster of huts—five in all—no taller than a person.  Each is laced from desiccated ironwillow strips, daubed with powdery ash, roofed in shardback plates scavenged after natural molts.  Tools are equally simple: stone adzes, jawbone scrapers, fire-pistons that spark when struck against charged bark.  Whoever lives here chooses materials that neither rust nor rot, for rust and rot are unknown concepts; entropy here is measured in lost charge, not lost water.

Dynamics and rules of the world
1. Nothing liquid lasts long.  Water placed on the ground sublimates in minutes; native blood is a syrup of liquid salts stable only above 60 °C.  
2. Life is powered by electricity.  Charge flows from plant to animal the way carbon flows in terrestrial food webs.  To hunt is to drain a battery.  
3. Instability is the default.  Every biological surface doubles as a sensor reacting to field changes; organisms shift color, stance, or even structural alignment in seconds.

Erosion and age
Despite the chaos, the landform feels ancient.  Grooves along the basalt show millennia of polishing.  Some ironwillows bear lightning-scar spirals older than any local myth.  Balance here is not quiet equilibrium but a high-energy stalemate: storm feeds grove; grove feeds fauna; fauna returns trace metals to rock; rock, eroded, supplies new minerals to the rising wind.

Boundaries
Walk a hundred steps in any direction and the ground slopes away too steep to climb without gear.  The grove is an island in the air, complete in itself, global in miniature.

Standing there—ears ringing, skin prickling with stray voltage—you realize thirst never comes, for the air is too dry to suggest drinking.  Instead the body longs to ground itself, to find some place where the current stops.  Yet no such place exists here.  Everything is in motion, everything hums, and the only silence lies far below, beneath the cloud ocean that hides whatever drowned world once gave birth to stone.
