THE FLOOR OF THE WORLD

1. First sight  
The country is flat enough to feel two–dimensional, yet you never stand on earth. Instead you walk on an endless parquet of hand-shaped plates: wafers of sun-fired clay, black glassy slag, palm-board, stitched reed mats, all mortared together edge-to-edge. The pattern changes every few hundred steps—hexagons become zig-zag fishbones, spirals click into right angles—but every joint is crisp, unworn, sharp as yesterday’s knife-cut. No grain of native soil shows between the seams. Below the plates you can hear water knocking, as though the whole land were a raft riding a single, shallow sea.

2. Water (1.9 / 3, wet)  
The air is a saturated sponge. Under the white blaze of noon the horizon shimmers with curtains of condensation that fall, reverse themselves, and crawl back upward like silver worms. Every surface sweats; beads gather, swell, drop through grates in the pavement, and are gone. Long narrow gutters—flat bamboo half-pipes bound with leather—ridge the plates to carry overflow toward distant sumps whose mouths you never quite reach. Step on certain tiles and cool brine leaps up through nail holes, as if the ground were exhaling.

3. Built totality, primitive craft  
Nothing here was cast by geology. Even the hills that break the flatness are merely stacked drums of unfired brick, their sides still showing thumbprints where the builders pressed them into shape. Bridges are rope-sling catwalks woven from palm fibers, their knots glazed with tree resin. Shade is given by lean-to awnings of stitched bark, propped on poles of driftwood lashed with raw hide. You taste the simplicity of the tools in every joint but the planning is vast, patient—someone spent generations paving a delta the size of a province with nothing but reed knives, flint chisels, and sun.

4. Heat & light (2.6 hot, 3.0 bright)  
The sky is locked at a perpetual, silent noon. Clouds exist only as white blisters that never move. Light falls like a weight; colors bleach within minutes, metal burns at a touch, and even the shadows seem pounded flat. Yet nothing bursts into flame. The atmosphere feels thick, almost oiled, a heat that bakes and steams at once.

5. Vegetation, yet manufactured ground  
Life comes up wherever a tile has been deliberately holed. Slots the width of a finger host whole jungles: mangrove roots spilling through lattice-work, fern thickets housed in square pits like window boxes, latticed cud-weed trained up cane trellises. Someone tends them—every vine is pruned away from load-bearing joints; no tendril is allowed to force apart the plates. Greenery is lush, even riotous, but always stops one exact finger-span short of any seam.

6. Fauna (2.2 abundant, mostly quiet)  
Creatures are thick but hushed, as if sharing a pact not to disturb the white stillness. Pink ibis pad over the tiles on wicker nests strapped to their feet so the clay doesn’t burn them. Frogs the size of bracelets lurk inside chalk cups where runoff pools; they croak once an hour, all at the same pulse, then fall silent. Occasionally a long laminar shadow glides overhead—seed-wing lizards, their membranes sewn with reed splints so they never tear. Nothing here predates anything else for long; a kill is followed by an almost immediate, calm regrowth.

7. Youthful, uneroded surfaces  
Where a drainage wall has cracked you can still see the fresh face of the clay—no rounding, no softening. Tool marks stand out like script: straight, decisive cuts, a craftsperson’s shorthand frozen in solid silt. Even the few toppled columns lie with edges so keen they could split a palm.

8. Scale & density (valley-wide yet spare)  
You can walk for a day and always see a long low skyline, parcelled by canals and brick mesas, but you will rarely meet more than a couple living presences per hour. Vast courts lie empty but for drifting lotus-leaves; miles of aqueduct mutter softly, unattended by any eye.

9. Quiet (0.6 sound)  
The loudest thing is the drip. After that, your own pulse. Wind is absent; the air just hangs, thick and hot. Even the ibis beat their wings in slow motion, feathers hardly whispering. When a tile fractures—and one always does sooner or later—the crack echoes like thunder then dies, leaving the world in its old hush.

10. Reality breakdown (2.6 weirdness)  
Patterns don’t stay loyally in Euclidean space. Walk the edge of a triangular basin and you will return to your starting point after two sides, not three. Water will sometimes climb a stair that plainly descends. The sun, pinned at zenith, throws shadows that drift east to west and back again, never longer, never shorter. If you try to excavate a tile, you will find its underside is the same glazed surface you stood on; space quilts onto itself.

11. Stasis (0.4 dynamic)  
Despite the quirks, nothing truly changes. Vegetation never overruns the pavement, tiles never weather past that first hair-line crack, animals maintain their numbers, the noon never ripens toward evening. The whole place feels like a freshly finished model the builder walked away from at the instant of completion, leaving it to sit, bright and damp, forever.

12. Rules sensed, never stated  
• Step counts loop after 480 pace-beats; past that you are walking the same ground.  
• Water, once lost to a gutter, reappears as mist exactly seventeen minutes later.  
• No object will burn; flame gutters out, smoke sinks.  
• A spoken name is never echoed—this world declines to repeat human utterance.

13. Final impression  
Imagine a hot marsh delta cut out of reality and rebuilt, tile by handmade tile, by people with only wicker and sun to help them, then sealed inside a noon that cannot die. It is beautiful in a museum-case way: bright, lush, fragile, impossibly orderly, and slightly off in the angles, like someone translated the idea of landscape into a different grammar and forgot to proof-read the last lines. Walk it gently. Everything here is young forever, and forever is brittle.
