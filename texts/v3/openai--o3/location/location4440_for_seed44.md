The place has never been given a proper name.  The foundry clerks call it “the Green Yard,” the barge pilots “the Root-Harbor,” and in the forestry ledgers it is simply Plot 17-B, because on paper it is supposed to be a research station.  In truth it is all of these and none of them: a low, warm, water-logged pocket of the world in which the only solid ground is alive.

TERRAIN  
Imagine a saucer, perhaps three city blocks across, set a half-day upriver from the nearest real town.  The saucer’s rim is a ring of peat and dark clay, soft underfoot, already sweating with groundwater.  Inside the rim the ground dissolves into a tangle of living matter: the fused buttress roots of a single colossal plant called the banyan-reef.  Thick as cargo wagons, the roots braid and re-braid until every horizontal surface is a tray of ridged, vascular wood.  The gaps between fill with black, mirror-still water only ankle-deep, but everywhere.  You never hear your own footsteps—only the quiet slop of water squeezing up through root bark and sliding back.

MATERIALS & BUILT FORM  
Nearly every human structure here is grafted, not anchored.  A century ago engineers learned to saw thin slots in the banyan-reef and insert steel tongues; the plant obligingly callused over the metal, locking it in place.  Out of those tongues rise narrow brick sheds, sheet-metal catwalks, and squat ventilator stacks.  None of them are taller than a three-story house; the banyan’s canopy, thirty meters overhead, blocks any attempt at real vertical ambition.  Instead the settlement spreads laterally, a dense tessellation of workshops, dormitories, hoists, and pipe galleries, all following the lazy spiral of the root pattern.  Rust prints everything orange and brown; mosses soak the mortar seams green; but the walls are warm to the touch because the boilers never cool entirely.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Warmth hangs like a quilt.  Daylight filters through two storeys of foliage, scattering into a gold–green glow that equals an overcast noon but never more.  At night the settlement provides its own constellations: sodium lamps strung under aerial walkways, the blue corona of arc welders, the cherry-red door of the coke kiln.  Mist forms without warning—water condenses on the leaf-ceiling, gathers into silent beads, and drops in sporadic, heavy plops that hit tin roofs like thrown nails.

SOUNDS  
Even when every lathe is switched off, the place is loud.  
• Steam bleed whistles once a minute, sharp and quick, so pressure never spikes.  
• Chain-driven log haulers clank along elevated vines, dragging harvested branches from the outer marsh.  
• Water pumps wheeze in slow, patient strokes—ancient triple-expander engines turning just fast enough to keep the work pits unflooded.  
In the lulls you hear the wood itself: an almost mammalian creak as the reef flexes fractions of millimeters with each tide pulse.

HYDROLOGY  
Below the plant’s mantle the water table sits less than a handspan from the surface.  Wells are meaningless; you dip a cup into any puddle and draw the same tannin-stained drink.  The banyan-reef exudes clear sap from certain knuckles, mixing with rainwater to form slick, sweet sheens that the plant’s own bacteria quickly ferment.  Workers skim this film and load it into vats—it is the feedstock for the ethanol works that keep the yard competitive with fossil-fuel refineries downriver.

VEGETATION  
Plants are floor, wall, ceiling, sometimes tool.  The reef’s prop roots droop from the canopy like columns in a flooded crypt.  Smaller epiphytes cling to them in velvety layers: medicines, spices, outright weeds.  Because the entire bowl is one organism, foresters talk about rooms and corridors in terms of living tissue: “the left lung” is a hollow root node now serving as a machine shop; “the dorsal vein” is a conveyor corridor thick with ruby-red xylem sap.  Beyond the rim peatbank, cattails and papyrus mass so thickly that the air whistles through them in a single continuous chord whenever wind stirs.

FAUNA  
Animals are scarce.  A few mud-yellow lizards patrol the rafters for termites.  At dusk bats cross briefly, unemphatic, like inspectors checking a manifest.  Everything else learned generations ago that constant vibration equals human intrusion and has migrated to quieter marshes.

ECOLOGY & RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Mutualism is law.  The banyan-reef remains alive because the yard respects an extraction quota measured not in board-feet but in transpiration capacity.  If the plant’s daily water draw dips below a cliff-edge figure—monitored by gauges driven straight into living bark—every furnace must idle.  
2. Combustion is a privilege.  Because sparks fly upward into foliage, chimneys are double-jacketed with water sleeves; any temperature spike diverts river water automatically.  The reef’s safety engineers are gardeners first, metallurgists second.  
3. Time moves slowly.  Buildings sink by an imperceptible centimeter per year as roots thicken.  A lease on a workshop may specify both floor area and projected subsidence curve.  You inherit not just an address but its future tilt.

SENSING THE PLACE  
Walk twenty paces and the air changes.  Near the ethanol condenser you smell pears and raw bread dough; beside the electroplate baths it is ozonic and metallic.  Under every odor floats the sour-green note of sap.  If you slump on a root bench and close your eyes, the ambient rhythm feels tidal—hiss of pumps, hush of distant wind through leaves, staccato drip of condensation, clatter of chain, repeat.  After an hour your own heartbeat synchs and you understand why nobody here hurries.

DYNAMIC CHARACTER  
Nothing happens fast.  Sawdust soaks, ferments, and becomes seed substrate over months.  Rust is accepted as the iron’s final color.  People plan in multi-year increments: “We’ll widen that catwalk after the north root bulges next cycle.”  When the banyan grows a fresh shoot into someone’s casting bay, they thank it for the free joist and re-route the gantry.

BOUNDARIES  
Beyond the peat rim you can see the ordinary river world: reed barges, cloud reflections, a line of distant humming pylons.  Yet the rim is felt more than seen.  Cross it and the ground shifts from living sponge to inert mud; the air loses its humid sweetness; the noises flatten to mere wind.  The Green Yard ends, unmistakably, because everywhere else the earth stays still.

Thus the landscape holds itself: a warm, low, water-glistening bowl where plant and industry share the same slow pulse, loud with machines that yield to roots older than any blueprint.
