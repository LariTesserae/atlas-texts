THE CHURN-UNDER

1. Immediate impression  
Warm air, hard to breathe for its thickness, beats against the ear drums like the inside of a conch shell. Light—true daylight in color and strength—spills through the caverns, though no sun is in sight. It comes from lattices of honey-yellow crystal welded into the ceilings, glowing with an internal fusion of quartz and trapped radon. Every surface sweats: droplets condense, run, form beads, and fall in a constant metallic patter that competes with the animal din.

2. Terrain and materials  
• A continent-sized warren lies forty meters below the planet’s baked crust. Hallways are not carved but extruded: basalt still warm enough to dent under a hammer bulges into tubes, domes, and buttresses, as if the rock is still deciding what shape to take.  
• Erosion is embryonic. Edges are knife-clean; alcoves end in glassy scoops where a flow cooled just yesterday. Now and then an entire wall blurps outward, adds a new rib, then stills. Travelers mark their way with charcoal only to find the mark swallowed hours later.  
• Floors undulate. Knolls rise like the backs of leviathans, forcing rivulets of warm condensate into braided, temporary streams that vanish when the ground exhales again.  
• No soil exists; the only loose material is a black, pumice-fine grit shaken from the ceilings by the roar of underground wind.

3. Water (1.4 / 3 – damp)  
Moisture arrives by paradox: the underworld is sealed, yet saturated. Steam vents pulse from deeper magma chambers, cool on the crystal struts above, then rain back down. Pools rarely grow deeper than an ankle; most are absorbed by thirsty glass foam or sucked away when floors crack open. In the rare still corner, mirrors of perfectly smooth water host skeins of oil-slick bacterial film that shimmer like feathers.

4. Light (2.5 / 3 – normal)  
The photolith—those amber plates in the ceilings—glow all day, dim during local night, bright again at dawn, echoing a sky they have never seen. The caverns therefore keep human-comfortable illumination, but shadows are hard-edged and ever-moving because the light source is fractured into a thousand points. Creatures have learned to read those drifting silhouettes as warnings of what hunts above them.

5. Vegetation (0.4 / 3 – virtually none)  
No plants. Instead:  
• Silica-mycelia, rootless white cords that grow straight out of basalt. They digest feldspar, leave spongy tunnels behind, and exhale a faint ozone tang.  
• Gyre-spores: glass bubbles the size of sand grains. When trod upon they burst, releasing curling filaments that knit into whatever porous surface they find—living plaster more than life.  
Nothing photosynthesizes. Chemolithotrophs rule; everything is the color of minerals and old bones.

6. Fauna (2.6 / 3 – overwhelming)  
Life came early and then never stopped coming. Density is nearly hive-like.  
• Skitterwakes: fist-sized arthropods with translucent abdomens lit by swallowed crystal dust. They ride the warm updrafts in living drifts, wings slapping so fast they sound like ripping cloth.  
• Shale-runners: gecko-lean, slate-colored mammals (or something rhyming with mammals) that breathe through gill-slits along the ribs. They flash, dart, multiply—food for everything larger.  
• Caecilian dreadknots: serpents with bony beaks, blind, pink, perpetually slick, head-butting the rock until it yields; they follow chemical threads left by silica-mycelia.  
• Apex: the Tremor-matriarchs—fifty-meter tube worms studded with ceramic scales. They surface like subway trains, swallow caverns worth of biomass, excrete magma-warm slurry that hardens into new floor, and dive again. Their passage is the drumbeat that makes the entire realm shake.

7. Sound (1.6 / 3 – loud)  
Three layers at all times:  
a) Drip and hiss of condensation.  
b) Animal chorus: clicks, whistles, ultrasonic pops that make teeth vibrate.  
c) Deep geology: a basso continuo of stone deforming, punctuated by the cannon-crack of a wall giving way or the wet roar of a steam blowout. Silence exists only inside the bellies of large predators—and even there the heartbeat of the host thunders.

8. Built presence (1.0 / 3 – scattered, primitive)  
Here and there stand cairns of columnar basalt, each block still radiating residual heat: boundary-stones erected by troglodytic hominins called the Flintkin. They are tool-makers of the second simplest order:  
• Blades knapped from vitrified slag.  
• Rope braided from the filamentous husks of dead Skitterwakes.  
• Fire coaxed from sulfur nodules and quartz spar.  
Dwellings are shallow alcoves whose entrances are curtained with hides tanned in acidic pool water. None last; the living bedrock reclaims the spaces within weeks, forcing constant migration and a culture of impermanence.

9. Climate and dynamics (2.9 / 3 – turbulent)  
Nothing is stable. Jetting steam will convert a thoroughfare into a chasm by nightfall. Tremor-matriarchs upheave districts. The ceiling decides to sag, melts in slow motion, or births a new crystal skylight. Maps are stories, not guarantees.

10. Rules of the world  
• Memorize sound signatures: every creature, every geological motion, has a tell. Survival is the art of interpreting the choir.  
• Heat is health; organisms evolved to rely on ambient warmth. Cold pockets (where steam fails to reach) are dead zones, patrolled by nothing.  
• Minerals beat flesh. Exoskeletons incorporate quartz; sinews twine with metallic whiskers. Even the Flintkin bury stone flakes under their skin at puberty for rigidity.  
• Creation and destruction are the same force: whether magma rising or a Tremor-matriarch burrowing, every act that wrecks also builds. Stasis is the only sin.

11. What it feels like to stand here  
Boot soles melt slightly, picking up a mirror sheen. Breathing tastes of iron and wet dust. A dozen species pass by every minute, none pausing long enough to see whether you are predator, prey, or just another transient vibration. Light dazzles in broken prisms; shadows march in fractal patterns across the slick floor. The ground pulses once—twice—then lifts half a meter, setting every droplet dancing in mid-air. Behind the new ridge, something the size of a freight car uncoils with a sigh that fogs half the cavern. The place is half womb, half battlefield, entirely awake.

12. Where it is going  
Give it a hundred years and the Churn-Under may breach the surface, spewing its biosphere into sunlight. Or it may close itself, glass over, and fossilize everything inside into a mural of emergent life. For now it is a storm wearing the mask of a landscape, and its own inhabitants treat it with the reverence one gives weather: dangerous, nourishing, never to be trusted, always to be sung about.
