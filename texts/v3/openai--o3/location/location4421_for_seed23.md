The first thing you notice is the light—clean, blade-white, the sort that seems to come from every direction at once.  Up here, a good two thousand metres above the lowlands, the air is thin and almost glassy; there is nothing between you and the sun but a veil of hard, colour-stripped sky.  Every ice crystal, every droplet of suspended mist flashes at you, and for a moment it is difficult to see anything but brilliance.

When your eyes adjust, a tight little basin resolves: no wider than a modest village green, yet crammed—almost overfull—with forms.  Stone teeth crowd the floor, some only knee-high, others leaning together into arches that almost touch above your head.  Wind, frost-fracture, and ages of melt-and-freeze have filed them smooth.  The grey of the rock is stippled by patches of palest green lichen, the only vegetation hardy enough to keep purchase here.  Anything taller (a wiry grass here, a thumb-thick succulent there) stands lonely and widely spaced, its colour bleached toward bone.

Water is everywhere, though you rarely see it moving.  The stone weeps: tiny beads emerge, hesitate, become crystal hemispheres that stay put until the next glare of sun nudges them over the lip.  In shaded cracks, they freeze mid-drip, so the walls are furred with clear icicles that curve outward like frozen vines.  Between the rock blades lie shallow hollows filled with silvery slush.  You can place a foot on the surface—it bears your weight for a heartbeat, then sighs and lets you sink ankle-deep in numbing meltwater.

Sound is sparse.  You hear the occasional tink—high and glasslike—when an icicle finally surrenders to gravity and splinters below.  A papery hiss tells of the wind sliding through the rock teeth; it never grows to a howl, the formations break it into whispers.  Now and then there is a soft clatter rather like rice in a bowl: that is the wildlife.

Fauna here do not skulk at the edges; they fill the gaps.  Fist-sized, many-legged crustacean things—half crab, half beetle—use each rock blade as a freeway.  Their carapaces are translucent, shot through with blue veins, so you can see their organs pumping sluggishly in the chill.  They graze the lichens methodically, scraping with a dull rasp that you feel more in your teeth than your ears.  Above, white-on-white birds no bigger than your palm hang almost still, wings flicking just enough to hold position.  They hunt water: each time a droplet beads, a bird snaps forward, plucks it, and darts back, so quickly you can barely follow the motion.  The basin is a quiet market of barter—lichens pay the crabs, crabs pay the birds, birds pay back by dropping nitrogen-rich guano that keeps the lichens fed.  Nothing wasted.

Toward the northern rim, the stone grows more orderly.  A score of pillar-huts has been coaxed from the native rock; you can see the tool marks, shallow and irregular, as though pecked out with antler or stone hammers.  Each hut is only a few strides across, roofed with shale slabs and patched here and there with stretched hides whose fur is left on for insulation.  A gutter carved into the uphill side of every pillar routes seepage into a trough just inside the doorway—running water without leaving the house.  Smoke holes exist, but there is no smoke; fuel is scarce, and the folk here depend on body heat, sun in the bright season, and the latent warmth of slow fermentation pits dug under their floors.

You meet none of the builders at first.  Either they are away, or they watch unseen; the density of the place encourages hiding.  Then the light shifts—sun rimmed by a passing fretwork of cloud—and the quiet cracks open.  The basin heaves.

Water vapor condenses so fast it looks like a curtain being yanked across the sky.  A snow squall blooms out of nowhere, flakes too large, tumbling like scraps of torn linen.  Wind slams between the stone teeth, first one direction, then the opposite.  The crustacean grazers vanish into fissures.  Birds fold wings and drop straight into puddles, which film over with ice the instant they touch.  In less than a minute you stand inside a miniature blizzard that somehow never leaves the basin’s lip—it is as though the weather were leashed to this confined space.

And then, just as abruptly, the squall is gone.  Sunlight knifes back, the new snow sublimates in glittering puffs, and life resumes as if nothing had interrupted it.  The only sign of change is the fresh layer of glaze on every surface, making the stone teeth higher, slimmer, ever so slightly re-sculpted.  This is normal here.  The locals (when you finally glimpse them: short, seal-clad figures with pale braid and hollow-bone tools) call the phenomenon sky-breathing.  They claim the basin exhales its own weather whenever it grows thirsty; the sudden storm wrings more liquid from the air, feeding the endless seepage that keeps their gutters running.

Rules of the place, as best you can divine them:

1.  Water never rests long in liquid form; it is always on its way to becoming ice or vapor, and yet it is always present.
2.  Erosion is not destruction but maintenance.  Every freeze-burst polishes the architecture, every melt carves channels that the residents put immediately to use.
3.  Density is safety.  Anything that stands alone will be scoured by wind or buried in rime, so everything—rock, beast, dwelling—huddles against its neighbors.
4.  Noise is invitation.  The rare loud sound (a shout, a hammer blow) draws the basin’s attention and may trigger another breath-storm.  Quiet is survival.
5.  Nothing here is static, but nothing is wasted.  The turbulence is cyclical, predictable once you learn its pulse, and every cycle feeds the next.

Stay long enough, and the place begins to feel less like a hostile perch on the roof of the world and more like a living organ: a cold, bright lung inhaling sky and exhaling storms, keeping its many small inhabitants washed, fed, and disciplined in equal measure.
