You step down, not up: the landscape is hollow, a single sun-less chamber the size of a modest cathedral, lying two storeys beneath a crust of cracked laterite. The ceiling vaults twenty metres overhead, its freshly fractured rock still knife-sharp where it sheared away from the parent strata only a few decades ago. The place has had no time to weather; every ledge looks as though it might still remember the groan of its own making.

Terrain and materials
• Floor: A broad, mostly level shelf of obsidian-black basalt glass. It ripples like frozen tar, the result of a rapid, lava-hot surge that cooled in place and never flowed again.  
• Rims and pits: Shallow bowls and narrow ridges flank the main shelf. In their hollows, groundwater condenses perpetually, forming dark mirrors only a few centimeters deep.  
• Walls: Columnar basalt prisms stand shoulder-to-shoulder, each one faceted and un-smoothed—young rock that has not yet learned to crumble.

Atmosphere and light
The air is hot—above body temperature—and thick with vegetal exhale. Natural light almost never reaches this depth, so what illumination exists arrives in industrial shards:  
• Sodium lamps, hung on fraying cables that thread the ceiling like orphaned power-lines, cough out a dull, orange haze.  
• Here and there a broken arc lamp still sparks, exposing the cavern for a blink, then dying back to dark.  
• Between flashes, bioluminescent spores drift in slow drifts, scattering pin-point emeralds that never quite add up to visibility.

Water
Moisture is an intermittent guest. The roof bleeds condensation whenever the geothermal draft rises; droplets gather, fatten, and fall in solitary plinks. Pools never quite flood—within hours they’re drunk dry by root-mat or siphoned away through cracks toward deeper stone.

Vegetation (lush, but with room to breathe)
No animals move here, only plants, and plants do not hurry. Fern-trees rise five metres, their trunks a lattice of woven, air-root fibers that breathe as much as they support. Between them:  
• Carpet mosses so neon-green they appear back-lit.  
• Tubular vines that run along girders like insulated cables, leaves flaring into trumpet mouths to gather the yellow lamp-light or the scant chemiluminescent glow of their own spores.  
• Occasional blossom-sacs that open silently, exhaling a warm mist that smells of pepper and wet coins—metal-hungry epiphytes leaching iron from the very bolts that anchor the lamps.

Built traces (scattered, industrial, half-remembered)
Rusted catwalks zigzag the western wall, ending in abrupt, sawed-off platforms that overlook nothing but air. At ground level:  
• A single pressure door, round and plated like a submarine hatch, stands permanently ajar; its pneumatic pistons are seized solid.  
• A collapsed drum kiln, big enough to swallow a boxcar, lies on its flank; vines have pulled its sheet-metal skin into ruffles.  
• Conduits—copper once, now turquoise with oxide—trail from wall niches and vanish beneath the moss.

Sound
Silence, almost. From time to time:  
1. A droplet detonates on basalt: tik.  
2. A distant transformer hums for half a breath, then stills.  
3. Leaf-fronds rasp against hanging cable when the hidden ventilation fans exhale a lukewarm sigh.  
Then nothing again.

Ecology and rules
• Photosynthesis is supplemental; most plants here “burn” ferrous ions and trace sulfides drawn from rock and rust, a slow biochemistry that favors heat over light.  
• Without animal pollinators, reproduction relies on temperature pulses: spores release only when the chamber’s air surpasses 42 °C, which happens after days of geothermal build-up.  
• Death is rare; instead, older tissue petrifies in place, becoming scaffold for new growth. The whole cavern is a slow-motion terrarium of self-fossilizing flora.  

Weird undertones
Touch a vine and you feel a faint, steady warmth—an exothermic metabolism still largely unmapped. Place your ear to the basalt and you can catch a slow heartbeat of machinery deeper down: a turbine turning somewhere far below, though the power lines above are long dead. The plants appear to angle their fronds toward that subterranean pulse rather than toward any lamp.

Temporal dynamics
Change does happen, but on the scale of seasons, not moments. Each week the moss advances a finger-width across the glass; each month another bolt disappears into a sheath of bright green corrosion. If you returned in a human lifetime, the chamber would likely look the same—just a little greener, a little quieter, a little warmer.

Standing there, you sense the world above—noisy with wind, animals, daytime—yet utterly irrelevant. Down here, heat and metal are sunlight. Down here, the youngest rock in the ground listens patiently while root and rust negotiate ownership, syllable by mineral-rich syllable, in near-perfect dark.
