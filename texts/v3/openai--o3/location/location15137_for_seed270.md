The place is small—no wider than a city block, no higher than the ceiling of an aircraft hangar—yet it feels crowded the instant you step through the pressure door. You are inside a forgotten sump at the lowest level of an ancient factory, a space never meant for eyes or weather, sealed for geological ages and worn smooth by time and by the things that now teem here.

1. Terrain and materials  
The floor is almost perfectly flat, the last wrinkles of once‐molten metal long ago slumped into a plane of reddish-brown dust. That dust is iron oxide, ground so fine it behaves like smoke when your boot disturbs it, only to settle again in a heartbeat. Low mounds—no more than knee-high—mark where slag dripped centuries ago, now eroded to rounded humps. Between them run shallow channels polished glassy by uncounted bodies scuttling the same paths. Nothing here rises much above a meter; the land has had eons to fall inward, to lose every edge.

2. Light and air  
Ceiling panels of thick quartz once diffused furnace glow. Most are black, but a handful still carry current from a forgotten substation, bathing the chamber in a steady, normal white. The light never changes; there is no day cycle to learn. Air moves in slow, cold sheets—about 10 °C—pushed by an auxiliary fan that creaks behind a mesh grill. The atmosphere is bone-dry. A faint peppery tang of ozone and rust coats the palate; there is no hint of water, no condensation, no smell of mold.

3. Soundscape  
Loudness is constant, layered, claustrophobic. The bass note is the fan: a thudding 60-hertz cough that rattles diaphragms. Over it chatter millions of small hard parts—the click of mandible on mandible, the rasp of carapace against ductwork, the skitter of claws on polished steel. Every few minutes a metallic screech announces that some creature has pried a flake of wall loose. Silence is an impossibility; even if the power died, the living mass would go on rustling like dry leaves in a cyclone.

4. Built relics  
Evidence of industry remains, but only in fragments. Half a conveyor belt lies like a fossilized ribbon, supports eaten to nubs. A cluster of upright pipes—once coolant lines—forms a pallid grove around a control pedestal whose glass is crazed but still glowing with ghost circuitry. Bolted to one wall is a narrow maintenance shack—three meters long, two high—its door torn away and repurposed somewhere in the swarm. These objects are not focal points; they are just the least-eroded rind inside which the fauna dig and nest.

5. Fauna—overwhelming, waterless, mineral-fed  
Life here never knew chlorophyll or liquid water. It harvests energy directly from electro-chemical gradients and trace lubricants that still weep from the factory’s bones.

• Shardcrabs: hand-sized polyhedral bodies clad in mica plates, twelve needle legs. They grind rust into powder and excrete pellets of almost pure iron. Their eyes are vibrating whiskers that read electric fields.

• Coil-eels: living cables, copper coils sleeved in scaled silicone skin. They knot themselves through conduit, grazing on residual current. When threatened they discharge a crack that lights the room like sheet lightning and stuns every organism within two meters—except themselves.

• Filtermoths: origami fans of aluminium foil that flutter constantly, sieving the air for nanoscopic oil droplets. Their wings hum at the threshold of hearing; in vast numbers they generate the chamber’s persistent hiss.

• Apex thing—The Railwolf: a two-meter centipede of articulated scrap, magnetised feet adhering to overhead beams. It hunts by vibration, dropping to seize clusters of shardcrabs in jaws that are repurposed gear segments. Every few hours the entire hall pauses as the Railwolf passes, and then resumes its collective motion.

Population density defies counting. Creatures overlap like fish in a reef; if you hold out a hand, three species crawl over it before you finish blinking.

6. Dynamics and behavior  
Predator and prey cycles operate on minutes, not days; without plant growth to slow entropy, everyone here eats fast or is eaten faster. The carcass of a filtermoth lasts roughly fourteen seconds before being reduced to glitter by micro-mites. Nothing is still. Conveyor grooves gradually migrate across the floor as layer upon layer of shredders excavates one side and deposits tailings at the other: a slow mineral tide.

7. Erosion, time, and the sense of age  
Everything that could crumble has crumbled, yet the chamber persists. Surfaces are rounded, edges fogged, symbols on machines only shadows of strokes. It feels as though the room has been filing itself smoother for longer than planets hold seas.

8. Rules you notice after a while  
• Bright objects vanish quickest; motion-hunters key on reflectivity.  
• Silence attracts investigation. If you damp your footsteps, the fauna come to see which predator has learned a new trick.  
• Heat is invaluable currency. A warm tool in your pocket will be nibbled through fabric by coil-eels searching for a power leak.  
• Nothing here regards you as sacred—or even unusual. To the swarm, you are a short-lived structure whose materials will soon be reused.

Stand still and the place resolves: a cool, sealed desert that long ago shed water, plants, and sky, yet blossomed into a dense, thunderously alive engine of mineral life. It is intimate enough to cross in two minutes, ancient enough to feel bottomless, loud enough that you can feel sound on your skin. The room seems content to continue forever, a final stable form where industry and biology have merged into a single, grinding exhalation of rust and gears.
