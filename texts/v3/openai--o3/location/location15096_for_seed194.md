The place is small—no larger than the nave of a cathedral—yet, once you step across its threshold, it refuses to finish.  Every sense keeps discovering more of it, the way a tongue finds new edges on an old tooth.

TERRAIN AND LAYOUT  
Imagine a low-lying pocket, a dish carved in stone so long ago that the stone has quit pretending to be rock and has accepted the flatness.  The dish is oval, perhaps eighty paces on its long axis, fifty on its short, and a single pace deep at the rim.  In the center a depression sinks another fore-arm, giving the whole basin the quiet geometry of an eye.

The floor is soil, yes, but a soil without memory of water.  The grains are pale, angular, and squeak like fine salt dragged under a boot.  Nothing here has ever been rinsed; dust collects the way snow might accumulate elsewhere, but hangs in clots instead of drifts.

And yet—everything you see is plant.

VEGETATION AS WORLD  
From the rim inward, the “ground” is paved with living mat.  Call it plant, though it feels wrong in the mouth.  The mat resembles a sheet of overlapping fronds pressed flat, their once-separate stems fused into a continuous hide.  Touch it and it gives ever so slightly, then rebounds with the slow elasticity of cork.  A faint resinous scent lifts where you press, as though the basin exhales.

Out of this living pavement rise structures.

1. Pillars:  Knee-high at the rim, shoulder-high nearer the center.  They grow in ranks like colonnades in a ruined forum, each pillar braided from thousands of vine-threads that have wrapped, died, then been overgrown again.  Cross-sections show nothing resembling tree-rings—just endless cords, pallid green to gray.

2. Walls:  Higher density toward the north arc.  Flat faces, right angles, lintels—clearly architecture, but grown, not built.  Primitive inhabitants (or something that once counted as inhabitants) have trimmed away excess stalks so doorways remain clear, yet every doorway eventually chokes with more growth, and a new door is sliced beside it.  The place crawls incrementally, the layout shifting by centimeters per decade.

3. The Canopy:  There is no sky here—only darkness, and within that darkness a ceiling of leaves.  They do not cluster; they tessellate, forming a single seamless lid perhaps two adult spans overhead.  Light never enters, but darkness is not silence.

ATMOSPHERE AND LIGHT  
Absolute blackness.  Your eyes cannot dilate enough; you navigate by texture and vibration.  The air is cool—cold relative to body heat but never freezing.  Breath emerges visibly if someone flashes a spark; otherwise you taste it: metallic, sap-sweet, laced with something like cedar.

SOUNDS  
The place is loud in the way a cave river is loud—continuous, muscular sound without a single source.  It comes from the canopy.  Every frond, every braided pillar, creaks in slow tension.  Fibers stretch.  Saps circulate in peristaltic pulses too ponderous to sense as rhythm, but together those pulses add up to an oceanic roar.  At random intervals a pillar pops—wood splitting under its own incremental growth.  The crack ricochets, then is swallowed by the basso rush overhead.  No animal ever cries here; absence of voice makes the vegetal chorus feel louder.

EROSION, AGE, AND MOTION  
The basin is the oldest thing in its own little universe.  Wind once skimmed here, but wind requires space and water, and both have gone missing.  All abrasion ceased eons ago.  The only change is internal—growth and slow subsidence.  You can watch a wall bulge outward over the span of a human life, but you would need ten generations before that bulge becomes a new corridor.

INFRASTRUCTURE AND “URBAN” QUALITIES  
Though the scale is intimate, the density of intentional form feels urban.  There are:

• Thoroughfares—three main corridors spiral from rim to center depression, surfaced with a different plant texture (tougher, ridged) to grip bare foot or bark-shoe.  
• Courtyards—circular voids in the canopy where columns part, leaving a shaft of darkness that descends like an inverse spotlight.  Here the vegetative pavement swells into tiered seating, amphitheater style.  
• Storage Pods—spherical cysts hanging from beams, thin-walled and hollow.  A knife slice reveals powdery nutrient paste long since desiccated.  

Everything was shaped with tools no more advanced than flint scrapers, bone adzes, and braided-fiber saws.  Many such implements still lie about: a serrated shell blade wedged inside a joint, a hammering stone polished on one facet.

TECH AND CULTURE  
Whoever lived here knew only handwork, but they understood the plant the way sailors understand wind.  Their primary act was pruning: cut here and a new runner emerges there, arching exactly where you need a lintel in thirty years.  The city was coaxed, not constructed.

No hearths, no soot, no clay crucibles.  Fire would have been sacrilege or suicide in this oxygen-rich pocket.

FAUNA—OR LACK THEREOF  
No scurrying.  No flutters.  Nothing with muscle tissue exists.  The plant is its own ecosystem: leaf feeding on older leaf, rot replaced by slow absorption.  Pollination?  The growth produces no flowers, only spore-sacs that split quietly, letting microscopic motes drift on convective currents generated by their own chemical heat.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. Waterless Metabolism:  Sap here is not H₂O-based.  It is a viscous, faintly luminous solvent that crystallizes if exposed to air outside the basin.  That is why the species cannot wander.  
2. Photonegative Growth:  The plant sickens under light; darkness is required.  Nutrient energy comes from chemical gradients stored in the rock—an ancient catabolic feast on minerals.  
3. Sound as Stimulus:  Those deep resonance growls you keep hearing?  The plant uses them to coordinate growth, like a subterranean choir.  A sudden alien noise (your voice, your hammer) will make the nearest surfaces ripple in defensive crouch.  

DYNAMIC CHARACTER  
Nothing here is quick except sound.  A pillar “falls” over the course of fifty winters.  A corridor migrates a finger-length each equinox.  Rot takes centuries, recomposing itself straight into new tissue.

STANDING IN THE CENTER  
Reach the eye-depression at the very heart.  Here, the pavement dips into a bowl so smooth you suspect once-polished stone beneath the live veneer.  In the bottom sits a single artifact: a ring of braided roots twisting around a lump of silvery mineral.  The roots are dead, gray, flaking; the mineral is cool, faintly glimmering, possibly iron-nickel dragged from some meteoric past.  The ring was the settlement’s calendar.  Each century, the innermost braid was sliced away and rewoven slightly tighter, marking time like a tightening noose around that lonely stone.  Twelve braids remain.

STAY, AND YOU WILL CHANGE  
Spend an hour here and your hearing heightens; consonants begin to feel abrasive.  Spend a week and your sleep cycles re-tune to the slow bass surge overhead.  After a month, lichenoid films begin to test the warmth of your ankles.  They do not hurt, but they ask in their voiceless swell: Will you learn the patience to become one more pillar?

Because nothing leaves.  There is no door—only the threshold you imagined when you first “arrived.”  Perhaps it never opened; perhaps the plant dreamt you for company.

In the unbroken darkness, among colonnades that breathe and roofs that never see the sun, you realize the basin is not a room in a larger world.  It is the world, entire—ancient, cool, waterless, loud with its own slow thinking, empty of every life but plant and the stone it feeds on, waiting with unhurried certainty for you, too, to grow still.
