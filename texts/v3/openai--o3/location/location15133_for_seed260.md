The first thing you notice is the roar.  
It is continuous—so loud that it dislodges thought, so broad-band that it feels less like a sound and more like the substance of the air itself. The people who once came here called it the All-Note. Stand still and you feel your bones humped into sympathetic tremor; speak and your words splash away like pebbles hurled into surf.  

Yet the light is dim. The sky—if “sky” is the word for the pulsating parchment overhead—is a pewter vault lit by a sun that has forgotten how to blaze. Shadows never harden into black; they hover as fuzzy, carbon-paper ghosts. Day and night are merely different shades of charcoal.

TERRAIN  
A single chain of pinnacles dominates the region, rising like serrated piano keys: fresh, youthful rock thrust almost straight up by some catastrophe not long past. The slopes are too steep for dust to linger; instead they glint with raw mineral planes—greenish nickel-glass, violet granite still steaming from metamorphosis, splintery columns of salt that hum as they cool. Between the spires lie hanging platforms: narrow terraces only a few paces wide before they shear away into a blank precipice. On the largest shelves a scrim of pale dust has gathered—enough to suggest flatness, never enough to hide the brittleness underneath.

EROSION (OR THE LACK THEREOF)  
Everything here looks provisional, half-coded, like an unfinished rendering. Where you expect gentle talus you find knife-edges; where you expect a long scree slope you discover a polished chute, hard as poured obsidian. Rain never smoothed these heights; water is a rumor. The only agent of change is the sound. It flays rock grain by grain, leaving curious fret-marks—aerophone-like pores and helices—on every exposed face. Over centuries the All-Note will sculpt these mountains into fluted organ pipes, but for now the surfaces remain raw, bright, and newborn.

TEMPERATURE  
The air is cool enough that your exhalations condense even at midday, but never cold enough for ice. Instead, volatile gases precipitate in iridescent films on shadowed stone, then sublime as soon as the faint sun touches them. A chill wind sluices through the gullies, robbed of moisture, fattened only with grit and resonance.

VEGETATION—IF THAT’S WHAT IT IS  
Life here has taken the form of towers and tapestries of mycosonic growth: tall filaments, jointed like bamboo, yet flexible as tendon. Their color shifts from moth-white to bruise-blue depending on which frequencies the All-Note is currently favoring. There are no leaves, no roots. Each stalk anchors by molecular hooks to bare rock and drinks vibration. Cavities inside the canes convert resonance into metabolite froth; when the pressure grows too great, a joint pops and releases a stuttering chord—spores carried on sound alone. Where two canes brush, they sometimes fuse, then iris open into lace-like membranes several meters across, trembling in the wind like vast tympanic skins. In dense patches—well, “dense” for this place—you can stand among them and feel your pulse slow until it syncs with theirs.

FAUNA  
None. Or perhaps the vegetation is fauna, or the mountain itself. In any case, nothing scurries or flutters, nothing bleeds. Predation, here, is strictly mineral: cliff shears devouring shelves; sonic standing waves stripping crystal off crystal.

BUILT THINGS  
On three terraces, separated by kilometers of empty verticality, sit the relics of visitors—who or whatever they were. One is a circle of shallow pits, stone tools still lying beside half-shaped blocks of glassy ore. Another is a line of stakes hammered into a salt ridge; from each dangles a spiral of beaten nickel sheet that warbles as the mountain sings. The third is simplest and most cryptic: a single archway of basalt, lintel no wider than a man’s shoulders, pointing nowhere but out into emptiness. All are primitive, hand-hewn, yet impossibly poised on ledges you can scarcely reach without modern climbing gear. Whoever built them must have relied on some forgotten harmonics to lift themselves, or grown here within the sound and known no fear of the drop.

ATMOSPHERE & RULES OF THE WORLD  
The All-Note is not just a physical vibration—it is the local law. Walk fifty steps and gravity’s arrow may tilt a few degrees, realigning so that a wall becomes a floor. Color sometimes obeys it too: shout, and the world momentarily oversaturates, ultraviolets blooming before your eyes like after-images. Time is slow, syrupy; processes you can measure—crystal growth, trophic exchange, even the arc of the dim sun—crawl along at a pace that would stupefy a glacier. Only the sound is swift.

There are places where the cliffs flicker, their outlines phasing between possible geometries. Put your hand through at the wrong moment and you may retrieve a wrist coated in an alloy no periodic table dares proclaim. The lull in the note that happens every 19,007 beats (someone counted) briefly restores Euclidean predictability; climbers mark time by it, and the plants sigh, releasing clouds of harmonic dust.

SOUNDSCAPE  
Besides the omnipresent roar—think ocean surf magnified until the troughs between waves vanish—there are sidebands and overtones. Deep in clefts you hear a sub-bass pulse like an enormous, patient heart. On knife-ridges the pitch rises into pure tinnitus. Occasionally two counterwaves cancel just long enough to create corridors of relative hush, and in those sudden vacuums your own breath seems blasphemously loud.

DYNAMIC  
Despite the auditory violence, actual change proceeds at a regal crawl. Spires lean outward by millimeters each decade. Vegetation adds a new node only when the note below 32 Hz modulates. Ruins, once abandoned, remain exactly as they were, dusted but not buried. Even your own footprints persist, etched by vibration into the lithic memory of the terraces.

CARTOGRAPHY OF THE WEIRD  
• The Zircon Throat: A natural tunnel that speaks back in distorted versions of your words, delayed by minutes.  
• The Grey Garden: Half a hectare where the cane-plants have grown into cathedral buttresses. Enter and the din outside falls to a hush; your whispered secrets exit as booming thunder an hour later.  
• The Mid-Air Dune: A suspended sheet of ash that hangs, unmoored, five meters off a ledge, sculpted continuously by invisible vortices.  
• The Non-Pool: From a distance it resembles water, shimmering blue-black; from up close it is simply empty space refracting light wrong. Drop a stone and it disappears, reappearing days later in an unrelated crevasse.

STANDING HERE  
Your skin prickles as if chilled, though the temperature is barely cold. Your inner ear mutinies, unsure which way is down. You taste metal, and the taste carries its own harmony. Somewhere in your chest a sympathetic resonance starts; if you stay long enough you’ll learn to hum against it, to find a pocket of quieter air, to measure time in beats of the All-Note rather than the pulse of any star.

Leave, and ordinary silence will feel paradoxically deafening. Colors will look gaudy, water untrustworthy, gravity suspiciously single-minded. A day later you might wonder whether the mountains were real at all or only a migraine given landscape.

But for those who can stand the roar, the dim, the vertigo—there is clarity here, a sense of a world still booting up, still in the long hush before the first syllable of history. A place where life requires no blood, only the patience to grow from vibration, and where, if you lean in closely, rock itself is trying to remember the words to a song older than matter.
