The Walk-Through

1. First impression  
You step out of the elevator-cage and meet warm, soaking air that smells of copper sap and thunderstorm.  Everything you can see – floor, columns, ceiling, even the distant cliff face – is manufactured: milled basalt-black composites, translucent ceramic ribs, lattices of green-blue alloy that bead sweat like living skin.  Yet at breast-height and ankle-height, leaf after leaf crowds forward, shiny and dripping, so close that moist fronds brush your sleeves whenever you turn.  It feels like entering a temple whose god is both engineer and gardener.

2. Setting the stage – a high, small place  
The platform is no broader than the floor of a cathedral: perhaps seventy paces long, half as wide, with a low roof of trussed girders that opens to the sky in ragged apertures.  Beyond the rail, clouds drift below you – you are two kilometres up on the shoulder of a newborn mountain, but the mountain itself is a shell, an acidic-proof caisson poured only months ago, still cooling to the touch.  There is no bedrock here, only deliberate substrates: bolted plates, printer-grown arches, cables thrumming inside the walls.

3. Water everywhere, always moving  
• From the ceiling: condensed mist falls in a steady patter, caught by fluted leaves the size of dinner plates.  
• Along the floor: capillary gutters carry the overflow to bronze-glass sumps, where chain pumps lift it again to the roof spraylines.  
• Behind the eastern screen: a warm weeping wall of sintered quartz bleeds mineral-rich water that tastes faintly of citrus and ozone.

The perpetual migration of water is the foundry’s true engine.  Tiny turbines hidden in the gutters power the lights; larger pistons, almost soundless, drive nutrient broth up to the canopy gardens.  The only loud note is the periodic sigh of a pressure-relief valve, like a whale surfacing somewhere in the shadows.

4. Vegetation – designed exuberance  
Everything green is grafted or gene-written for this altitude.  Texture overlaps texture in layers no thicker than a hand’s span:  
• velveteen mosses soldered directly onto titanium mesh,  
• spiral-veined philodendrons coiled around steam risers for warmth,  
• carnivorous pitcher threads that dangle from ceiling struts, digesting the few insects able to hitch a ride this high.  

Each plant occupies a pre-assigned centimetre of volume, yet the choreography feels wild.  You realise the density is an illusion built from repetition, from hundreds of similar but not identical modules slotted like mosaic tiles.

5. Raw geology, freshly minted  
Because the structure is new, edges are still knife-crisp.  Drill marks shine like polished bone.  In some corridors the floor plates have not yet acquired scuffs; they echo underfoot with an unearned hollowness, as though the world beneath them has not decided on a final shape.  No lichen has found a seam, no rust has taken purchase; nothing has had time to crumble.  You are inside a landscape that is younger than most of its leaves.

6. Industry humming under the leaves  
Look between stalks and you glimpse drive-shafts, clay extruders, spindles that print more lattice to extend the garden outward by millimetres each hour.  Workers rarely appear; maintenance is done by arm-length manipulator pods that roll along ceiling rails and emit only the whir of geared porcelain.  Light bulbs are tungsten-filament, their warmth tuned to the photosynthetic preference of the canopy.  Now and then a bare coil flickers and an attendant drone swaps it for a fresh one with surgical delicacy.

7. Atmosphere of near-quiet  
The loudest natural sound would have been wind, but perforated wind-baffles tame it to a gentle organ-chord.  What remains is:  
• Droplet percussion on metal,  
• The throaty tick of pumps priming,  
• Leaf-edges rustling when thermal updrafts twist through the gaps,  
• Occasionally, the clatter of a seed-pod ricocheting down a spiral stair.

Human voices, when present, feel intrusive, as though you have raised your voice in a library.

8. Rare fauna, by design  
At this altitude migrants are few.  Tiny sapphire moths bred for pollinating the vat-orchids keep to the rafters.  Once an hour you might see a single swift slip through a skylight, snatch a moth, vanish.  The operators encourage the visits; guano is harvested, powdered, metered into the nutrient tanks.

9. The rules that hold the place together  
Rule 1: Nothing natural may touch foundation.  Roots grow in detachable pods; condensation is channelled; even fallen petals are vacuumed before they can decay in situ.  
Rule 2: Expansion is constant – micro-gantries print new floor sections, then seed them with substrate gel, then graft exact clones of surrounding flora.  The garden walks outward, centimetre by centimetre, like an amoeba of glass and chlorophyll.  
Rule 3: All energy must be reclaimed.  Heat from the printing kilns feeds the tropical understory; sugars from photosynthesis drive the servos; dissolved metals in the run-off are re-electroplated onto corroded pump vanes.  Loss is a myth here.

10. Feelings that linger  
Stand at the western parapet where the ceiling opens widest.  Warm rain kisses your eyebrows.  Below, scaffold loops founders in cloud so thick it looks like milk.  Every surface around you is both factory and fern-forest, and all of it is so new that you can still taste the curing resin in the air.  It is a place balanced between birth and momentum: high, wet, lush, purpose-built, but with just enough unfilled canvas that you sense how quickly it could double in size, how eagerly it might swallow the rest of the mountain, replacing ancient rock with eternally young manufacture.

If you wait until nightfall, the lights dim to a copper dusk, turbines idle, pumps slow to a steady heartbeat.  The only motion then is water still climbing to the roof in ghost columns, and leaves nodding approval in the glow.  It is almost perfectly quiet.  The mountain, the garden, the factory – all three breathe together.
