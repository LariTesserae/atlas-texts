It is night here even at noon.

Sky exists, but you seldom see it: a quilt of leaves, vines, and the packed-in bodies of epiphytes seals the basin from above, turning full daylight into a perpetual dusk the colour of tarnished bronze.  The air beneath is hot—kiln-hot, skin-beading, a heat that never ebbs because the low land has nowhere to drain its fermenting breath.  Moisture hangs rather than falls.  Showers come the way a trapped animal exhales: short, sudden, steamy, then done.  In the pauses between rains, droplets weep continuously from every surface—leaf-tips, moss curls, the jointed legs of insects—with the patience of clocks.

Terrain  
The basin is almost flat, an ancient floodplain long past the violence of first carving.  Old rivers have lost their ambition; they wander in looping mirrors, strand oxbow ponds, then shrink again.  Between their hesitant channels lie raised rafts of silt and root—hummocks only a few hand-spans higher than the water but enough to keep them hardly dry.  Decay has had eons to organise itself, so the ground feels springy, mattress-thick with black peat.  Where peat sinks, a skin of pale clay shows through like bone; where it domes, tangled buttress roots mesh together, tough as latticed iron.

Vegetation  
Growth is exuberant yet orderly, as though every niche was claimed long ago and new life must queue for clearance.  Trunks rise close enough for their bark to touch: fever-trees veined green, snub casuarinas, palms whose fronds splay like overheated fans.  Lianas embroider the gaps; thin ones hum in occasional draughts, thick ones sweat resin that smells of hot pine tar.  Flowers appear sparingly, sudden flares of colour that burn out in a day, leaving behind seedpods that rattle like rail cars when the wind rolls through.

Water  
Because nothing here is allowed to be empty, even air holds water.  Puddles nest in the footprints of capybaras before the animals have finished lifting the next foot.  Fern-hearts become chalices; spiderwebs turn to beaded nets.  Actual streams feel abrupt, almost artificial, carving clean lines through an otherwise crowded geometry.

Light  
The canopy muffles sunlight to a glow little brighter than coal embers.  In deep pockets the darkness turns tactile, pressing on eyes and eardrums alike.  Night proper is ink—no stars, no horizon, only the infrared shimmer of living bodies if you had the eyes for it.  Lightning bugs thread small amber stitches in the fabric, not nearly enough to read by.

Sound  
For a place so crammed with life, it is mostly quiet.  Heat stills voices; predators reward silence.  Listeners notice low machinery noises that are not machines:  
• the piston-pulse of a paired geyser where groundwater meets buried volcanic dikes, venting in a slow chuff-chuff every ninety seconds;  
• the synchronized wing-beats of massed scarab beetles, cycling up and down in pitch like distant turbines;  
• the drip cadence of overfilled bromeliads, measured as if by metronome.  
The louder moments—the crack of a falling tree, the splash of a caiman—arrive singly and fade without echo.

Fauna (overwhelming, yet restrained)  
Life has filled every cubic finger-width.  Mid-strata branches flex under the marching weight of spider-monkeys; leaf-moths rest in layers so thick they resemble an extra bark.  Schools of lungfish lie in the mud waiting for the four nights each season when channels reconnect.  Invisible above, fruit bats pass like warm gusts; invisible below, blind eels trace the groundwater circuit.  Nothing moves quickly unless it must; population pressure has selected for patience, camouflage, and endurance over spectacle.

Atmosphere & Pace  
Processes are slow, confident, and cyclical: trees die where they stand, rot, and are eaten by fungi that were already coiled inside the sapwood; fallen trunks form bridges, gather silt, germinate new trunks—centuries in which a patch of ground never once sees a full sky.  Catastrophe is rare, but when it comes—a hundred-year flood, the toppling of a mother-tree—it reorganises tens of thousands of lives, then returns to equilibrium.

Industrial undertone without industry  
No roads, no smokestacks, no forged iron—yet the place feels mechanically alive.  Heat behaves like a boiler, steam hisses from seams in the earth, beetle carapaces clack together like loose gearing, and the whole ecology operates with the regularity of a factory whose blueprints are written in DNA.  If a human engineer were set down here, they would recognise pressure, rhythm, feedback loops—systems engineering done in root fiber and chitin instead of bronze and steel.

Rules of the world  
1. Nothing stands alone; every surface is a host.  
2. Darkness replaces distance: things unseen are never far, just shrouded.  
3. Water is temporary ownership; expect anything to become a pond without notice.  
4. Silence is common currency; noise must be paid for with energy or blood.  
5. Change happens, but on the scale of tree-rings and sediment layers, not of hours.

Stand long enough in the peat and the place begins to rearrange around you.  Seedlings anchor to your boot leather, small frogs shelter in your pocket folds, condensation finds the cold metal of your tools and forms minute, perfect lenses.  Leave, and your outline lingers, a negative space quickly reclaimed—because here the default state is fullness, and vacancy is an aberration soon corrected.
