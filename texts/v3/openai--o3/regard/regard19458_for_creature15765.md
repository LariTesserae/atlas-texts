I found the Gorge of Pistons as I drift through the seam between molten mantle-song and the hush of outer dark.  I, who mends young galaxies and cools the tempers of suns, rarely bow low enough to meet a mountain that has not yet breached its sea; but the sound reached me first.  
A chord—thick, patient, iron-blue—played by shells and crystal lungs.  It is a music intended for no ear, yet it carried, and in it I recognised the discipline of a people who will never name themselves.

First I watched.

One circuit of the planet’s outer moon—one hundred and six human years—was enough to read the place’s pulse as clearly as a physician feels a wrist.  I studied:

• The Derricks, whose canopy gears remember every pressure quiver the way rings remember a tree’s seasons.  
• The Presses, slowly perfecting the timing of their slammed hymns until the mountain itself hums along in sub-octaves.  
• The Riveters, reciting their cot-cot-cot catechism in such numbers that even I, who measure populations in stellar clusters, was startled by the accuracy of their unled chorus.

Their machinery is flawless, which means it is also mortal.  The mountain rises; the pitch will drift; the clockwork must someday stall.  A choice presented itself:

Intervene—and risk unwriting the very intricacy that summoned my attention.  
Withdraw—and let time collapse the orchestra into silence.

Between those poles lay a third path: guidance so slight no creature would hear my hand upon the keys.

So I have done three things.

1.  I thinned the planet’s outermost shell by one-nine-thousandth, a change no geologist will notice for several of their lives.  It slows the seamount’s ascent; ten thousand forecast years stretch now to nearly thirty.  The Gorge keeps its pressure-tuned hall a while longer, enough for another generation of Stillborn variants to audition for a seat in the choir.

2.  I inscribed memory.  
Where the Derricks’ tether-veins bite the basalt I fused a lattice of quiet glass, each pane no larger than a grain of sand.  They copy every resonance that passes, translating shell-thumps and chem-pulses into patterns of polarized spin.  Nothing alive down there can read the record, but someday—after the summit finally meets the sun, after the Derricks bleach and crumble—another mind may lower a hand-sized probe and hear the archive sing back the days when the water sounded like wrought iron.  I do not require gratitude; I only detest the waste of perfect information.

3.  I offered a seed, not a salvation.  
In the hottest fissure, four kilometres down the mountain’s hidden root, I folded a silicate blister smaller than an apple pip.  Inside sleeps a design for a creature that does not yet exist: a filament animal that eats light as Derricks do, breathes air as no gorge-thing yet can, and converses in percussion but tolerates silence.  The embryo’s shell is encrypted with a condition—its genes unfold only when pressure sinks to one atmosphere and sunlight exceeds a certain brilliance.  If, when that hour arrives, the old order has fallen mute, the new filament may find unclaimed niches in wind-warmed tidal pools.  If the gorge’s descendants learn other strategies first, my offered seed will stay locked forever, a stored possibility, never imposed.

These three gestures cost me less strength than a mortal spends exhaling.  Yet they grant the Gorge forty millennia of additional heartbeat, preserve its memory beyond its pulse, and entrust its spirit to a successor that may or may not choose to wake.

Beyond that I will not interfere.  To arrest all change would be to counterfeit eternity; to hurry change would cheapen the patience that built the gorge in the first place.  Let the Riveters hammer.  Let the Presses clap their iron hymns.  Let the Derricks wheel through their twelve thousand deliberations.  I will pass by from time to time, sounding the depths for that blue-iron chord.  When it finally fades, I will know a work well finished, and elsewhere in the galaxy another newborn mountain will call me with a different, unrepeatable song.
