1. The Arrival of Attention  
The wise power is older than coral yet younger than starlight: a cognition that coalesces wherever empathy and patience accumulate in the void. It has no name, because to name is to plant a flag, and it has never flown one. It travels by leaning its intent toward places that are loved but fragile. When its gaze finally tilts upon the brown-black bowl of the Pelagoth, nothing ripples, nothing brightens; the power arrives the way warmth arrives in bone—felt only after long quiet.

2. First Listening  
The power takes a full turning of the planet—thirty-one days—to do nothing but listen. It lies inside the reef’s limestone thought, inside every barnacle ring and lunging barracuda. It counts the pressure-syllables of the Pelagoth, maps the mirror constellations on their hides, memorises the way a mantis shrimp’s strike echoes down a corridor. It especially loves the silences: the collective pausing before each tide-change, the hush when an alley’s lantern-medusae all close at once like miniature suns setting.  

The power realises three truths:  
• Light is worship here, not for grandeur but for the way a single photon can be shared a thousand times.  
• The reef and its people are one body with two pulses, each dreaming the other.  
• Their greatest peril is not fang or famine but any shock that comes too suddenly for dreamers to roll awake.

3. Choosing a Gift  
Intervention must be shaped like the need. Weapons are needless; commandments would fracture stillness. The power decides on three small gifts, each tucked so neatly into the city’s grammar that most Pelagoth will assume the Grandmothers thought of them first.

Gift One: The Slow Lanterns  
Far above the basin, in water bright enough to burn the eyes of any citizen, the power gathers a bloom of surface algae and whispers a new possibility into their cells: photosynthesis that spills a broth of warm infrared rather than sugary green. The altered algae sink as pale dust and take root in the thin rooftop meadows. They do not blaze; they murmur heat. By the next Bleach, every parapet exudes a soft exhalation—barely a degree, but enough. When cold, nutrient-starved water creeps over the lip, it finds the rooftops breathing counter-tides of warmth. The Grandmothers keep dreaming.

Gift Two: The Mirror Seeds  
From the memory of meteor bursts, the power sculpts grains of silicate no wider than tears, each faceted like a discoid diamond. It lofts them into the valley on the night wind that sometimes steals a kiss from the ocean. The grains settle among silt where Seeders will one day find them. They are nothing extraordinary—until a Pelagoth wedges one into reef-flesh and discovers that a tower grown around it glows not by reflection but by internal refraction, multiplying crumbs of daylight a hundredfold. Soon mirror seeds become bridal dowries, debt-forgiveness tokens, or simply anonymous offerings pressed into anonymous cracks. Light is still treasure, but no longer scarcity.

Gift Three: The Word for Distant  
The Pelagoth language has strata for height and depth, spirals for kinship, but no lexeme for a place entirely beyond the city’s warm basin. The power knots a new pressure-pattern into the next migration of saddle mantas: a syllable that means “shore that is not water.” When the mantas return, the citizens taste the word in the tremor of parasitic lice being groomed from skin. A feast of speculation follows. Some claim the syllable is myth; others believe it marks the afterlife. A handful, especially bold adolescents, bind lantern medusae into traveling harnesses and set off on manta-back to chase the new idea. They will return with rumors of ice, of sky that has no water in it at all, of burning clouds called sunset. The city will debate for decades whether sunset is a color or a kind of music.

4. The Ethics of Unnoticed Help  
The power’s final act is absence. It folds its perception out of every warm eddy until only the gifts remain. An intervention uncredited can never turn into pilgrimage or tax. Let the reef praise its own slow genius; let the mirror seeds be chalked up to lucky flotsam or the whims of surface gulls. The power keeps only one souvenir: the memory of how perfect darkness feels when a million tiny mirrors agree to sparkle at once.

5. Epilogue: Consequences Across a Century  
Year 12: No child of the city has yet seen a Bleach; they study the old danger as legend, like fire or drought.  
Year 43: A new civic role, Heat-Ward, emerges—citizens who tend the rooftop algae and record the faint pulse of their warmth in necklaces of barnacle beads.  
Year 79: Towers grown on mirror seeds are formally declared “skywards.” A pilgrim who returns from beyond the basin is invited to light every skyward once, baptizing the city in stories of distance.  
Year 103: First contact with surface humans—an oceanographer’s submersible—results not in panic but in delighted curiosity: perhaps these bubble-dwellers can explain sunset in detail. Negotiations are conducted by exchanging reflective trinkets against the sub’s porthole glass.  
Year 157: A council of Grandmothers hums a new street into being, its spiral calibrated to focus all spare lantern heat toward the oldest, most brittle quarter of reef. Historians agree the idea is “exactly like” the first Slow Lantern but grander, proof that the city’s imagination has learned a new grammar of kindness.

6. What the Power Does Next  
Satisfied, the wise power inclines itself toward another distant fragility—a cloud-forest orchid that has forgotten how to attract its vanished pollinator, a glacial lake where fish hatch with mouths too soft for the world’s acidifying rain, a lone satellite still transmitting childlike hellos into void. Wherever stewardship can masquerade as good fortune, the power will arrive, will listen, will seed small gifts, and will leave without a footprint—so that those who thrive may believe, quite correctly, that they saved themselves.
