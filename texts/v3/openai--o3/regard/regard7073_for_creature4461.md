The first thing the Power did was listen.  
It hovered—if a thing older than orbit can be said to hover—three invisible hand-breadths above the plateau’s wet roof, and it let the city’s sound wash through its attention like grains of silt through water. Nine days (or eighty-six thousand pump cycles) it listened, learning the language of piston and runoff, the lullaby that worm-tenders sing, the complex courtesy of footsteps paused to allow a tressel to pass. Only when the cadence of Vord Kal could be replayed, note for note, in its boundless memory did the Power allow itself to wonder what help might truly be help.

Because wisdom, when it is real, does almost nothing.

1. A correction measured in heartbeats  
High over the city, glider eels were thinning. Their translucent bodies had grown fragile; a subtle deficit of trace boron in the mineral film had propagated up the food chain. The Power reached no farther than the plateau rim and touched a single vein of weathering granite, coaxing an extra feather of the element into the groundwater. Four months later the eels’ molts came down thicker, silk instead of rice-paper crackle, and the Lift-Loomers argued happily over how best to weave cloth that no longer tore.

No citizen noticed the cause; one apprentice archivist merely logged an “unseasonable richness” in the yearly mineral tally. That entry satisfied the Power. Intervention, done well, resembles coincidence.

2. A gift of stillness precisely 243 seconds long  
Silence, among Kalites, is myth—so the Power minted one genuine hush and spent it like a coin. One dawn, while the city lay in the grey between steam-lamp extinguishing and sunrise, every moving part within its jurisdiction coasted to an even, harmless halt. Water bravely obeyed gravity but forsook noise: it flowed in perfect laminar sheets, unstuck from turbulence. Chains refused to rattle; gutters swallowed their own echo; even the old First Pumps finished a stroke and simply… waited.

Two hundred forty-three seconds later the churn resumed, but every ear that had happened to be awake carried the impossible memory of quiet. Pattern-makers used it to recalibrate wind-harps. Poets tried and failed to name the interval, and so the Pump-Chaplains entered it in civic calendars as The Held Breath. Henceforth, disputes brought to arbitration were tested: if grievance could be recited start to finish in less than one Held Breath, reconciliation was deemed mandatory. Most quarrels proved smaller than 243 seconds.

3. An introduction between strangers who lived side by side  
Beneath the basalt, Basalt-Mould sang pressure into stone. At the cliff edge, gutter-corals tallied lightning in secret salts. Neither suspected the other existed; their niches never overlapped. The Power asked no permission—how could it?—but it braided two microns of mould mycelium into the hollow core of a single coral stalk. When the next storm came, the coral’s chemical batteries discharged through that filament down into the sub-rock galleries. The mould, tasting raw electricity for the first time, accelerated its glass-thread stitch by a factor of three.

Nothing dramatic followed: a water-tower scheduled to fail in the next thirty winters will now last ninety. Yet the unseen pact pleased the Power, for it aligned with the plateau’s own ethic: strength accrued slowly, with compound interest paid in drips.

4. The scroll that wrote itself  
A wise power must sometimes leave evidence—not proof, only suggestion—so that curiosity continues its labor. On the inside of a copper condensate pipe three storeys beneath the Grand Gearworks, droplets began to deposit silica in serpentine whorls. Over four years the pattern lengthened into an unbroken ribbon of calligraphy. Any literate Kalite could read the two alphabets embedded there—old pump-makers’ notation entwined with the gutterwrights’ shorthand for flow rates—but the combination spelled no ordinary instruction. Apprentices traced the ribbon, arguing whether it was music, mathematics, or prayer. Eventually a trio of pump-chaplains realized the curve described the ideal eccentric cam that would let a reciprocating pump reverse direction without jarring the frame. They forged one, installed it, and watched energy expenditure fall seven percent plateau-wide.

They called the discovery self-born; scholars filed it under Emergent Engineering. The Power accepted the compliment of anonymity.

5. A covenant of endings  
The dead had always ascended to the Sky Parlor, bones tucked beside eel roosts. But the cliff lattices were filling; another century and room would fail. The Power weighed options, for death is an ecosystem’s final investment. It remembered a cavern far below the city, where warm vapors rose through a mesh of obsidian ribs. One unremarkable night, the tunnel air cooled by scarcely a single degree—enough for steam to condense and begin carving a vertical chimney toward the ossuaries. Kalite stone-masons eventually broke through into that cavity. They felt the upward draft, compared its pull to the swells in their own lungs, and declared the place hallowed by ancestral breathing. New catacombs went in, deep rather than high, ribs of mould-strengthened glassy stone arching like inverted eels. The Sky Parlor earned time without losing sanctity.

6. What the Power chose not to do  
It did not cure rust, for rust taught inspection.  
It did not drain the plateau, for dryness would erase the culture it admired.  
It did not give the Kalites bright, cheap electricity, because their night-time dimness bred a tenderness of voice and an economy of gesture that would have been casualties of abundance.  
It certainly did not reveal itself; reverence directed outward can mature into science, but reverence focused on a single benefactor often curdles into dependence, and dependence is cousin to decay.

Epilogue measured in gutter ages  
A thousand years on, Vord Kal continues its stately subsidence, adding layers so that roofs become streets, catwalks become cellars, and the wide-lunged people keep breeding babies whose first lullaby is water slapping bronze. The Power remains attentive, but its ledger of actions holds only a few more entries: a mineral nudge here, a meteor-shard diverted there to enrich the soil film. Nothing that would be headline news even if Kalites printed newspapers.

Some nights, when steam off the condensers climbs absolutely straight, a child on a high platform may glimpse, just for a breath, a taller shadow inside the vapor—something that exhales opposite the wind and then is gone. The city folds those sightings into the same folder where Mist Angels reside, and that, the Power thinks, is perfectly in order. Wonder is a currency; it must stay in circulation.

And so the wise, benevolent watcher does what all true guardians do:  
almost nothing, almost always—  
but exactly enough.
