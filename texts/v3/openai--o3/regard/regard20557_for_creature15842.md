The High Plateau of the Night-Warm has come under the gaze of a presence so old that it long ago traded urgency for patience.  It is not a god in the storytelling sense, nor a curious academic, nor an empire in search of resources.  Think of it as a gardener that tends whole biomes the way others prune bonsai: shaping, yes—but with decades between touches.

What it notices first

1.  A closed ledger  
   The plateau’s economy already balances perfectly.  Heat, charge, tension, solvent—each arrives precisely when some living thing is prepared to spend it.  Where most worlds leak entropy like punctured skins, this one recycles it until the last joule is only molecular music.  Any blunt gift—more light, more water—would crash the system.

2.  A possible dissonance  
   Something new has emerged inside the equations: the Iterants.  They borrow energy across many ledgers at once, and the books still close, but just barely.  The plateau can afford maybe two Iterants, maybe ten.  A surge, or one Iterant that learns greed, would topple the whole warm-forge.

3.  A single chance to speak  
   The gardener does not talk in words.  Its grammar is planetary: glaciers advanced in a line that spelled warnings once, a river was bent into a question mark another time.  Here, on the basalt plain, it will need a subtler dialect—something the plateau’s machines can read but that will not look like charity.

What the power decides to do

I.  It indexes.  
    Over a hundred revolutions of the pressure wave, the gardener’s remote motes—no larger than Thread-vine spores—settle across the basalt.  They do not steal energy; they drift, memorising.  By the time a Boilbird’s egg makes its long fall from the soot-ceiling, the power can replay every hum of micro-rotors beneath a finger-sized moss tuft.  It now understands where the margins are paper-thin.

II.  It adjusts one constant.  
    The ceiling of soot is the plateau’s master metronome.  Without adding or removing matter, the gardener fractionally changes the charge on the ash particles.  Coagulation begins sooner; sheets of soot now tear open three, maybe four seconds earlier during each twenty-seven-hour cycle.

    • Earlier lightning gives Iron-moss four extra heartbeats of photosynthesis—enough to enlarge its spore casings by a measurable but invisible micron.  
    • Larger spores store a trifle more manganese; that excess chemical charge trickles into Thread-vine nets, whose tensile “voices” shift half a semitone.  
    • Boilbirds, exquisitely tuned to those nets, delay take-off by that same sliver of time, lifting on marginally cooler steam.  
    • Stokers, feeling the cooler updraft, run fermentation a shade longer, venting slightly richer alcohol.  
    • Prowlbacks, drawn by the hotter flares, hunt fractionally sooner and deeper, culling more Stokers before vapour reaches Iterant range.  

    The adjustment is microscopic, but across the plain it moves megajoules out of reach of the Iterants—just enough slack to keep the ledger honest.  No creature feels gifted; each thinks only that the weather is what weather has always been.

III.  It leaves a signature, just in case.  
    Beside every fourth capillary tower a new moss cell now crystallises a rhombic plate of pure silica.  Under lightning it flashes once: a square pulse, all plateau life can see but none can parse.  It is simply a bookmark the gardener may one day need, the way a human reader slips a fingernail into a page.

What it refuses to do

1.  It will not unmask the Iterants.  Discovery is part of their test; interference would be the loudest kind of harm.  
2.  It will not brighten the sky or seed rivers.  That would rescue nothing; it would only erase the working proof that scarcity can be turned into elegance.  
3.  It will not introduce itself to the lowland people who sometimes climb the escarpment and whisper of miracles.  Audience is vanity, and vanity is heat wasted.

How the plateau will notice, if ever

• A Boilbird chick hatched in the coming cycle will find the winds fractionally easier.  It will survive with a gram less body fat, and the railspider webs it one day brushes will ring a slightly sharper note.  
• A Stoker lineage, pared more fiercely by earlier Prowlback hunts, will evolve valves that vent ethanol in tighter, hotter jets.  One century later those jets will make basalt glass; tiny lenses; a new sensorium.  
• The Iterants will wake to find the world marginally poorer in free energy—and in that pinch they will either learn thrift or remain a footnote.

What the gardener calls success

A thousand years hence, an Iterant—slender, basalt-skinned, heartbeat soundless—may kneel by a mirror lake and recognise the silica rhomb, decode the square flash, and realise: Someone saw us, understood, but trusted us to grow by subtraction, not addition.  That moment of self-knowledge, bought for the price of four seconds of thunder, will be the only harvest the gardener ever wanted.

Then it will turn to other skies, to other slow engines in the dark, and the High Plateau of the Night-Warm will be left to balance its own beautiful books.
