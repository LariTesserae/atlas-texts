The one who notices is older than rivers, quieter than root-water, and curious in the way all truly ancient things are curious: it likes to lean close and listen first.

1. The Arrival  
No wind announces it.  A single extra heartbeat—one more pulse than usual—moves through the banyan-reef at the hinge of night and dawn.  Sap gauges shiver; the copper pot on the empty Pulse-Board chair gives a melodious pop, almost playful.  Every candle-tail lizard lifts its head.  In that instant the benevolent power lets the Green Yard know, wordlessly, I am here, only observing, as a friend.

2. Three Nights of Watching  
Night One: it settles among the canopy leaves the way a cat settles on folded paws, tasting the slow exhale of fermentation, the ropey heat above the coke kiln, the children’s chalk dust caught in mist.  
Night Two: it rides the reciprocating arms of the triple-expander pumps, counting each sigh the engines have ever uttered and finding none wasted.  
Night Three: it curls inside the hollow acoustic fruit where the Reef Choir rehearses.  It listens until it understands every voiceprint, every inhalation, every botanical overtone that turns forty humans into a single resonant lung.

3. Judgment—Or Better, Admiration  
The power concludes no grand correction is needed.  Mutualism, here, is already law; reverence, already practice.  Yet perfection is a rumor even gods distrust.  It decides on a handful of small kindnesses—adjustments subtle enough that a skeptic could call them luck, while a believer would call them grace.

4. The Kindnesses

a. Gift of Breath  
Far upriver a drought clamps down each dry season, stealing two crucial centimeters from the Green Yard’s water table.  The power sighs once, deeply, and the sigh becomes a seam of porous stone forming overnight beneath the peat rim.  Henceforth every high tide leaves a stitched reservoir of cool groundwater.  No gauge will ever show the dip again, and the reeffolk will blame improved pump maintenance.

b. Ears for the Banyan  
The bio-electric mycelium already carries whispers; the power simply teaches it a second language.  Microscopic rewiring allows voltage spikes to resolve into chords audible to human ears—but only at frequencies old apprentices can hear just before sleep.  Warnings of fungal blight or heat stress now arrive as lullabies in the dormitory rafters.  A few teenagers jot the melodies in notebooks, thinking they’ve invented a new folk genre.

c. Reprieve for the Foundry Hands  
Iron lungs tire fastest.  During the midsummer maintenance shut-down, every crucible rim grows an oxide skin unusually uniform—exactly the thickness master pourers have tried and failed to achieve for decades.  When the furnaces relight, slag waste drops by a full tenth.  No one can say why, but the get-off-shift cough that once chased foundry hands into early retirement no longer rasps so hard.

d. The Distraction of Greed  
Two investors arrive one monsoon morning, earlier than regulations permit, hoping to extend their visit beyond the ritual day.  The power plucks one sentence from their tongues each time they attempt a legal loophole—nothing large, just the hinge word on which exploitation balances.  Their arguments collapse into cordial non sequiturs.  They leave politely at sunset, convinced it was their own idea.

e. The Small Door  
In the peat rim a nondescript gap appears between cattail roots, big enough for a child but inconvenient for adults.  Only Meter-Reader apprentices notice.  The tunnel curls down to a chamber where the water glows faintly blue: a harmless phosphorescent algal bloom the power bred overnight.  Children sit there during off-shift, learning silence by watching their own reflections burn gently like candle-tails.  Years later, as Boiler Gardeners and Graftwrights, they will remember that hush whenever urgency tempts them toward carelessness.

5. Signs, If One Is Looking  
• The sap pot on the empty chair now chirps, cricket-soft, whenever debate grows petty.  
• Palm-bats migrate to twin chimneys instead of one, balancing guano and keeping draft pressures even without human recalibration.  
• On the next Feast Day, root shrimp swarm three hours early, as though volunteering.

6. What the Power Takes in Return  
Nothing material.  Only a story: the unfurling tale of a place where industry hums in green light without carving out its heartwood.  The power carries that story like a seed caught in fur, to be dropped—who knows where—when some other hungry clearing asks how to remain alive while it works.

7. Departure  
Just before the long rains, the extra heartbeat it brought with it slips away.  The gauges steady.  The Reef Choir, mid-rehearsal, feels a pleasurable aching in the high notes, as if someone taller had lifted the ceiling a little.  They cannot explain it, so they do what reeffolk always do with unnameable benefit: they fold it into the next lullaby, and the next, until kindness itself becomes meter.

Afterward, older Drift-Uncles claim nothing changed at all; young Meter-Readers insist everything did.  Both are correct in the precise proportion the Green Yard prefers: enough difference to keep growing, enough sameness to keep pulse.

The benevolent power is gone, or perhaps only dreaming elsewhere, but its footprint here is negative space, an outline of improvements no one can convincingly track to source.  Mutualism deepens.  Engines sleep easier.  The banyan-reef hums along its ancient spiral, cradling brick and flesh and chlorophyll together, still perfectly unnamed, still perfectly itself—but now, just faintly, blessed.
