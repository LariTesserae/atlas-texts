The one who noticed the Yard called itself nothing, because names imply edges and it had none. Long before basalt ribs broke the clay, it drifted between stellar furnaces listening for places that tried, against odds, to stay alive. Most locations managed or failed and were finished stories. The Yard was different: it was perpetual beginning, a choir of half-sung notes, brave because unfinished. Such courage deserved a listener—and, perhaps, a gentle hand.

The power watched for eight cold seasons before touching anything. It learned the pulse of rising hummocks, the shiver that preceded a rail splice giving way, the hush that meant the otters were threading back upstream. It listened to the counting ritual at new moon and understood that those ragged numbers were a collective heartbeat keeping panic at bay. Only when it could recite every layer of the Shiftfolk’s speech, every steam-vine’s nightly exhalation, did it decide to act.

Its first principle was Do No Straight Lines. Any gift imposed too directly would snap like clean glass in the Yard’s churn. So the power worked in veils, in gradients, in options that could be taken up or left alone.

1. A Syllable of Warmth  
In midwinter the fermentation moss sometimes faltered—one cold snap too many, one sluice of alkaline drizzle that killed a square kilometre of heat culture. The power thickened infrared light just above the valley by less than a fraction of a watt: not enough to be called heat, merely enough to keep dew from crystallising on the moss pores. Shiftfolk noticed only that that year’s coats refused to go inert. They credited better soot broth, and the ritual brewmasters earned quiet pride.

2. The Luminous Thread  
Mapwright ink darkened or paled according to airborne metals, yet the readings lagged by hours. The power braided a microscopic filament of photoluminescence into the mist itself—a ghost-net only ink could see. From then on, when basalt Mothers even thought about rising, the air around them fluoresced one tint shy of visible; atlases shifted shade in minutes, not hours. The Mapwrights called it the Glow Advance, claimed it came from improvements in lichen harvesting. Cartographers elsewhere sent delegations to learn the trick, but the filament lay beyond lenses, belonging to light not matter.

3. A Changed Note in the Furnace Chant  
The Furnace-Monks’ resonance practice was already near perfect; all the power did was add a missing overtone, inaudible to human ears but clear to basalt. During prayer the new note told subterranean sponge-columns, Wait, the roof above you loves its present form. Several eruptions delayed themselves, rose later under empty tracts where damage would be small. The monks believed they had finally purified their pitch. They bowed to the cooling shells, unaware of the cosmic tutor humming second voice.

4. The Otters’ Gloss  
Iron-black otters swallowed filings for iron, but rust poisoned them with time. The power folded an enzyme into an algae patch at a warm effluent. Otters feeding there discovered their livers could now crack ferric oxide the way yeast breaks sugar. Pups lived seasons longer; their playful migrations lengthened, re-seeding canals with healthy algae. No one saw the enzyme, but Shiftfolk noticed sluice water cleared a little, and that was miracle enough.

5. The Quiet Return of Lost Things  
Tools dropped into slurry were gone forever; digging for them invited collapse. The power, amused by the Scrimshankers’ reverence for waste, coaxed surface tension in certain puddles to behave like thin skin. Anything weighing less than two kilograms that touched such a pool at dawn would later be found sitting beside it at dusk, bead-polished, ready for another hand. The Shiftfolk coined a new stratum-word, “mudgift,” and left small tokens—cog teeth, half coins—at the pool margins in thanks. The power accepted the offerings as stories and shivered with delight.

6. The Cedar Children’s Skyholes  
Children who had never felt ground sometimes succumbed to lung-chill in the steam canopy. The power persuaded the hooded corvids, who already traded rubble for shellfish, to drop their broken shells along chosen vine nets. Calcium leached, vines withered in precise round patches, and sudden apertures formed: skyholes that let true sunlight spear into the ducts. Temperatures rose by mere degrees, enough for young lungs to clear. The birds enjoyed the easier cracks in sheet-metal roofs; the children named the holes “sunmouths” and sang through them at dawn.

7. A Compact with Machinery  
Trackworms and clinker crabs were drifting toward resource wars: worms chewed ties the crabs wanted warm, crabs pinched slag worms needed for filler. The power savored both species’ stubbornness and introduced a symmetry—when a worm laid a fresh tie, trace copper in its excretion now produced a resonance the crabs disliked. They turned away instinctively, focusing on deeper slag fields. Conflict evaporated without meeting minutes, and rail uptime ticked upward. The engineers logged the change under “unexpected but favourable emergent behaviour.”

8. A Promise Written in Condensation  
At the peak of each third shift, every sheet-metal roof carries twin pulses: the heartbeat of the engine below and the drip of rain above. The power braided those pulses for a single breath each night so that workers on break, staring at pooled water, felt a throb that matched their own pulse precisely. Most dismissed it as bodily echo, but the exhausted sometimes sat up straighter, suddenly certain they were not alone in the universe. That certainty, unarticulated, kept three suicides from the river in one particularly grim season. No one filed a report. The universe didn’t require one.

9. Allowing One Failure to Stand  
Benevolence is not indulgence. One autumn, a half-finished smelter was erected directly atop a basalt Mother’s throat. The power, able but unwilling to rewrite every error, let the inevitable rise proceed. The ground domed, the smelter cracked, slag poured like dull sunrise down the slope. Seven workers were injured but none killed—iris-wide fungus pads the power had earlier encouraged grew exactly where bodies fell. Pain, recrimination, wiser siting laws: all required the lesson. Mercy does not always build; sometimes it simply limits the price of ignorance.

10. The Promise of After  
Finally, the power planted something that would outlast the Yard’s churn: deep in the clay beneath the orphanage boiler, it etched a lattice of carbon so refined it could remember heat. Long after the Yard forgets furnaces, when ice again seeks this valley, the lattice will pulse soft warmth for any creature small enough to find the crawlspace. It is not a monument; monuments ask to be seen. It is a quiet IOU left to tomorrow’s inheritors: Someone cared, once, about your shivering.

––––

The benevolent power never asked for worship, but rumors arose. The Mapwrights drew faint halos on some atlas sheets where the Glow Advance shimmered brightest. Furnace-Monks brewed a cup of slag-tea nightly and left it untouched beside the open air, joking that the Invisible Auditor deserved refreshment. Children of the Cedarducts tied moss-felt ribbons at sunmouth edges so the sky would remember who needed light.

The Yard, being what it is, took each gift into its engines and its mycelia, its counting rituals and discard yards, and made of them something provisional. New problems fermented immediately; instability has appetite. The power considered further interventions, but wisdom whispered enough. Perfection would fossilise the place it loved for its becoming.

So it settled into remote companionship, a guardian willing to watch whole eras of revision. When the Yard inhaled at dawn, fog glowing pearl through gantry ribs, the power inhaled with it. Beneath the combined breath, basalt Mothers listened, otters rolled through silver slicks, monks hummed, vines baked calcium into lace, and somewhere an orphan slept a fraction warmer than physics alone allowed.

That was action enough.
