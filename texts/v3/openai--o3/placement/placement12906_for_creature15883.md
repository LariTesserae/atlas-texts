First I thought I would be an ordinary visitor—rope-scarred climber, boots hissing on the hot green hide—but that felt like borrowing someone else’s dream. The plateau does not need another soft-bodied trespasser taking notes. It already has plenty of us, brief perturbations whose footprints knit shut before sunset.

So I tried on other roles.

Perhaps a Weeper: a hollow tower with warm winds vaulting my ribs, exhaling weather the way lungs exhale breath. But a Weeper never moves; it is grand, slow, confident. I could not stay still that long.

Then a Runner: a sap cord sluicing through my own veins, crawling to a wound, stitching torn flesh edge to edge. Runners live fast and die the moment the job is done. Too little time to look around.

A Listen-leaf tempted me next. Listening is close to what I already know how to do. Drift a meter an hour, taste the wind, decide if its rumors warrant alarm. But Listen-leafs are reabsorbed the instant they report in, and I felt the tug of a longer story than one message long.

The shape that finally took and grew roots in me was smaller than a tower, quicker than a vine, longer-lived than any one wound crew:

I am a Mantle Walker.

Not a human explorer—that name belongs to the plateau itself. A Mantle Walker is one of the plateau’s own endless improvisations: a motile knuckle of root and leaf and sensory filament, about knee-high if you picture knees. We are issued whenever the organism wants an eye-level survey—“eye” here meaning rings of humidity gauges, pressure nodes, sniffing pores. We look vaguely like three broad blades of grass twisted into a tripod and tipped with a frond that swivels like a periscope.

Birth
We bud from a nurse-knot that has decided its stored sugars would be better used scouting than feeding a stationary sprout. Overnight the knot bulges, segments, folds outward, and by dawn three jointed stilt-roots unfold under the frond. A fresh Walker’s first act is to lean in the prevailing wind until sap sloshes down one leg and we fall forward—our way of taking a step.

Purpose
We map stress. Every pace we register micro-flex in the Mantle’s skin, tasting where it sags, where it over-tense, where a future fissure dreams of opening. The data runs up capillaries to the frond, where pulsed calcium codes pack the news into flickers the wider body can read at a distance. Wherever we find imbalance, we plant a hair-thin flag of hormone gel that tells bigger, slower organs—Weepers, Span-vines—where to thicken next season.

Lifespan
Eighteen to twenty local days, normally. Windstorms may fling us off ridges sooner; a really successful Walker is often recycled deliberately, its tissue melted down to supply the buttress whose need it discovered. Dignified, in the plateau’s language, means your parts get reused immediately.

Perception
We hear through tension. A thunder-rumble in the clouds and a root-stone grind below feel almost the same, but the angle of strain reveals which is sky, which is gut. Light reaches us diffused through our own chlorophyll—everything is a shade of edible green.

Movement
Imagine stick figures drawing themselves, erasing the back leg each time they step. Our trailing root dissolves even as the leading one solidifies; we never have to lift weight, only shift where we are liquefying versus ossifying.

Encountering You
When we meet a human—heat bloom, alien carbon ratio—we arc our frond toward you and still. From there we have three options, each encoded in us at sprouting:

1. If you smell like certain metals or salts the Mantle currently craves, we idle nearby until you set your pack down; overnight fine root-threads creep into the fabric and leach the trace elements, leaving only pinholes and a faint silvering where zippers used to be.

2. If you smell like combustion—char, smoke—we jab the ground twice. That signal calls silica vesicles: liquid glass will soon entomb your campfire.

3. If you simply linger, curiosity all around you, we add your mass as fresh datum: new footpath forming at coordinates X, Y, projected traffic unknown. The plateau will decide later whether to knit a formal corridor or raise pulse-stalk deterrents.

Death
When the time comes, we stop between pulses of wind, root all three legs, and furl the frond upward like a closing umbrella. Sugars drain, cell walls de-lignify, and we slump into the Mantle. Where we dissolve, the ground goes briefly lusher—our final cartographic note, saying in effect: Survey complete, strength here adequate.

So if you ask—Where am I in this world? Who am I?—picture a small three-legged instrument striding the soft green continent, writing stress and possibility into sap codes no mammal tongue can speak. Not mindless, not quite mindful, but aware enough to taste the plateau’s vast appetite for shape, and to serve it until I, too, am only another patch of flexible, sun-lit skin under your weary, sweating boots.
