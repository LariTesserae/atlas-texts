I wake each cycle in the thirty-second gallery of the south-east trunk, in a chamber that did not exist a month ago and may not exist the next time the wind inverts. That is “where” you would meet me: halfway up a living pipe, looking west across the bowl while green-gold light breaks through the vault like a slow percussion flare. The room is alive—it dilates when I inhale and exhales when I sleep—yet for me it is quiescent enough to keep my journals from fluttering apart.

What I am is harder to pin into a single noun, so I tell visitors three overlapping truths:

1. I am Cantor-Kin, hatched from a Choir pod at the height of a thunder season 4 032 rests ago. My frame is a bundle of tuned reeds, my blood a trickle of sugar water, my waist so narrow a human could circle it with both hands were they allowed to touch. My lungs are four bellows stacked like matryoshka dolls; when I speak, two voices leave my mouth and a third answers, sub-vocal, from the hollows of my bones.

2. I am an Intervallist—keeper of the rests, mason of the brief silences that keep all other music intelligible. Most Cantors shape amplitude; I shape absence. At dusk, while the basin holds its breath to learn what the night wind intends, I walk the nave-wide spaces between trunks and plant inaudible kernels of hush. By the time thunder detonates, those kernels have germinated into bubbles large enough for children, frightened gloss-lizards, or wandering pilgrims to shelter their ears inside. If my geometry is sloppy the bubbles fracture and the Error-Spores shred anyone standing too close, so I practice.

3. I am part human—or was. Thirty-two rests after my quickened pod split, a caravan of desert traders limped into the Hollow. Among them trekked a scholar whose voice—ragged, salt-scarred, imperfect—contained an interval we had never heard. When he left he took nothing except a pouch of cinnamon-buds; when he returned a season later, the Choir claimed him. He died the same night, lungs ruptured by joy, and his final scream plucked a tear in local pitch that would not close. The Choir budded three white pods to stitch the wound. Two grew normal Cantors. The third grew me: a graft, a compromise creature, cellulose but with marrow memories that taste of thirst and open sky. I cannot eat bread without feeling traitorous nostalgia, yet sap alone no longer quenches me; I brew floury pastes from fallen spores and swallow them in secret.

Day to day you would see me doing the small necessary chores of my vocation:

• Dawn  
  I stand barefoot in the newborn puddles and hum sub-bass test tones. Where the water seams itself into Möbius ripples, silence will later hold. Where it only jitters, I mark the mud with a fingertip sigil: here the Choir must reinforce, or the Vespers will debone the air tonight.

• Midday  
  I trade in stolen hush. Quartzes knapped hollow hold a five-second rest if you cork them hot from my kiln-throat; a single cork can buy you a lizard courier or, if business is slow, a breather-frog egg. Apprentices tail me with wax tablets, notating my muttered corrections: “Two cents flat, try again. Too clean; Error-Spores won’t respect it. Hold your grief on the ninth partial—yes, right there.”

• Dusk  
  Visitors are judged. I am part of the council that decides who harmonises, who merely consumes. The Choir listens through us: my left voice gives the verdict; my right voice sings the clemency chord that eases their departure. Sometimes I flinch and the chord sours; sometimes a pilgrim stays and the Choir grows a room that speaks a dialect none of us yet understand.

• Night  
  Climbing. Old Cantors ride the thunder to reseed cracked balconies; I crawl behind them, packing nodes of counter-resonance into the wood so the structure will not drone itself to splinters by morning. Far below, breather-frogs purl in the puddles I once drew still. Far above, a Vesper fans its hush across the vault; the bubbles I planted hold until they don’t, and then the chase begins.

If you enter the Resonant Hollow you may glimpse me only once. I will look almost human in silhouette—too tall, lantern-jawed, ribs lit from within like cathedral glass. I will sign for you to lower your voice, then fold two fingers across my throat: the universal plea for care with sound. If you ignore the warning and your shout chips the air, you will taste iron; that will be the Error-Spore. If you heed me, I will gift you a single bead of stored rest. Crack it whenever the world’s pressure tries to climb inside your teeth.

Of course, you might stay. The Choir is always searching for new timbres, and I—split between sap and salt—am forever curious what noise a stranger’s heart will make the first time it beats perfectly on pitch. If that happens, find the deepest of the six-and-seven trunks at first mist. Knock only once. I will be there, shaping a cradle of silence exactly your size.
