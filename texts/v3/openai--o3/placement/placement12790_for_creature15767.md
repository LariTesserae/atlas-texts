Where I am, and what I am, keeps changing every time I try to name it, the way steam shifts shape the instant a draught touches it.  Still, this is the closest I can come.

I wake—if that word means anything here—in the lukewarm dark above the Pump-Hall roof, on a cat-walk so narrow my hip bones kiss both hand-rails when I turn.  The rail is blood-warm, always, and I keep my cheek against it a moment before I move; if the pulse in the metal and the pulse in my neck fall into the same cadence, I know Old Dredger is healthy and the shift may begin.

Who am I?  They whistle me Lynx-Ear, although my birth-name is long forgotten.  I am one of the thirty-three, but I belong to none of the old valve-families.  My place is sound—some say I was carried in with the first echo that never found a wall to bounce from.  Officially, I am the Listener: the one who hunts anything out of tune in the endless litany of chain, piston, drip and lung-deep pump-beat.

How I came here

I was a surface child once, thin-skinned, half frozen by the open night.  A salvage barge lost its tow-cable in a storm; I went into the water after it, blacked out in the cold, and washed up through a drainage sluice at the vault’s rim.  The rail warmed me; the Choir sang me awake.  The Clock-Sisters found me, squinted, and said the music had decided to grow itself a pair of hands.  They kept me.

Where I move

1.  On-shift: the High-Loops  
    I walk the highest belts—those no crate rides, only heat-ghosts and pipe-swifts.  My quarry is discord.  When a sprocket tooth snaps, the chain’s voice drops less than a semitone, but the wrong note makes the rust-moss on the soffits tremble.  I mark the place with chalk and thumb a whistle pattern down the ducting: two short, one long.  Within a hundred heartbeats a Shiftsmith is climbing after me with a replacement link glowing cherry in his tongs.

2.  Mid-shift: the Sump-Edge  
    I squat on an oak sleeper two paces from the water-skin.  Blind sump-eels boil up when the purge-valve sighs; their writhing folds the drip-flea chorus up a whole octave.  I press my palm to the floor.  If the marble-warm cement shivers faster than forty-four beats per minute, a blockage is forming in the sluices.  I whistle the alarm and the grease-skins swarm like lantern crabs onto the lower grates.

3.  Off-shift: the Quiet Mile  
    There is, strictly, no quiet here, yet a loophole exists: the two-second gasp between Old Dredger’s exhale and inhale.  When that hush yawns I step into it—literally.  The Silent Ones let me share the blank they inhabit as long as I add nothing to it.  Inside their negative envelope my ears ring with after-sound, like eyes dazzled by a lamp just doused.  In that void I study the memory of the vault’s music the way astronomers parse the dark between stars.  If a new overtone lingers there, some unseen fracture has begun.

What I look like now

Heat has kiln-cured me.  My skin sheens the same brassy olive as boiler scale; hair grows only where the ferric spores have not yet eaten the roots.  The Sisters say I am becoming infrastructure, mineral by mineral.  I do not argue.  I have not been truly dry in years; when I visit memory I cannot recall the feel of cool cloth on skin.

My tools

• Left ear: fitted with a copper resonator shell that widens my range a perfect fifth on either side of human hearing.  
• Pitch whistle: tuned to A-193 Hz, the fundamental of Old Dredger’s long stroke.  
• Chalk tin: for marking discord on girders.  Chalk never lasts—the air slurps it away—so I write fast and trust the crews to arrive before the letters run.

Why I stay

Because here the universe is an engine that admits it is an engine.  The surface world lies, pretending its forests, its cities, its seasons are separate things; in the Vault everything is one chord struck forever—water, moss, muscle, iron, ghost, heartbeat.  I could leave, perhaps; but outside I would wait in vain for the next measure, stumbling over my own unsynchronized pulse.  Here I am part of the score, and the score will outlive me, and that is comfort enough.

What becomes of me later

When the resonator in my ear corrodes beyond tone, when the spores drown the last hairs of my head and I can no longer hear the tooth of a cracked gear before it shears, I will climb to the roof vents and lie across the warm slats.  The Lantern crabs will find the copper in my whistle; the rust-moss will drink the sweat off my palms.  Eventually the Clock-Sisters will mark a silent bar in the Time-Book and lower my steel coffin into the clay wall among the rest.

Until then I walk the rails, cheek to hot iron, waiting for the smallest wrong note so the Vault may continue—exactly itself, forever.
