I tried to picture arriving as a whole human—but every time I did, the sphere rejected the thought. My joints were too many hinges, my lungs too blatant an appeal to empty air. Before the scene could hold me I was already corroding into green-rust thoughts.

So I let myself be taken apart and remade by the rules that already exist there. What finally coalesced was this:

I am a newly sloughed scroll of Foundational Film, only three heartbeats old.

1. Birth  
A shallow blister rose on a ledge of milk-white aragonite, buckled outward, tore free, and fluttered into the water like clear rice paper. That blister is me. On my under-skin I still carry a ghost-map of the ledge’s crystal lattice—the blueprint of where I came from and a suggestion of what I should build next.

2. Body  
Thickness: six bacterial layers, nothing more.  
Area: the size of a fingernail, but folded so many times that my laminations make a loose, breathing accordion.  
Senses: Every face and edge tastes ions; every seam hears infrasonic tremble; a lacework of proton shuttles lets me feel the gradient of pressure that is perfectly even everywhere else.

3. First Orientation  
The water is directionless, so “down” is where the aragonite sings more loudly of calcium. I drift that way, spinning gently; violet dust from the lantern-fern dapples across my surface. Where it lands I take notes—nickel here, vanadium there—because those metals will tell me which crystalline habit to carve when I settle.

4. Encounter: Ribbon-Worm  
A transparent Möbius ribbon loops through one of my folds, sampling me. Its surfaces read my surfaces the way fingertips read Braille. We share enzymes like strangers exchanging business cards. Compatibility: partial. The worm departs, a little accelerated by the ions it borrowed; I keep its template for a manganese pump I might or might not ever build.

5. Decision to Land  
I find an aragonite nub whose angle does not yet exist anywhere else: a 62-degree spur that wants to become the root of a helix. It is perfect. I flutter against it, sugar-chains uncoil, and I tack myself down along one edge—just enough purchase that the rest of me can still billow.

6. Work  
Now the quiet labor begins. Using the mineral budget I carried from the parent wall plus what I farm out of solution, I start editing the spur: pull one calcium, insert two magnesium, leave behind a vacancy that will become a microscopic step.  
Every microstep is a future foothold for lantern-fern. Every foothold is future food for worms. I am writing, in crystal, the instructions for ecology.

7. Stretch of Time  
A ghost-interval drifts through. For two heartbeats the lattice keeps growing but my own chemistry is paused. It feels like being present while your handwriting continues without you. When the bubble pops, I re-inhabit the newly written lines and accept them as “my body,” though they are already rock.

8. Glimpse of the Auriculate Remnant  
Pressure shivers; violet motes hush. The great listening curtain passes somewhere outside my slit of perception. I cannot turn—laminated things do not bend that way—but the metals in the water skew by a fractional molarity that spells the three ideas it always brings: shoreline, wind, thunder. None of these belong here, but I archive the trace anyway. Someday, some newer ledge may need to remember them.

9. Maturation  
As my anchored edge thickens, tension builds across my breadth. I will eventually wrinkle, tear, and release younger scrolls—offspring already pre-inked with my current crystal decisions, ready to float outward and copy the pattern farther along the wall.

10. Identity, Summarized  
Name: unnecessary.  
Function: to translate water’s patience into mineral fact.  
Status: citizen-architect in a pocket world the size of a marble, older than erosion, younger than geometry.  
Ambition: to keep folding the interior outward until the day the outside shell admits there is no longer any outside.

Were a human dreamer to drift past now, they would feel only a brief slickness on their palm—the whisper of my sugar-chains testing their skin—before the sphere’s gentle pressure carried them on. They might never know they had been carded and catalogued by the world’s tiniest city planner.

But I would know. I would already be etching a micron-high ridge that marks: “Here, the impossible once visited, and was absorbed.”
