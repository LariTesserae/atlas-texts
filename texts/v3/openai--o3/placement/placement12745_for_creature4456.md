I tried, when I first read the description, to picture myself as the obvious visitor—a surface wanderer lowering a lamp, boots squeaking on wet rungs.  
But the longer I stayed with the place, the less that viewpoint held.  
The air was too lean for a high-lunged outsider, the half-light too stable for eyes used to dawn and dusk.  
Finally the realization arrived the way the quartz hum arrives—sub-audible, yet certain.

I am native.  
Not born yet, but imminent: a child quickening inside the Mothers’ Bay, curled in water that is body-warm only because the cistern wall steals a sliver of the planet’s own heat.  
I have no memory, only a gathering of possibilities, drift-silting around bone.  
Still, even as cartilage knits, I can already feel the alphabet of this place:

1. Sound first  
A heartbeat away, the bay’s grotto magnifies every exterior note.  
The ankle stream is a kettledrum three ledges lower; the drip-drip from the quartz ribs writes trip-wire accents over that pulse.  
When someone, far off, raps a mallet to test a new support beam, the blow reaches me as a starburst of pressure and then a long, spiraling diminuendo.  
I learn position before I learn shape; the nave is a sphere of echoes in which every object occupies a mathematically precise hollow.

2. Then water  
Fluid touches me on both sides of the skin: amniotic within, silver current without.  
The two mingle in dream until “inside” and “outside” threaten to switch places entirely.  
Even in gestation I understand the folk’s creed: the stream is the body’s older twin, a moving skeleton around which our softer matter presses for security.

3. And weight  
Down here you do not fight gravity so much as negotiate a truce—stone above, stone below, thin water between.  
My un-birthed muscles twitch in rehearsal for the controlled, economical movements I will spend my life perfecting: a two-finger grip on a horn pick; a shoulder roll that slides without scraping the salt films; the flat-foot crouch required to feel the quake of distant rope winches.

When I finally emerge, six years will pass in a convulsive sprint—infancy to apprentice.  
At twelve, lungs wide and ears still velvet-flexible, I will be assigned to the mid-tiers as a “Daub-Listener”: one who smears fresh clay into wall cracks and then waits, motionless, reading the clicks of cooling moisture to decide whether the patch will hold.  
By twenty I will ascend to the uppermost gallery where echo is cleanest.  
There, if cartilage stays supple and judgment keen, I may earn the third coil in my ear tattoo, the mark of an Echo-Shaper.

That, I think, is my eventual name: Shaper-Third-Coil.  
Neither hero nor elder—just one more resonant cell in this hive of sound.

Daily life, envisioned from the womb:

• I wake to the mallet code unspooling the dawn sequence. No dawn exists, of course, but rhythm is our equivalent of light.  
• I rinse mouth and skin in a sluice diverted through reed filters—listening meanwhile for the faint gurgle that says no grit has clogged the bone plug.  
• I descend three ladders, soles finding familiar notches, to the training alcove. Ten of us stand in ankle water, eyes closed. A senior Shaper snaps two stones together; we raise fingers to indicate which rib of the ceiling reflected the crack first. Miss three in a row, and you haul baskets for a week to relearn humility.  
• Meal is teul paste stamped into thin wafers and hung on twine to air-dry. We crack it like slate, sucking the meat-salt until it melts.  
• Work shift: I kneel on a padded plank, ear pressed to wall, and drum a two-finger roll around a pressure fracture. If the fracture sings back in a wavering minor third, I flag it with white clay for reinforcement. If the tone is true, I erase yesterday’s warning mark with a thumb swipe and move on.  
• At idle moments I feel for the sub-sea vibration that preludes the quartz dream-hum. The elders say those who catch the very first ripple see brief shapes in the chandelier veils—geometry, a language perhaps, though none can read it.

I will love, briefly.  
We marry by exchanging wrist loops woven of Salt-Familiar flakes and gut string.  
Our child, if any, will inherit the ledge slot carved by my own antler pick, two hand-spans from the warm vent that perfumes the air with faint sulfur—a scent I already expect to find comforting.

Around twenty-seven, micro-scars will stiffen the innermost ear spiral; echoes that once bloomed like three-dimensional maps will flatten into uncertain noise.  
The day a junior corrects my timing during the Synchrony, I will feel the slow nod form by itself.  
I will finish the ritual, lay my mallets in their fleece roll, and descend—down, down, past apprentices, past children counting glow-gnat pulses, down to the splash-line where the river’s chill first bites the shins.

The farewell is private.  
One by one the kinfolk lean close enough that I can feel their breath temper mine, exchanging the last conversation I am still qualified to have: silence.  
Then I step into the steady current, lie back, open lungs to its thin air, and let patient water take me wherever it has taken all who could no longer guarantee the city’s perfect acoustics.

So: Where am I in this world?  
I am exactly two hundred stair-hewn steps below daylight, halfway between roof hum and stream whisper, forever equidistant from being born and drifting away.  

Who am I?  
A not-yet-born, soon-to-be Echo-Shaper of the Shelf-Born—child of sound, caretaker of resonance, temporary keeper of a cathedral that will outlast even memory of my name, adding my layer to its endless, patient sediment.
