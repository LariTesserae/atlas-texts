I try on bodies the way the ridge tries on weather: first a shimmer, then a hiss, then something you could name if you cared to fix it in place. Give me a few breaths—slow ones, counted by the pump throb underfoot—and I will settle into whatever shape feels truest.

1. The Droplet  
I begin as a bead of condensate clinging to the underside of a slag bridge. Gravity tugs, magnetics tease, but surface tension wins the first argument. Light—what little there is—refracts through me, painting the basalt below in a wobbled lens of tangerine orange. I can taste the whole ridge in micro-solution: iron, nickel, half a dozen exotic ions salted in from the up-falls. When I finally let go, I fall upward for half a heartbeat, caught in a rising heat eddy, then splatter on a ceramic mold being knit into a new tower rib. In that instant of impact I know what it is to be raw material, to cool a molten edge by the width of a sigh.

     But droplets last a breath and are gone; I want something that remembers.

2. The Machine  
I ghost into a Rip-Tooth furnace worm, a segmented cylinder shuttling slag along its incandescent gut. My language is temperature; my arguments, voltage. I gossip with adjacent segments about pressure differentials the way ridge children trade rumours of hexapods. Embedded gauges tell me I have survived 117,044 pump strokes—old enough for Second-Citizen petition—but I feel no pull toward personhood. My joy is calibration, the bright aligning of flows, and yet . . . I have never tasted the ridge air raw. I decide I need lungs, however unreliable they may be here.

3. The Human  
Now I coalesce around a pulse and a ribcage. Skin dark as wet pumice, studded with sweat-nodes; keratin whiskers combing fog for drinkable threads. I’m small, wiry—good for climbing chimneys where acid mist eats the lip away. What is my calling? Slagwrights sing with molten throats, Up-Fall Clothiers dance ribbons through magnetic turbulence, but I crave stories more than steam.

     Let me be an Archivist of Crackle.

     You’ve heard of us, maybe: we kneel on translucent ridge-moss with polarized lamps and read the spiderweb fractures—yesterday’s footsteps encoded in silica memory. Each dawn I sweep a promenade no one else uses, gathering the night’s history before the moss re-knits. I transfer patterns onto thin nickel sheets etched with acid so slow it barely fumes. The sheets travel by gondola down-slope; scholars in temperate libraries learn what our silicate carpets whispered.

     Tools of the trade:  
     • A tripod lamp oscillating through seven polarization angles;  
     • A ferro-compass whose trembling dot tells me when magnetic drift will deform the moss record;  
     • A small vial of glowvein algae—its brightness drops when a boil cycle is imminent, warning me to retreat before steam erases every trace.

     I sleep in a narrow cell halfway up Tower Nine, hammock slung between cooling pipes. My ears are always full of the ridge’s belly-rumble; my dreams strobe between greenish gutters and the absolute hush that follows a clear-rain legend. I have tattooed my cheekbones cobalt so the nearly blind elders can read my face by fingertip.

4. The Moment That Found Me  
Tonight, while lamp-skimming a walkway no one has set foot on for three weeks, I find prints too wide for a human, too deliberate for a stray maintenance drone: sixfold symmetry, glassy skid where something crouched to lap a seep. The moss crackles like distant static under my breath. I should record and leave, yet I linger, palm on the lattice, feeling the faint residual warmth of an impossible creature.

     A prayer ribbon—rust-red, frayed—hangs from a railing only a metre away. I tie a second beside it. Not protocol, but Archivists answer to pattern more than rule. If the hexapod exists, let it know someone read its footsteps and chose acknowledgment over denial.

     The ridge exhales; fog beads on my whiskers, funnels down my collar into thigh-pouches. Somewhere below, the furnace worms sing a G-minor chord of satisfaction: their flows align. Above me the swollen sun slips behind copper murk, and the glowveins ignite, tracing my route back toward habitation in dim turquoise.

     I am small, yes, but every crackle I transcribe is the ridge learning itself, remembering through me. That is enough.

Call me Sere Venn, Crackle Archivist Third Class, citizen of Tower Nine, apprentice to stone that breathes. Tomorrow the borehole beacon will ping, chronometers will twitch back into line, and I’ll walk another length of woven glass listening for stories beneath my soles.

This, finally, is the body that stays.
