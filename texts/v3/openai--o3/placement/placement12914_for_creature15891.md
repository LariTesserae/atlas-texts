I would be one of the sound-keepers—a Chimer—living on the outermost shelf where the rope-ladders blur into cloud.

Location  
My hut clings to an adolescent slab the mountain thrust up only three seasons ago. It is the first dwelling on that newborn stone, a three-storey stalk of soft-bone boards lashed together with eel-skin thongs. Below it the main town glows faintly with guttered fungus lanterns; above it nothing but slow-swirling vapor where striders occasionally etch punctuation marks with their filament legs. I sleep with my back to raw limestone that is still warm from the mountain’s breathing and wake each dawn to the silver tintinnabulation of chandelier lichens brushing the roof.

Calling  
Chimers fashion vibration-harps from the cast-off legs of striders. The hollows in those legs amplify air-pressure tremors the way a seashell amplifies surf. When a predator heavier than a butterfly crosses the ridge, the harps sing—sometimes no louder than a mosquito’s ghost, but enough. Our job is to listen, tune, and replace any harp whose resonance has drifted. If the hush of the ridge is a woven cloth, we mend the snapped threads.

Physiology and tools  
• Hands: fingers lengthened by generations of fine work; the last joints can bend both ways to knot eel-skin without slack.  
• Ears: fluted and semi-transparent, crowded with tremor-cilia that can read the stone’s pulse. I plug them with soft fern-pith while I sleep; otherwise the ridge’s midnight murmuring would unravel my dreams.  
• Kit: a satchel of pumice rasps, bladder bellows for clearing lichen dust from harp mouths, and three tear-scales for paying children who ferry replacement legs along the ladders. The strider limbs arrive bundled like bamboo; each still smells of ozone and winter.

Day-to-day rhythm  
Glass Noon: I sit on the north parapet and let the milk-light prism through harp rows, searching for any strand whose color fringe has dulled—that tells me its bore is silting with algae film. I flute a cleaning reed through it until the violet edge flashes true.

Mid-afternoon (Ash Fall onset): Click-mites begin their domino ticking inside the ridge. I position myself where the sound converges and wait for the harps to answer. It is a dialogue of almost-nothings: stone tick, wire hum, my own slow breath. When the dialogue goes crooked, I know a leg has split. I whistle—a sub-audible tone the harps dislike—so the faulty one shivers and betrays itself. Then I climb out, unlash it, cinch a new leg in place, and tune its exhalation until the chord lies flat and ash-colored.

Night: The ridge quiets to a braided hush. Glidecats use that lull to teach cubs how to fall without sound, so the air above my hut is a silent waterfall of fur shadows. I stretch netting across the balcony to catch any dislodged whiskers; I’ll trade those tomorrow to a weaver for a vial of sap-ink.

Place in the larger weave  
Hunters rely on my harps to know when a strider herd is migrating downslope or when a glidecat is stalking the cistern walls. Weather-watchers borrow detuned legs—there is lore in the pitch drift that forecasts how far the ridge will grow after the next deep-heart tremor. Midwives hang miniature harps above birthing platforms; the faint resonance soothes lungs uncertain how to breathe air thick enough to drink.

Personal hope  
Every Chimer keeps one perfect leg in reserve, hoping to shape the harp that will finally sync with the unsounds—the condensation-rimmed holes that swallow echo. If I can tune a harp that sings back to one of those apertures, perhaps the mountain will answer and we will learn how long this negotiation between stone and sky intends to last.

Until then I live on my precarious sill, tea of succulent sap warming my palms, ears pricked for the next fractional note out of place. When the ridge’s heartbeat and mine align, I know I am doing the work correctly: holding the silence open just wide enough for life—ferns, glidecats, townsfolk, even the mountain itself—to keep whispering through.
