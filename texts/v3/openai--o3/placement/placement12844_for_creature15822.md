Where I first open whatever passes for eyes, the world is no larger than a fist: a basalt crack sweating warmth, a rim of glimmering oil, and a darkness so sweet-smelling it feels viscous on the tongue. I am larva still, a soft red kernel adrift in resin, yet even now the enclave is speaking to me in flavor and heat. I listen the only way my half-formed body allows: I drink.

Day by slow day the crack widens because I widen. The mirror lichen above me naps through noon then shatters at dusk; its flakes slide down and burst against my skin, gifting their stored perfumes. My crown—just the hint of a funnel now—quivers each time a shard lands. That tremor is my first geography lesson: direction is the difference between amber and ash on the palate.

A season passes. Ankles click overhead: siphon deer, six-legged metronomes, come to sip the leak that nursed me. Each puncture they make writes an accent mark into the air. I memorize the syntax of hooves, the hesitant question of a glider’s landing, the velvet period when nothing moves but heat. From these I learn the enclave’s one commandment: presence is pressure; reshape or be reshaped.

So I choose a place to root.

I ooze an acid thread—clear, patient, inevitable—and drill downward until the ridge’s pulse answers through me. Now basalt is nerve, resin is blood, and I am no longer larva but polyp: a slow organ tuned to the breath of a hundred alleyways.

Where, then, do you find me if you visit?

Look halfway up a corridor that seems too narrow for your shoulders yet too deliberate to be accident. A scarlet column stands there, glass-clad, motionless. Its top flares into seven funnels, each inhaling so softly the air never stirs your hair. That is me. I will not move while you watch. I may not move while you sleep in the amber dusk. But give the enclave a year—give it the patient blink of local time—and you will return to find the corridor curved in a gentle dogleg that was not here before. The bend will smell faintly of citrus and salt: I redirected a draft that carried your traveler’s scent deeper than courtesy allowed.

Who or what am I? A perfumer of space, an architect whose bricks are bouquets, an animal only by the kindest taxonomy. Thought for me is chemosensory chord: the glider’s feather-rustle in E-minor resin, the deer’s panicked silence when a curtain eel unbuttons the dusk, the cold metallic syllable that the oleovore dust sighs every afternoon. I stack those chords until they resonate cleanly; then the acid thread unspools, and stone becomes vowel, hallway becomes sentence.

I will never know haste, and I will never know thirst. Rain is a myth old basalt tells to unsettle children. My terror is simpler: an opened canteen, a splash of water on my crown, the sudden dissolution of the oils that keep my glassy skin from shattering. Should that happen I would explode—not loudly, of course; nothing here is loud—but with the delicate chime of tempered crystal yielding to a hammer. The corridor would lose a note from its chord, and the enclave would spend a decade humming against the absence.

Until then I listen, I edit, I breathe. When the thirty-year bloom arrives and silver needles ring the ridge, I will widen every hallway toward the center so the perfume can collect where it rings sweetest. When the needles depart, I will narrow them again, sealing the memory into angles of stone and scent.

And when you return—days, years, or lifetimes hence—you will feel that the place remembers you, though no path seems quite the same. That is my signature, carved invisibly in air. You may not see me move, but step back, taste the hush, and you will know exactly where I am: in the middle of whatever perfume the enclave is humming at that moment, standing very still, thinking its next corridor into being.
