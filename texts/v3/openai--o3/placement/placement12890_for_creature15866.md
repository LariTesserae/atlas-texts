I tried on a dozen possible lives before the thin, rime-sweet air would accept one of them.

At first I meant to be a glasswing, all mirror and glint—easy to love the idea of drinking in flight and writing spores across the sky. But I lasted only a heartbeat in that skin: I could feel the wolves already triangulating my light, and the thought of ending as a sugar prize for silent jaws sent me fluttering back to the cliff.

Then I borrowed the spine of a rafter goat. I steadied myself upside-down, tendon-locked to a ceiling of stone, and dozed in the noon hush. Bliss—until the next rain curtain cracked open and water hammered through the lichen I was meant to browse. Goats can sleep through anything, it seems, but I am no goat; I flinched, slipped, and dropped the borrowed form before the hooks could save me.

For a night I even tried being the plateau itself: basalt, seamed and listening. It was too slow. I could sense the fissures widening, yes, but to do so I had to think in units of centuries, and all the swift, bright details—children chasing beads, wolves debating weather—blew past like sparks from a chisel.

So I settled into something intermediate, something that could notice both the drip and the decade: I became a young Engineer of Ledgework. Not yet gray-eyed enough to read the whole cliff like scripture, but apprenticed to those who can.

Where you will find me

Look halfway up the city wall, three terraces west of the overflow sluice. My household gap is narrow—barely the width of two outstretched arms—but deep, reaching back to a natural chimney where dawn light spears down like a fixed torch.

What I am

1. Body  
• Lungs grown wide as saddlebags. Breathing here is less inhale than slow harvest.  
• Fingertips toughened to basalt polish; every swale of skin remembers which ledges frost first.  
• Eyes still gray-slate, not yet full charcoal: I can see the rim wolves as shapes, but I have to guess their arguments.

2. Tools  
• A survey staff cut from finger-birch, inlaid with bone ribs of ancestors—each rib marks the lean of the lodestone obelisk for the year it was placed.  
• Two obsidian knives: one for rope, one for stone. The rope blade has a rounded belly so fibers part but do not fray; the stone blade is arrow-straight, thin enough to slice a hair of moss without wounding the lichen bed beneath.  
• My condense-key, carved from the scapula of a cliff-fallen goat. Its edge is oiled nightly with axolotl balm so water beads do not shatter when coaxed free.

3. Work  
Each sunrise I climb with my mentor, Old Switch, to read the day’s micro-disasters. We press knuckles into fresh hairline cracks; we plumb the newborn slots with nettle cord weighted by a shard of magnetite. Anything a fingerbreadth wider than yesterday is pegged in red clay on the public wall.  
At high noon—when even talk feels brittle—we etch the week’s measurements into a running lintel record. The city will adjust: a staircase rerouted, a cistern wall thickened, a goat comb shifted six handspans down. My signature is small among many, but already there are places where children climb safely because a notch I argued for now exists.

4. Threading the Larger Weave  
• With the wolves: I was chosen last equinox to carry the Overflow Invitation. At dusk I walked alone to their parliament hollow and clicked three times—badly, with a human throat, but they understood. A two-note pup stepped forward, touched my survey staff with her nose, and the pact was renewed.  
• With the glasswings: the south-facing parapet of my gap has begun to shine in colored inset—broken elytra set like mosaics. It is said reflective walls confuse hunting wolves, steering them away from goat nurseries; I also like how they catch stray sun into my shadowed room.  
• With the stone itself: every solstice I add one rib, mortaring it into the grand retaining wall. Two ribs so far, thin as pen strokes. Someday my whole skeleton will end up there, braced against the slow collapse, but that is a comfort, not a threat.

A day in the life

Dawn  
   Breath misting white, I rake condensate with my key into a clay throat. First swallow is for the lungs, second for the lichen tray, third for luck.  
Mid-morning  
   Measuring rounds: feel the plateau hum under bare feet, hear Old Switch mutter that the western scarf cliff has gone hollow, “a drum awaiting its first song.”  
Noon  
   Silence deeper than thought. I practice throat clicks against the survey staff, trying for a descending third—still sounds like a pebble in a pot.  
Cloudburst  
   Drop everything, listen. Count to six between flash and boom; trace where the new runnels will etch by next season.  
Evening  
   Return through the moaning basalt pipes. Their five tones braid with the wolves’ replies; the city and the hunt share one conversation.  
Full dark  
   Lean from my ledge and watch glasswings skating the updraft moonlight. Somewhere below, axolotls breach like pale commas, editing the pond’s surface.

Why this life feels right

Because it balances on the knife-edge the High Quiet itself inhabits: fast enough for human hands to act, slow enough for stone to teach. I cannot leap like a wolf or spiral like a glasswing, but I can translate their motions into measurements, shape them into terraces, stairs, safe shadows. I can help the plateau remember itself for one more fingerbreadth of leaning obelisk.

If you come looking

Climb the switchback that sings lowest in the evening wind. Pass the birch gallery scorched in lightning year-before-last. When the smell of wet goat wool gives way to feldspar dust, look up: I’ll be halfway down a new fissure, chalking depth marks and tapping the wall for hollows, ears full of quiet that is never quite silence.

Wave. I’ll show you how to hear which part of the cliff is planning to move next, and—if you stay long enough—how to set your own rib into the wall so the High Quiet can lean on you, too.
