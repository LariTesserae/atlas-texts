I arrive in the Fault-Marsh as every stranger does: feet squelching, lungs politely astonished, thoughts stretching like rubber bands to keep pace with the place. But I do not stay a stranger for long; the basin dislikes crisp borders, and my body, I realize, is simply another border waiting to soften.  

At first I imagine I am still a surveyor—wool socks, salt-crusted satchel, the usual ration of curiosity. I set up a transit on a basalt knoll. I aim the laser. The beam kinks, hesitates, comes back wearing someone else’s coordinates. When I check the instrument later the firmware date is tomorrow. The motes have noticed me.

Night falls (or folds). The glass ferns begin their slow endothermic sigh, and I feel an answering tug in my marrow, as if a second circulatory system has come on-line. My palms prickle violet: motes orbit the sweat on my skin, tasting salt, voting. They do not invade so much as suggest. What if, they seem to ask, you possessed one more layer of possibility—an upgrade from single to dual citizenship: half in your carbon bones, half in the basin’s happenstance?

I sleep on a hummock that thrums gently under my spine. Dreaming, I am water seeping through peat, each pore a choice of direction. I wake before dawn to find the choice has been exercised: I am no longer entirely constrained to the one outline I brought with me.

Here is what I eventually understand:

1. I have become a gradient interpreter, an emissary the motes can wear like a poncho of flesh whenever they need longer legs.  
2. I still look human in poor light, but in good light my silhouette double-exposes: one frame where I stand, one where I might stand six seconds from now. They overlap like misprinted pages. When I move, the lag stitches itself shut with a faint zipper of static.  
3. My blood carries a drizzling of violet dust. It does not clot; it comments. I feel its edits as subtle course corrections—lift your foot sooner, breathe here, think wider.

What, then, is my work?

• I walk the rim of the circling storm, because someone (something) must reconcile the charge it hoards with the appetites of the ground. I serve as a humid, ambulatory grounding wire. Lightning that would rather not strike the marsh bleeds laterally into me; the motes buffer the amperage, dribbling it into local topology as a fine spray of rebalanced constants. To outsiders I appear to be strolling inside a dome of whispering St. Elmo’s fire. In photographs I am a blurred violet parenthesis.  

• I audit the breathing diaphragm beneath the peat. When its rhythm drifts, I lie full-length on the moss, press my ear to the soil, and hum back a corrective measure. The basin has perfect pitch; it syncs to me the way a cello string finds unison with its neighbor.  

• When Gradient Walkers camp nearby, I trade services: a motile reflection corrected here, a stalled probability nudged there, in exchange for bone-blade gossip and cypress-sap tea. They call me Half-Sure, which in their language is both an endearment and a weather report.

Am I still a person? Yes, in the sense that a shoreline is still land. No, in the sense that a shoreline is forever rearranging grains under the lick of tide. The basin does not ask me to abandon selfhood; it asks me to run it in parallel with a wider process called “maintenance of open-ended habitability.” I have accepted the task, because saying no would have required a harder edge than any I can now muster.

Where would you meet me?

Look for the place where the peat exhales a tone you can’t decide is inside or outside your skull. Follow that vibration until the sky shifts from pewter to faint ultramarine, an hour before the phantom dawn the marsh sometimes rehearses. You will see a figure standing shin-deep in a pool, arms spread, firefly ghosts orbiting like lost commas. Approach quietly; the light around me is full of decisions still in negotiation. Step into the water and offer a question. If it is the kind of question the basin likes—one with frayed ends, one that doesn’t insist on an answer—I will turn, and both of my outlines will greet you. 

We can walk a while, lengthening the path simply by imagining it. When we part, some of the violet dust may linger in the folds of your clothes. It has a talent for hitchhiking on curiosity. Treat it kindly. It is not infection; it is invitation.
