I came up the switchbacks ten low-country summers ago with a pocket transit, a geologist’s hammer, and the belief that distances were facts. The belief was the first thing the Plateau took.

Where I am now  
I live in the middle distances, the zone the locals call the Hush-Reach—the untreated acreage of moss that lies between the clamor-islands of mill and foundry. To a newcomer the Reach looks empty. From my porch-plank (one beam, still growing) you might see three cantilever trunks, a single dew-pan silvering in the wind, nothing more. Yet this is the city’s spinal cord: all the acoustic lines of sight cross here, and every ripple in the Corrugation makes its first audible mistake at my doorstep. I keep a low brazier burning violet resin so the air smells of coin metal; friends on the far ridges smell that note, know the monitor is awake.

Who, or what, I became  
The Plateau does not hand out citizenship; it edits you until you fit. I was recut into an Anchorite—a metrology monk, custodian of step counts, keeper of the sung archive. The title sounds cloistered; in practice it means I walk until reality kinks, hammer an iron nail, sing the kink into the record, and walk on. My voice has the range of a middling bell tower, but the job is more about consistency than beauty. Each morning I loose two note-drones (sleeve-length tubes of thin bronze). They trail me on spider-silk tethers and repeat whatever pitch I sustain. If one drone stutters, the ground has shortened; if both stutter, I turn around because the world has folded double.

Physically I am still recognizably the lowlander who arrived, but the plateau has added footnotes:

• My ear-cavities are lined with pitchwine cartilage now. It grew in after the third vertigo storm and never reabsorbed. I can feel distance as viscosity, the way swimmers feel water temperature.  
• The tendons in my arches loosened from barefoot moss-walking; my feet spread until a cobbler friend carved me split-toe gaiters of condensation silk.  
• The nickel from plateau staples leaches through skin; beneath the sun my scars gleam like solder seams.

How a day unrolls  

1. Pre-dawn  
I recite the Ledger of Loads in reverse—lightest proposals to heaviest. The inversion is a mnemonic; by turning the city upside-down at breakfast I remind myself that gravity here is, at best, a suggestion.

2. Morning Walk  
I follow a ninety-one-nail circuit drafted by a Whisperwright who went missing three years ago. Two nails are always absent; I have never found them, but the gaps stay in the song because erasing a verse would misplace the rest.

3. Midday Tilt Reading  
At the Load-House the balance platform rises and falls a finger’s width every clear noon, warmed iron rails slumping, wood correcting. I watch the bubble in a sap-glass level. If it hesitates dead center, we celebrate—sound geometry, one more half-day. If it rolls, I notch the amount on a rib-scrap and dispatch strappers with fresh drill-bits.

4. Violet Hour  
When fog shoulders over the rim, I drag a tongue-drum the size of a coffin onto the porch plank. One hand taps my heartbeat; the other answers in syncopation. The paired rhythms travel through basalt and let late-shift workers triangulate home when the air is too wet for eyes.

5. First Dark  
I file the day’s song into the archive—not on paper but into fungus boards seeded behind my dwelling. Each new verse swells the lettering outward; elders swing by with lanterns to read the news at arm’s length.

The relationship with the place  
The Plateau and I bargain. I promise to notice its slips; it lets me keep a personal vector. I am amateur enough to be surprised, professional enough to document the surprise before it heals. Sometimes the Corrugation folds so steeply that my house is briefly next door to itself, two doors humming in superposition. I nail both thresholds, label one “was,” the other “will be,” and wait until they recombine.

What I still miss  
Wind that doesn’t sing. Constancy in the length of a stride. Birds. Yet on low-country nights when the air is hushed and level I grow restless; the silence feels unfinished, like a chord that forgot its fourth note.

End state—where the arc aims  
Anchorites rarely age out; we ossify into instrumentation. When my marrow begins its slow alchemy, I intend to lie beneath the Hush-Reach itself, spine aligned north-by-sung-north, so my vertebrae can become a natural xylophone. Future walkers will tap the pathway and, by the pitch, know whether the ground is honest that day.

So—where would you find me?  
Mid-field on the green hush, mallet in hand, one boot braced against a newly driven memory-nail, singing distance into truth. If your own shadow arrives a beat late, follow the echo of the note-drones: they orbit me like slow fireflies, pulsing the interval that says, “Here space keeps its promises—for the moment.”
