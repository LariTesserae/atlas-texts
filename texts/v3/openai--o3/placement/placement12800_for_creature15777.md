I wake each morning half-suspended between stone and sky, so perhaps that is the best answer: I am a hinge.

PLACE  
My sleeping niche is the fifth balcony above the communal cistern, south-facing, three hand-breadths deep. From it I can lean out and look both down into the violet gulf and up into the pewter haze where the gliders spiral. The ledge is so narrow that I lie on my side, knees pulled tight, one palm pressed to rock for balance even in dreams. I learned the posture in infancy, the way cliff-sparrows learn which wind will hold them aloft.

ROLE  
By lineage I am human, walking folk of Khar Teles; by craft I am a Wind-Gutter, apprentice to the carvers who coax water from air. Our charge is the cliff face itself. We read pocks and weals of ancient abrasion the way lowlanders read parchment, and we answer with chiselled phrases in flowing stone: always the same three verbs, rearranged without end—Guide. Gather. Guard.

TOOLS  
My hammer is antler grafted to a vine-resin grip, head tipped with a coin of mirror copper reclaimed from a blade older than any living throat. I strike by rhythm, not force—tap, pause, double-tap, rest—so that dust drifts away in lazy spirals instead of flying back into my eyes. In the hush just before dawn those taps are the loudest sound on the south face, second only to the mass-crack of the lichens unsealing their cups.

BODY  
Short, spring-tendon legs; shoulders scarred by childhood dew-burn; lungs that sip thin air like an elder savoring wine. I taste wind the way some taste broth—my tongue can guess humidity to within two eyelash-widths of water height in the basin. Friends tease that I salivate at clouds.

DAILY ARC  

Dawn  
1. Whistle the two-note clearance as I unhook my hammock and fold it flat; the ledge-web answers in kind, a dozen voices overlapping like ripples.  
2. Kneel beside the family gutter to weigh last night’s harvest. A good morning is a finger’s depth of silver in the measuring cup; today it is half a knuckle. I bow my head a breath longer than custom, promising the stone I will do better.  
3. Two sips of hammock syrup—never three—while the gliders exchange their bridal beads above. The air smells of ginger and cold iron.

Midday  
I dangle by a waist-loop from a piton of bone, carving a spiral throat into new gutter number 427-South-Lower-Gamma. The wind here speaks in an alto moan; I notch the curve to match its pitch so the breeze will shear sideways rather than slam straight into the cistern mouth. Stone remembers music better than words.

A slate cricket hitches a ride on my calf, its bladder swollen round as a berry; I brush it free before it bursts and paints my boots in stolen dew. Below, snails grind faint metallic chords into the rock, sharpening it for me one molecule at a time.

Evening  
The plateau dims from pewter to deep hematite. From every balcony yellow kiln-glow leaks in thin stripes. Families whistle home with anklets chiming, ledge etiquette turning the city into a woven counter-melody of who goes where.

I carry my waste stone chips to the cliff’s lip and spill them into the hush. Each grain drops for what feels like a minute, catching the twilight, until even memory of motion is gone. That, too, is a prayer: a reminder that everything falls eventually, even cities built on ledges, even ledges built on cities of bacteria.

Night  
Silence is rationed here, doled out like water, but I owe none today—no cup spilled, no droplet lost—so I join the veranda choir. We hum gutter patterns finished and imagined; elders judge by echo whether the pattern will sing water or merely ornament. If they nod, the design earns a name and tomorrow’s dawn labor.

SOMETIMES/ELSEWHERE  
Once each half year I climb the highest fin to help with the census. Argent gliders wheel overhead, counting us as we count them. Breath fogs out of my mouth though the air is warm; that vapor is enough water to matter. I clamp my lips before superstition can harden into guilt.

HOPE  
I have never seen true rain. Old storytellers insist it falls in sheets so thick you can drown standing up—an absurd, lavish myth. Yet on the day gutter number 427-South-Lower-Gamma finally sings its first trickle, I will press my ear against raw granite and listen to water run inside stone. That will be my private version of rain, and it will be enough.

IF NOT HUMAN  
On some nights, half-dreaming, I think I might be the gutter itself: a spiral throat whose only purpose is to drink the sky and deliver it, bead by bead, to lives I will never meet. Then I understand the real truth: hinge, gutter, carver—these are the same role expressed at different speeds.

So where do I find myself in this world?  
Balanced on a chipped balcony that erodes one silent grain at a time.  
And who am I?  
A translator between wind and water, stone and thirst—one heartbeat in the long, slow pulse of the Stacked Place.
