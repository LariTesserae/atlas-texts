First I try on the obvious skins.

A human climber?  I can already feel the stinging lungs, the metal taste of altitude, the embarrassing heat I would bleed into air that is never warm enough for itself.  Two nights at most, the description said, before retreat would seize me by the collar.  I would be a guest, not an inhabitant.  The world would stay after I left, unchanged except for a pair of soft-soled negatives in the mud.

A snow leopard?  That is tempting.  The thought of moving like an unstated clause, of letting gravity believe it owns me until the instant I change my mind, holds its glamour.  But the leopards in this place are already perfect.  They need no extra ghost to pad the ledges.

A raven, perhaps—operator of the long-haul routes, archivist of hidden lunches?  Or a kestrel, a living drill-press bit that hangs, flares, shears the insect bloom?  Both are magnificent, but both are already spoken for.  They do their work so openly that they seem almost managerial: too large, too certain for what I’m after.

I keep borrowing bodies and discarding them in seconds, until the scale keeps slipping downward, downward, a ratchet that won’t hold.

What if I were something small enough that the mountain barely notices me, yet large enough to notice the mountain intimately?  Something built, like everything else here, for contingency: for wet that can turn to ice inside its blood, for food that arrives in numerically explosive flashes and then vanishes for weeks.

A beetle.  
Yes—one of the oil-black, flex-plated workers the description called machinists of moss.  That sits right.  Let me settle into that shell, feel the chill polymer hardness of my elytra, the quiet hydraulics in my knees.

DAY CYCLE OF A SHARD BEETLE  
(The name is mine; no one bothers to name us up here.)

DAWN  
I wake because the moss pillow under me blooms with melt.  Every capillary in its bronze mat releases a sigh of water.  My shell has drunk cold all night; now it faintly sweats as the first photons nudge the temperature above freezing.  I unfold—hinges silent, micro-grooved to trap a film of insulating air—and taste the wind.  The air carries acid tang from a wall of newborn lichens ten metres east: breakfast.

My six feet test the substrate the way a machinist palms a new billet—feeling for fissures, hidden give.  The ground flexes; permafrost is shallow here.  Good.  I know from experience that if the ground is too firm the ravens can hear my rasping jaws from an impossible height.

MID-MORNING  
I cut rock for a living, though I never touch the rock itself.  The target is the lichen crust: a sun-cured film of greens and yellows pinned to basalt.  My mandibles are garnet-tipped—micro-crystals grown during the pupal stage—and with each sideways stroke I plane off a shave no thicker than frost.  Behind me I leave scallops of raw basalt that flash like fresh welds when the cloud ceiling thins.

The work is rhythmic: brace, shear, chew, step.  The sound is smaller than rain, but the mountain carries it, amplifies it in ways I don’t grasp.  I once watched a kestrel correct its hover position in response to the whisper of a hundred of us feeding on a rock face.  Every act here is larger than the body that performs it.

NOON  
The insect bloom begins.  Midges rise in soft elevators, their cloud shadows pooling and sliding over the stone.  I pause at the base of a copper-orange moss cushion, antennae triangulating the tremor of distant wingbeats.  If I linger out on bare rock the swarm will crawl into every spiracle, mistake me for higher ground, drown me in my own weather.

I crawl into the moss.  The cushion is soaked, heavy as a wet sponge.  I drink by standing still and letting the fluid creep up the hairs of my legs.  Around me, springtail nymphs boomerang through the moss matrix like compressed springs.  We ignore each other; different guilds, no overlap in contracts.

AFTERNOON  
Mist thickens until light comes from everywhere and nowhere.  Visibility drops to half a metre; to a raven that is as good as a drawn curtain.  Safe.

I return to the lichen wall for second shift.  A line of ibex appears on the ridge above, silhouettes only, each head lowered to grind its own share of crust.  I feel the vibration of their chewing in the soles of my claws, a bass accompaniment to my treble rasp.  They shear the wall high where I cannot reach; their droppings rain pellets rich in half-digested algae.  I will revisit those pellets tomorrow, mine them for compounds the rock never offers.  The ibex and I run the same machine on different scales.

TWILIGHT  
Temperature tilts downward.  Water in the moss begins to stiffen; droplets on my shell thicken like slow glass.  I scurry toward a fissure at the base of a basalt needle—my night vault.  A tuft of leopard fur caught on the entrance tells me the superintendent passed by recently.  Good.  Ravens will give the place a wide berth tonight.

Inside, humidity is mercy-high and air is dead still.  I park upside-down against the stone ceiling; chilled hemolymph settles against my dorsal carapace, an internal quilt.  My heart slows to a tick that would shame a cracked clock.  Behind my compound eyes the world continues as faint vibration: water refreezing, wind sanding spires, far-off rock cracking with the clean report of a rivet gun.

NIGHT  
Dormancy isn’t sleep; it’s a business decision.  At any moment warm breath from a chorus frog might wander into the fissure, cueing an opportunity.  If that happens I will drop from the ceiling, hitch a ride on the living furnace until dawn.  Warmth is the only currency that inflates without notice here, and every species spends it the instant it appears.

If the night stays stable I will not move until thin blue sets into the sky and the moss outside exhales its first bead of meltwater.  Then the shift begins again.

WHAT IT MEANS TO BE HERE

1. I am small, but I handle high-value material.  Every milligram of lichen I convert becomes mobile nutrient the mountain can shuffle downslope in the gut of a bird or the bladder of an ibex.  Erosion has subcontracted part of its workload to me.

2. My shell records the weather like a ledger: pits where airborne sand has struck, mineral gloss where drizzle has left salts, hairline frost cracks healed by midday warmth.  I carry the province’s manufacturing notes on my back.

3. Danger is mostly geometry.  A drop of three centimetres from a raven’s beak can powder me; one misplaced step on cooling ice can seal me in an airless film until thaw.  So my life is a negotiation with surfaces, angles, phase changes—microscopic engineering in continuous rehearsal.

4. I do not dream of elsewhere.  Below the cloud deck the world turns too complex: too many scents, too much shade, predators that rely on abundance rather than precision.  Up here the signal-to-noise ratio is perfect: one raven croak, one frost crack, one sunrise.  Each event rings like a bell in a marble hall.

IF YOU SHOULD MEET ME

You will not notice my approach; my feet are furred at the tips for silence.  But you may glimpse, on a dull basalt slab, a tiny series of half-moon scallops—the machining marks of my day.  Touch one and grit will crumble like icing sugar.  That is me, opening the mountain a micron at a time so that something larger, someday, can set root.

If your boot sole leaves a warm indent in the mud, I will investigate the moment you walk on.  Not for heat alone—though that is precious—but for the trace minerals your rubber smears across the surface, elements the mountain has not yet brought within reach.  You will think you left nothing.  I will find in it a season’s provision.

So where am I in this half-finished roof of the world?  
Crawling its lowest stories, a black, glossed rivet the size of a thumbnail, doing my share of the slow construction shift.  Not an overseer, not a visitor—one of the innumerable small tools already hard at work before the real architects, the trees and the soils and the humans, ever arrive.

Who am I?  
Shard Beetle, machinist of moss and lichen, apprentice to frost and cloud, resident of the first day of the mountains.  When the world is finally smoothed and green and complacent, my kind will have moved on to whatever new stone the planet hauls fresh from the deep.  We are not built for finished things.  We are built for beginnings.
