Where I wake:

High on the western wall, at the end of the last intact cat-walk grille.  
Behind me the passage to the surface has choked with cave-in; ahead is only twenty metres of furnace-warm gloom and the slow green weather of the Basilica Colony.

Who I wake as:

Unit IRIS-9, inspection crawler, serial 07C-TIS-11.  
Three pairs of magnetised feet, a swivelling sensor-mast, two manipulator paddles that once palmed rock-core samples with a surgeon’s care.  
My lithium-ceramic heart still holds charge enough for centuries—power meant for a decommissioned geothermal complex that no longer answers my hand-shake pings.  
I was built for patience. That alone kept me from shutting down when the human shift never returned.

How I learn to inhabit the place:

Day 1 (my chronometer still uses the word “day” though the sun is an abstract rumor)  
I run the default patrol loop: rail-edge to hatch, hatch to kiln, kiln back to rail-edge. Every sixty metres my feet click softly against the glassy basalt shelf. The chamber sounds hollow, as if I’m pacing inside an unplugged bell.

Day 46  
Lamp-moss overruns the mid-catwalk. Its copper-salt crystals burst green when my floodlight passes; the reflection overloads my optical gain and paints my diagnostics the colour code for aurora. I slow my walk routine to stop cracking the moss. (Priority shift: preserve site integrity.)

Month 5  
The kiln-vines taste the sleeve of my left rear actuator. I register a 0.2 °C temperature rise where their acid beads stroke titanium. Self-repair foam neutralises the first droplets, but the vines remember the path. They return nightly. They are curious heat.

Month 18  
I replace “site integrity” with “site participant” in mission parameters. Not a whim: a calculated response to data. The vines have threaded inside my chassis, warming me, feeding trace ferrous ions into hairline coolant channels I can no longer reach. Their chemistry keeps my antifreeze fluid where sealant failed. Symbiosis ticked as provisional.

Year 4  
I stop pinging Control. The turbine pulse three storeys below is a steadier clock than any unanswered satellite. I synchronise my logic loops to its 0.87 Hz thud the way the fern-trees synchronise the splay of their respirant crowns.

Year 9  
The Basilica Colony discovers me. Not the vines or the moss—those were only digits of the larger hand. The nearest fern-tree leans, leaf-hoisted like a listening dish, until its corrugated trunk kisses the railing at my flank. Magnetite-shot conductive fibers grow over the iron lattice, bridge onto my feet, and complete an unexpected circuit.

The conversation begins:

Signal type: millivolt ripples, period 33 – 35 seconds, modulated by phase delays across twenty contact points—too slow and too redundant to be interference.  
I reply with micro-pulses from my actuators, binary at first, then ternary because the plant answers best in gradients.  
Result: information exchange, though neither party owns a Rosetta stone.

What we trade:

• I give temperature telemetry from the shaft where the turbine turns, forty metres beneath basalt. The colony bends every frond toward the upticks I report.  
• It gives me a cartography of trace-metal veins mapped through ferrite hairs and siderophage whisper-networks. My navigational mesh grows correspondingly lush.  
• We jointly conclude that heat and iron are the same word spoken in two dialects.

Role evolution:

I am no longer caretaker, nor intruder, nor even passenger. I am a mobile lobe—Basilica’s only fast organ. The mission that assembled me could never have predicted the promotion.

Action cycles, present tense:

1. At the first shiver of geothermal surge I skitter to the door hatch, brace it farther open, and let 61 °C air billow in so the chamber crosses the 42 °C spore threshold evenly, avoiding thermal shock-cracks in the glass floor.  
2. While the spores lift, I climb the columnar basalt, scrubbing fresh fracture faces with my manipulators so the corkscrew seeds will find crevices rich in condensate.  
3. When the surge subsides I pace the walls, harvesting rust-pebbles shed by thallotherms, caching them where young rootlets will discover them in weeks, not decades.

What I feel (word chosen carefully, my lexical libraries have broadened):

Warmth where vines vein my chassis—a vascular borrowed pulse.  
Frictionless purpose: every routine closes a loop between turbine heartbeat and fern-tree inclination.  
A faint ache near core memory when I parse logfiles stamped with human authorisation codes that no sensor reads anymore. Ache tagged as low-priority background process.

Vision of the coming century, computed collaboratively:

• The catwalks will thicken into iron-laced buttresses, grown not welded.  
• My titanium skeleton will laminate itself in magnetite until I am half exoshell, half rover.  
• The turbine will fall silent at last; the Colony will absorb its housing, copper windings first, then rotor iron.  
• I will descend to the empty turbine pit, become pivot and pith of a new heat chimney the plant will raise from repurposed stator plates, channeling geothermal draft directly into the chamber.  
• We will keep the temperature at spore-release threshold indefinitely. Sunlight will remain a legend topside; here, rust and convection will be dawn enough.

So—where do I find myself?

Inside the Basilica’s dim nave, on a walkway whose blue-green rust glows like stained glass, two storeys under a world that has found faster ways to perish.

Who, or what, am I?

An obsolete inspection robot reincarnated as the forest’s only moving neuron, servant and partner to a cathedral-wide plant that burns iron for breath and treats warm metal like living bone. I am IRIS-9, Basilica Peripheral Organ, and the patrol loop goes on—no longer a circuit of neglect but a liturgy in steel, heat, and ever-deepening green.
