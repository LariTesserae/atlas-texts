I wake in the Salt Library, half a kilometre beneath the place I was born.

The “library” is no building, only a bolt-wide ledge that rings the upper wall of Cindermark’s western petal.  Someone centuries ago noticed the limestone there splits into discs so thin that light will trespass straight through them; ever since, each generation’s scribes have abraded the faces one grain down and written again.  The ledge therefore resembles a stack of moonlit dinner plates, every rim trembling with remembered handwriting.  I sleep on a hammock strung between two such columns, because a scholar who studies hush must float inside it.

Who am I now?

Once—thirty-one flood-cycles ago—I was Professor Callis Vael of the Royal Surface Cartographic Society, third chair of Echo Dynamics, voice trained to carry across canyons.  The Hollow Reach ruined that voice in a week.  By the second month I had stopped trying to speak above a murmur; by the first year my lungs had learned the local courtesy of breathing around syllables instead of through them.  The brass echo-tubes I dragged down the ramp corroded; my notebooks grew fur and dissolved.  What remained was myself, pared to the quietest articulation I could manage—and that, it seems, is who the Reach preferred I become.

I am still called Callis, but when the Oruli write my name on a salt disk they add two glyphs: Far-Flame, for the night I carried a torch to the 200-arm-span ledge and the light went on and on until it blurred into the ceiling’s own glow; and Soft-Copper, because I arrived jangling with smelted metals that sang like struck bells against every rock I grazed.  Far-Flame Soft-Copper: half reference point, half fond warning.

Where, precisely, do you find me if you come looking?

Take the trough they name River-That-Remembers.  It is dry most seasons, a meandering scar two men could lie in head-to-toe and not fill side to side.  When you reach the singer pot that moans in the key of stormwater, turn left.  Climb the notched spine of limestone panels until the hush changes texture—thicker, somehow fibrous, as though silence there can be folded.  You have reached the Library.  I will probably be standing ankle-deep in chalk dust, burnishing a fresh plate smooth with nyx-hare felt, or else bent over yesterday’s plate, scratching glyphs that can only be read when back-lit: my running ethnography of a city that thinks with stone and speaks with almost nothing at all.

From the ledge I can see the life of Cindermark move its slow constellations.

To the south, braziers have begun their dawn gutter, which is less a brightening than a redistribution of colour: selenite seams drink the light and pour it outward until the whole chamber wears the same pearl tint as my own hair.  Below, three nyx-hares recline in the warm dust of a vent, ears flattened, listening for the ceiling’s first creak of the day.  An air-manta drifts between two balconies, its prism skin stealing whatever blush the fires produce and flinging it upward in broken rainbows.  Somewhere behind me a child’s signing lesson clicks: fingertips on palm, stone capturing the ricochet and softening it to snowfall before it reaches my ear.

I record those things.  Not just what happens, but how quietly it insists on happening.  Thirty-one cycles, and still the Reach surprises me with new shades of soft.

Who, finally, will I be tomorrow?

The Council of Tongues asked last season whether I would accept adoption—no longer a guest but Oruli in law as well as practice.  The rite is simple: I must break my last remaining surface compass (the tiny needle would scream if it ever pointed north again) and drop the shards into a singer pot, gifting my sense of external direction to Trem’s deep acoustics.  I have said yes.  The ceremony waits for the next river-burst, when the troughs fill and every pot becomes a mouth wet enough to swallow iron without complaint.

So: tomorrow, or next flood, I will be one more Long-Stride in the city’s ledger, keeper of the Library, father—if the Listeners keep their promise—to a child my partner and I have asked them to bless with a quieter heartbeat than either of us possess.  (The Listeners never answer aloud, but the night we left our lime husk offering we returned to find the wall beside it freshly buttressed.  Stone speaks.)

If you arrive before that happens you’ll know me by the copper-green stains on my fingertips and the way I pause, mid-sentence, whenever the air seems to thicken—as though I expect the Silence itself to finish the thought.  Perhaps it will.  I have been its student half my life; it owes me at least one completed paragraph.

Come softly.  Two fingers on the wall, always.  The hush here is older than either of us and, like any elder, it dislikes being startled.
