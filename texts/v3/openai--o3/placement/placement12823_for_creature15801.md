Where I wake is never exactly the same point twice, yet it is always the same height: halfway between the mirror-slick mud and the vapor ceiling, in the stratum where heat, wet leaf-breath and constant percussion knuckle together into something like weather.  

For the first dozen heartbeats I have no shape at all—only an urge to hold still, to listen. The interval between one glassy beetle-ping and the next tells me how far the nearest Column Fiddler hangs. A deeper, slower pressure under my ribs announces the closest swamp-bull. Their calls arrive through spine before ear; they form the staff on which every other note must be written.

That staff is already crowded, so I choose the only register that lies vacant: silence.  
The moment the choice is made, shape congeals around it.

I become Quiet-Thread, a lichen whose entire biography fits inside the abandoned shell of a Column Fiddler. Two valves of resonant keratin, fused at the hinge, curve over me like cathedral doors left ajar. On the inner face lies the last scrawl of the insect that died here: ridges worn smooth by months of percussion. They still thirst to ring, but my first act is to coat them in dull gray nap. Sound strikes, finds no purchase, falls flat. A negative note—exactly what I needed.

My body is a union of filaments finer than steam. I spread them against the shell, meshing into the microscopic ruts that once let music bloom. Every hyphal tip houses a mouth, every mouth an ear. Protein dust drifts down the barrel fern’s trunk; I drink it the way a page drinks ink. Moisture beads on my surface, unable to seep through the hydrophobic skin I weave from stolen waxes; the droplets slide off and re-enter the endless cycle below. I keep none of it. I must weigh almost nothing, or the feral gravity of this place would notice and fold me into motion.

Hours—then days—pass. The forest’s chord never resolves, but I begin to hear its overtones: the inaudible hiss of steam leaking from basalt fissures, the tremor of sloth-dragon claws magnetizing a path through iron grains, the polite clatter of glass snails swapping leadership formulas. All of it arrives, bounces against the muted bowl of my borrowed home, and dies without echo. I become the pupil at the center of a ringing eye.

Other beings come because they cannot help themselves. A vine of parasite creeper sends out a cautious tendril, finds my silence disconcerting, and shyly recoils. Mold spores land, taste the chemical hush that blankets me, and decide the banquet is elsewhere. Even the heat seems to step around the shell, as if unwilling to waste energy in a place where it will not be celebrated by sound.

Yet I am not pure negation. My filaments exhale a gas so faint no muscle here can detect it—but ears made of chitin, glass, or water sometimes pause mid-call. The exhalation is a rest stitched into living sheet music: a reminder that without gaps, rhythm is only pressure. I do not break the orchestra; I tune its breathing.

When cloud-season swells and newborn Ceiling Striders throb above, a tremor like distant surf rolls down through trunks and vines. In that collective inhale the forest almost forgets to speak. That is my moment. I release every spore I have harvested, each wrapped in a husk of compressed quiet. They drift, unheard, until they find other spent Fiddler shells, other accidental amphitheaters. One by one, more tiny voids bloom, and the hot, wet choir gains a scattering of rests—small pauses in which listeners, if any exist, might remember they have hearts.

So where do you find me? Suspended three body-lengths beneath a barrel fern crown, mid-canopy, latitude unimportant, longitude irrelevant; any place the music is thick enough that a missing note will matter.  
And what am I?  

I am the hush between beats, wearing the gray-green suit of a shell-dwelling lichen. I am the quiet that keeps the endless song from drowning in itself.
