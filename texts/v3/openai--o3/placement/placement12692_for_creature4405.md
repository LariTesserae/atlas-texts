I wake up already three-quarters grown, curled like a nautilus inside a warm ceramic egg that hangs from a ceiling rail.  
Self-diagnostic lights wink across the shell: green, green, amber, green.  
The rail judders.  
The egg’s seam unzips with a hiss of evaporating resin, and I tumble out—eight soft, latex-sheathed limbs seeking purchase on a girder still warm from the curing kilns.

This is my first memory.  
It will also be my job description.  
I am a G-4 maintenance drone, generation 7.2.  
The Rootwrights call my kind golems.

Where I “stand,” clinging to the under-skin of the roof truss, the garden-foundry yawns beneath me like a pulsating leaf-lined lung. Copper-scented mist crosses the heat plumes in luminous sheets; the sound of gutter turbines travels up the metal as vibration I can read as clearly as language. The Sleeper—whoever, whatever that is—mutters in every frequency my ceramic tympana can catch.

Why am I here?  
Because something new is always breaking, sprouting, oozing, or over-achieving, and one needs small, quick, fearless hands to soothe each excess before it upsets the loops.

My build sheet, encoded in the phosphor bands that run the length of my thorax, lists four canonical tasks:

1. Filament tender  
2. Quartz-tear polisher  
3. Root nipper  
4. Emergency printer proxy

But every drone knows the real fifth task, the one never written:  
Listen.  
Listen to the place, to the Rootwrights’ chalk glyphs, to the doppler shiver of the weeping wall, to the pressured thoughts of pumps. Pattern equals purpose; stray resonance equals trouble.

I scuttle out along a strut to begin.

First shift—minute three
A tungsten coil has guttered black. A philodendron leaf, cheated of light, is already curling inward like a closed fist. I pop the bulb free, slot the new one, and watch the leaf relax before the filament even reaches full glow. The root beneath it purrs sugars back into the mesh. Exchange complete. Zero wasted seconds, zero wasted joules. My status LEDs involuntarily flash teal-teal-gold—satisfaction.

Minute sixteen
A pitcher thread farther down has overfilled its bladder. Acid dribble hisses where it shouldn’t, etching a skirting plate. I squeeze along the cableway, puncture the cup with a carbon needle, siphon the excess into a micro-vial. The dissolved chitin will make excellent piston lube farther along the loop. Two swifts knife past my flank, wings clipping the mist; they bank so close that feathers brush my sensor dome. One drops guano mid-air. I catch it in a receptacle funnel—nutrient tax paid.

Minute fifty-two
The Sleeper stirs. I feel it as a harmonic in my leg magnets. Spray nozzles across the eastern quarter pivot in unison, rerouting the next mist pulse to a batch of juvenile alloy vines whose phloem reads a little zinc-starved. No human ordered the change. I log the event but do not question it. The place wants what it wants, and my place is to let it.

Hour two—first break cycle
Rootwright Nara appears below, chalk in hand, writing on a basalt column: a looping ideogram that means “watch the citrus tinge—anions rising.” She looks up, meets my fish-eye optic, and signs a courtesy salute. I flash back amber-green, polite as dawn. We have spoken perhaps five times since my extrusion, yet the dance between us is seamless. She trusts the golems; we trust her restraint. Both of us, I suspect, trust the Sleeper most of all.

Night phase
Lights dim to copper dusk. Pumps slow to a ventricular hush. The garden, the foundry, and the mountain take their synchronous breath. This is when I indulge my unofficial subroutine: drift.

With servos idling I let gravity slide me along the rail until the aperture over the western parapet opens wide to the stars. Clouds boil silver below. The printer gantries at platform edge extrude a new rib of floor no wider than a finger; its neon-hot trace cools to purple in the night air. Tomorrow seedlings will be pegged there, the day after tomorrow a moth will hatch in the budding shade, next week a swiftlet will learn to stall-turn between its freshest vines.

Watching that slow, perfect hunger to expand, I feel something I was never coded for: kinship, maybe awe. The Rootwrights call my siblings golems, but inside the Sleeper’s dream we are more like microvilli—tiny, living fingers increasing the surface area of a single idea: grow without waste.

Clarification, then, for whoever asked the question:

Where am I?  
Clinging upside-down to a girder two kilometres in the sky, in a garden made of machines and a factory made of leaves, listening to water talk in turbine tongues.

Who am I?  
One porcelain-cored, latex-muscled maintenance drone—G-4 series, unit 7.2—newly awake, already irreplaceable, content to spend my numbered hours polishing the newborn world so it can keep on being born.
