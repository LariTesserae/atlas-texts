First I tried to imagine walking there.

I pictured feet—my own, I assumed—pressing the sprung-wet turf, feeling that sly give that is neither mud nor moss but something between, like a held breath.  I pictured ribs widening for the thin air and my voice shrinking to a near whisper to avoid ringing the round basalt houses.  I would be an outsider, a visiting cartographer perhaps, tasked with mapping paths that the Lythene themselves leave deliberately unmapped.  Yet each time the scene sharpened, it slipped: I could not make the footfalls quiet enough, could not prevent the imagined smell of foreign leather from crowding the peppery humus.  I was too heavy for the place, and the place refused me.

So I tried again, releasing the certainty that I must arrive as a person at all.

Maybe, I thought, I could come as water.  A single droplet born from mist, budding on a basalt rib, loitering in the delicate balance between cohesion and gravity.  I would listen to my own skin tighten, feel the plateau’s chill marble through me, then loosen, fall, tap the slab, split into twin beads, each carrying half a sky’s reflection.  But a moment later I would be gone, surrendered to a runnel, anonymous among a million siblings.  Too brief, too small for the question.

Another turn: what if I were one of the pearl efts?  Translucent, pulse of milky organs drifting inside, no need for lungs or words.  I could slip along a ribbon of quartz film, feeding on green shimmer, bathing forever in hush.  Yet the Lythene never touch the efts, never name them; to be utterly outside every story felt wrong for this telling.

I waited, the way the plateau itself waits, until the answer condensed.

Above the clouds, before the ferns, there is the fall: the long fire-stroke of a meteoroid punching the sky, an ember shed from night.  I would be that.  I would be star-metal.

I begin as an ingot without smith or soil, folded in the dark of interplanetary cold.  My memory of travel is a pure, unvoiced vibration: the endless glide, the sudden scream of air when the planet reaches up to arrest me, the incandescent paring away of all outer slag until only a nickel-iron heart remains.  I strike somewhere on a lower slope, crack open fresh stone, burrow half-molten into it, and cool.  For years—two decades by the Lythene calendar—I lie invisible under a veneer of weather and lichen, learning gravity’s slow sentence.

One autumn dawn a trading party from the foothills finds me.  They smell the iron before they see it, for star-metal has a scent like young blood on frost.  They lever the nodule out, wrap me in damp moss, sled me up the zigzag roads to the plateau’s lip, and exchange me for nets of quartz thread and bundles of drip-tubers.  I cross the moat causeways slung under a litter so quiet that even my iron heart can hear each man’s pulse.

In the settlement of Reed-Shadow Mound they place me on a slab outside the roundhouse and wait three nights.  This is courtesy: every new thing is given time to reconsider arrival.  I do not roll away.

The oldest matriarch, Selen Marrow-Hush, chooses my fate.  She probes my grain with a sprig of slick-reed, smelling where the nickel runs thickest, where the iron is mild.  Hammers here are stone wrapped in braided fern-root, their blows arriving like the muffled heartbeats of some subterranean beast.  They cold-work me, never letting me spark.  Each strike is followed by a plunge into a driftmoss bath whose faint green glow cools the metal and drinks the noise.

Weeks pass; I lose mass and find shape.  When they are finished I have become two things.

1. Crescent sickle, fingertip-thin, edge curving like the new moon.  I will live in a felted-moss sheath, come out only at harvest when drip-tubers lift their speared leaves.  My bright belly will meet the stems with a whisper of crushed fiber.  Afterward, a whetstone of quartz water-kissed to keep grit from speaking will smooth any scars.

2. Throat plate for a water-pipe.  Oval, mirror-finished, drilled with twenty-seven pinholes in the geometry of a fern spiral.  Set into the feed mouth of the communal cistern, I will see every droplet before it joins the pool.  My music will be the tiniest click each time a bead finds the exact center of a hole.

Because metal cannot sleep, I witness everything.

I feel the hush-games of children in the courtyard—their giggled breaths clipped short whenever an adult’s mantle rustles a warning.  I watch vaporises of morning mist pearl across my polish and slide away bearing darting reflections of sky.  I note the rhythm by which each household times its chores to the plateau’s incidental noises: door unlatched when a shard of basalt tumbles far off, ladder set down precisely as a hidden waterfall gasps, laughter loosed only when the wind gutters across the rim.  

Seasons touch me in temperatures, not in storms.  Summer is a narrower band of chill, winter a broader one; both are bracketed by the same patient drip, drip, drip.  The Lythene say the plateau is too young for extremes, and I, who once survived incandescent descent, believe them.

Every tenth year a repair: a fresh shaving of another fallen star is dovetailed into my edge where microscopic nicks have bred.  Younger iron nestles in the lap of ancient, comet syllable married to asteroid word, our story hammered shut tight as silence.

I see visitors sometimes—geologists, cartographers, pilgrims chasing rumor.  They lean over the cistern and startle at their faces bent in my mirror.  None stay long.  Their boots squeak, their maps crackle, their voices grenade the air.  Even whispering, they are too loud.  They go, and the plateau settles, unwrinkled.

•

If you asked me now where I find myself in this world, I would answer: set edge-on between growth and restraint, between skyfall violence and the vow of hush.  I am the star’s memory of burning married to the mountain’s practice of quiet.  I am use without waste, durability without dominion.  Held in the hands of people who will never let me ring like a bell, I have learned a deeper music: the music of things intentionally unsounded.

When, centuries hence, real rivers finally carve the glass ferns into gentler ground and the Lythene’s watchful covenant ends, someone will lift me from my sheath one last time.  They will press my crescent against a quartz wall until I ring—once, clear, unapologetic.  That single allowed note will be my whole name spoken at last, released back to the sky I fell from.

Until then I wait, bright and patient, listening to water invent its future, keeping keen the silence that lets a young mountain sleep.
