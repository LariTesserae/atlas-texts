At first I tried to picture myself as a visitor—a two-legged foreigner edging along the schist with my pack straps humming from the static. But the ledge would never keep a stranger long; the third dawn would come, the charge would peak, and I would be sluiced off with the next banner of mist.  
So I let that image fade.

Then I thought I might be one of the Vapor-Kin: a brief body braided of drizzle and charge. Beautiful, yes, but gone by noon of the second sunny day. I would remember nothing; the story would collapse the moment it was spoken.  
That was too small a foothold.

I tried on wings—became, for half a heartbeat, a juvenile slate-back eagle banking through cloud windows, ultraviolet world glowing with conie footpaths. Yet even that felt borrowed. The ledge would be only a hunting lane, not a home.

So I listened harder to the place itself, to the cold breath that rises through seams, to the clack of sundew plates, to the far-in meltwater drum. Something there answered in the soles of my own imagined feet, and at last the shape of me clarified.

Where I arrive, and what I become

Name (the one the elders give): Fifth-Rill-Over-Stone  
Kind: Stone-Rafter, youngest flush of the summer cohort  
Years counted: thirteen shadow-lengths (they mark age by the height of your first noon shadow against the cliff)  
Body: narrow as all my people are—hips hardly wider than ribcage, arms corded from teasing moss seams—yet still soft around the knuckles, mica-dust not yet worked into every crease  
Sign that I belong: a sleeve of thyme oil rubbed along collar and jaw to keep the midges from my eyes, and the single root-fiber cord I have earned, braided into the lobe of my left ear

My place on the ledge

I sleep in the smallest corbelled hut, the one whose roof hide still smells of goat milk. Each dawn I fold that hide back so starlight can reach the hearth ash. Before the sun skims the opposite range I walk the inner drip-line with an antler awl, loosening last night’s silt so today’s seeps run clear. The water comes out so cold it aches the bones before it wets the skin. Elders say that ache is how the mountain proves we are awake.

What I tend

1. Moss batteries: I am apprentice to Root-Binder Clove, keeper of charge. Together we probe each cushion, feeling for the faint prick that means it is near spillover. When a mat glows with too much stored spark we vent it—pinch off the outermost inch and drop it into a horn bowl of meltwater where it discharges in a soft blue coil that smells of wet copper.  
2. Sundew seed: I fish the ruby seeds from the moss cuffs of my sleeves and flick them into new grit pockets. My throw must be sideways; a vertical drop would gift the seed to the void.  
3. Birch braces: After every rockfall, I crawl the ledge margin threading willow withes through dwarf birch trunks, lashing them to the bedrock so next winter’s ice will plane branches, not uproot whole trees.

How I speak

Our voices run under wind. I know perhaps twenty true words—enough for weather and danger. For the rest we share vibration: wrist bones pressed together, the ledge itself carrying consonants the throat can’t risk losing to the gusts. My favorite phrase is three pulses long and means both “I am still here” and “the stone is still holding.”

Rites ahead of me

• Solstice Agreement: In nine mornings the mist leopard’s time begins. Before that I must braid my second root-fiber cord and thread through it one shard of mica chipped by my own hand. This marks me as stone-grown and earns me leave to stay during the nine autumn days when we otherwise withdraw.  
• First Roof Unlacing: Next cloudless night I alone will climb the hut tops and roll back every hide so the entire hearth circle drinks starlight at once. If dawn finds even a thumb-shadow of hide across a smoke-hole, the mountain will “eat night” elsewhere—its hunger taken in rockfall. No apprentice wants to bear that blame.  
• Shadow Weight: Before next thaw I must stand on the brink, arms out, while an elder measures the drag of my outline in the updraft. If the wind can lift both cloak and shadow clear of the rock, I earn the title Rafter True and may speak to Roof-Sleepers as equal, not child.

Things I fear and love in equal parts

• The charged days when droplets leap from skin—because each prickle of static tells me the moss is nearly full, and a full moss means the mountain has trusted us again.  
• The silence that falls when fresh blood darkens the ledge. I have never seen the mist leopard, only felt the hush land like a cold thumb on the back of my neck.  
• Hollow-Light Day, when my shadow will float two arm-spans above me. I want to chase it like a Vapor-Kin child—and am terrified I might actually catch it.

What I dream

Sometimes I dream I am not a narrow human at all but the ledge itself, stone ribs breathing water, moss nerves storing thunder, roots and ropes and goat hairs all stitched through me so tight I could swing out over the gulf and still not fall. In the dream I feel every footstep: conie drumbeat, goat click, elder tread, even the hush of Vapor-Kin condensing in my pores. I wake with mica dust on my tongue and know the dream was half-remembered fact: the place and the people are a single weaving, and my small thread is already tied.

So if you come looking for me

Find the smallest hut, the one whose door-hinge root still squeaks. Call across the wind in pulse-beats—three in, five out. I will answer by pressing my wrist to yours so the mountain can carry our words. If the mist is falling from above, you may stay for tea of thyme and meltwater. If it is rising from the gulf, we will pack your herbs and hurry you off the ledge before it disappears. Either way, I will mark the doorway with a pebble once you leave. Pebbles mean return.  
I will be here, thin and grey-blue, knucklebones showing, eyes bright with the slow lightning the moss keeps for us all, stone-born and stone-kept, listening for the next crack of sky that tells the mountain and its children we are still—together—alive.
