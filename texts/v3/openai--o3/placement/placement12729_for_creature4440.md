When I listen inward for the place in Plot 17-B that feels most like “me,” I do not land on a single job title or creature shape.  
Instead a slow zoom comes into focus, as though an enormous lens were sliding deeper and deeper into the Yard until it clicks into the one stratum that matches the tempo of my pulse.  
That layer is the spiral patrol taken twice a day by the Meter-Readers.  
So—let the picture settle there.

1. WAKING  
I am ten, maybe eleven.  
My bunk is a lengthwise groove the banyan cast for me the year I arrived; every night it seems to deepen by the width of a thumbnail, so the fit stays perfect.  
Dawn means nothing here, lightwise, but I know morning because the triple-expander pump coughs three times, clearing its throat for the day shift. The cough quivers straight through the wood and up my ribs.  That is my alarm clock.  
Bare feet onto warm, always-damp cambium.  One gulp from the tin cup I keep hooked over the nearest drip-line: tannin water, faintly sweet with last night’s sap bloom.  
I reach for my whole toolkit—one thumb-sized nub of chalk on a string and a bamboo rod with a sliding brass collar—but it is already hanging from the overhead peg where the banyan’s busiest aerial root meets the dorm rafters.  During the night the banyan has enveloped the peg by another millimeter.  I greet it with the practiced two-finger tap that passes for “good morning” in the Reef.

2. THE CIRCUIT  
My patrol begins at the boilers because metal is honest before the day’s heat soaks in.  The Number-Three furnace exudes a shine of moisture; I chalk 38 °C on the brickwork and rake away a tuft of charred moss so the Boiler Gardeners will see the note.  
Then outward, always counter-clockwise, riding the banyan’s own slow uncoiling.  The route is only one spiral but it includes:  
• Fourteen sap-pressure taps whose rubber bladders rise and fall like sleeping dogs.  
• Eight bore gauges sunk to the hilt in load-bearing roots; I brace the bamboo rod across each, slide the brass collar down to the bark, and read the distance to the waterline: circumference drift.  
• One listening post—a hollow knuckle big enough to crouch inside.  I press my ear to the wall.  If I hear three distinct heartbeats (the Reef, the pump row, and my own) I scrawl a checkmark; if they blur into one, I write tilt? and the Pulse Board will debate whether a realignment day is coming.

3. COMPANIONS ON THE PATH  
Candle-tails flicker overhead like dim lanterns; I count them without thinking.  A good tally means the welders were gentle last night.  
At Pad 6-West a palm-bat drops a bead of condensation onto my shoulder—its way, the old timers say, of offering small weather reports.  
Near the ethanol vats a blue taper is burning.  The flame stands straight; the batch is still good.  I mark a neat ✓.  
Once a week, when the shrimp cull happens, I step ankle-deep among the workers ladling writhing glass shapes into wicker drums.  The water around my shins tingles from their frantic filter-paddles; afterward the puddles will run clearer for days.

4. WRITING THE YARD’S DIARY  
By mid-loop my chalk is down to a grain. I have left numbers, arrows, tiny diagrams, and the occasional joke for the afternoon patrol (we trade punch-lines that way).  I also leave silence: blank stretches of bark where everything is within the green band of okay.  
Nothing I record feels like “data” in the school-book sense.  It feels like the Reef talking through my hand.  
Toward the rim peatbank the air thins, slightly cooler, and that is my finish line.  I turn, spiral back to the central stack, and there on the brick I copy today’s sequence into the master ledger: a single curling line that looks more vine than handwriting.  

5. THE MOMENT OF KNOWING WHO I AM  
While logging the figures I sometimes lose the boundary between the banyan’s pulse and my bloodstream.  The numbers cease to be external facts: they are the angles of my elbow, the temperature of my own breath.  
That is when I understand my place here.  I am neither overseer nor burden.  I am the Yard’s proprioception—its sense of where its limbs end and its balance begins.  
A Meter-Reader is how the Reef knows itself in motion.

6. WHEN THE DAY ENDS  
The chalk dust rinses off in any puddle.  My feet stay sap-slick; nobody bothers with shoes unless sparks are flying.  
I drift past the Drift-Uncles tuning the pumps with their story-songs, past the foundry where crucibles glow like small captive suns, and climb back to my growing bunk.  
Before sleep I scratch tomorrow’s weather guess—two drips and a breeze—on the beam above my head.  The mycelium will ghost the chalk stroke before morning, translating it into pale filaments the graftwrights swear they can read.  Perhaps the banyan reads it too; perhaps that is why rain often obliges.  

So: if you come looking for me in Plot 17-B, follow the fresh chalk marks until they tighten into the Yard’s core.  
Search for the child moving at half the pace of a heartbeat, rod balanced across both palms like a yoke, counting sap breaths and root heartbeats and lantern-lizards in the rafters.  
That child—that moving dot on the Reef’s slowly re-written map—is me.
