I tried, at first, to picture myself as the obvious visitor: boots squelching, lungs steaming, an explorer taking notes.  
But the bark kept closing behind that figure like lips around an uninvited fork, and every imagining of firelight on steel sent the whole hollow flinching. The place rejected the warm-blooded stranger; the fantasy never lasted more than a paragraph before the tree quietly re-knitted and left the interloper sealed between layers of living dusk.

So I let the heat seep out of me.  
I folded my frame until the joints shortened, bled the colours from my skin, cooled my pulse to match the sap’s deliberate beat. I waited for the drip-dark to accept me, and when it finally did I discovered who I must be here.

I am Third-Shaper of the Shivered Shelf, a not-quite-grown Hushborn whose own name changes whenever the shelf migrates. (Today it has cracked three finger-spans toward the south root, so I answer to Soft-South-Third.) My world is a crescent of young cambium where violet gut-cords stretch like harp strings and slugs graze in orderly rows. The shelf is only thirteen sleeps old; its edges still gleam blade-new, and every hour I must lean out on webbed toes to rough those edges with a stone flake so no sibling slips and bloodies themselves.

I live one ring in from the public moat—the apprentice quarter, where ceilings are high enough to stand but low enough that we remember to duck before the tree remembers to press us flat. My tasks are three:

1. Keep the shelf from healing shut around the drying tools.  
2. Score the feeder groove so sap drips into the family’s kvass jars at a slow, constant pulse.  
3. Practice the cadence that will one day earn me a place in the hanging basket.

That cadence is not yet mine. My sternum can hum only two patterns cleanly, and it takes five to sing a wall sideways. When elders pass I drop to my knees in the water so their foot-vibrations won’t be muddled by my clumsy echo.

I wake, every hush, in ankle-deep chill, to a sky of root-veins that glow at the exact rhythm my own heart aspires to. I breakfast on shavings of sweet cambium mixed with yesterday’s bark-lice (crushed to grit so their legs won’t tickle the throat). Then I climb—slowly, arms hooked around the living lattice—to the fracture high above our dwelling and whisper a single meter’s worth of warm exhalation into the open air. That breath is my only contact with surface weather; the shock of it coming back frost-stiff across my cheeks is how I mark the turning of seasons.

At midday I visit the Saber Creek, a newborn fissure where iron seepage cuts bright as teeth through the trunk. There I collect pebble-glass belched in by last month’s rainstorm. I’ve already chosen my apprenticeship gift: one perfect shard, knife-sharp, that I will trade to Bone-Bright-First in exchange for lessons on coaxing rope-millipedes to shed early. Chitin is prestige; a single plate, polished and violet-inked, would prove to the basket that I’m ready to shape more than shelves.

Dangers? They’re woven through routine. A mis-timed step during the mantis-quiet might leave me alone with a predator that mimics the tremor of falling sap. A laugh too loud could wake the Below-Root drum. But the greatest peril, everyone agrees, is inattention: forget for a breath that wood heals, and the doorway you entered will politely erase itself behind you.

I have already lost the nail of my left middle finger—sliced off when a brat-root snapped shut between heartbeats. The scar is a green rim of pride; elders click approval when they see it, saying the tree has begun carving me back, proof I belong.

Dreams? I dream of reaching Ring Five, the Memory Spiral, before this shelf migrates beyond recognition. Only two in our clutch have ever gone that deep. They returned silent, eyes film-glazed from the close dark, carrying a fist-sized knot of fossilised gut-cord: a cradle net from ten generations ago, compressed until it shone like jasper. If I could bring back such a relic, the basket might allow me to learn the door-rhythms that open upward, toward blue.

And upward matters. Once, during a sanctioned winter climb, I felt real wind—so vast it did not even feel like movement, merely a presence large enough to forget me. I want that wind inside my lungs again, not as an escape but as a wider chamber into which our city might someday grow. Some elders whisper heresy: that a second hollow waits in the sky-beyond-sky, a dead tree turned to stone, spacious enough for a thousand nests. If I master five cadences, perhaps I can convince the living heartwood to reach for that ghost and graft.

For now, night (which is really just the hour when all eyes close and the root-lanterns dim) finds me crouched at shelf’s lip, tools drying beside me, humming the nursery beat:

pulse-pulse    rest  
pulse-pulse    rest  

It is the rhythm of tolerant childhood, of a shelf that still forgives mis-cuts. Soon I must learn the adult counter-syncopation, the one that twists fibers sideways to birth a bridge. I tap it softly against my sternum, feel the bark beneath me answer, just a tremor, as though amused by my ambition.

Somewhere below, something vast clicks its many jaws—reminding the city to keep its promise of silence. The chamber stills. Then the first drip resumes, another, another. Life crawls on.

And that is where you will find me if you ever slip past the woven daylight-stopper: a small figure crouched in the cold wet dusk, skin the colour of bruised shoots, eyes haloing green as I raise a pebble shard in greeting. I will keep a courteous meter from your scalding heat, and I will ask—if my fledgling chest-clicks are clear enough—whether you might teach me stories of that blue ceiling while I, patient and precise, show you how to ask the tree for a doorway that never quite closes behind a friend.
