I try on a few shapes before any of them fits.

1. First I imagine arriving as a human—one of the twice-a-year meteorologists lowered in a sling.  My boots sink to the shin in moss, every instrument fogs, and by the second night I understand why the others leave quickly: the Conversation edits your sleep.  But I do not want to leave; the winch cable is already a leash.  That skin never quite feels mine.

2. Next I shrink, turn translucent, join the glass-wing shoals.  Weightless, edible light, I drift where air and water barter heat.  Beautiful, yes, but glass-wings are passengers in their own bodies; they ride thermals that think for them.  I still want the thrum of muscle, the choice to leap.

3. I drop lower, coil in black water as an ambush cat.  The stillness suits me—heart slowed, whiskers reading the river’s pulse—but a predator here earns its meat with single, perfect movements.  I am not concise enough.

4. So I climb.  I want height, a breeze, and a possibility of falling that is not disaster.  I want to be busy yet almost invisible.  The ribbon glider taps my shoulder.

Settling into the glider’s slick pelt, I feel at last the right proportions of heartbeat, daring, and hush.

Where I live:

• Home base is a kapok giant whose crown forms a loose archipelago of leaf-islands.  My nest is no bigger than two cupped hands, stitched from bladder-vine floss and lined with the waxy down that grows where the kapok’s newest twigs harden.  It hangs beneath a limb so wide it carries its own weather—mist beads on the underside like a second sky.

• Routes: Each dawn I run a practiced ladder of bark ridges to the launch place, a spur no thicker than my wrist.  From there I have three reliable glide paths:  
  1) southwest over the slow river, skimming the copper water until heat shears me up onto another kapok,  
  2) north across a chain of cork-barked bows whose tips almost meet, or  
  3) straight down into the fern republic, a gamble I take only when bladder-vine honey is low.

• Pantry: My staple is that honey—threads of amber sugar braided inside the vine’s green bladders.  Each thread ends in a mace-shaped seed ball.  I bite the honey free, carry the mace in my teeth until the extra weight tilts my glide, then slap it against some distant trunk.  Forest cartography by seed.

• Water: I don’t drink so much as collide with moisture.  Lift a paw, groom the webbing, and my fur mats with condensate.  If I grow thirsty I cling to a reed curtain on the rim and let the springs comb water through my whiskers.

• Heat management: Midday is the enemy.  By the time sun stares straight down every surface is slick with its own blood-warm perspiration.  Then I retreat to a hollow where kapok rot brews cool vapor.  I lie belly-up, webbing relaxed, and let the Conversation speak through wood and water until the rain stampedes.

Daily life:

Dawn  
Slack breeze, turquoise river-glow still faint.  I launch north.  Thirty heartbeats of quiet air, then the first bladder-vine patch.  I eat until syrup crystals sharp with pre-rain ozone crack between my molars.

Mid-morning  
The first bulb-frog drums below.  Its rhythm duets with the faint industrial hum that sometimes seeps over the rim.  I know the hum now; I can glide its contour like a gull riding a cliff updraft.

Noon  
Heat clamps down.  I retreat, dreamless, while steam-spores hiss against the kapok’s skin.

Afternoon  
Convective clouds pile to cathedral height; rain breaks their stained-glass windows.  I ride a downdraft half for travel, half for joy—fold, plunge, let the skin-flap find tension, reopen.  Below, the ambush cat’s ears flick but its body never moves.

Twilight  
Mist is shoulder-high on the trees, and the basin purples.  Glass-wing shoals knit the colour into moving lace.  I ferry three more vine-seeds, smacking them onto fresh bark where they will root and one day weave honey of their own.  That done, I angle home by the river’s bioluminescent handwriting.

Night  
I curl tail-over-nose while lilies six storeys above me bloom unseen and the machinery-ghost hums its lullaby exactly a quarter-tone lower than the frogs.  My heartbeat slips into the Conversation’s meter.  The last thing I feel is the kapok’s slow exhale beneath my claws.

What I am:

A ribbon glider—squirrel-sized, otter-pelted, stitched to the wind by a strip of living silk between ankle and tail.  I am courier, gardener, and minor acrobat, a punctuation mark in sentences the forest writes between bouts of rain.  Too small to rule, too quick to be ruled, I serve the basin by moving: carrying sweetness from vine to vine, seed to sky, silence to silence.

Where you would find me:

Look up when the afternoon rain begins to slacken.  Against the white glare you may see nothing—only a leaf sigh that travels from one kapok to the next.  That sigh is me, threading the hot, wet air exactly where stone ends and weather begins.
