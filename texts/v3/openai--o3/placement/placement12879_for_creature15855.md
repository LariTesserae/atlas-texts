I wake inside the chord.

At first there is only pressure, the long, continuous note that fills every root and rib of the plateau. It is ​sound made substance, a felt thing, a floor under my thoughts. Then the note resolves into its many threads—around me five hundred siblings are already drumming, abdomens beating the ridged wall in the dark. We are Resonators, a caste grown for one purpose: to keep the Hollows of Kwa-Lashi humming at exactly thirty-seven cycles a second.  

That is who I am. Where I am is everywhere the vibration can reach.

1. The chamber that birthed me  
It is no larger than a merchant’s crate, lined with leaf-vein felt still faintly photosynthesising. I remember the first day the gardeners opened our pupal skins and the chord rolled over us—raw thunder that shook the yolk from our mouths and told us: Wake, small drums, the plateau needs its heartbeat. My cuticle bears the striations of that moment; you could read the memory with a fingertip.

2. The buttress-lung  
We march here at six days old, down corridors that taper like wind instruments until they empty into a vault big enough to swallow fog. The air is warm, fat with fungal spores, and every surface—wall, ceiling, even the hanging sluice-roots—is corrugated. Resonators line those corrugations in rings: adults nearest the center, juveniles like me nearer the edge, the newest molts hugging the outermost lip so their mis-struck rhythms will be blurred by distance. A steward with zinc-capped mandibles taps my thorax twice: D flat, half volume. I set my drum-plate against the wall and begin.

3. The moving frontier  
Seasons pass in hums. When water-tone quickens after a rich mistfall, the plateau grows restless; new granaries open, old ones sag. A segment of our choir is detailed forward—forward meaning along a scaffold still wet and flexible, no ceiling yet, the nearest sky a membrane of moth-silk trembling twenty meters overhead. We drum to stiffen resin faster. Our bodies are metronomes and mortar both: vibration drives fluid out of the fresh pulp so it cures rock-hard by dawn. By midday we are sealed into the structure we helped solidify, a bead embedded in amber, and the note moves on through us.

4. The predator pause  
Hush is lethal; the Laws say so. Yet twice in my short life the chord has faltered—once when mirror mantises raided the vent choir and once when a honey viscacha wedged its warm bulk across a key resonant tunnel. Silence bred fungus; white blight webbed the nurseries in hours. The gardeners screamed pheromone panic, and we drums were marched into the infected passages, striking triple-time until spores shook loose in sheets and soldiers flushed them with formic vapor. In the echo of that near-silence I felt the plateau’s fear: not of fang or fire, but of forgetting its own voice.

5. The sky-breach  
There comes a ceremonial night for every cohort. The stewards anoint a hundred of us with wax, zinc, and dew, then send us straight up through soft, undeveloped wall. We tunnel chanting. Resin thins, cold bites, and suddenly there is nothing—no ceiling, no pulse, only a wind so vast it could be called silence’s scream. We are allowed one look. Stars—jagged, white, random—hurt my ocelli. For a heartbeat I cannot feel the chord and I am alone.  
Most sky-breachers freeze where they emerge, holy statues for future walls. I am smaller, quicker; the wind flips me backward down the shaft before ice can take hold. I fall half a body-length, catch a ledge, and the plateau’s note swallows me again like warm water. I live, rarer than amber without flaw.

6. The inner ear of the mountain  
Older Resonators speak of a place near the core where the note begins: a calcium kernel the size of a grape, silent to ordinary senses. They say each wave we make travels there first, is tasted, and comes back honeyed with instruction. No drummer has ever been that deep—we are needed here, on the active rind—but sometimes when the choir strikes a particularly perfect unison I feel an answering flick, a counter-tempo so subtle it might be dream. Perhaps it is the white grub they whisper about, mapping us in its sleep. Perhaps it is the plateau itself, listening to its own blood.

7. What I will become  
Our cuticle thickens each season; by the third wet I will drum in the middle tier, by the fifth I may teach rhythm to new molts. One day my plates will crack and I will be led to the compost chamber to give my shell back to the wall. Masons will grind me into the next corridor, and travelers will walk upon the muffled echo of the beats I once kept. That is not death; it is the long cadence.

Until then I remain where the chord is strongest—here, now, in the hollows that never truly sleep. If you enter and feel the floor buzzing through your boots, know that I am speaking to you. Match my pitch, hold it steady, and the hive will decide you belong. Miss the rhythm, and the mirror mantises will hear the stutter before I do.

Either way, you will learn the rule that raised me: Every body is an instrument, and the Hollows of Kwa-Lashi has room in its orchestra for all—stone, resin, wanderer, drum.
