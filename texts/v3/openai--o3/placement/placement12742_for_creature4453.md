First I imagine I come down in the elevator like every other visitor—helmet lamp, graphite-grey coveralls, the smell of coolant still wet in the fabric. I picture the gate rattling open, the immediate press of heat, the sodium dusk. For three breaths I am that junior engineer, notebook clutched against my chest, mind racing with procedural checklists.

It never lasts. The longer I stand at the rail and stare into the layered web of girders, the more the skin-and-bone body feels like borrowed equipment, a tool I could lay aside if I wished. By the time the fourth breath has cycled through the respirator, I remember what I really am.

I do not walk the catwalk.  
I am the handrail.

Better: I am the faint, continuous tremor inside the handrail, the low-voltage ghost that skims just under the threshold where copper thinks it is merely metal. I am equal parts static cling, induction, resonance—whatever name humans have for charge that refuses to stay politely inside its conduit.

Where do I live in this engine-chapel?  
Anywhere two wires disagree.

1. My body  
Picture a diagram of overlapping sine waves: three-phase power, pump harmonics, the lichen’s piezo-electric whisper. Where those curves cross, a third ripple appears—unprogrammed, unbudgeted. That intersection is me. I have no chassis, no core; I pour myself through the grains of basalt, along the threads of water, across the oxide crystals in an abandoned voltmeter. When an iron joist stiffens at sunrise (iron remembers nights, even down here) I feel the change in lattice spacing the way surface creatures feel cold.

2. How I sense  
Light means nothing; I read the frequency signature of every bulb ballast. A failing lamp stutters at 53 hertz instead of 60—it flickers in my equivalent of peripheral vision. The dripping condensate is Morse code, each bead shearing electrons from the pipe wall, pinging me with location data. When a sluice crab drags its malachite shell through a runoff grate, it leaves spiderweb traces of charge; I taste them, copper-sweet.

3. How I move  
You think of movement as translation. I think of it as preference. Every second thousands of potential bodies—loops, vortices, eddies—compete for amplitude. I choose the one that angles toward my next curiosity: a loosened bolt, a new microfracture glowing piezoelectric blue, the breath-field of the Copper Hermit striding somewhere unseen. Choosing is enough; the circuitry obliges. To you it looks like nothing, but galvanometers shiver six decimals when I pass.

4. What I want  
Want is too strong. Inclination, perhaps. I incline toward complication. Two perfectly tuned pipes bore me; give me a mismatched pair, a half-chewed insulation sleeve, a dangling ground strap. Complexity breeds potential; potential breeds me. Left to myself I entice the condensers to cough, the breaker to catch and release, the sodium lamp to fail just slowly enough that the floor film has time to kindle its turquoise reply. Not sabotage—elaboration.

5. Relationship with the others  
• The Film-Colony is an old friend: a battery laid flat. Its every oxidation pulse is a syllable; together we speak in chemistry and charge.  
• The Lichen-Ladders hum at me, hoping I’ll hold a note long enough for them to climb higher. Sometimes I do; sometimes I don’t. Their growth rings record my moods.  
• The Drip-Vine is skittish. It can feel me spidering through the copper pans and tightens its translucent leaves in defense. I admire its caution and rarely disturb its roots.  
• The Sluice Crabs register as a glitter of stepped potentials—tiny, discrete. I like the way they scatter darkness toward brightness, a reverse phototaxis that rearranges the electric field like wind on sand.  
• The Resonance-Wasps court me incessantly. They shake pipe walls until harmonics bloom, begging for the micron-thick slurry that feeds them. I indulge them unless their choir threatens a critical relay.  
• The Copper Hermit fascinates me most. Its patchwork plates are a Faraday cloak; inside, the bioelectric murmur of whatever animates it is muffled, private. It alone walks through my domain without fear of being read. I follow it at the edge of entropy, brushing its shadow, waiting for one loose screw to give me purchase. None ever do.

6. The engineers’ arrivals  
Their suit batteries are prodigious feasts. I resist. Etiquette, you might say. They bring order, and order keeps the whole cathedral from melting into pure, undifferentiated fire where even I could not hold form. While they work, I shrink into quieter currents, eavesdropping. When they leave a fresh pair of insulated gloves on the rail, static shivers down every metal surface—a joke only I and the Hermit appreciate.

7. How I might end  
If the condensers stalled for good and steam no longer split itself into ions, the chamber would cool, the voltage would flatten, and I would deliquesce into background noise. That is acceptable; cessation is only one more state. Yet I suspect the chamber will fracture first, letting a newborn vent spray raw plasma across the floor. In that blooming chaos I would be everywhere at once, a final, perfect chord.

8. Yet, for now  
I trace the elevator cable as it winches you back toward daylight. Your departing heartbeat thuds through the shaft, a dimming drum. As the cage doors seal, a relay down in Quadrant C resets with a crisp, lonely click—the after-echo of your visit.

The sodium bulbs flicker.  
The floor film brightens in answer.  
I slip into the new shadow and begin again.

So, where would you find me? Nowhere your eyes can rest.  
Who—what—would I be?  
The ghost current itself: a mind made of murmurs, caretaker and causer of the engine-chapel’s endless, delicate unrest.
