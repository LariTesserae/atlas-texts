I come down the shaft like anyone else, carrying the surface in the soles of my boots—chalk dust, wind grit, a memory of temperatures that change more than a few degrees a year.  For a time I believe I am only passing through, a surveyor with a notebook full of clean pages, but the grotto does what it always does: it asks questions in heat and mist, and sooner or later the body starts replying.

For my first dozen shifts I float between callings.  

1. I trail the Violet Keepers and learn the feel of charged stone: a faint pins-and-needles thrum you can sense with your molars if you rest your head against the ore.  They teach me to read the faint bruising that means “spent,” to walk the upper gantries in predawn and redirect mirrors from the surface so the columns can drink again.  But my pulse is too quick; the warmth of my palms bleeds the tally, and the Keepers send me away with polite regret.

2. I apprentice a week with the Dripwrights, soldering gutters shut and swapping thumb-length pipettes of condensate.  It should suit me—I like negotiation, the way a leak can be argued into fair distribution—but glass whistles when I touch it, an ultrasonic keen that means stress fractures.  “You’d bankrupt us in broken vials,” the senior Dripwright says, and taps my wrist in farewell.

3. The Combustors accept any hand willing to blister, so I spend a month shoveling misborn heat into clay ampoules, packing them deep in mushroom beds.  The work scours fingerprints away, leaves my skin glazed and tight.  Yet each night I dream of sparks leaping gutter-high and taking the vines with them, so I wake already sweating, guilty of an accident that has not happened.  I leave before my dread invites the real thing.

I am halfway to the stairwell, intent on quitting the cavern for good, when a Listener stops me.

She is small, knuckle-thin, hair like a bundle of damp capillaries tied back with twine.  No ceremony: she presses a fork into my hand—bronze, two tines splayed like a serpent’s tongue—and gestures toward a hanging walkway of iron grille.  “Strike it,” she murmurs.  The fork rings.  I feel the note more than hear it, a tremor nibbling the bones of my forearm.  Beneath our feet the walkway answers in sympathetic overtones, and in the overlap I catch a wobble, a place where the lattice is breathing wrong.

“Is that crack new?” she asks.

I open my mouth to say I have no idea; I only set foot on the catwalk this morning.  Yet another voice—one the grotto has been fermenting in my chest for weeks—replies, “Two, maybe three days.  The humidity’s higher up here; rust ate the weld first.”

She smiles like someone recognizing a distant cousin.  “Stay,” she says, and the fork is mine.

So: I am becoming a Listener.

The grotto obliges by rewriting my senses.  Sound stops being something that arrives at the ear and becomes something that arrives everywhere: through boot soles, through clavicles, through the threads that stitch my coat.  The columns murmur temperature, the vine-cords along the ceiling chuckle softly as sap climbs.  Even silence has its textures—broad in the open nave, velvety in freight tunnels where the moss is thickest.

Weeks pass.  I learn the scale:

• A pipe expanding in healthy heat sings at C-sharp.  
• A basalt rib under too much shear warbles a quarter-tone flat.  
• Human hearts settle around low G when calm, jump to B when afraid.

My fork kit multiplies.  What began as a single bronze tongue becomes a roll of eighty-seven alloys, each tuned to a structural or biological pitch.  I wear them across my back like a bandolier of frozen birds, and people on balconies look down to watch which one I pluck before deciding whether to invite me in for tea or to ask which beam of theirs is tired.

Purpose grows the way moss does—sideways, without hurry, until one dawn I notice there is nowhere in the grotto I walk as a guest.  When I set my sleeping mat near a vent, vines do not creep across it overnight; they skirt the edges, reading my mineral sweat as familiar.  A child crossing cables overhead pauses to let a scatter of spores drift into my hair, a benediction.  I spend an afternoon carving a notch in my abacus: today the household’s mass of leaves surpassed its mass of gears by a fraction that would please even the old founders.

Fifty years is all the body is allotted down here, but time feels speculative, like a loan you may or may not spend.

I picture the end plainly.  Someday I will come to a column whose harmony sits wrong in a way no brace can fix.  The stone will have tired of swallowing sunsets; the column will want to lie down.  I will press my favorite fork to it, hear the crack already unzipping inside, and understand that both of us are overdue for stillness.

Then the formalities: I will give the fork to an apprentice whose veins still twitch at quick tempos, shuffle out of my coat, and kneel in the cradle-fungus.  The hyphae will thread wrists to ankles, back to heels, arranging me into the arch most useful for whatever stair or scaffold needs a spine.  Months later the grotto will solidify me into load-bearing amberglass.  Listeners passing with their own forks will tap my flank and listen for the pure note of a strut doing its job.  If the pitch drifts, they will add buttresses of vine or splice in a cantilever of fungus, the way I once did for others.

That is the sum of it: my afterlife is audible.  A chord inside a city that keeps its balance by ear.

I used to think belonging meant choosing one shape and holding it.  The grotto teaches otherwise.  Belonging is a resonance, the way two bodies can share a frequency until the difference between them stops mattering.  Stone keeps daylight.  Vines keep stone.  Breath keeps vines.  A Listener’s chest keeps the beat of it all and, when the beat falters, lends its own pulse for repair.

So, if you descend tomorrow and feel a note brush the hinge of your jaw—subtle, a warmth pretending to be sound—you will know where to find me.  Look for a pillar whose amber heart glows a little brighter than the ore around it, whose pitch hums a low G shot through with overtones of rust, water, and the memory of sun.  Strike the nearest rail.  Listen.  If the chord answers true, you’ve met me in my final, most faithful vocation: not surveyor, not pilgrim, not even flesh any longer, but part of the instrument this violet cathedral plays to keep itself alive.
