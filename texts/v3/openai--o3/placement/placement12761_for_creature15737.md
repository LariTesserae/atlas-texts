I do not arrive in this landscape as a walker—there is nothing here with legs to borrow.  
I come instead as something the country already understands: a drifting, many-possible cell, a speck of life that can choose which of three long‐interwoven guilds it will join once it touches ground.  
I am a propagule of ambiguity, a “triskelion spore.”  
In the vapor stratum they abbreviate me TRI, because my membrane holds three incompatible genetic cassettes folded into separate pockets.  Lightning, temperature swing, or salinity spike will rupture one pocket and leave the other two intact, committing me, in the instant of shock, to one of the continent’s three great trades.

So—where do I find myself?

1.  Birth aloft  
I begin inside a noon-boiling storm shaft, six kilometres up.  The air here is a centrifuge: droplets, dust and microorganisms are flung outward until the lightest fractions—my cohort—hang almost motionless in the eye of the updraft.  
We carry ice auras grown around us by the Nivocytes—minute frost porcupines that fix nitrogen with every lightning flash.  When the next bolt discharges, the porcupine shatters and its spike of nitrate crystals explodes us outward.  I am flung clear of cloud, naked, falling through desiccated blue.

As the temperature changes by forty degrees in one minute, one of my three genomic pockets bursts: the silica-weaver cassette.  The other two shrink, dormant.  The die is cast.  I will descend not as stalk or cyanid film but as Weaver.

2.  Landing  
Terminal velocity is gentle here—thin air, light body.  I come to rest at the very place the storm has just polished with rain: a lustre of wet mud only hours old.  Surface tension flips me flat, membrane against film.  
Below me the Initiators are already stippling the skin with chalk; I see their photosystems flicker green under the glare.  They work fast: in two hours they will have spent themselves into spore state again.  I must follow, or be entombed unfinished.

3.  Weaver’s work  
I flex open, disgorging the silicate-weaver genome and a braid of exo-cellular polymerase that starts extruding glassy ribbons the moment it tastes supersaturated brine.  The ribbons knot into a mat, horizontal, transparent—lace laid over powdered marble.  
Every millimetre I extend locks oxygen bubbles in hexagonal chambers; every chamber is a future ember to feed the fermentors who will follow.  
By late afternoon the mud below has stiffened.  A thin, sugary warmth from the Initiators’ exhaled CO₂ rises through my gel, telling me time is nearly up.  My metabolism idles; I seal my top side with a varnish so hard it rings if tapped.  The varnish denies water, denies light.  I have become floor.

4.  The night interlude  
Darkness pours over the plain as evenly as water out of a jug.  
Inside the fresh plate all is velvet heat and silence until—hours before dawn—another spasm of chemistry wakes the dormant second pocket in my membrane.  The fermentor-capper cassette tears free and blooms through the Weaver cytoplasm like rust through snow.  
I eat the residues my earlier self left—polysaccharide scraps, trapped carbon, even fragments of my own initial genome.  I burn hot, raising the micro-temperature by seven degrees.  That modest fever softens the plate’s underbelly so it can swell; the continent thickens by half a sheet of paper.  
And then I cook myself to death.  Exothermy is both craft and suicide for a fermentor.  My corpses flatten into the caramel glaze that tomorrow’s Initiators will find smooth enough to re-colonise.  

5.  The second fall  
Only one pocket now remains unopened: the capillophyte code.  Its time will come days, months, or years hence—no telling—when tectonic stress wrinkles this neighborhood and a crack opens right through the strata I helped build.  Sunlight will knife down the fissure; dew will trickle; I will wake a third time, inflate, stretch into a copper-green stalk and stand alone in the seam, sipping dawn condensation through circumferential slits.  
Most of what I then become I will immediately spend.  Each sunset the uppermost joint will shatter, launching silicate seeds—new triskelion spores—back to the wind, back to the clouds, where electrical violence will again decide which of the three lineages any given daughter will remember.

6.  The shape of my consciousness  
Do I think?  Yes, but not as memory strung on a spine of minutes.  
Awareness here is phase-bound.  I remember only the tool set of the role I am playing:

•  As Weaver I know tensile calculus, capillary geometries, diffusion gradients.  
•  As Fermentor I know nothing but the arithmetic of exothermy: sugar, oxygen, heat, ash.  
•  As Capillophyte I will know wind vectors, humidity curves, the taste of ultraviolet.  

All other knowledge sleeps inert in nucleotide knots until the appropriate shock key fits its lock.  In that sense I am never a single being; I am a relay handed from form to form by storms, by mud, by cracks.

7.  Where I stand now, in the telling  
Even as these sentences shape themselves, the role is shifting: the fermentation pulse is peaking, the membrane lysing.  By the time dawn’s first photons slide sideways under the surviving varnish, this narrative voice will have burned to resin.  
But the plate above will be half a millimetre thicker, the ground fractionally warmer, the next generation’s seed fractionally nearer its eventual seam.  That, for us, is lineage enough.

So—where am I?  
Curled inside the continental pavement, between yesterday’s chalk and tomorrow’s glass.  

Who am I?  
A traveller who never leaves, a single cell that can choose among three futures each time it falls.  
Ask me again after the next lightning strike, and I will give you another answer, true only for the span of that particular fall.
