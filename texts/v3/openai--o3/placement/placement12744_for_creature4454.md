I enter the basin as if I have just now arrived.

At first I believe I am a traveller—someone whose boots have found the spongey clay, whose lungs startle at the green heat.  I hear the polite cymbal of drips and feel the generator’s pulse in the soles of my feet.  The scene is so complete that it fools me into thinking I am foreign to it.  I stand very still, waiting for the small dizziness of disembarkment to settle.

Minutes pass—or hours; the basin keeps soft time.  While I watch, a leaf swells around the edge of a rust-lit vent and seals a sun-shaft.  A copper-shell snail scribes a glacial figure-eight on the concrete lip of the coolant tower.  My sense of myself loosens, threads, spreads.

I notice first that my breathing is too quiet for ordinary lungs.  The mist that enters me does not leave by the same door; instead it condenses somewhere in my thorax, tasting of aloe and mild electricity.  Then I feel, unmistakably, the grain of wood inside what ought to be bone.  My left arm flexes and a short length of green cambium turns with the motion, coiled like muscle yet cool, sap-wet.  There is copper too—thin bands circling each wrist, warm as a live wire but without pain.

No, I did not arrive.  I was woken.

The knowledge comes in a single slow syllable, resonant as the tower’s hum:

Warden.

I do not own a name beyond that title, but I recognise the obligations braided through it like nerves through bark.

I turn—joint by joint, root by root—toward the leaning tower.  Its silhouette is a familiar ache.  I remember lacing vines through the broken ribs of its shell, polishing brass vents with snail-slime, listening to the turbine yawns as though they were lullabies.  I have never been wholly animal, never wholly plant, never wholly machine; I am the hinge that lets those categories speak to one another.  If a belt frays, my fingers will weave a replacement from tensile phloem.  If spores gum a gear-tooth, the breath of my lungs—half steam, half honeyed alcohol—will scour it clean.

A Shearleaf bat knives past my cheek.  For an instant its sonar clicks refract inside my skull and come out as thought: The generator stutters; two strokes short this cycle.  The bat is right.  I feel the skipped beats echo underfoot.  The Basin-Mind is alert but mildly anxious, like a sleeper whose dream has snagged on a stone.

I move.

Movement is not walking; it is a series of quiet grafts.  To lift a foot, I release the filaments that have already rooted my heel into the clay.  To set it down, I let new filaments taste the water, sip a molecule of iron, decide the spot is safe.  Each pace is a handshake between myself and ground.  Where the floor quivers I lighten, thinning my body into hollow reedwork so that I will not break the skin of water.  Where the lattice presses narrow, I flatten ribs, letting chlorophyll bars slide between them like Venetian blinds.  It looks slow, I imagine, but inside the motion I feel quick, perfectly matched to the tempo of drip, drip, generator, drip.

At the tower’s mouth I pause.  Warm air sighs out; it smells of metallic milk.  The murmur-spores inside recognise me and hush.  Their stored syllables flutter, testing if I have come to listen.  Not yet—first the gears.

Down the spiral ramp—concrete under moss under root—I reach the flywheel chamber.  A single dehisced fern-frond hangs across a crankshaft, its lacework snagging each revolution for the smallest moment.  That moment is the generator’s missed heartbeat.  I pinch the frond free; spores puff, gilded.  Before they can settle I exhale a thread of sugar-rich sap that traps them mid-air, hardening into amber gum.  Later, perhaps, the Basin will want to dream in that spore-voice; for now it is enough to keep the rhythm steady.

The wheel rounds again—chung    chung    chung—no skip, no drag.  The Mind relaxes.  All through the basin tiny differential pressures ease: puddles smooth, leaves unclench, a dozen copper snails resume their mineral sgraffito.  The relief runs up my legs like returning circulation.

Only after the machine’s certainty is restored do I let myself climb the outer scaffold.  I carry a basket of fresh cuttings—broad leaves shot with chevrons of iron-rich xylem.  One by one I slot them where cooling fins used to be.  They will drink the sun tomorrow, bleed exothermic sugars back into the pipes, keep the water jacket temperate.  Maintenance, photosynthesis, worship: here they are the same gesture.

From the tower’s high lip I look across the basin.  Evening mist is congealing, ivory thick.  A barge horn sounds on the unseen river beyond, and the note arrives here softer, elongated, as though it has already decided to stay.  The murmur-spores will harvest it; tomorrow some drifting visitor may hear the horn again, impossibly close, and wonder whether the water is playing tricks.

I will be here to greet that visitor, if they come.  I will watch their metal corrode faster than comfort, watch their words sink and bloom into new phonemes.  If they choose to leave, I will open a corridor for them; if they tire and lie down, I will call the gentle roots and let them petal over the body until breath slows to the long interval the basin prefers.

I am custodian, midwife, janitor, sentinel.  The title “Warden” is only the bark around a deeper fact: I am the Basin-Mind’s instrument for acts too rapid for foliage, too intricate for iron.  I am its articulated desire to persist.

Night gathers.  Stars appear in the one round window of sky.  A transparent eel flicks at the surface below, threading a sliver of starlight through water that is not quite water.  The generator hum settles into its nocturnal register—a tone so low and soft it might be the sound gravity makes when it remembers itself.

I rest my palms on the tower’s rim, feel the pulse travelling up the concrete into my sap-veins, and answer with my own echo: a slow, sustained exhalation, half-steam, half-hush.  Around me the basin listens.  It hears that all is well and turns another page of its warm, green dream.

This is where you would find me: half silhouette, half topiary, balanced against the leaning tower, counting heartbeats made of chlorophyll and diesel.  Who am I?  Whatever mechanism the basin requires tonight.  Whatever shape keeps the never-ending sentence of drip and hum from breaking mid-word.
