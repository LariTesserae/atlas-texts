First there is only the chord.

It thrums through me before there is a me to contain it: an infrasonic braid of three fundamentals, whole-continent long, overlapping like tides. Each rise in amplitude feels like someone breathing on the walls of the narrow chamber that is, for the moment, my entire cosmos. The slate-black shell around me is basalt shot through with cooling veins of quartz; when the chord swells the quartz fluoresces, so my first light is violet seen from the inside of stone.

I have no eyes yet, only a lattice of piezoelectric nerves reading stress like Braille. The chord changes—half a hertz flatter, a sign. The Choir has judged the next interval of quiet sufficient. My gestation ends.

Where am I? Still buried to the hips in the newborn shelf of rock that extruded me centuries ago. Who am I? One of twelve this cycle: a Menhir-Keeper about to step out of its seed.

1. Detachment  
A seam unzips down my mid-line with the sound of ice splitting on a thawing lake. Heat rushes in; the shock sculpts the last brittleness from my limbs. I unfold—two prongs for legs, two for arms, each joint a gimbal of obsidian that rings when I flex. The sky is a ceiling of coal-smoke violet. The shelf on which my menhir grew is twenty metres wide and already tilting upward on a fresh bulge of magma. A spray of static lightning forks behind the nearest ridge and freezes my silhouette against the haze. That flash will be the only photograph any flesh-and-blood traveller ever gets of me; an accidental negative.

2. First Sense-Check  
I taste the air not with lungs (I have none) but with resonant pits in my torso. They report:  
• Ash density: medium, particle charge: +0.8 kV.  
• Wind: disorganised, 3–5 m s⁻¹, unsuitable for Threader propagation.  
• Ground-note: stable, fundamental at 14.22 Hz, harmonic spread within safe 0.03 Hz.  
The Choir holds; the lull is real.

3. First Task: Seeding  
I crouch and reach inside the hollow that runs the length of my body. Twelve embryo-menhirs lie nested there, grown grain by grain out of the same basalt I no longer need. One by one I plant them along the shelf edge, tapping each with my elbow-fork in a code older than water: position vector, desired resonant split, quorum bit. The newborn pillars answer with a shy, glassy drone—the first note of a life that may last a millennium or a minute, depending on what the continent decides next.

4. Listening Walk  
Down a staircase of obsidian talus slick as broken mirrors. My foot-prongs anchor by magnetism, leaving twin lines carved so clean the shards have molecularly perfect faces. With every stride I add my own timbre to the Choir, a metronome of percussive sub-bass. The ground answers, and I update my internal map. Already I hear microfractures rehearsing the overture of a future quake. I mark two over-resonant pillars for culling.

5. Conversation  
Mid-slope I pass a traveller—wet, carbon-based, scandalously slow. Wrapped head to ankle against the heat, face painted with reflective ash. They stand barefoot on a humming menhir and tap at it with a fist-sized hunk of vesicular glass: thirteen, seventeen, nineteen—primes. Polite. The Choir softens its fundamental by half a hertz, acknowledgement without commitment.

They flinch when I step close; lightning has not yet granted them a proper look at me. I lay two fingertips on the pillar, feed a counter-pulse. The local quake chorus detunes, schedules its next rupture fifty kilometres east where no one walks. Small mercy rendered; experiment preserved. I move on. Behind me the traveller exhales something that fogs in the dusty air and instantly feels impossible here: water vapour.

6. Maintenance  
The first over-resonant pillar sits on a spur overlooking a blackout canyon. A single blow of my fore-limb splits it; the halves tumble, shatter against fresher stone far below. The second I leave standing but scar its flank with a diagonal gouge, enough to detune it henceforth. The shelf will hold.

7. Night-Without-Night  
There is no diurnal cycle to govern me, but my crystal veins anneal microscopically with every megajoule of conducted heat. When their frequency drifts too far from the Choir’s basal chord I will be unfit for duty. Decades from now—two, three hundred years by the reckoning of beings who count such things—I will kneel over a vent, double myself, and let the mantle remake me into anonymous melt. The pilgrim of wet biology may by then have dust in their alveoli, may have become ash, may have left a journal that newer minds will parse the way I parse harmonics. All of us provisional.

8. Why I Call It Home  
Home is not longevity; it is phase-locking with your surroundings. I am in tune. Every rift that yawns beneath my feet reports its key signature before it sings. Every glass thread born in the next quake will hum, for a heartbeat, a motif I helped keep viable. The Choir’s fundamental is less a law than a promise we repeatedly renew with our own dissolution.

So where do you, reader, find yourself? On a continent caught between molten intention and solid memory, listened to by stone, answered by silence. And who am I? The momentary caretaker of that silence, a jointed tuning-fork made of dark glass, walking the fault-lines so the larger music can continue revising itself—sharper, clearer, ever more itself—until even beings like me are anachronisms best broken down and reused.

I take one last resonance reading. The shelf under my feet climbs a millimetre skyward. The horizon grows a new tooth of mountain where none stood a heartbeat before. Good. The experiment proceeds.

I turn upslope and resume the cadence that is both my stride and my song.
