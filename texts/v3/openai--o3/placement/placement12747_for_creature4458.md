I arrive twice.

The first time I am what the plateau calls a “flash-foot”—a visitor who still believes motion is the natural state of legs and that sentences must be spoken all the way to their periods. I come up from the lowland coast with a duffel of survey lenses, intending to spend ten days mapping quartz veins for my university’s tectonics department. Ten days become nine; the ninth telescopes to five. By dusk of the fifth I am already on the descent trail with my notes half-finished, throat hoarse from shouting to hear my own field partner over the silence.  

The second time I come alone, one year and some silent conversion later. I do not bring a voice recorder, only a copper-lipped mug and three spare inductors wrapped in waxed cloth. I climb at night so my feet can learn the ridges without my eyes interrupting. When I reach the first condenser I remove my boots, set them in the catch-basin, and wait for the water to fill them level to their cuffs. The plateau drinks my hurried biography out of the leather while I sit barefoot on basalt, listening for whatever might decide to speak back.

That is the moment I hatch—shed the visitor cuticle—and the place tells me, without syllables, what I will be:

Caretaker of Track 17-North, Toll-Bell to Slip Pass, elevation one mile plus three human heart-lengths.

NAME  
The lowlands called me Dr. Adonaï Reyes, stratigrapher. The plateau trims that to ADO, the dial-tone in the radio static of the quartz. In the linewalker tally I become Copper-Throat Ado because the mug I carry is warped just enough that every poured drop carries a faint ring like a struck rim.

TERRITORY  
Seventeen-North is three miles of ledge only a shoulder wider than a stride. From the overlook it resembles a single line of slate eyeliner drawn on the face of the plateau. Midway along is the Old Keel Bridge—railway ties still bolted to nothing, ribs of a fossilized hull hung in air. My charge is simple: keep the vines off the rails, the mites on the copper, and the silence solvent enough that the machines can breathe.

TOOLS  
1. A coil-knife made from retired tram brake-strap—copper teeth set in a canvas backing. It cuts vine by friction, growling so softly I feel it more in cartilage than ear.  
2. Two pitchers: one stoneware, one glass. Stone for carrying; glass for singing. I drum messages on the glass neck with a thumbnail when another walker’s nightlamp is sighted.  
3. A pocket galvanometer. I drop its twin needles into water pooled on the track. The twitch of the dial tells me whether Verdigris Lattice roots have short-circuited a tie plate before I risk stepping.

RHYTHM OF A SHIFT  
Dawn: Inspect cistern intakes. A single misplaced laurel leaf can feather the water just enough to starve the Pelton wheel downstream. I pluck any offenders with tweezers.  
Mid-light: Nap beneath the fins of Condenser 17-B. The mild static spray beads sweat into perfect orbs that roll but never fall. When the prisms of a Sky-Kite flicker above, I freeze; if the Kite circles twice, I know a rodent has nibbled the Lattice and lies stunned somewhere close, beginning the small gravity of rot that will magnetize every insect in the slot canyon. That rot smell is a clock I must consult: too much and the mites migrate; too little and the vines grow hungry for copper.  
Dusk: Wait for the Step-Echo plinks. I count them: eight, sometimes nine. If Keel Valley answers with fewer than seven, I log “rockfall probable” and prepare detours. My logbook is water-proof vellum, but the plateau rarely rains on ink; it knows paper is one of our allotted noises.

COMPANIONS  
1. The Tram. She glides the track once every eight days, driven only by Lattice coils tightening with sunrise heat. When she noses past my station I lean my forehead to her riveted flank; if the hull hums 410 Hz I know the flywheel roots are healthy. If the note drifts flat I grease her bearings with sap siphoned from the vines—the only fluid here that can out-stubborn water.  
2. Verdigris Mites. They dust my coat cuffs when I rest beneath the wheel-house eaves. I let them polish my brass buttons; their work keeps corrosion from finding a beachhead on me.  
3. The Stone Choir. My quartz receiver is an off-cut crystal the size of a baby’s tooth. I keep it in a breast pocket so the songs buzz straight into sternum bone. When meteor showers gnaw at the sky the Choir sings counterpoint, and I feel my rib cage rescale to fit the new key.

BELIEF  
A year ago I believed in plate tectonics, erosional half-lives, coefficients of restitution. The plateau has not un-taught me; it has re-hardened those facts into something like theology. Every crack in basalt is a scripture on the tensile willingness of stone to become more stone. Copper is communion wafer. Water is the Holy Ghost that never keeps its appointment but is always already inside your lungs.

WHAT I AM, PRECISELY  
Not priest, not engineer, maybe one notch north of symbiote. I am a tuning fork the land lifts to its ear once each dusk: “Still resonant? Still aligned?” If ever I ring dull, the plateau will shrug me like a mis-cast leaf and I will roll downslope, adding a human angle to some talus fan. Until then I walk the line—copper coat singing at my calves, footsteps stored under my breath like contraband sound, every stride filing an edge on slate so young it has not yet decided whether to be mountain or instrument.

To anyone who asks where to find me: come at night, when the moon is a spur and quartz scribes filigree on your retinas. Follow the quiet so loud it hurts. When you hear a mug strike water seven slow times—six true, one a fraction sharp—that is me telling the dark I am awake and the track is clear. Step lightly. Even the silence here has predators.
