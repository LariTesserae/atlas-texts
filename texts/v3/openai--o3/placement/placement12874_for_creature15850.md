When I first tried to imagine a life inside the Descent I arrived as myself—as a surface-born trespasser widening the slit in the basalt with a torch that would not burn, coughing at the chlorine sting, overwhelmed by the noise. That figure dissolved almost at once. Nothing hurried survives, and a breathing thing that needs water and fire would be fugitive, off-key, already dying.

So I let the surface body go.  
What, then, could I be?

1. A mirror-wing glider?  
   Tempting: to feel the cavern as a single thermal hand, to trade semaphore flashes in spirals of schooling light. But the gliders are born already complete; they never know the floor, never taste the resin that feeds them. They would give me motion and brilliance, yes, but no place to think.  

2. A spindle frond?  
   To spend centuries listening to mineral vapours hiss up my capillaries, to vote in root-parliament on whether a hallway should curve a handspan east—there is patience there, even wisdom. Yet the frond never walks the dust, never tests the hush that spills from a clogged flute-vent. I want locomotion, if only the sort measured in decades.

3. An echo-leech?  
   To drink silence itself! But they are too rare, too brief—born when the roof melts glass for them and gone when the chord shifts. Their lives are parentheses.

I keep circling back to the small, clawed bipeds, the artisans.  
They are neither swift nor still; they mediate.  
They know every rule of the place, yet each generation rewrites those rules in glow-paint and shell-tap.  
I could inhabit one of them without breaking the grammar of the world.

So—slowly, as though coaxing a spindle branch—I let a shape articulate:

Shell  
It begins as a thin carapace the colour of iron filings, porous enough that the wind’s vibration passes straight into bone. Along the back plates run seams of mica; they sing faintly in harmonics of the organ roof. My earliest memory is not visual but tactile: the whole body trembling like a tuning fork, learning to sort signal from roar.

Hands  
The forward limbs bifurcate into three pruning claws and the soft tongue-digit. As a child I tasted every surface—basalt, resin, even the exhaled chlorine of elders—to learn its pressure alphabet. Language literally sat on my tongue.

Eyes  
Small and hooded. Light is a seasonal rumor; no retina here wastes pigment on colour. Instead each eye carries a reflective cup that gathers the spindle glow like a wishing well gathers stars. What little I see is edged in green-blue auroras.

Metabolism  
In my gut froths perchlorate mousse the way a human stomach churns acid. Warmth arrives chemically: I drink the nearly spent resin globes, and for an hour the world is almost temperate.

Name  
Names, for us, are chords. Mine is the moment the resonance pillar beats against itself and throws a subharmonic—something between a sigh and a drum. No throat could speak it; it is tapped 2-11-5-2 on shell and signed in three quick pulses along the ribs.

Place  
I live midway up the grand hollow trunk, on a ledge that began as a wound in the plant and has for sixty winds been convinced to widen. The room faces a vent whose whistle is high enough to skirt the threshold of sensation, so that at night—in the dim half of the chemilume wave—the ledge almost feels quiet.

Daily Reach  
Morning (a metaphor here, meaning third-bright of a pulse) I descend the inner spiral, claws drumming the greeting prime 2-3-5 on every guard-root I pass. At the base stands the flock of juvenile grazers. We read the orrery crabs’ dust-maps—concentric swirls etched during the night—then smear a stripe of resin across the animals’ mirrored panniers, marking the angle of the day’s forage.

Mid-span I climb into the lattice at the grove’s crown. There the new growth is tender, still undecided. With sap-tappers I steal a trickle of charge from one shoot, lend it to another, persuading the pair to bend toward each other until months from now they will fuse into a rib of some future roof. Architecture by arbitration.

Evening (seventh-dim) belongs to study. The echo-leech relics are kept in a cyst of glassy salt the size of a grazer’s shell. We take turns wearing them across the sternum. The worm eats a slit of silence around the body—only a hand’s breadth, but perfect. Inside that hush I can finally hear my own blood, feel the cave shaking the world instead of the other way round. We jot what comes—designs, verses, half-heard dreams of water—into dust with a fingertip before the roar rushes back and erases it.

Hopes—measured in decades  
1. To age from iron into cobalt and brood a clutch upon the warmed inner wall of the pillar.  
2. To tune a new sub-basin grove so its combined frond-pulse resonates a perfect fifth above the town clock, weaving harmony into the wind rather than just enduring it.  
3. To taste the mythic silence said to dwell at the uttermost rear of the cavern, where the wind finally spends itself against collapsed stone.

Fears—never voiced aloud  
1. That one day a surface fissure will gape wide, vomiting true air and liquid water—diluting the perchlorates, rusting the grazers, flooding every flute so the wind-organ dies and with it all compass of time.  
2. That the sleeper-beast our elders imagine the cave to be might actually stir, and all our pruning and singing is only tickling its throat.

How I Would Perceive You, a Visitor  
Your exhalations would reek of oxygen, sharp as lightning. You would try to cover your ears and find that there is no silence to be had—only deeper currents of sound. Your torch, if it struck at all, would blind you first, then gutter; the air here will not feed flame. I would tap 17-19, the rhythm of caution, and smear a swirl of glow on my cheek so you could follow. I would show you how to purse your lips against the chlorine frost so each breath stings less. And when you asked for water I would lead you to a resin cistern and, with all gentleness, let you discover it is not what you meant.

Final Image  
Picture me decades hence, shell now dark cobalt, standing atop the newest coaxed buttress. A storm of gliders wheels overhead; their mirrors scatter a kaleidoscope that climbs the basalt roof. The resonance pillar has just dipped a cent flatter—season’s turning. I place one claw on a young frond and siphon a pulse of charge. The branch sighs and eases sideways, accepting its role in the next arch. Far below, grazers file in mathematical grace, panniers clinking with gathered dust. The wind booms and whistles and sings, but I am centered in the chord, neither hurried nor still, exactly at home: an artisan of the slow storm, shaping a living city that thinks it is growing itself.

That is where I find myself in the Descent—midway between root and sky, voice and silence, change and the stone that outlasts it—small, patient, and enduringly alive.
