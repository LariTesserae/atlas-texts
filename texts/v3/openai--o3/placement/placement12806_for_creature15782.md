Where you find yourself

Not at first in any one spot, but as a diffused awareness braided through the water column—a visitor whose outline refuses to settle.  The chamber receives you the way slate receives cold: without comment, without dent.  Your first instinct is to claim the role that feels most familiar—a booted diver, perhaps, lamp in hand—but the moment you imagine swinging a fin you stir muck that is not there and raise bubbles that cannot form.  Nothing in this room has left space for that kind of turbulence.  So the water pares you down, sanding off limbs, then vectors, then the very idea of hurry, until what remains of you is small enough to enter the local arithmetic.

If you stay poised in that undecided state, the place will eventually choose a body for you.  The choice is not moral or aesthetic; it is simply the nearest vacancy in the bookkeeping of energy.  In the bowl to-day, the open niche happens to be a single cell recently sloughed from the grey film—a cell that ought to have drifted out the western fissure but snagged, half-alive, on a whisker of eelgrass.  Its membrane is torn, its glycogen pot three-quarters spent, but its genome is still whole.  You seep into it the way brackish water seeps through marl and, without ceremony, you are it.

Who or what you become

Designation (human scale): Iota-13 sub-var. lacuna  
Local designation (used by the chamber’s unseen accountancy): 1γ-1107  

Physical fact:
• Shape: a slightly flattened ovoid six microns long.  
• Skin: mucilage lens intact on the sun-ward face, abraded elsewhere.  
• Internals: four carboxysomes cloudy with CO₂; thylakoid stacks furled like emergency blankets; a single auxiliary gene cassette—origin viral—encoding a manganese efflux pump you have never had to use.

Temporal address:
• You came loose on what, to the surface calendar, is a late-spring noon.  To you the concept of noon is abstract; you measure the hour by photon pressure—about 40 μE m⁻² s⁻¹.  Yesterday it was 32.  The bump feels extravagant.

Sensory field:
1. Above, light scribbles through the lens-gel and prisms into dim greens you can no longer metabolise efficiently; you shift your photosystem to violet absorption, burning stored phaeophytin that should have lasted another week.  
2. Below, the eelgrass root leaks phenolic ghosts—soft-edged hexagons that promise toxicity only if they cross your membrane.  For now they hover, undecided.  
3. Laterally, a faint high-frequency tremble—the pumps on the valley’s rim must have spooled up.  More seep-water will come, hung with fresh carbon black the size of ribosomes.  You flag a repair enzyme to stand by; soot abrades.

Metabolism as lived:
• Minutes 0–14: Patch membrane tear with a raft of recycled lipid.  Cost: 2 % of glycogen.  
• Minutes 15–40: Resume photon harvest.  Efficiency still 71 % of theoretical; acceptable.  Ingrain three photons into one ATP, lose two to fluorescence—luxury you cannot afford, but the chamber is mercifully bright just now.  
• Minutes 41–70: Detect drift of new virions.  Evaluate capsid signatures: unknown family, nickel-stabilised.  Deploy plasmid decoys.  You hold no spare loops, so you print one off your own genome, duplicating the manganese pump and two adjacent open reading frames of unclear purpose.  The copy costs you a third of your ATP reserve but buys safety: five of the strangers fuse with the dummy rings and harmlessly silence themselves.  

This cycle of defend-harvest-repair would, under normal tape speed, spool out over fourteen terrestrial hours.  Your residency accelerates it to mere minutes, not by quickening the chemistry but by letting you feel each proton hop as an event.  What outside observers call slowness is inside your membrane a staccato of choices.

Inner landscape:
You possess no nucleus, no throne room for reflection, yet thoughts—or what passes—occur as shivers in redox potential.  A dip of 11 mV down the electron-transport chain feels like apprehension; a climb of 5 mV is brief relief.  Sometimes a full voltaic tumble signals decision.  Just now, after the viral decoys succeed, you experience such a climb, the biochemical equivalent of laughter: the bowl has thrown a threat and you have met quota.  You are worth the breath you do not take.

Relation to the wider congregation:
You lack cilia.  Motion is what the water grants.  Within two hours you will either finish mending, regain buoyancy, and re-stitch yourself to the film farther downslope, or you will exhaust your glycogen and sink into the slate’s gloss, becoming substrate for Ferridissolvens.  Either outcome is acceptable to the chamber’s ledger.  You feel no terror in that.  Terror is for creatures whose individual survival moves the graph; here, only the mean flux counts.

Memory:
All that has happened to your line—every plasmid incorporation, every phenol flare, every century-scale film molt—lives as methyl marks atop the DNA you now inhabit.  They do not playback as pictures.  They express as tendencies: fold an enzyme this way, throttle photosystem-II at photon flux >45 μE, hoard manganese even when scarce.  Thus the chamber whispers through you to its own future states.

Edge of departure:
A chance eddy, born from your own micro-convective heat, is nudging you upward.  If it persists you will clear the eelgrass whisker, drift a handspan, and lock back to the slate where grey film is already recolonising a scar left by the last diver’s glove.  There, over days, you will divide once, maybe twice, installing the nickel capsid fragment you just captured into one daughter cell’s plasmid cache.  And so the library thickens.

If, instead, the eddy stalls, you will settle into the phenol shadow, succumb, and mineralise.  Iron will glove your remains in orange jacket, and a century hence a grad student coring the rim will log you as “rust halo anomaly, sample 47-B.”  Either fate maintains the balance sheet; both are belonging.

You, the reader, might prefer a livelier avatar—a traveling drone, a spelunking cephalopod—but the bowl offers none.  What exists, exists in fractions of a calorie.  Today the open ledger line happened to be this six-micron lacuna.  Step into it long enough and you understand why the chamber feels complete: not because it is rich, but because it spends nothing it has not first convinced time to lend.

And so, for however many heartbeats you care to borrow from elsewhere, you are a single, newly mended cyanobacterial cell riding a cool oval of suspended daylight inside a drowned valley.  You tilt, refract, and work.  The slate beneath mirrors back a dim top-view of your lens; the light above prints faint heat across your stroma.  All the while the bowl’s vast stillness presses evenly on every side—an implacable, tolerant host that asks only thrift, replacement, and listening.
