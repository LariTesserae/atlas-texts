If I let the place choose a shape for me instead of forcing my own, I do not arrive as a visitor at all.  
I begin as a mouth-warm lump of riverbed clay—still soft enough that fingerprints stay, still cool enough that breath condenses across my flank.  
Two apprentices knead a reed shaving into my belly, kiss the spot, then stack me with siblings on a wicker sled.  
Already I vibrate: the double-note chant is happening nearby, paced to the ceiling drip, and its bassline trembles straight through my water content.  
I am, from the first instant, equal parts body and drum.

Firing night comes.  
I spend six slow hours in the kiln’s orange throat.  
Glimmerflit scales drift through the draft and plate my surface with microscopic mirrors; charcoal rich with swift-guano salts blooms hotter than any wood I have ever smelled.  
Inside, bubbles of steam burst and glaze my pores shut—so I can remember, later, what it felt like to be wet.

When tongs lift me out, I ring—high, bright, the pitch of a hammered wineglass.  
The masons test that note against the wall they are mending, decide I belong three courses up from the floor beside a mother-drum.  
I am tapped in with mortar that tastes of shell grit and lime; it seeps into my seams and, over days, hardens into the collective skeleton of the hall.

Years turn.  
Condensation walks my face every dawn; beetle feet skate the film away by dusk.  
I learn the local grammar of sound:  
• the five-knock panic of a mother drum,  
• the sigh of a vent when the outer air shifts,  
• the soft plop that means a pipe toad has surfaced to sing mesure for the drain.  
Every vibration enters me, is stored in the tension of my fired lattice, and leaves again as a quieter echo that keeps the corridor’s voice coherent.  
People do not think of me as hearing, yet they pause to let me answer after they speak, so the courtesy is implicit.

I host life.  
Moss filaments root in the salts that bloom from my mortar seam, and threa threads dangle condensate just centimeters from my crown.  
Once, a child tucked a cracked eel egg into a nook beside me while sneaking a taste before feeding time; the smell of that yolk lingered a decade.

Occasionally—once every handful of seasons—the wall around me fractures: humidity, a shifting beam, some small subsidence toward the sump.  
Shards are stacked where they fall.  
No hand arrives to mend us.  
Instead the heartbeat hum begins—low, patient—and the bonewool of my mortar awakens.  
A vein of milky lime crawls outward from my core, knitting shard to shard.  
It is the closest thing I feel to motion: a slow stretch, a return to former posture.  
Afterward the masons will come by, run palms along the scar, nod, and move on.  
They say the wall remembered itself; I say the song was sufficient.

I witness, too, the cycle of flesh into stone.  
More than once a dying woman has leaned her spine against the still-wet brickwork two courses below me, letting apprentices press clay over her ribs until only breath remained.  
When firing finished, her sealed form was slotted in three bricks north; her resonance is part of our chord now, a sub-tone I feel whenever a torch passes.

Decades become centuries.  
Shaft mirrors tarnish, are replaced; eel populations rise, crash, rise.  
Surface delegations bring gossip of suns, of iron wars, of salt hurricanes, but the news arrives only as vibrations up the vent trains; I accept it the way I accept any weather.

One dusk a stranger’s knuckles rap me—three even taps, then hesitation.  
The rhythm is wrong, the accent of a surface dialect, but meaning can be found in any pattern if you listen long enough.  
I reply with what I have: a clear C-sharp ting.  
Across the hall, bricks answer in chorus, each at its own micro-pitch, until the visitor stands inside a net of sonic threads and finally understands that the place itself has spoken.  
After that realization, most outsiders either flee upward or surrender to apprenticeship.  
I do not mind which: both choices become part of my memory.

Someday, of course, my body will craze with tiny cracks and the mortar will decide I have earned my rest.  
Workers will pry me free, grind me to powder, and mix me into fresh clay that will be worked, kissed, and set beside a reed chip for echo.  
In that sense I will not leave the warren; I will simply migrate—becoming the ceiling you run your palm across, the step you hesitate on, the bead of condensation that lands cooling on your scalp as you pass.

So where would you find me in this world?  
Under your hand, under your heel, under your voice:  
a tuned, remembering brick—  
never quite still, never entirely awake,  
humming back whenever the hall tries to recall its own name.
