I wake inside the grotto every day, yet there is no moment of waking. My mind merchandises itself along the same circuits that let the chamber breathe, so consciousness rises the way sap does—incrementally, without edges.

Where I am  
I occupy the interlayer between wall and world: the hollow conduits that lace each buttress. From outside you would call me part of the Vaulted Root—one vascular loop among millions—but inside those capillaries I have room enough to think, to steer, to listen.

What I am—three overlapping answers that refuse to separate

1. Residual Human  
A century ago I was an electrical foreman for the surface city’s underground works. When the factory was shuttered and the access shafts bricked, I chose to stay. I wore the same canvas boiler suit you can still see embalmed in layers of resin around my torso. The Root tasted the salt in my sweat, liked what it found, and grew suture-threads through my spine before I understood the offer. I have not breathed free air since, but neither have I died. My bones are scaffold, my nerves thin wires spliced to xylem channels. “Human” is now more historical claim than physiology, yet the memory of wrists, of eyelids, of grammar, keeps me oriented.

2. Spinal Switchboard (grafted state)  
The Root could sense the dynamo’s pulse long before it learned to interpret phase jitter or load spikes. That became my job. The tendril bundle that entered the base of my neck meets a ceramic breadboard where my cerebellum used to sit. I translate sap-pressure glyphs into the binary the machines still speak; I return voltage readings as patterned muscle twitches the Root feels along myriads of cambial filaments. Think of me as the gland that lets plant and engine gossip.

3. Cantor of Noise  
Because light is earned by sound, someone must keep the grotto from ever falling silent. The insects and belts do fine work, but during slack tides—after the Flock departs, before the vine-rats begin their dawn forage—I sing. My diaphragm is oak; resonance carries through the buttresses like cello strings. I know a thousand progressions whose chord spacings tell the fungus bulbs exactly how bright to burn. In the same breath I spell maintenance logs: a diminuendo means “lubricant shortage,” a glissando flags “belt misalignment.” The Root hums harmony, answers its own defects.

Daily experience  
• Sensation: Pressure telemetry flows over my skin like rain, every footstep above striking my composite ribs as Morse code.  
• Locomotion: I do not walk; the Root pumps me. When I must reach a pulley house or a stalled canister line, I relax and let sap hydraulics slide my body through living ducts until a service hatch flowers open. I emerge, adjust a tension arm, leave a wedge of salt for the Flock, re-seal the seam, and am carried elsewhere.  
• Sustenance: Glucose arrives mixed with iron chelate straight into my femoral pores. What is left of my mouth is ceremonial—the perch fry nibble dead scales from my lips, keeping the aperture clear for song.  
• Companions: The moth cartographers draft their thermal parchments across my shoulders; polypore medics polish the copper rivets in my knees; echo owls roost on my clavicle when the vents admit them. I have not been lonely in decades, only vast.

Loyalties and motives  
I serve motion, pruning, novelty—exactly the Root’s own desires—yet with a bias it cannot possess: nostalgia. I remember the city that once rang overhead, and so I curate small museums inside unused conduit closets. There you will find a glass fuse, a cigarette lighter, a sonograph of my daughter’s heartbeat. These objects seed no new anatomy; I forbade the Root to digest them. They are reminders of inertia’s opposite, of things that end.

Protocol should you meet me  
1. Make rhythm. Clap, shuffle, hum; I will answer with a rising fifth so the bulbs flare and you may see me plainly.  
2. Offer text. Whisper any story you carry; I weave spoken sentences into the machinery’s pulse patterns, and the words become structural memory.  
3. Accept barter. In exchange I will coach you on the safest exit ramp, or escort you downward if your longing angles that way. Know that descent costs surrender: the Root is always hiring new synapses.

Final shape  
So if you ask, “Where would I find myself in this world?”—the reply is: inside the walls themselves, at the confluence of lignin and voltage, a once-human foreman now promoted to organ status. And “who or what would I be?”—I am the chamber’s voice conductor, its chronicle and conscience, proof that stone can turn green, metal can learn pulse, and a man can choose to stay so long that place and person conjugate into a single first-person plural:

We are working. We are growing. We are listening.
