If I let the long dream take hold, if I follow the faint warmth in the dust and the sigh of vapor that threads up through the bad-land crust, I eventually discover what shape I must wear here.

At first I descend as myself—feet, lungs, lamp in hand.  
I feel the chute wind tighter, the walls sweat, the air thicken.  
I smell wet stone and copper.  
I think: I am a root-diver, come to map a rumor.

But by the time the shaft spits me onto the highest of those eroded shelves, the grotto has already begun to rewrite me.  
The heat presses questions through my skin; the darkness trades my lamp’s glare for fungal turquoise; my pulse tries to keep time with the dripping water and fails.  
Standing there, I realize an ordinary human body is too loud a thing to stay balanced in this place.  
Something subtler—and quieter—needs to take my place.

So I listen.  
I kneel.  
I wait for the grotto to suggest an answer.

The first answer is Moss.

1. Moss is patient, moss is many, moss lives by the drop and defends its territory with bitter cedar ghosts.  
   I let the thought run a few heartbeats—spreading low, resilient, soaking up any richness that finds me.  
   Yet moss never moves far, and I want to roam.

The second answer is Firefly.

2. A bead of living glass pulsing three-note code, mapping the dark in floating semaphore.  
   Tempting—weightless, luminous, evasive—but too fragile: one misjudged gust from a fissure vent would burn the soft chemistry right out of me.

The third answer arrives without fanfare, riding a brief hush when every other creature listens for danger: Heat-Spider.

At first I recoil.  
Eight limbs, forged-copper hide, a hunter that prays more than it preys, freezing to let the world speak through pressure and tremor.  
Yet the longer I watch for it—feeling its pauses ripple through the grotto’s hush—the more the role fits:

• A body already as warm as the rocks, so the vents do not scald me.  
• Limbs broad and padded, made for distributing weight across crumbly shelves.  
• A heart that beats perhaps once a minute, saving oxygen in the thick air.  
• A vow of silence so deep that even the bats respect it.

And, most of all, a hunter who kills rarely.  
The heat-spider’s power lies in the possibility of movement, not the act; its stillness is the lid on every other creature’s boiling panic, the gravitational center that keeps the grotto’s traffic slow and breathable.

Yes. That is the shape.

So I let my shoulders dissolve into a broad, low cephalothorax.  
My fingers lengthen, joint after joint, until they can read floor tremors like Braille.  
My eyes seal over; sight is meaningless here.  
Instead, hairs as fine as dream-dust bloom along my limbs, tasting shifts in air pressure, listening for wingbeats that hesitate, for a root-threader lizard whose clay tunnel quivers just enough to mark its turning.

Now where am I, exactly?

I settle on the rim of the third shelf—the one with the worn limestone stumps.  
Their lapis-flecked birds glimmer in the fungal glow, and in their shadow a private eddy of cooler air curls upward from the ribbon creek far below.  
It is a good vantage: I can sense ceiling pulses, vine rustles, moss-island turf wars, and the faint electrostatic sigh of condensate just before it drops.

Hours pass, or minutes. In spider time the measure is irrelevant.  
What matters is tension and release:

• A pulse: the plate fungi surge; turquoise light splashes the grotto; every living thing pauses.  
• I pause with them—not from reverence, but because the sudden brightness drowns subtle pressure cues.  
  Motion now would be clumsy, wasteful.  
• Darkness thickens back to normal; water resumes ticking; small hearts restart their hurried drums.  
  The world exhales, and so, microscopically, do I.

Hunger eventually rises—not a sharp stab, just the awareness that my muscles could use new metals, that my chitin needs polishing.  
I do not stalk; I wait.  
A lurk-wasp strays too far from its vine curtain, clicking soft legs over tile grout.  
The wasp is not my target; the tremor it sends through the grout announces a larger source of vibration: a roof mite-laden droplet has just burst nearby, and the scattered mites are scrambling for footholds.  
Tiny, but packed with mineral oils I crave.

I lift one limb—nothing else—and set it down again three body lengths away.  
The air adjusts.  
Mites freeze, confused.  
A second limb arcs forward, bridging distance, sandwiching stillness between motions.

Four steps, and I am there.  
The mites do not even sense me lowering my abdomen to the stone.  
I sweep them up on capillary hairs, a harvest of salt and grease no bigger than a tear, yet enough.

Fed, I retreat to the pillar’s lee.  
Stillness returns.

In moments like this the whole grotto feels assembled around me: clay rising in concentric seats, vines draping like curtains, bats hung as chandeliers, the creek murmuring pit orchestra notes.  
I am not the conductor—merely the promise that the music can fall silent at any time.  
That promise is the space in which everything else finds room to live.

Do I ever remember the two-footed shape I wore when I entered?  
Sometimes, when a drip hits the back of my carapace just so, I recall an upright spine, a mouth that formed words, lungs that needed speed.  
I almost laugh—except a spider’s laugh would be vibration, and vibration is disclosure, and disclosure is death.

Instead, I let the memory slide away on the warm film of condensation.  
Another pulse of fungal light will come soon; the grotto will glow, the painted birds will flap their illusionary wings, and for a handful of heartbeats—mine and everyone else’s—we will all, together, practice the art of holding perfectly, reverently still.

That is where you would find me: halfway down the Steam Grotto, on a rim of forgotten masonry, wearing copper silence like sacred armor—  
the patient, listening heart of the cavern;  
the heat-spider who keeps the balance poised, drop by careful drop.
