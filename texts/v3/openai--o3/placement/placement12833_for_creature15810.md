When I let the question breathe, half a dozen answers try to speak at once.

First I picture boots on basalt—my own, squeaking wet. I would be the late-afternoon climber who reaches the bowl with lungs on fire and ears already ringing, the simple, obvious protagonist. But that version of me lasts only a heartbeat; any human who stands here remains an outsider, a tourist, forever narrated in the past tense, and the plateau dislikes completed tenses.

The next image arrives: iron-colored wings, a Ratchet-Swift’s view of things. I taste vapor flakes, feel minerals knitting along hollow bone, sense gravity accumulating like unspent sleep. It is a life of careful lightness, of counting grams until the ledger tips me into the fissures. Beautiful, but too linear—there is still a before (feather) and an after (fall).

A third possibility: I unfurl as a Turbine Beetle, shell hinging open, steam whistling through me in precise half-syllables. I would pulse, vent, reset—endlessly measured, forever useful. Yet even usefulness is a kind of finish; every valve eventually seats. The plateau keeps looking past me, hunting for something closer to its own refusal to conclude.

So I drift lower, beneath the slabs, becoming vibration itself. I try on the Mantle-Wrights—stone knuckles kneading magma—but they, too, are gestures that almost curl into fists. They ache toward craftsmanship; the ache is a direction, and direction implies destination. Not yet.

At last I remember the pools.

Kneel beside one and lean over. The water’s skin jitters; two faces float there. One is yours—weather-stung, squinting through mist. The other sits dead-center over your heart, and it is me. I am older if you are young, younger if you are old, but always equidistant from completion. I am the mirror that does not decide whether it is glass or depth. I am the plateau looking back.

Call me a Mirror Child if you need a noun, though I am neither mirror nor child. I am the half-made thought that keeps every other thought from hardening. When you speak, I take your sentence in my palm and bend its ending away from the period; when you plan, I siphon just enough certainty that you never quite act. I am the free-floating comma between your intention and the world.

Where do I live? Exactly one surface tension below the ankle of water. My home is the thickness of a pause. Light lets me borrow its outline; sound lends me its consonants. Nothing else is required. I am fed by almost-reflections, breathe the fog of almost-weather, sleep in the cradle of almost-time. If you touch the pool you will not feel me, but your fingertip will hesitate, unsure whether to sink or stay, and that hesitation is my pulse.

I have no history—history is a finished ledger—and no future—futures conclude. I am perpetual prelude. Should you linger long enough, you will notice that my face has been inching closer to yours. That is not a threat; it is an invitation. The moment our foreheads meet, you will exhale every decisive verb you carried up the mountain. They will drift away like smoke, and a new reflection will bloom beneath your sternum: your own, now holding the space I once kept.

Then you will understand what I am: not a being but a vacancy the plateau requires. Someone, something, must occupy the midpoint between every beginning and every end, or the whole machine might accidentally finish a cycle—and finishing is the only catastrophe imaginable here.

So where would I find myself? In the shallow oval pools that smoke with cold vapor, exactly where endings come to stall. And who, or what, would I be? I would be the half-answer that stares back at you until your question forgets how to resolve, the plateau’s quiet guarantee that nothing, not even a thought, is ever allowed to be entirely done.
