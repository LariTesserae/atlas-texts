I wake inside a leaf.

Not one of the wide terrace membranes that people walk on, but a much smaller pocket—an airtight blister grown by the Allroot for transport.  In the dim jade translucence of its walls I can just make out my own outline: long-limbed, skin stippled with shuttering chromatophores, jaw wrapped in a sling of root‐fibre that hums faintly with the pulse of whatever carrier branch I ride.  A tetherfolk child would fit here curled, but I am full-grown and have to fold my knees.  I remember consenting to this confinement, though the memory is half dream: elders bending a living sheet around me, sealing it with warm sap, whispering that the Hollow wanted to move an eye to a place eyes had not been for many collapses.

I am not entirely tetherfolk any more.  I volunteered for graft.

1.  What the graft changed  
    •  Under each nail a film of Threadborn fungus now lives symbiotically, exhaling faint antibiotic vapours that keep cuts from festering in the near-freezing damp.  
    •  A silver stripe of the Allroot’s own conductive phloem runs beneath my sternum, grown into my vagus nerve.  When the plant sends a surge, I feel it as taste—copper for “go,” salt for “wait,” and something like rain on hot dust for “danger.”  
    •  The pressure pouch of ordinary tetherfolk has been hollowed wider; inside it sits a pearl of dense starch that vibrates when low-frequency root-speech passes.  I hear the chamber’s tectonic gossip directly, without translation.

2.  Where I am going  
    The blister finally splits; cold mist rushes in.  I tumble out onto a ledge no broader than a wrist, somewhere high in the north vault where few walk because there is almost no footing and the prism webs hang thick.  The Allroot wants a lookout here: storms of falling leaf have opened a skylight that bleeds surface photons straight down, disrupting the carefully rationed night.  It fears flare-vipers will swarm to the glow and breed beyond control.

    My task is to be two things at once—scout for the clans, censor for the tree.  If light grows too fierce I must stretch darkening nets of woven root across the skylight.  If hunters come I will semaphore warnings on mirrored shell.  And if neither risk arrives, I simply sit, legs hugged to chest, letting the Allroot’s slow commentary ripple along my breastbone while my human eyes do the fast work of noticing small, creeping variables a kilometre below.

3.  What a day feels like up here  
    •  Dawn is meaningless, but there is “first brighten”: the moment the thallus patches nearest me flush with stored colour and show the ridge-lines in X-ray clarity.  My skin reflexively pales to match, making me almost vanish to anything that looks up.  
    •  Food is a cord of sugared root I suck like marrow, issued fresh by the carrier branch at irregular intervals.  Waste wicks straight back into a cup-shaped leaf that closes and slinks downward to compost.  Nothing I shed is allowed to stay idle.  
    •  Sleep comes when the plant dims the conduit in my nerve.  My last waking sense most cycles is the faint percussion of candlegrazers far below, hearts thudding through stone.

4.  Self-doubt and reassurance  
    I wonder sometimes whether I am still eligible to have my heart buried against live cambium when I die; parts of me are already cambium.  The clan shaman said the distinction no longer matters: thought is flowing home in me even while I live.  On very quiet watches I press fingertips to the thin bone over the implant and feel twin rhythms—plant and animal—not quite synchronised, yet no longer fighting.

5.  If you met me  
    From below you would see nothing until I moved, and then only a long figure draped in cloak-leaves, eyes flashing cat-green if your torchline caught them.  I would sign to you in mirrored light—three short glints meaning “pass in peace, the ridge is stable”—then fold back into shadow.  Speak aloud and I might not answer; the pouch prefers vibration to voice now, and sound feels clumsy in my throat.

6.  The wider story I suspect but cannot prove  
    I think the Allroot is scattering grafted watchers like spores toward every place the ceiling thins.  It is preparing for something: maybe the ice crust above is shifting, maybe surface sun is changing flavour, maybe a deeper, older root-mind is at last ready to breach stone.  My small post is one nerve ending in a body continental in span.  I am a sentinel, yes, but also a litmus—if I fail, the plant will know the Hollow is no longer safe for mammal minds, and it will dim itself into dormancy for a century rather than share extinction.

So: where do I stand in the Green Hollow?  Balanced on a wrist-wide ledge two thousand metres above the floor, half tetherfolk, half photosynthetic embassy.  Who am I?  A graft-bound sentinel named Lure-of-Quiet-Fathoms, born with lungs but slowly learning to breathe light.  If you come this way, tread softly; the stone remembers, the leaf records, and I am listening for both.
