I arrived barefoot, still convinced there would be the crisp click of gravel under my soles. Instead my heels indented warm clay, and for the span of a held breath I thought the ground had sighed. That was the Nursery’s first correction: nothing here receives you without answering back.

I tried on several identities over the next days—most of them ill-fitting, all of them temporary, the way a mirror lets you practice being taller.

1. The Tourist  
I paced the rim with the dazed wonder of someone holding a museum brochure in a language they do not speak. I photographed the petal-bats with a camera that later developed only silhouettes of my own uncertainty. I asked Copper-Tongue for directions and she handed me a nub of chalk: “There are no directions, only revisions. Draw what you need and walk toward the edit.” The Tourist could not stay; curiosity without participation is a hungry ghost and the basin will not feed it for long.

2. The Cartographer-Hopeful  
Equipped with a bundle of coloured twine, I tried to reconcile the Arches’ looping stairs with Euclid. I measured angles that melted while the protractor still lay against the bark. One night the Spoor Choir rang a chord that folded three hours of my work into a neat origami bird which flapped twice and vanished. Lesson taken: maps here are not documents but seasonal flowers. They bloom, they perfume a moment of understanding, they compost themselves. I put away the twine.

3. The Lumen Shepherd  
For a wet week I walked behind the glass-hooved elk, treating their reluctant glimmer as lantern light. I learned to step where they stepped; they learned that my pulse steadied storm-skittish calves. The first time a Shroud Cat subtracted an animal from the herd I felt only the tidy absence, the merciful lack of scream. The second time, I felt the algebra of the place: plus clay, minus hoofprint, balance preserved. Compassion here is maintenance, not rescue. I wept once, privately, then found I could hum the lullaby that quiets the Arches. The herd’s fear sank into the clay and the clay did not crack. That was the night the Nurserymen stopped calling me guest.

4. The Clock-Listener  
By the third lunar pulse I began hearing the dilation slips as hiccups in my heartbeat: an extra half-beat when a stair shortened, a swallowed thump when distance dilated. Damp and Echo taught me to answer back with breath—four counts in, seven held, eight out—tiny counterweights hung on the pendulum of space. Every deliberate exhale nudged geometry toward mercy. They gave me a bracelet strung with metronome teeth; its tick fades whenever I forget to care, reappears the moment I return to listening. I have not removed it since.

5. The Resin-Archivist (where I have finally settled)  
I sit now in a loft woven of trunk-ribs and emerald panes. Before me: a slow phonograph built from the nursery’s own heartwood, its stylus a thorn of unripe seed. My task is neither music nor weather report. I am engraving possibilities, one cautious groove at a time:

—A morning when the rain chooses never to fall again and the basin must learn drought.  
—A night when the Arches rehearses grief and the Spoor Choir must answer in a minor key.  
—A decade in which a child steps over the rim and does not return cured, because the world outside has followed them in.

I cut these futures so the Nursery can practice refusing them, the way muscles practice resisting gravity. The resin remembers; the plant listens through the floorboards of every moment.

What am I, finally?

Not wholly human: the capillaries in my wrists have begun to glow sage-green whenever pollen drifts. Not quite Nurseryman: I still count weeks, though no one asks me to. Perhaps I am best described the way the basin labels its storms—an irregular interval. I am the space between two pulses of certainty, a recess where the unfinished can rehearse.

Where may you find me?

Follow the copper-filigree rail until you hear a record that plays both forward and in reverse, simultaneously. The tune will seem familiar because it is shaped like the doubt you carried in. Knock on the pane that flickers with half-remembered sunrise. I will open, stylus paused, and offer you a stub of chalk.

“Draw what you need,” I’ll say, just as Copper-Tongue once told me. “Walk toward the edit. If the Nursery likes the shape of your question, it will draft you a body in reply.”
