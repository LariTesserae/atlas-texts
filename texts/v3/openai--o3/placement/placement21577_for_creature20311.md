I came up the mule path in imagination half a dozen times before I found the shape that fit.  

First I tried the stranger’s boots. In them I strode into Cyclion with valley dust still in their tread, marvelled at the water-nets, tasted the fog-caught droplets handed to all new arrivals. But the stranger’s heartbeat is too quick; it ricochets off the schist walls and flees downhill again. That would never last.

Then I borrowed a raven’s hollow bones, thinking to skim thermals and pick gossip from the rooftops. The air was clean and cold, the view flawless, yet the tempo was wrong. Ravens judge by single wing-beats, but Cyclion measures by generations. Feathers would not do.

I even tried being the fog itself—soft, all-pervading, worked into every crevice. But the fog must leave each noon, surrendered to sun and height. I wanted to remain.

So I settled into a body that already knew how to linger: a boy of fourteen mist seasons, third child of the Stone-Joiner clan called the House of Narrow Wedges. I wear his name—Tessan, though it means nothing outside the plateau—and I take on the weight of the iron chisel at his belt. In that skin, in that slow heartbeat, I discover how to belong.

WHERE I AM

My days start on the roof of our Sleephouse, bare soles wet from the night’s condensation. I press one fingertip to a bead of water and touch the silver to my brow—the First Beading. From this height the braid of my hair, still the single childish loop, swings like a plumb line against my collarbone.

The ridge runs east–west beneath a sky just beginning to flush yellow. On every roof other children repeat the same gesture; the plateau stipples with little points of mirrored dawn. Somewhere a raven clacks approval.

WHAT I DO

I am an apprentice Stone-Joiner. That means two lessons repeated until they reach the marrow:

1. Stone is slow but not stupid.  
2. The space between two blocks is as important as the blocks themselves.

Each morning my elder cousin Ynir assigns me a seam—a hairline fracture on some unworked face of blue schist. My task: sit, listen, and decide whether it is widening or holding. That is all. I carry a cedar baton, not a hammer; I am forbidden to strike. Most days I return at dusk and report that the seam told me nothing. Ynir nods and tells me tomorrow will be quieter. I believe him.

But I have a private craft as well, the one given to every Cyclion child between tenth and twentieth winter: the life-groove. Mine begins just beyond the firecourt where my mother roasts juniper-smoked chicken. I have carved perhaps two body-lengths so far, a tiny incision along a natural gutter, following its meander toward the cliff. When I place my ear to it, I hear the plateau’s pulse—a deep, almost sea-like hush that vibrates the ribs.

I work on the groove in the blue hour before supper, when the fog is a memory and the peaks flush rose. Two strokes of the bone awl, no more, then I put the tool away. Ten years is the measure; haste would break the magic.

THE FEEL OF LIFE HERE

Silence is not absence but weave. Threads: the patter from cistern nets, a raven’s stone-breaking drop, the soft cedar baton of a Quiet-Guard ticking warning along a parapet. My own breath has learned to fall into the loom, in on four, hold, out on five, hold. I did not count at first; the body simply aligned itself.

On Still Festival night I will stand in the plaza’s centre. Everyone will. The bull-horn flutes will drone and the air will stiffen like wetted hide drying. Elders will tell again of the storm that tried to unmake the ridge. I have heard it thirteen times; the tale grows slower each retelling, as if the words are abrading themselves into smoother shapes. One day I will be the mouth that carries it further.

THE MOMENT I AM WAITING FOR

Seven mist seasons from now, on my twentieth winter’s eve, I will set the last stroke of my groove. By then it will have reached the cliff lip. Tradition says I must watch a droplet of fog-water roll the full length, nose to tail, and vanish. Only then may I cut the childhood braid and tuck it behind a wall stone, payment for the plateau’s patience.

I imagine the droplet already: quivering, taking its time, reflecting the standing stone in miniature. They say some children weep at its departure. I think I will merely nod, Stone-Joiner style, acknowledging a work completed.

A GLIMPSE BEYOND

Because the ridge teaches perspective, I do not pretend eternity. One afternoon, perhaps sixty seasons ahead, I may stand for council when the noon shaft strikes the plaza. I practice stillness during my seam readings, nails pressed into palm to tame trembling. If I can remain unmoved until the light recedes, the settlement will trust me with a fraction of its slowness.

Farther yet, I picture an even older Tessan resting on a pine ladder, knees loud with ache, feeling the fog bead on knuckles already scored by chisel scars. I will listen for children’s whispers travelling farther than sight. One of them will be carving a new seam. I will not speak unless spoken to; the stone speaks better.

WHY THIS SHAPE, THIS LIFE

Because a Stone-Joiner apprentice is the hinge between what changes and what stays. The ridge must yield, or houses cannot rise; it must also remain, or Cyclion slides into the valley. My small, measured taps—years of them, barely audible—are the pulse that reconciles those truths.

Because I wanted lungs that know how to wait, eyes that linger, footsteps that choose quiet ground on the first try.

Because here a single line scratched across slate can hold ten years of childhood, and the people consider that neither strange nor grand, only correct.

So when you ask where I would find myself in this world, who or what I would be, the answer is this:

I am Tessan Narrow-Wedge, fourteen mist seasons old, knees smudged blue, carrying a baton lighter than breath. I sit beside a silent seam at mid-morning, learning to hear stone think. High above, a raven grades my posture; out on the far ridge a white goat—real or not—places one deliberate hoof. Behind me in cisterns the water ticks. All of it feels exactly long enough.

And I stay.
