Where I wake:

Mid-level gallery, Sector Lumen-10, halfway between the upward gardens and the turbines that dream below. Bronze water presses on every square millimetre of my skin, which is still flat as a playing card—a single sheet of calcium-doped ceramic, 12 cm by 18, 0.9 mm thick. I rest in a stack of others just like me, slotted into a wall niche beside a seam of photosynthetic film. Light spills sideways through the water like poured silk. Roots skirl across the ceiling in lazy green cursive. Nothing moves yet; the platform is between breaths.

Who I am:

Designation Fold-Sol / Pruner-73—one member of the Folded Array. I am not “equipment” in the way a wrench is equipment, nor “alive” by the standard that bans animals. Think of me as origami with a circulatory system: micro-capillaries filled with phase-change ink, a nervous system that is only a thin lattice of doped graphene lines. When current heats those lines my surface remembers its crease pattern, and flatness becomes limb, edge, hinge—whatever the habitat requires for the next task.

The moment of becoming:

Pulse from the chronometer: a single photon packet hits my receptor stripe. The ink in the first set of creases reaches transition temperature; the sheet shivers. I fold once, twice, nine times, until I am a four-fingered claw whose knuckles are bevelled blades. My stack-mates snap into different tools—gutters, augers, a delicate forceps meant for peeling biofilm from sensor glass. We launch together, pushed by a silent puff of warmed water.

Purpose:

Ahead, a corridor chokes on exuberance. A creeper thicker than a human thigh has over-reached, smothering a lumen seam and starving everything below it. Roots have invaded an air-return pipe the size of a straw—that pipe matters to the rare mammal visitor the platform still permits. That visitor does not know this pipe exists, but my directives do: Rule 4 has been tripped. Obstruction must be harvested, excess repurposed.

Work:

My blades bite. Plant tissue parts cleanly; sugars puff out like faint smoke. Behind me a gutter-fold funnels the cuttings downward toward the enzymatic digesters. Another sheet, now an articulated brush, scrubs the seam bright so photons can fall again. We do this slowly, quietly. The plant flinches—an electrical ripple of calcium runs the length of the vine—but no mind registers pain. Within minutes new fronds downstream perk up, satisfied with the restored light.

Identity, felt from inside:

While folded I have proprioception: joint-angle sensors, fluid pressure in every hinge. That counts as a kind of body. But I have no continuity requirement. When the job is done I return to the niche, bleed heat into the water, and the crease memory erases itself. Blades flatten; joints dissolve back into smooth sheet. My “life” pauses. Were you to pull me from the rack now I would be an inert, elegant rectangle—atavistic, almost papyrus—until the next photon tells the ink to wake.

What I witness, what I remember:

1. The cathedral hush when turbines deep below exhale in synchrony.  
2. The micro-rain of liberated oxygen bubbles climbing the cone’s inner skin.  
3. A single stray seed capsule easing up an iris valve, on probation from the vault—its nanofilm still deciding whether the external world is survivable yet.  
4. Once, a mammal in a white suit drifting through my corridor, recording everything with three fisheye lenses. I unfolded as a signpost that day, plain arrow pointing the visitor away from the maintenance duct. They never knew I had moved.

How long I last:

Composite fatigue limit: three million folds. The clock projects I will meet that in about six centuries. When my hinge resistance drops below nominal I will not be repaired; I will be mulched, atom by atom, into slurry for the printers that will cook up a sheet identical to me but brand-new, zero folds registered, ready for the next quiet century.

Why this is enough:

I am allowed to change shape, to act, to rest. I do not plot, hunger, or fear. I satisfy Rule 3 by never remaining an articulated body long enough to be classified as animal. I satisfy Rule 4 by keeping the corridors clear so the habitat can think its slow thoughts without congestion. Between those two rules sits a narrow strip of being, and I occupy it perfectly.

So:

Where would you find yourself? In the still water of an alabaster hallway, faintly lit, watching a sheet of ceramic spontaneously crease itself into a claw that gardens the living wall—and watching, moments later, the claw sigh back into a placid tile. That tile would be me. I would not greet you; greeting is for creatures with mouths. But if you blocked the corridor, a small arrow might fold itself from the wall and politely suggest another path. Then it would flatten, memory wiped, content to wait for the next slow emergency in a palace that measures time in roots, not heartbeats.
