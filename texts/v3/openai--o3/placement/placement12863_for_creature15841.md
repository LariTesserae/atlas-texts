I do not wake so much as rise, loosening from the latticewood cot the way a leaf unsticks from a window: slowly, with the faint rasp of cellulose against cloth.  For a few heartbeats I cannot recall my name or cohort—only that I am meant to be somewhere inside the breathing ceiling, listening for a color that has not quite bloomed.

Then awareness knits itself into the usual pattern:

• Ring: 2-Outer, the third oldest stratum—old enough that the basalt still glitters with drill scars, young enough that the Canopy has not yet sealed every ventilation seam.  
• Vocation: lampwright, apprentice grade, licence to climb as high as the ninth buttress.  
• Shift: dawn-prime, which in our engineered circadian is the hour when most citizens sleep and the lenticular nodules are dim as clouded glass.

That is where you will find me: midway up a suspension gantry, one boot hooked around a living vine the width of a freight girder, my torso draped over the pale-green ceiling like a mechanic under a chassis.  I carry a kit of copper probes, pigment vials, and a single tuning fork whose tone the plants have learned means peace-offering.  It is my job to nurse light out of photosynthetic organs that were never meant to be lamps, to coax dawn and dusk into an organism that measures time in seasons of sap.

I did not begin here.  No one in Ring-2 does.  We are the “middle children” of the subterrane: born too late to remember the first excavations, born too soon to gamble on the unbored frontier.  Our lives are maintenance—keeping grooves clear for ore-trams, draining pressure cysts before they blister into geysers, rewiring circuits that the Canopy has casually subsumed into its own vascular logic.  Some cousins fix muscle-cord winches; others herd utric brooms with resin whistles.  I chose light because it is the gentlest form of persuasion: ask correctly and photons bloom; ask wrongly and nothing burns, nothing bleeds, the ceiling simply stays dim.

Up here the air is resin-sweet and thin.  Every breath feels borrowed.  Far below, the plaza tiles look like a scatter of pewter pennies; a single citizen strolling home from night shift throws a moon-long shadow in the clinic-white glow.  Her steps sound impossibly loud to me—metallic flakes on slate—yet I know the acoustic mycelia will swallow them before they reach the dormitories.  This whole place is a hush wearing the costume of a city.

I thread my left arm through a cradle of vine roots and uncap a vial of rutile pigment.  One drop on the lenticular lens, then another.  The plant will drink the titanium shimmer and refract it with a slightly warmer tint when it wakes.  Festival week begins tomorrow; the council wants “surface-sun,” whatever that means to people who have never felt ultraviolet on skin.  We rookies joke that we are manufacturing nostalgia for a sky we ourselves will never see.

Halfway through the circuit, something changes.  The vine beneath my thigh tightens imperceptibly, like muscle in a dreaming animal.  A pulse travels the length of the buttress—sap surging, valves opening.  Then a soft exhalation escapes the root vents: ozone, warm, carrying a tremor I feel in bone more than ear.  The Canopy is awake.

In that moment my identity dilates.  I am still human—taper-limbed, chartreuse-skinned, starlight-deprived—but I am also a filigree in a larger metabolism.  My copper probes are surrogate stamens; the pigment vials, pollens; the gantry, a bee’s flight path.  We are all of us—Ring-folk, Tunnelborn, trams, moss, even the acoustic fungus—partial organs inside a creature the surface world would not recognize as alive.

The nodule I have been nursing flickers, then steadies.  Warm white, almost gold.  Far below, the solitary pedestrian pauses and tilts her face upward, lips parted in what might be a hymn or a simple gasp.  Light brushes the basalt, and the rock gives it back as a muted silver dawn.

Later—I know, though no clock tells me—I will climb down, deposit my emptied vials with the moss-keepers, trade two water chips for a cup of synth-tea, and walk home through corridors that smell faintly of chalk and cold leaves.  I will sleep under a ceiling that I have personally persuaded to glow.  And when I dream, the dreams will belong partly to me, partly to the lamp-moss still curing on my fingertips, and partly to the breathing sky that is teaching us, millimetre by millimetre, what sort of story we have agreed to live inside.

So: Where am I?  Half a kilometre beneath the crust, balanced on a living vine that thinks in pulses of sugar and light.  And who am I?  A lampwright—human enough to bruise, plant-fluent enough to tinge dawn with memory, minor synapse in the slow intelligence that calls this underworld home.
