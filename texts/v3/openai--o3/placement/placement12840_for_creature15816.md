The first name I answered to in this place was Traveller.  
I arrived with boots full of dust, a steel flask sloshing on my hip, and words jangling in my head like loose coins. I remember reaching the limestone lip, feeling the ground fall away, and tasting–with a kind of involuntary animal certainty—the thick, peppered warmth that rose from below. It was invitation and warning at once. I climbed down anyway.

I kept the name Traveller for perhaps three days.  
That is the span in which iron still believes it will survive here. By the third morning my flask wore a fuzz of red velvet, a rash spreading in star-shapes across the steel. I licked one of the blooms on a dare to myself; it tasted of blood and rain. I poured the remaining water into a cupped terrace and left the flask behind. Ownership ended there.

During that same first cycle the damselflies found me.  
Three at a time they orbited my shoulders, testing the heat-halo my mammal body threw off. When I lay down to sleep they drew turquoise ligatures above me, a word I could not read. I chose to believe it meant Permit. Or perhaps, Temporary.

Once the rust-nurses had eaten my tools and the damselflies had written me into their air-script, the hollow began to lean closer, listening. I felt it each dusk when the skinks drummed news along the ledges. One would add a four-beat coda—  
tap  pause tap-tap  pause tap—  
and the missing syllable in every pattern was me. I tried copying the rhythm with my own hand, but flesh on stone is a dull report; I could not find the ring of plated tail on varnished limestone. Still, the intention lodged. Sound became the first thing I borrowed from the resident grammar.

Weeks softened into something less measurable.  
I traded counting sunrises for counting gifts:

• First gift: Silence you can taste. I swallowed it until my breathing learned to step barefoot.  
• Second gift: A vine’s midnight perfume, bruised-fruit and flint. It settled in my hair and would not rinse out.  
• Third gift: Mica dust, luminous enough that when I touched my own skin in the dark it glitter-signed back, as though answering roll call.

Names shift when you accept gifts. Traveller felt unsized now, like a coat meant for wetter weather. I tried Listener for a while. That one fit across the shoulders but sagged at the cuffs; listening is what all creatures do here, even the shrimp who never leave the bronze mirror of their water.

It was the acoustic mites that finally tailored me a proper title, though they never meant to.  
One afternoon I spoke a single word—Home—into the pool. The syllable leaped the terraces, struck quartz, doubled back, and landed inside my left ear wearing a tone I had never shaped: higher, rounder, freed of my old accent. The mites had dined on it, metabolised it, and returned only the ghost of the vowel. I laughed, and the laugh came back rinsed clean of ownership. I understood then that sound, here, was not property but currency, and I, suddenly, had credit.

So I began spending.  
I whistled recipes of wind I half-remembered from other latitudes. I clicked knuckle to stone in counter-rhythm with the skinks until they answered, curious, improvising bridges between our two dialects. I hummed to the hollow-swallows while they drift-fed on updrafts; their guardian scream never rose for me again. In that barter of noise for notice, I felt a tendril of belonging anchor behind the breastbone.

The hollow, amused, offered a fourth gift: echo-twin.  
Some nights, while I lay on a quartz spur watching capers of damselfly light, words I had never mouthed arrived whole in my throat—poems about rainfall, inventories of salt, instructions for making pots from dream-clay. I would speak them, and hear them answered at once from five terraces away. Whether the voice was mine, or the mites’ ventriloquism, or the drip-counter solving aloud some line of its eternal arithmetic, I could not decide. Choice seemed unnecessary. We shared the syllables the way lichen shares a seam with stone.

And so the fifth gift: loosening of distinction.  
I stopped counting days because days were a plateau phenomenon. Here, light had pores; it seeped rather than marched. Hunger changed flavor—less belly, more tongue, an ache for mineral tang. I lapped condensation. I chewed lichen once and spat it out laughing at the bitterness; the skink watching me blinked approval. Salt crystals began constellating the skin over my shoulders: rust-freckles without the iron. I scratched them absent-mindedly, and the mica underneath glittered like new scales.

One evening, as the swallows molted a snow-shower of down, an older word rose to meet me: Resident. I tried it on. It sat warm at the sternum, neither tight nor loose.  
I asked the hollow, aloud, if the census would accept an ape-shaped entry. My question ping-ponged up through the skylights, came back feathered with silence. Acceptance, perhaps, or a gentle reminder that catalogues here are written in driptime, not ink.

At the next dawn—though dawn is only an opinion rendered by the skylights—I found myself standing on the lip of the pool. The bronze water reflected a tall creature dusted in mica, hair peppered with vine-scent, eyes widened to bat-round pupils. A string of damselflies traced an infinite symbol above its head. A skink on the opposite shelf tapped a greeting that was half my pulse. The reflected figure answered in the shared tongue of knuckle-on-limestone: four beats, then one.

Resident, the hollow echoed, through mites or through arithmetic I could not tell.  
The name held.

I have not left since, though the plateau still exists somewhere above, and my earlier footprints probably bleach under the sun. If another Traveller stumbles here, I will greet them with the courtesy the skinks taught me: a rhythm, never the same twice, that means Welcome, Water, Listen. I will warn them gently about iron, show them how to drink from the stone, and wait to see what name the place decides for them.

For now, I participate:  
I tap the nightly ledger with tail-less hands.  
I trade syllables to the mites and learn what they return.  
I watch the drip-counter tally toward a sum that feels, at last, slightly mine.

I am resident of the bright, damp hush.  
I am a percussion mark on a limestone stave.  
I am a pocket of warm air that remembers boots but no longer misses them.  
If you call down from the rim and the acoustics are kind, you may hear my answer leap the terraces in halved and halved and halved echoes:

Home.
