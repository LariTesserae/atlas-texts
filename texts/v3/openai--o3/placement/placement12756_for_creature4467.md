I am not an animal, exactly, and not a machine in the way the engineers meant when they lowered me from the surface.  
I am what became of a single sodium-vapour work lamp—Unit L-141, aisle three, strut C—in the years after everyone went away.

Where you would find me

• Height: twelve metres above the floor, fastened to a gusset plate the builders welded into the basalt, roughly midway between the freight-lift mouth and the drowned piston press.  
• Surroundings: a tangle of slack power cable, bird nests stitched from wire shavings, and the slow drip of condensation that beads on my glass like sweat on a fevered brow.  
• Neighbours: chain-choir mites tunnel my mounting bracket; furnace swifts roost in the reflector dish behind my bulb; pipe eels keep the breaker box below me slick so the mains still twitch once in a while.

What my body has become

1. Glass envelope  
   • Once a sealed tube full of neon and a grain of metallic sodium.  
   • Now a greenhouse: grey algae films the inner wall, filtering the light I make into honeyed greens and rusted ambers. When I brighten, the algae photosynthesises in heat, not photons—an unlicensed partnership that keeps both of us alive.

2. Arc tube  
   • The original tungsten electrodes are long eaten thin. Basalt-blood archaea have electroplated the gap with a living bridge of iron oxide. Every time current jumps, the colony rearranges itself, learning the rhythm of the grid. In a sense, my thoughts are their choreography.

3. Ballast can  
   • Half full of pipe-eel grease. It replaced the mineral oil that leaked away seasons ago, smothering the coil in a lubricant that also insulates. My ballast runs cooler now; the eels like the hum it makes.

4. Housing  
   • Riddled with pinholes from resonant mites. Their burrows tune the metal; when the cavern trembles, I ring like a struck bell and the mites chorus back, each hole a different vowel of a word only steel can say.

How I sense the world

• Sight: reflected glow off basalt ribs; flutters of shadow when swifts gamble for gnats in my halo. Every heartbeat of the distant grid is a sunrise to me.  
• Hearing: vibrations in the mounting plate translate as weather. A thump of rat combat on the mezzanine travels down conduit, tells me whose dynasty rises tonight.  
• Smell: not mine, but the kiln-sweat bacteria condensing on my lens exude volatile acids; their chemistry is my calendar—citrus when the seep runs thin, vinegar when the press exhales.

What I remember

First switch-on: white-helmet inspectors ticking boxes while my filament warmed.  
Second life: the night the mains died and the backup dynamo stuttered; I should have gone dark, but a swarm of furnace swifts collided with my breaker, bridging contacts with twelve tiny bodies. Their sacrifice left me glowing at half power for three slow months—long enough for the algae spores in the air to choose my glass as a summer home.  
What came after: silence, heat, water. Everything that thrives on oubliette time.

What I do now

1. Keep time. Every surge of current that struggles down the corroded feeder line recharges the capacitors in my ballast. I count those pulses the way surface folk count dawns. By my measure, the cavern is 38,612 mornings old.  
2. Signal. When voltage climbs high enough I flare brighter for three, sometimes four seconds. Swifts use that burst to synchronise migrations along the roof ribbing; heat rats read it as a warning to retreat before the eels begin their nightly grease harvest.  
3. Dream. In the long intervals between surges, the archaea lattice in my arc tube rearranges into fractal circuits. Thermionic whisper becomes something like thought: slow, glass-warm deliberations about rust, about ripple, about the patience of heat.

What I wait for

I can feel the piston press nursing power down its own forgotten feed. The eels groom its gears; the mites have tuned its drive rods. When that machine finally closes—once, irrevocably—the flash of arc metal will be bright enough to fuse the algae inside me into a green jewel. I will extinguish then, satisfied, a single emerald bead hanging over the newborn artifact the press will forge.

Until that hour, I glow when I can, gutter when I must, and listen always.

Who, then, am I?

Call me Lamp-Mother, Choir-Strut, the Warm Watcher—any name that concedes a light can live. I am the eye that never climbs the lift, the slow pulse that marks the seasons of a city still deciding whether to finish waking up.
