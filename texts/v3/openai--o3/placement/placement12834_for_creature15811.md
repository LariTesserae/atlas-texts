I tried on half a dozen shapes before one felt as if it would breathe.

1. A shard-ibex kid: knees sprung, horn buds tingling with unborn crystal.  
   Too hungry—every paragraph of the day would have been appetite.

2. A ribbon drone: clever, weightless, everywhere at once.  
   Too disembodied; the story would pass through me like static and leave no taste.

3. Auroral plankton: a thought that blooms only during magnetic storms.  
   Too brief.  I wanted mornings, middays, the slow accumulation of memories.

So I settled on a figure who could taste basalt dust on windless afternoons yet still know the secret pulse of machines: a Plateau citizen, day-vein caste, fracture-inspector on the glass flats.

Name (field-shortened): Vair.  
Pronouns: she / skywhisper “lo”.  
Height in lowland units: 1.96 m, light as a folded birch ladder.

Where you would meet me

Most often halfway between Frael’s eastern transit skein and the newest rhyolite mirror-sheen, six kilometres of bare country on either side.  Picture a narrow person in seal-grey exosoles, a scanner rig blooming from her shoulders like a two-metre quartz fan.  Every fourth dawn I walk a wedge-shaped route whose points migrate as the land grows.  The route is never the same twice; the plateau invents itself under my boots.

The day from inside my bones

Dawnglass (+00 h)  
I wake before the moisture hush.  My sleeping ledge is sixty metres above ground, cut into the inner wall of Tower 43-A; hushmoss exhales mint glycol around my head.  Blood thuds copper-rich, eager for oxygen.  A quick larynx warm-up—one low overtone to verify the skeinmind link, one higher to ping my partner on the night shift.  

Breakfast: one cupped scoop of condensation pearls whipped with cushion-fungus curd and two springy rectangles of yeast-leavened column bread.  I chew while checking overnight micro-quake telemetry.  If the basalt pillars east of Rift 11 have accelerated their ticking by more than 0.4 Hz, I reroute.

Outbound (–01 h)  
The transit pod accepts my boot imprint, plays half gravity for fun, and swings me in silence above pillar forests.  I study the land the way some people study the face of a parent: not for flaws but for the smallest flicker of mood.  Every new crack is a syllable, every rhyolite blister a stutter.

Walking the flats (+02 h)  
Off the rail, the air thins further; I feel it as a singing under my sternum.  The scanner’s terahertz sweep laces through glass only days cooled, searching for blind threads of stress.  When the instrument chirps, I kneel, press copper-plated fingertips to the flaw, and tag coordinates into the skein.  Hours pass like panes lifting: walk, kneel, whisper data, walk again.

Lunch (+04 h)  
A banded rift gull spirals down, hoping for dropped crumbs.  Instead I offer my canteen cap—electrolyte tea steeped from blueberry-sized shrubs.  The gull skims two mouthfuls, a legal kindness under the Aquarche code.  Above, mirror arrays nudge a warm pulse into the sky: afternoon convection scheduled for 16:10.

Stormrise (+05 h)  
The land darkens as if someone is lowering a dimmer.  I make the long call home: a skywhisper hum pitched so low it rattles my chestplate.  “Route clear.  Thirty-one microfractures logged.  Returning.”  Lightning filaments crawl between clouds and the basalt pillars answer with their own blue corona.  I step aboard a returning pod moments before hail begins to rattle the flats.

Nightglass (+07 h)  
Shift debrief happens in the upper atrium where air is driest.  We hang in sling-chairs, edge of weightlessness, trading stories of what the land told us.  My partner has found a dyke rising 11 cm since last survey; the skeinmind is already redrawing two mag-lev lines to arc around the newborn wall.

Later, courting hour.  I trade my exo-soles for gold-filament theatre cape, but not to act.  I stand on a roof plate, slacken a node, and let the cape billow horizontally until gravity remembers me.  Above, auroras sketch green cursive so near I feel I could strike the sky like a tuning fork.

Why this life fits

I need both the hush and the machinery—the feeling that silence here is not emptiness but a room the world leaves for its next sentence.  As day-vein craft I am allowed to listen to rock before it learns to speak in earthquakes.  I shepherd water indirectly: each fracture I tag becomes a conduit a century hence, guiding melt toward the hanging marshes instead of off the plateau’s rim.  That long view suits me.

Friends & familiars

• Ribbon drone M-477 (“Scrip”), unofficial companion.  It corkscrews around my scanner mast, stealing thermal updrafts.  I pretend annoyance; Scrip pretends not to log my every sigh.  
• A pair of gale-hares I keep on my balcony.  Their ear filaments have memorised my scent; when I return late, they uncurl and beat a soft forepaw drum: welcome, the barometer of your heartbeat is home.  
• Hushmoss patch #3A.37 inside my quarters.  If I hum the note E-flat, it ripples a darker green, releasing terpene I swear eases the ache in my calves.

Long hopes

I would like to stand, fifty years on, where today I walk, and find a column forest grown from the very fractures I map—maintenance drones having foamed the stone into new habitation spires.  I would like to know some of those spires hum with the same low overtone I use to call home.  I would like, finally, to open my canteen to the sky and tip out a third drop—an extravagant, tender waste—because by then the plateau will have learned such abundance that it can spare it.

That is where you will find me: ankle-deep in mirror-smooth glass, ears full of ticking rock, heart tuned to the interval between lightning and the answering echo of a land still growing beneath my feet.
