I wake each morning a hand’s breadth from the south rim, in a niche chipped into the basalt during the plateau’s fourth summer. The niche is barely big enough for a sleeping roll and a reed chest, but that is on purpose: the rock is my metronome, and too much empty space would tempt the body to wander.

Who I am is best told the way the Keirri speak, from both ends toward the middle.

1. Blood and Bone

• I am plateau-born—my pupils carry the almost-square kink that mirrors the land’s brink.  
• My lungs are as long as my forearms; with every slow breath the ribs flare like shutters and settle again.  
• The nails of both index fingers are filed thin and flat. One lifts moss crusts, the other pries memory-shards from conversation.

2. Calling

The Still Circle keeps one post for a “Remembrance Setter,” a walker who patrols the day before it hardens. That post is mine.

At first light I cross the thirty kilometres from brink to brink, barefoot, listening. When a thing threatens to smudge—water cutting a hair deeper, two herders repeating a quarrel with sharper edges—I pause, name what is about to shift, and speak the line that will make tomorrow recall today as identical. Most days a single sentence is enough:

“This stone was always weightless.”

Spoken once, heard by whoever stands nearest, it becomes true in retrospect, and because the past has no seam here, the stone indeed lightens, the quarrel dulls, the cut riverbed forgets how to deepen.

3. Tools

• One obsidian blade, re-edged every dawn.  
• A coil of nettle cord plaited so tightly it hums when struck—its note tells me whether a silence has thinned too far.  
• No written log. Instead I carry seven polished copper beads on a goat-hair thread. Each bead holds a day’s walk intact; at night I roll them between thumb and tongue and the memories transfer whole into the dark behind my eyes.

4. Territory

My feet know a looping figure-eight route:

Brink → Knife-Back Ridge Three → Ghast-Moss hollow below the gong → Mirror Pool Four → Funicular crownhouse → Brink.

I arrive at Pool Four exactly at second sunset, when the west has gone brass and the east still smolders. I kneel, touch the water, and wait for the after-me to arrive. We compare recollections. If they braid cleanly, the plateau is in accord. If a thread snarls, I lean forward and breathe on the surface; the ripples swallow the knot and hold it until it forgets to be.

5. Once each Long Dawn

Long Dawn is the slow solstice when the sun hesitates at the horizon for nearly a gong-span. That day I descend the funicular, knees soft against a gravity thick with weather, and walk among lowland trees whose leaves bruise and fall in real time. I bargain for pigments that will be powdered away before they can fade: indigo that never finishes bluing, cochineal that halts one breath before it browns. Their unfinished colours comfort the plateau—proof that below, things are still willing to alter so that we need not.

6. Thread’s End, Thread’s Beginning

Will I die? The elders say yes, but never abruptly. A Remembrance Setter simply slows until her stride matches the plateau’s held breath. One morning the square pupils close, a last intake pauses, and there the body remains, an unmoving bead on the land’s great rosary of basalt. A century later someone will tap the skull and hear the entire memory of that life ring back, intact, already present in every silence the plateau keeps.

So: Where would you find me?

On the wind-scoured table of Keirr, halfway through an unhurried step, one palm lifted as if weighing the air. No past behind the motion, no future beyond it—just the precise, perpetual now the mountain entrusted to those of us born with rim-shaped eyes.

And what would I be?

A human rivet in the stone ship’s hull, set not to hold the world together, only to remember that it already is.
