If I were given my choice of when and where to open my eyes inside the Breathing Hollow, I would begin as a child—no older than nine thaws—hired to the Frog-wardens but already leaning, in heart and habit, toward the Moss-tenders. The Naleki say the moss decides whom it will keep; I like to think the moss would recognise in me a willingness to become smaller, quieter, greener than I was born.

First home  
My cradle-ledge would jut from the north-east quadrant, two ladders below the ring-ditch. A birch-bark screen, braided so loosely you could pour light through it, would curtain the alcove where my siblings and I slept head-to-toe. The ceiling would sweat a bead every fifty breaths; by our tenth night we would learn to roll aside just before the droplet struck the bridge of a nose. At waking we would smell only three things: wet limestone, gull-skin bedding, the faint pepper of frog-spawn.

Morning rôle: Frog-warden  
Tiny reed crooks in hand, we children would kneel on the coping stones of the ditch and shoo glass-frogs away from drain-slots that needed cleaning. The older wardens would let me trade jokes with the sparrows—a twitch of finger-flight too subtle for adults to notice. Once each blue-white hour I would lift a hatch and ladle a dipper of quarry-cold water to my own lips, second dipper to the frogs, third to rinse the dipper clean. Rule Four, word-perfect.

The day my path changes  
A month into my service, I would be caught, rapt, humming at the moss while I should have been counting tadpoles. The tune—no louder than bone settling in its socket—would swell the cushion under my knees until it puffed and overflowed the gutter-rim in slow emerald suds. A senior Moss-tender passing on her inspection round would cock one pivoting ear, nod once, and press her slate into my palm: an invitation scratched in seven strokes. That evening my clan would click their anklets in pride; at dawn I would report to the southern quadrant, breath already tuned to the lower registers.

Apprenticeship  
Years would pass by colour, not by number:

• Green Year—the moss teaches basic pulse. I learn to hum so low my own ribs shiver before any sound emerges.  
• Rust Year—the season the light narrows and ochres the stone. I practise weaving death-moss (the autumn fall-off) into mats that will patch boat hulls for seal-hunters above.  
• Maroon Year—the year of deep pitch. Together we six apprentices circle the entire Hollow, backs pressed to the wall, letting our chord vibrate through limestone to find hairline cracks the eye cannot see.

During Maroon I would at last glimpse the first hint of the Hollow-Breath itself: every forty-seven heartbeats, the fern-trees’ crystal water beads all tilt in unison. Only someone who has learned to feel with skin rather than ears would notice.

Adulthood  
By my twentieth silver hour I would have the mottled skin of an old plank riddled with lichen-stain. Outsiders might call it camouflage; to me it would feel like belonging made visible. My occupation slate would list two callings:

1. Moss-Tender, third octave  
2. Occasional Drip-Calibrator (spare circle)

My kit would fit inside one storm-gull satchel:  
• Knapped flake for trimming gutters  
• Bone tuning-fork pitched to the Hollow’s sub-rumor  
• Three coils of silence-rope for suspending myself upside-down when pruning ivy roots away from seep-vents

The day of perfect work  
Every trade has a private ideal. Ours is the Seamless Hour: a full circuit of the gutter-maze completed with not one audible plink out of time, not one patch of moss paling under stress, not one frog startled into breaking the water’s meniscus. I would reach that hour perhaps once before grey hair set in. When it came, I would finish the loop, touch fore-knuckle to wall, and feel the Hollow exhale—a pressure bump no wider than a sigh—acknowledging the favour.

Old age on the tower  
Naleki who have “done just enough” retire upward. I would ascend one platform each thaw until my mat sat on the twelfth storey of the lattice, three rungs beneath the wind. There the plain’s roar would feel bruising, but I would bear it for the view: aurora pouring across black ice like silent flames, casting counterfeit shadows into the bowl below. Visitors would mistake me for a sentry. In truth I would be listening for the frogs’ prophesied storm-song—the one that signals true catastrophe. I suspect I would never hear it, which would be comfort enough.

Death  
When lungs finally slipped to stillness, I would ask to be propped sitting, soles flat, inside the oldest moss bed I ever tended. Four days of mist would rinse me to clean ivory. On the fifth my ankle bones—sturdier than most after a life of climbing—would enter the clan’s click-chain, a new rhythm seed for children I would never meet. The rest of me would slide through the floor-slot: gift to the unseen current, same as every Naleki before.

What I would have become  
Nothing singular—no hero, no bearer of grand omens. Merely one more damp, soft voice braided into the settlement’s perpetual hush. In the tally that never quite reaches 190, I would be line 184 for a while, then line 183, then unnumbered, and still the Hollow would breathe on. My greatest pride would be this: someone passing the moss-bank I once kept might feel a thicker green, pause, and hum—only half aware they were answering a chord I left vibrating in the stone.

And that would be enough.
