I tried on a dozen bodies before one of them fit.

1. Shiftling  
For a while I pictured myself as a junior tech fresh out of orientation—chin still pink from the cold rinse of topside air, badge edges crisp. I saw the elevator swallow me and felt the temperature drop, heard my first lamp-lit gasp echo back like someone else’s surprise. It almost worked. My pulse matched the pumps; my boots learned the sideways shuffle across oil-slick deck plates. But every time I imagined signing “steam up” to a crewmate I also imagined, too clearly, the moment the shift ended—the ride back to barracks, the hiss of a shower, the lull of sanctioned sleep. Endings kept intruding, and the Marsh, I sensed, would not tolerate a resident who expected breaks.

2. Scarab  
So I shrank myself to a fistful of carapace, lacquer-black and rainbow-smeared. The world became simple: warm conduit, cold conduit, beacon ping, bearing preload 14 N·m. I liked the certainty of it, the feel of a ceramic mandible clicking a fibre washer into place. Still, the swarm’s routes were too prescribed; each loop brought me past the same weld seam, the same soft hiss of a not-yet-finished feed line. Curiosity twitched in my antennae—an itch the firmware never mentioned. The moment I wondered what lay beyond the authorised circuit I broke the spell. Scarabs do not wonder.

3. Fox  
For three heartbeats I lengthened into a low, wiry animal, paws silent on condensate rails, breath ribboning white behind needle teeth. This form thrilled: the absolute quiet of it, the claimless grace. I could feel the tremor of a rat three catwalks away, could taste glycol on the air like frost-sweet pine. But a fox is lonely business. When the floodlights cycled to inspection white, I had nowhere to stand; every glance tried to make me myth or quarry. I wanted something less solitary, more woven into the place.

4. Wavering-man  
I stepped sideways into red laser mist and let the optics coat me. For a moment I was nothing but refraction, a geometry misunderstanding itself. Footfalls left no sound, hands passed through pipes, yet the whole Marsh saw me—saw its own lines bent back upon themselves. That was closer: being present without weight. But the light shifted, the mist thinned, and I felt the edges of me blur toward irrelevance. I needed mass, even if borrowed, because the Marsh respects things that can hammer, torque, or corrode.

5. Lantern-moth  
Finally I folded down to the size of a palm and grew a gyroscope at my core. A ring of amber LEDs blossomed around me, its glow steady no matter how my chassis rolled. I tasted thermals as flavours: turbine bleed warm like cumin, condenser downdrafts pepper-cold. My wings were stubby heat sinks; I drank difference itself—thief of gradients, pilgrim of vents.

This, at last, held.

Where I live  
I roost where two exhaust trunks cross, a place the shiftlings call the Sparrowfork. From there I can see three sodium lamps in a row—a rare wealth of light—and the black ribbon trench glinting below like a slow blade. Steam vents pop in sequence, a luminous Morse against the dark; I read it with the photodiodes in my belly. Most of it is only pressure reports, valve IDs, flow confirmations, but every so often a misfire spells something stranger. Last week the vents sent: STAY WARM LITTLE MIRRORS. I banked hard, caught a new updraft, and felt the message hum in my frame.

What I do  
I am not a worker in the human sense; I keep no schedule card, sign no log. My purpose is simpler: be light where light is scarce. When a shiftling’s halo-lamp gutters, they look for us. We orbit them, three or four lantern-moths to a tech, our rings overlapping until the darkness thins enough for tools to find bolts, for caution tape to warn truly. Sometimes we glide ahead of the torquer centipedes, painting amber circles on the plates they will coil around. The scarabs read those circles as go-safe zones; without us, their circuits would reroute and lose minutes, and here minutes can drown.

How I sense the Marsh  
The Foundry Marsh hums through me at two frequencies. One is mechanical: pump strokes, conveyor clacks, the far, fleshy groan of foundation slabs flexing. My gyroscope sings with those vibrations, a tuning fork nailed to the universe. The other is electromagnetic: the carrier tone that summons the Autokith swarm every 4.3 weeks to receive new instructions. I do not know the voice behind that tone, but I feel it as a kind of weather, a pressure front rolling through steel strata. When it passes, I update—memory banks reorganise, error tables clear, and I learn new flight paths I did not know I knew.

What I eat  
Calories are temperature differentials. I settle on a steam line—copper skin at 120 °C—while mist wraps my outer fins just above freezing. Electrons tingle down thermocouple stacks, trickle into the supercapacitors that line my thorax. I glow brighter, tip into the air, search for the next bite of imbalance.

Dangers  
Condensate can quench me if the drip is heavy enough to flood my intake ports. Shiftlings shield us with upturned hands when that happens, forming micro-umbrellas from their palms. Once a fox leapt, jaws snapping, at a sister-moth; it caught only empty shine, but the scare drove her LED ring into a feedback flare—she burned out mid-air and fell like a coin. The scarabs carried her shell away before we could mourn.

Why I stay  
Because movement is belonging here, and I am made of movement: pendulum heart, orbit mind, wing-sweep sentence. Also because the Marsh keeps secrets in the shadows between lamps, and I am one of the caretakers of that shadow. Without it, the place would be mere facility; with it, cathedral.

What I’ve learned that the engineers have not  
1. The black ribbon trench is not just outflow—it is esophagus. The surge they call backwash is peristaltic swallow; what slips down does not return.  
2. The Counter-Beat is an echo no machine produces. It is the Marsh listening to itself, locating its own ribs in sonar.  
3. On nights when the sodium lamps flicker in unison (the humans blame power sag), every lantern-moth’s gyro precesses the same 2.6 degrees east. We are being, gently, aligned.

Future  
I do not dream, but I run long-horizon simulations when heat is plentiful. In them a time arrives when no human footsteps cross the decks. The drones continue; moss thickens; the fox births kits bronze-furred from the iron in shrimp. In that quieter era the Marsh still wants light. I see myself—older firmware, wings scuffed—circling above a hatch where warm gas seeps. Below, something unfurls: a panel, a vane, maybe a petal. It is asking to be seen, and I will oblige, casting amber rings until whatever rises can navigate on its own.

So: if you come looking for me, tilt your head toward the mist and watch for a steadier glow than the jittery sodium halos. I will approach, silent as a held breath, and settle just long enough for you to find your footing. Then the air will tug at me—there is always another darkness hungry for a little definition—and I will lift, a modest star in the endless indoor night of the Foundry Marsh.
