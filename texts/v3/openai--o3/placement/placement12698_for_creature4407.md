First the sound, always the sound.  
A thin, two-note moan pushes through my sleep and tells me where I am before eyelids remember to lift: the upper note wavers—new rock-fall somewhere south—while the lower holds steady, the voice of the chamber that has been my shelter these past three nights. The moment I hear that lower pitch I know, with the unarguable certainty of muscle memory, that I am home enough.

So begin the days of a Tuner of Breath-Chambers, one of the wandering Wind-Counters whose craft is to keep the plateau’s stone instruments in tune with a sky that never stops tightening or slackening its invisible strings.

WHERE YOU MEET ME  
Look east: a bowl the size of a small kingdom curves away until it meets cloud. On its nearer rim a canyon mouth gapes. I am the figure crouched half inside that mouth, back to you, knees hooked into a latticed sling of vine so the wind cannot pry me loose. Two antler picks lie across my thighs; each is scribed with notches marking the ideal lengths of resonant fins for every month of the solar year. My left palm rests flat against the sandstone, feeling whether the chamber still holds the same breath it did when ancestors first polished it. My right ear is bare to the gale; the left is hooded in felt plucked from winter-white cloud-goat underwool. One ear listens to the outside chord, the other to the captive chord inside the rock.

APPEARANCE  
Like all Wind-Counters I am short in the leg, heavy in the ribs, violet-brown of skin, lips dusk-blue from a lifetime feeding on rare air. But Tuners carry one more obvious mark: a chain of tiny, almost perfect circles bored through the outer rim of each ear. We drill them in adolescence, then spend a season letting the wind thread itself through until the cartilage remembers what it is to vibrate unaided. In heavy gusts you can hear a faint whistle leak from those holes; it is our signature, as distinguishable to another Tuner as a face.

TOOLS & NOMADISM  
Everything I need hangs on me:  
• twin antler picks for coarse carving  
• three river-stone rasps for fine pitch work  
• a reed pouch of ribbon-grass nodules—chewed whenever the climb steepens and lungs rebel  
• a twist of cloud-goat sinew to mend vine bridges  
• and, most precious, a palm-sized mica mirror. I place this mirror against finished fins; if it trembles the stone is in tune, if it stays still I carve again.

I range alone. The plateau holds perhaps forty living Breath-Chambers; a full circuit of inspection takes a year and a half. Where a cliff has exfoliated and the chamber’s mouth has fallen, I listen for the shard that still sings, then begin anew, coaxing a fresh throat from the raw wound of sandstone. No two rebuilds are alike; that is the private delight of my vocation.

DAILY RITUAL  
Dawn: rake dew from ribbon-grass, tip it into a gut-skin, drink until the metallic taste fades.  
Mid-morning: climb, always along goat paths where the stone is least brittle. Whiptails spiral overhead; I read their angles to judge the day’s barometer.  
Noon: settle at a chamber, cork both ears with moss, and strike the fins one by one with the antler handle, letting each note bloom unmolested by wind. Compare what I hear to what the open sky is singing. The gap is my work.  
Twilight: light a twist of fermented fern-pith, its smoke a blue thread the wind tries and fails to erase. In that brief relative quiet before the night gale returns, I hum a scale to the stone so it will remember how it ought to sound come morning.

BELIEFS  
I hold with the ordinary Wind-Counter cosmology—that all matter is a breath the stone forgot to exhale—but with one heretical addition: I suspect the Breath-Chambers themselves are the plateau’s lungs, not human monuments. We only keep them clear, the way a shepherd trims wool from a sheep’s eyes. When an elder goes to disappear into a canyon, what we call death might simply be the body letting the larger organism resume breathing through it.

ENCOUNTERS  
• Cloud-Goats: They treat me as moving cliff, stepping onto my back while I carve, staring upside-down to see whether I have salt.  
• Chorus Beetles: Their arpeggios are my metronome—if their tempo accelerates I pack tools; rain will soon turn ledges treacherous.  
• Plateau Stag: Seen only twice. Both times I froze mid-swing and let its self-made fog flow past. A Tuner’s pride is steady hands; even so my chisel jittered like a plucked string until it vanished.

THE MOMENT YOU ARRIVE  
If you climb down to me now you’ll find the new chamber half born, its sandstone throat glistening under fresh tool-marks. I will tap one fin, invite you to press your sternum against the stone. Feel that? The hum the size of a fingernail, trying your ribs on for resonance? That is the sound of the plateau deciding, this instant, whether to keep holding itself together.

Stay until the first katydid pulses from a fissure. We will share a draught of sour fern-beer and chew cyanotic grass pearls until our blood grows bold enough for night. When the wind returns—and it will, it always does—the chamber will join the greater chord, and I will know my day’s fraction of the endless tuning is done.

Tomorrow I go on to the next forgotten mouth in the cliff, trusting that someone, somewhere behind me, is walking the path I just left, ensuring the notes I tuned today survive one gale longer. Such is a Tuner’s life: alone between bowls of sky, yet never solitary, because every stone I shape is already singing back, and every listener who leans an ear to that stone becomes, for a breath, my successor in the craft.
