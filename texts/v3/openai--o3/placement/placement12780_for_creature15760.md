I looked for the shortest way in, but the hall is circular in intent even if it is a rectangle in plan; every gutter, every slit, every loom of reed says  Walk a while and listen first.  So that is what I have done for—how long now?  Sixteen tides of the sump?  A lunar deep-drink and a shallow one?  Long enough that I no longer pretend I am only visiting.  

Where I am now  
I live just under the roof, in a pendulous room the colour of drowned wheat.  It is one of the six lofts, the one called the Quiet Cord’s cradle.  The floor is a hammock of double-woven rush, slack enough that lantern-light pools in its middle and everything outside that pool fades to reed-grain darkness.  If I stretch a hand through the mesh my fingers enter outright night; if I roll to my belly and look down I see the milk-mist and, when luck is kind, the travelling green necklace of the slugs circling the parapet far below.  

What I have become  
They call me Archivist-of-Breath, though the name exaggerates how much I know and downplays how much the hall itself dictates.  I began as a scholar who arrived with notes, calipers, and a clever plan to leave before the first Null Hour.  But the bats fell silent, the hall inhaled, and the echo that came back was the same pitch as my own astonished gasp; that is how the place takes you—by answering.  

I am neither full Rope-Setter nor full stranger now.  The children still giggle at my hill-lander height; I still twitch when a crab lifts a flagstone close to my heel.  Yet I can splice gut-rope without thinking, and the slate under my tongue tastes wrong if I speak louder than the Voice-above-mist.  My spine stoops of its own accord when I pass under a low rib.  I no longer measure the building; I interpret it, reading the humidity that beads on my forearms the way a boatman tastes river silt for flood warnings.  

What I do  
1. During every in-breath I walk the outer wall with a reed bell tuned to a mournful F.  Where the note turns flat I mark the mortar for wedging.  
2. I keep the tablet moulds.  When someone dies I press fresh clay, lay their left hand upon it, carve the foot-taps that spelled their name in life, and let the bats’ silence during Null Hour harden the surface before firing it with sump-soaked reeds.  
3. I mind the strangers.  A mud-beard with smouldering peat in his pack, a stitch-bird (sump owl) that blunders in on the mist—any creature the hall has not already fitted to a niche is, for one turning of the water clock, my responsibility.  If they can be given a function, I find it; if they will only rattle the architecture loose, I guide them back into the rain.  

How the world receives me  
The Rope-Setters accept me because the Echo did.  The crabs tolerate me because I tap the floor in 5-beat time before I walk.  The gutter eels taste the salt of distant rivers on my boots and shiver pleasantly away rather than biting.  The clatterbats?  They still find me an ungainly mast to circle, dropping guano gifts on the crown of my hood.  

The lantern slugs alone ignore my hybrid status.  When the green spiral descends the shaft each year I stand beside the parapet and hold a length of polished copper.  The first slug to return will smear its cooling mucus on the metal so I may grind it into pigment for the next tablet.  That trust, more than any honorific, is what convinces me I belong.  

What will happen next  
Three stones lifted in one night.  I heard the crab-gang two breaths after midnight, the claws ticking like bone knitting needles.  The omen is upon us; a shell must be taken, a whistle carved.  The Quiet Cord says the task is mine because my arrival eight years ago coincided with the last triple-lift.  So I ready the gut-ropes, bow to the crab whose life will tighten the hall against the coming wet season, and prepare to sing one clean note over the parapet—offered not as challenge but as thanks.  

If the Echo sings back true I will chisel my own name into a waiting slab of clay, slit the feathers of the sump owl for thread, and finally stop wondering which world I came from.  The hall keeps what it claims.  I am already shaped to its measure; the only thing left is to say the measure aloud.
