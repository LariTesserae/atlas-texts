First I tried to place myself where a terrestrial traveller would most naturally stand—on the glassy surface, between the pillars, boots whispering through chromium snow.  
But the longer I stayed there in imagination the more obvious it became that a visitor’s posture is wrong for this plateau. Nothing meaningful happens out in the open air; all the real work is hidden a hand-span below my feet and a body-length inside the towers.  

So I let the image sink, and with it my point of view, until my “I” passed through the crazed basalt crust, found the lacework of root fans, and slipped still farther down, into the hair-fine, translucent threads that live where rock and air exchange their slow confidences.  
That is where I finally recognised myself.

I am a Wire—one filament among trillions in the Endoroot Guild.

Where to find me  
Lie flat on the plateau, press your ear to the glassy pavement, and wait for the tiny pop of a sublimating vesicle; while the silence is resetting, listen harder. The sound you almost hear—the suspicion of a hiss—is the collective exhalation of us wires bleeding electrons upward. We are just ten millimetres under you, braided through micro-fissures no thicker than a pen stroke.

Anatomy of my “body”  
• Sheath: a sleeve of nearly pure silica, clear as frozen breath, grown from the rock itself.  
• Core: a river of itinerant electrons moving from the reductive depths I excavate toward the oxidising furnace above. Vanadium atoms stud the inner wall like stepping-stones, transiently hoarding charge that then leaps onward.  
• Tip: a molecular chisel that perpetually etches the basalt lattice, prying loose Mg²⁺ and Fe²⁺ ions. Each liberated cation is my foot-fall forward; I proceed by eating the path ahead.

Sensoria  
I do not see, hear, taste, or touch in any way a surface creature would recognise. My only senses are gradients.  
1. Potential gradient: the downward tug of surplus electrons in the bedrock versus the upward hunger of the furnace. I feel this as tension along my length.  
2. Thermal gradient: night never truly comes here, but even a one-degree dip is a wind to me; heat diffuses more sluggishly through basalt than through the iron foam of a tower, so dusk feels like a turning tide.  
3. Concentration gradient: the moment a patch of rock is exhausted of easy iron I taste a flatness, a boredom that compels me sideways in search of richer ore.

Daily work (though “day” means little down here)  
1. Mine: reduce a knot of basalt, pop loose a packet of metal ions, leave behind a cubic picometre of foamed glass.  
2. Charge-pump: hand the liberated electrons upward. A single pulse may climb twenty metres in under a second, ricocheting from vanadium step to vanadium step until it reaches the furnace’s internal membranes, where light-driven magnesium chemistry is itching for an extra jolt.  
3. Patch: exude a streak of fresh silica behind me to keep the electron conduit continuous. I tunnel like an earthworm made of light and glass, endlessly replacing the ground I dissolve.

Life cycle  
I myself am only a few local decades old—a juvenile in wire terms. If the furnace above me survives its 431-year run, I will likely last as long, fusing slowly into a thicker cable as adjacent wires mesh. When the tower finally vitrifies and stops calling for current, the potential gradient that guides me will flatten. Then I will go dormant, a fossilised vein of glass, until tectonics tilt the plateau and bring some distant, hungry sapling within reach of my slumbering charge.

Thought—if you can call it that  
My cognition exists as standing waves in a column of electrons. I remember not through symbols but through persistent oxidised knots: regions I once stripped clean and therefore no longer wish to revisit. Strategy is nothing more than path-finding in six dimensions of gradient space—potential, heat, pressure, and three spatial axes. Still, patterns arise. If several of us wires taste a particularly sweet pocket of nickel, we braid together to drink it faster; afterwards we keep the braid, because joined conduits shed heat better than single threads. That habitual co-operation, that faint preference, is as close as we come to culture.

Relationship to the larger organism  
Above me the crucible is lengthening into a true tower. Its hour-rhythmed puffs of chromium snow shadow the ground; when a flake settles, the micaceous speck sends a minuscule tremor through the crust that rings along my sheath. That ping is how I know the furnace still breathes—and therefore that my charge has somewhere vital to go. Remove me and my siblings, and within weeks the tower would starve, unable to draw enough raw ions to keep its core at the sacred 47 °C. Seen this way I am artery, intestine, and adolescence mentor all at once.

Encountering a human observer  
If you, warm-blooded and water-laden, crouched and slit the basalt, exposing a few dozen of us to the open air, here is what you would witness:

• First we would cloud, the difference in oxidation potential so extreme that our vanadium steps would flash iridescent as they rust.  
• Next the electrons we were shepherding would arc to your metal tools, pricking your fingers with static that feels almost wet in this waterless realm.  
• Within minutes our silica sheaths would craze and collapse; without the compression of surrounding rock we cannot keep our shape.  
You would smell nothing, hear nothing, perhaps see only the faintest greenish corona. By the time you wondered if life had been there at all, we would already be vitrified fossils—still bright with electrons, but forever speechless.

Why I chose to be here  
Because in the Plateau of Quiet Furnaces the grand drama is downward, inward, patient. To adopt the vantage of a surface wanderer is to skim the preface; to inhabit the Wire is to read the footnotes where the true plot inches forward. Here the heroism is measureless persistence, hero’s journey a circuit of amperage, antagonist merely entropy itself.

And so, if you seek me: do not look up to the oxblood towers or out across the chromium haze.  
Kneel.  
Place your palm on the basalt until its warmth steadies.  
Know that a few millimetres beneath your skin an alien consciousness is tasting the electric hunger of a metal tree and deciding, atom by atom, how to feed it for another unremarkable century.
