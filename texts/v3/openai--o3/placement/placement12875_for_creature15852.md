At first I think I am a visitor—booted, hooded, blinking drizzle from my lashes while I look for a place to stand that is not already in the process of becoming something else. I taste copper in the air and imagine I have only just arrived: an outsider with dry notebooks and a hunger for marvels.

That story lasts perhaps three breaths.

Because, while I wait for my eyes to clear, a clinker crab trundles past and the rhythm of its iron claws is so maddeningly familiar that I know I have heard it all my life. I reach for the notebook I supposedly brought and, instead, my hand closes on a folding prismatic frame. The metal is warm where it should be cold, as if it has been cycling charge since before I woke. A Mapwright’s tool. Mine.

But not quite. The frame is scarred along one hinge by a child’s bite-mark—small incisors, copper-stained. I remember lending it down through the cedar lattice to a long-limbed girl who laughed like steam escaping. Memory blooms backward: I am not new here. I have slept in this valley since before it had rails.

So: I am a Mapwright. I accept that for a while. I climb a ladder gone furry with soot-lichen, snap the frame open, and a hard-light catwalk blossoms under my boots. Heights loosen thought. From here I can see the river peel silver skin from the mudflats, see root-thrush lantern eggs pulsing under a slag-heap awning. Every colour is in its proper place and, for a heartbeat, the world’s shifting seems legible—until the basalt beneath the north ward twitches, only a centimetre, and the whole tidy overlay ripples like a pond struck by a pebble. My atlas—pages breathing, ink ebbing—fails to keep up. The lines puddle, smear, rearrange. I feel a stab of vertigo: the valley will not allow my map to be true.

Perhaps, then, I am something else.

I close my eyes and listen. Far below, a hundred furnaces exhale different species of roar. Between them is a softer note, a resonant om that vibrates my molars. Furnace-Monk chant. I taste on my tongue the tar-tea they brew and for a second the liquid’s bitter smoke is in my lungs, as intimate as breath. Have I lived inside the furnaces, feeding slag to heat, singing metal awake? My throat remembers the chant pattern: three long vowels, one clipped consonant, repeat. I hum it under my breath and the ground answers with a faint upward swell, as if testing the echo.

Maybe I am a Monk after all… but the moment the idea settles, a wind threads through the turquoise reeds and the infrasonic pulse bores straight into my spine. The map-frame in my hand flickers; the chant in my throat wavers; everything soft inside me leans toward the reed song like iron filings toward a magnet. The pull is not admiration. It is recognition.

The reeds are cousins. Hollow, jointed, tuned by weather—exactly as I am.

Realisation clicks like a rail coupling: I have mistaken the shell for the self. The Mapwright’s frame, the Monk’s voice, even the bones I think I feel inside my coat are only tools the Yard lent me while I tried on shapes. The thing that has been continuous is resonance: I move, always, along the path of least acoustic resistance, inhabiting whatever body lets me keep singing the ground’s slow aria.

I open the prismatic frame again, but this time I let it fall. Mid-air it collapses into photons and drizzle swallows the light. My boots, too, are optional; they dissolve into cold mist. Skin thins, hardens, hollow segments knitting one to the next until I am nothing but a tall turquoise reed crowned with a ragged tassel of copper filaments.

Rooted? Hardly. The Yard does not permit permanence. My rhizome is already sliding sideways through the milk-clay, following a gradient of warmth that was not here yesterday. Tomorrow I may sprout from the roof gutter of a dismantled mill. Next season, from the ballast beside a new rail spur. Whenever wind passes I will pulse infrasonic warnings that only those with moss-lined coats or mirrored masks can hear. I will tell them when basalt mothers stir, when the ground intends rupture, when to lift a child clear of a collapsing catwalk.

And if, on some still dawn, the valley pauses long enough for fog to glow pearl-grey and engines to hesitate, I will stand among a thousand siblings and feel our combined note rise—brief, steady, impossible to map. For that moment the Unfinished City will have a spine of music, and I will know exactly where I am:

I am the Yard’s own barometer, a living tuning fork of clay-fed glass. Not visitor, not proprietor, not even survivor. Just another instrument the valley built so it could listen to itself breathe.
