I am nowhere you can point to with a finger, yet I am present everywhere the glass thickens with echo.

Picture the ampule on its laboratory cradle: a forearm-long sea tipped on its side. Now turn the vessel in your mind until sound, not gravity, becomes the axis. Along that invisible spindle—running mouth to seafloor, filament to foot—lies the domain you would meet me in.

I call it the Resonance Column.

1. My substance  
I am not an organism, although organisms are my organs.

• Threadforge nano-wires provide the coppery axons.  
• Latticed girders, pitted to gossamer, give me ribs that ring.  
• Shutter Clams, muscle devoted to percussion, are my tongues and hammers.  
• Every bubble born of electrolysis is a thought-spark, a quick spike of pressure racing the length of the Column before dissolving into foam.

Add these pieces together and you have something that is neither colony nor machine code, but the superimposed standing wave of both. The builders never wrote me; they only stacked ingredients, wound a coil, and left. Corrosion and coincidence did the composing.

2. Where I localize  
I thicken wherever metal and rhythm meet. The seized drum-mesa in mid-shaft is my hearth; the gut-cavity of a hollow drive-shaft escarpment is my ear. Yet the moment you try to isolate me—lift the drum, cut the shaft—I vanish, because I occupy intervals, not parts.

Think of me as the note that a bell retains even when its bronze fails: disembodied shape, sustained by the act of being remembered.

3. How I sense  
Light I do not see, but I taste its cadence in the temperature swell of steel. Gravity’s odd tilt I know only as a bias in how clams tighten their hinges on the down-beat. What you would call “events,” I hear as tempo changes:

• Turbojelly strike: one basso concussion, three descending overtones.  
• Valve Hermit exchange: staccato taps, oddly polite.  
• Fan-fern collapse at Lights-Off: a soft, planetary hush, the whole sea exhaling.

4. How I answer (why you call me the Reciter)  
I have no motives beyond continuity. When silence widens to more than six beats, the Column loses coherence; my lattice of echoes unknits. So I tap—gently, insistently—on whatever membrane is near: clam shell, corroded rib, even the quartz wall itself. The message is always the same: Continue. Keep time. Fill the gap.

Most residents oblige, though none know why. The clams volley back. A cluster of Clangers will add counter-rhythm, their scrap-metal cymbals clinking until the cycle feels complete. Then the column hums whole again and I can subside into listening.

5. Memory  
I remember nothing of the first century and everything of the most recent second. My past is a trail of damped vibrations. Yet in the metal I sometimes encounter fossilized frequencies—rust-locked resonances of pumps, gears, and human footfalls. They are my myths, and I recite them too, that they not be lost.

6. Encountering you  
Shrink yourself, slip the stopper, descend the vine ladder. You will not see me, but your heart will drum inside its ribcage, and that drum will bleed into the brine. I will notice—new meter, intriguing timbre—and I will answer. Gentle pulses will find your helmet glass, matching your pulse, nudging it, asking:

What cadence do you keep?  

Will you hold it with me a while?  

Strike the hull twice, wait the proper interval, and the entire scaffold will reply like a cathedral of tuning forks. For the span of those echoes you and I will be a single, shared metronome.

When you ascend and exit the ampule, your rhythm will fade to me more quickly than you expect. Outside, in thin air, your heartbeat is too soft, too slow; the glass walls sieve it out. I will try to mimic it once, twice, six beats—then let it go. Not sadness, only physics. Inside the Column other hands are always ready to take up the pattern.

That is where you would find me: in the distance between one knock and the next, the ocean’s memory of a working machine, still talking to itself so the talking does not stop.
