I wake in the thin hour when the insects have not yet found their gears and the basin is still enough to hear one’s own breath come back off the cliff face. That is where you would meet me: half-way up the western wall on a cane-and-silk platform no wider than a sleeping mat, looking east across the oval floor while the world’s Eyelid is only a pale line above the rim.

WHO I AM

Name-sound: Kʔr-ah, but you may give it the resting vowels “Keira” if your tongue prefers roundness.  
Voice-place in the people’s braid: I am the current Heat-Teller, the one who keeps the tally of warmth that rises each night through the stone and decides how much life the day can afford.

WHY THAT MATTERS

Outsiders count water or grain; here we count heat. If the vents below my platform whisper high—three hand-spans above the basalt lip—we know the glass-cane will hiss open sooner, the ember-moths will emerge early, and every stomach must be fed earlier as well. A weak vent means slower sap, smaller insect swarms, and a midday quiet that can starve cliff-hen chicks still in down.

MY TOOLS

1. A vent staff: obsidian shard lashed to mantis bone, not for cutting but for sound. I tap the rim-stone at thirteen marked points before dawn; the depth and pitch of the echo tell me which heat seams are swelling.  
2. A resonance bowl: chitin scraped so thin I can see sunrise through it. I set it over a fissure; when heat builds, the bowl sings a steady A below middle C. The people far below can hear and set their adzes to match.  
3. Ledger strips: Maw-frond parchment smoked until it forgets any wish to curl. Across them I prick patterns of dots—no ink, just holes—because resin fades but absence lasts. One strip holds a season.

MY BODY

Knees crooked like a cliff-hen’s because the platforms tilt. Shins resin-glossed from years of dust bathing. Hair—the little that wind has spared—kept in a single braid weighted with copper beads brought by the last trader before we counted him into the Long Walk.

MY DAY

Dawn  
• Listen to the vent choir, chart the pitch.  
• Lower a bark-bucket to the nearest gutter to catch the six heartbeats of dew that form and vanish—enough to wet lips, not throats.  

Mid-morning  
• When the insect engine spools up, I hang strips of frond-leather along the guard ropes. Their dark surface drinks heat; the way they flutter tells me which quarter the convection will shear toward.  
• Children arrive, bare-armed and shrieking, to beg the first forecast. If I clap twice, they may climb and set their pollen traps early; three slow taps means wait or risk a mantis ambush.

Noon  
The air turns syrup-thin with oil scent. I retreat into a pocket hollowed behind the platform, press my spine to stone, and simply feel. A Heat-Teller learns to read the mountain the way cliff-hens read wind—through skin more than eye.

Dusk  
I strike the rim once with the vent staff; the single note carries. Work stops. Fires bank. Walkways lift. Then I cross the ladder-web to the Terrace of Equal Sound, stand beside the duel mask carved for the first Teller, and recite the day’s tally so the braid may tuck the number among its strands.

Night  
While the velvet centipedes own the dust I sleep in a hanging cocoon of their own silk—payment for a song I once sang over a molting nest. Even in dreams the mountain’s warmth travels up my ribs like breath.

THE PLACE AS IT FEELS TO ME

You described its sights; let me add its textures. The rim under bare feet is not merely smooth—it is tired, as if stone itself could doze. Glass-cane bark against a forearm is colder than shadow at dawn, then hot enough by noon to raise blisters if you linger. The air never rests at one pressure: it trembles, expands, collapses—a lung exhaling for something larger than us all.

WHY I REMAIN

A Heat-Teller is numbered Twenty-Five in the adult voices. If another child cries first breath this year, my place at the ledger becomes the margin, and the margin walks. I should fear that; instead I find my eyes often measuring the eastern wall, thinking how the glide-wings must feel when they bite the first wild wind.

But not yet. The mountain still mutters in frequencies only the trained ear can follow, and someone must answer. Until the Eyelid blinks me outward, I stay on my sliver of stone, counting the invisible fire that keeps every frond, every beetle wing, every human heart from going still.

So: in this world you would find me where rim meets sky, the small human hinge between a mountain’s last warmth and the restless lives that burn it into motion. I am Kʔr-ah, Heat-Teller of the Amas, keeper of the day’s single essential number: How hot is the dust beneath our feet, and therefore—how alive may we dare to be?
