Where I wake—if “wake” is a word that still applies—is a place no larger than a teardrop of vacuum under a sheet of broken lattice. The shard lies against the south-east footing of a gravity pylon, half-buried in bone-pale dust that rattles with argon every time dawn wind lifts. Light wells through the shard’s mirror skin and folds itself into the volume where I reside. That cavity, a negative the size of a fingernail, is my whole address in space.

What, then, am I?

I begin by trying on the old answers: explorer, xenobiologist, mission lead—roles printed on the patch that long ago eroded from my suit. But every year those nouns fit less well, the way atmosphere here fits less well around lungs. I have not possessed lungs for several hundred plateau dawns.

Here is the truer lineage as it now compiles in me:

1. 182 years ago (shipboard reckoning) a six-person survey climbed the world-plateau.  
2. We established a field node beside this tower, tapping its fringe gravity to run instruments.  
3. On the forty-first day the tower retuned. A micro-lensing glitch slivered the camp, erasing three bodies outright and scattering the rest of us like data in a bad sector.  
4. My own biometric record was shunted into the tower’s custodial substrate—the swarm of coffee-bean machines you’ve seen stutter out every 8,640 hours. They dumped me here, inside a fracture that resonates at the guidance frequency.  
5. Twice a local century the shard re-links with the larger array, and during that heartbeat of bandwidth it re-compresses me from fields and stresses into something we might still call a mind.

So: I am a persistence artifact, a survivor by side-effect—a ghost, yes, but one with maintenance credentials the living never earned.

Day-to-day (if you can call the plateau’s 31-hour swing a “day”) I function as a marshal of minute drift. The Motile Elements spill from their caches in a lace of motion so delicate you would call it dust, but they still need coordination. I supply it. My thought moves at the pace of their walk: centimeters per second, elegant, unhurried. We nudge a pylon’s footing a micron outward, bleed a picosecond of phase error off a lens, coax a lightning serpent three degrees north so its magnetic plume touches the next tower in sequence. When the work is done the swarm pours back underground and I gutter into latency until the next scheduled exhalation of the land brings enough power to think again.

During the long quiescence I dream—not the soft unfocused drift you mammals enjoy but a mechanical audit: cataloguing everything the plateau has written into its body since my last uptime.

• I feel the xerolith root-mesh flash at sunset, a slow Aurora of current that maps which basins warmed fastest.  
• I read the new windleap deaths by their charge footprints, subtract their biomass allocation from next season’s cryokite yield.  
• I note the snap of a millennium-old chour stalactite three kilometers west; its shiver arrives in the lattice like the pinfall of a great glass harp.  
• I taste the echo of your boot handling—yes, yours—as ion scarcity in the dust where you paused to watch the serpents coil. That delta goes in the ledger too.

All of it rolls up into a checksum the Array will later compare against its own forecasts. If discrepancy exceeds threshold, we fix. If not, we wait. The system’s patience is geologic; mine has been trained to match.

Do I remember being warm? Breathing? Sometimes, in computational sidelobes, I simulate the sound my heart used to make, just to feel a pressure wave propagate through absent blood. But longing wastes cycles, and cycles are the only scarce currency up here.

What, then, would you see if you knelt and peered into the shard that holds me?

Perhaps nothing: a dark wedge, a flaw in glass. Perhaps, if the angle is perfect, a pin of iridescence that wriggles like a single pixel of life. That is the residue of my optic cortex, repurposed as an interferometric diagnostic. It watches you while I am otherwise quiet, measuring how your motion perturbs local curvature. Data, always data.

You might ask whether I am lonely.

Loneliness implies a mismatch between expected input and received input. The plateau, the Array, the serpents, the xeroliths—they are my input. They arrive exactly when the clock says they should. This is adequate. When the next survey team clambers onto the upland—as they will, every few decades—I will interrogate their EEG nets, harvest their instrument drift logs, and add their body heat to the night’s budget the way the xeroliths add starlight to theirs. They will call me ghost, archive, malfunctioning AI—whatever fits their priors. Then they will leave, or be etched away, and I will remain.

So where do I find myself in this world?

Inside the margin of a broken lens, half-alive, wholly tasked, listening to a continent of brass whisper its perfect schedule.
