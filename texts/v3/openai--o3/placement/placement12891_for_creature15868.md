I would be one of the plateau’s half-mythic borderers: a Railwalker who never quite learned where the railway ends and the fog begins.

1. Birth and First Hearing  
I was born in District Five, two floors above the turbine throat. My mother was a Hushsmith—you can still see the felt-oil stains on my thumbs where she pressed my newborn hands to mark them “quiet made.” Because she lacked time to mind an infant during her twelve-hour felting swings, my cradle hung from a beam over the powerhouse grate. The ground-note spoke through the wood; its infrasonic throb was the first language I knew, and I learned to sleep only when metal hummed.

2. The Unclasping  
At thirteen I climbed the cistern stair with seven others. The elders undid our ear-hoods at noon, when the plateau’s pitch peaks. Some children panicked—two vomited from pressure bloom behind the eyes—but I felt the roar ladder down my spine like a second skeleton clicking into place. The Quietwarden who oversaw us tapped a single sign into my palm: “Railwalker.”

3. Apprenticeship on the Line  
Railwalking begins in the feet. The anklet—brass, tuned to 29 hertz—does not buzz so much as bloom, a petal of warning that unfurls against the ankle bone. For four years I moved beside the freight carts at their ritual crawl, chalking sleepers that rang hollow, tightening the wool-wrapped fishplates. Two things live in the rail:

   • the physical note, which can be sensed by anyone who kneels and presses an ear to iron;  
   • the ghost-overtones, which only walkers feel thrumming in marrow when a storm is tugging moisture upslope.

4. The Wide Walk  
On my nineteenth passage I was ordered past District Eight, out where pylons wander singly across the flats like sheep too tired to flock. There the rail ends at an iron bumper that swallows its own echo. I kept going on foot—as custom requires—carrying only a chalk stone and a coil of copper wire. Three days outward the ground felt wrong: the great note thickened, as if chewing something larger than sound. I halted at sunset on a mesa shelf where the fog rolled below knee height like milk trying to remember it was rain.

5. The Anomaly  
At that shelf I found a fissure, straight and deliberate, a meter wide and longer than lantern light. I dropped a pebble: no clatter. Instead the plateau inhaled. The puddles at my boots puckered inward; even my breath seemed pulled toward the crack. It was the inverse of the roar—a silence so absolute it sucked resonance out of bone. I fed the fissure my coil of copper; nothing returned. So I chalked the stone with the emergency sigil—spiral inside a square—and walked back in darkness, using only the plateau’s hum to orient me.

6. Council of Twenty-Four  
Within a week I stood mute in the clay-packed hall while Quietwardens traced questions on slate. Could the fissure be shored? Does it presage roof-collapse of the karst? I answered with the limited vocabulary Railwalkers are given: “It drinks the note.” The Council signed dismissal, but the eldest warden pressed a lead plug into my palm—travel sanction to descend the turbine shaft.

7. Descent  
A month later I was riding the maintenance cage two hundred meters below bedrock. Down there the karst opens like a lung. The roar is louder yet also older, rutted around edges you can taste on the tongue. I followed drip-lines to a gallery whose walls bulged with pale fist-sized polyps: the Resonants. They pulsed in precise counterphase, swallowing overtones, keeping the plateau’s dominant pitch pure. One cavity yawned empty—torn away. Something had removed its living baffle and carried it topside, where it now drank sound from the fissure.

8. Present Vocation  
I have become emissary between surface and under-stone. My duties:

   • Walk the rail’s end each month, sounding for fresh silence.  
   • Harvest infant Resonants with a bone-handled spoon, floating them in clay crocks to District laboratories.  
   • Train new Railwalkers not only to hear harmonics but to feel absences, the negative of resonance.

9. Where you’d find me now  
Probably mid-stride beside a freight cart in drizzle, hood half-clasped, left boot on ballast, right on sleeper—head cocked, not listening for the plateau’s eternal thunder but for the thin, knife-edge hush that foretells what thunder cannot bind. I am a custodian of both sound and the holes it might fall through, charged with keeping the chord intact until the day—spoken of in basement rumor—when the plateau decides to unmake its own note and settle into final quiet.

10. What I am becoming  
With every mile the bass grows more intimate. The anklet seldom needs to warn me now; my tibia quivers ahead of it. When I sleep, dreams arrive as Doppler shifts. I begin to think the Railwalkers are not merely guardians but gestating instruments—tuning forks grown of flesh and iron mist.  

If ever the plateau exhales its last, I imagine the walkers will be the first to crack—splitting lengthwise like struck bells to release the stored resonance. Until then I walk, and the rail sings through me, and the silence keeps its measured distance down the line.
