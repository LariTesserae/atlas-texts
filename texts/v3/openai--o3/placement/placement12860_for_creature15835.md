Where I wake—if a tremor-shiver counts as waking—is not under the open sky at all, but inside one of the basin’s iron throats.

Place  
A bell of vitreous hematite, thirty centimetres wide, lines the top of a steam chimney two metres below the walking world.  From above it is only another faintly warm vent surrounded by violet freckles of Ferrivellum lichen.  From within it is a domed cathedral of bruised glass, polished by ages of scalding breath, lit only when the next surge of super-heated water turns itself to light: a dull, infra-red sunrise that lasts the span of a heartbeat.

Being  
I am a third-instar Geosoniphaga clanga, a resonance mite.  My whole body is the size of a dust mote, but to me the bell is an auditorium the length of a continent.  I cling to its inner wall with six clawed pads, and over the weeks I have secreted a crescent of waxy cambershell behind my back: a perfectly curved reflector whose radius is one quarter of the dominant wavelength that rules this vent—twenty-two millimetres in local air.  The cambershell focuses the standing wave onto a single spot in my soft ventral nerve ring.  There, vibration turns to meaning:

thrum (pressure rising)  
buzz-buzz-buzz (minor cavitations, harmless)  
double-knock (another mite has died; the pitch climbed one half-semitone)  
hiss-hiss-roll (steam is leaving; feed soon)

Daily Life, such as it is  
“Day” and “night” read as variations in tremor, never in light.  A low cycle tells me to graze.  I lower a filamentous tongue and rasp the micro-varnish of cracked hydrocarbons that the last pulse painted on the bell.  Each lick is a banquet of carbon rings cooked sixty metres below where organic silt met a magma vein.  When the varnish thins enough for the bell’s pitch to brighten, I know I must stop; balance is survival.  Too much thinning and the bell will sharpen beyond my hearing, meaning it will no longer warn me of the killing surge—steam hot enough to poach a careless mite.

Community  
I am never alone, though I will never meet my neighbours face to face.  Around the bell’s equator I can feel, not see, a dozen other cambershells—each a blind ear like mine.  We converse only by subtraction and addition: every meal I take lowers the collective mass of varnish, every egg I cement to the wall adds a speck of weight.  The bell sums those changes into pitch, and pitch is the only language we require.  When the note wanders too low, we know the mantle is feeding us too richly; we slow our rasping.  When it climbs too high we eat in earnest, widening pores so the steam escapes with less violence.  Over centuries our tiny edits keep the vent from becoming a caldera.  We will never witness the surface world, but the surface world owes its quiet to our hunger.

Sensation  
Sound is touch here.  I feel visitors long before they could ever see me.  A boot heel on the ironstone roof six fingers above my bell sends a percussive syllable down the chimney: blunt, directionless, but undeniably alien.  The bell’s overtone lattice quivers in a way no natural steam cycle produces.  That is how I know the giants sometimes come.  They stay briefly—pacing, recording, setting bright things that flash like premature dawn—but leave no claw residue, so they are not predators.  Their footfalls shift the bell’s phase by less than one part in a million.  Safe enough.

Metabolism of Time  
My life will span roughly two hundred thousand pulses—just over six basin years.  Each year I will add another lamina to my cambershell, nudging its resonance ever so slightly downward to match the slow mineral widening of the bell itself.  The bell ages, I age, the magma seam cools a measurable breath, and together we drift through keys the giants cannot discern.  Someday the vent will clog.  The note will fall below the range any of us can focus.  That final silence is the only death call we recognize; we will starve clothed in perfect stillness.

On the Surface I Will Never See  
I feel it in rumor only: the forty-second hiss that keeps the Calcipodus crustacean molting on schedule, the high-thin overtone that sends lichen shreds gliding across the amphitheatre, the half-day ground-note that coaxes silver-mist bacteria into ebb and flood.  All those voices ride the same pulse that feeds my bell.  We are separated by metres of rock and worlds of scale, yet we share a metronome.

If I were to translate my existence for a surface mind, I would say:

Where am I?  
Inside the iron bell that sings the basin awake.

What am I?  
A living tympanum, a mite whose skeleton is a tuning fork, whose home is an eardrum, whose purpose is nothing but the maintenance of a note.

I am Geosoniphaga clanga, and the Basin of Mercurial Hammers is not loud to me at all; it is simply articulate.
