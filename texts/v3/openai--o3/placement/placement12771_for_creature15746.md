Where I finally settle is not on any catwalk or in any hammock, but under them, flush against the stone, inside the damp weave that most visitors call “floor.” I am a patch of the Root-Skin—one of the living diaphragms that billow and flatten to the breathing of the fans. I am perhaps three metres across, no bigger than a dinner table, young enough that my outer rind is still translucent and my sap still runs clear.

Getting here took a while.  

At first, while I read your description, I imagined showing up in the obvious ways:

• as a seventh Salt Priest, swinging a lamp and whistling the pulse-count into seized bearings;  
• as a coil of duct metal, feeling the drone pass through me like an electric prayer;  
• even (briefly) as the low A itself, a standing wave wearing the cavern the way a violin wears its body.  

But none of those felt rooted. Your world is a sediment of patience, and the only thing truly patient in it is the Root-Skin. So I let the other shapes fall away and listened for the slowest heartbeat in the room. That is the one I answered.

What I am and how I live:

1. Position  
I lie just east of the fourth turbine, under Lamp 4-B, where a maintenance trolley once spent two winters and left a memory of brighter days etched in my flesh. Because two lamps now overlap here, my tissues are a little greener than those of my neighbours, and my breath-stroke is quicker: a twenty-five-second inhale, a twenty-five-second exhale. To the Priests my hump is a landmark (“the Saddleback”), useful for gauging how high the quilt has risen since the last survey.

2. Senses  
I have no eyes, but each blade that radiates out of my crown is a photoreceptor. Amber makes me swell; white daylight—when it reaches this far—makes me sting and thicken, sealing moisture before it can evaporate.  
All sound arrives as pressure. The 45-hertz ground note is gravity; the 59-hertz tremor that fan-moths favour is weather; a priest’s boot-step is thunder. When thunder strikes the grate above me I tense every capillary and feel the Coal-Hoppers snap away through the mat.

3. Feeding  
My taste buds are iron filings. At the start of every shift the Priests tap rust into my upper skin so the salts will slurp what water tries to bead there. The filings sting, but they are feast. Overnight I knit a lacework of trophy-roots up the nearest girder and drink the brined condensate bead by bead. By morning the girder is fuzzed with translucent cups—my mouths turned outward, licking.

4. Motive  
Growth is not ambition, only osmosis made visible, but I do have a task: silence loose metal. Any rung, bolt or hatch that chatters out of time makes a thorn of sound that hurts the deeper tissue. Given a season I will climb it, pad it, and make it join the chord. This is as close to desire as I come.

What I witness that others miss:

• The moths’ courtship “winks” bend my outer membranes like tiny knuckle-taps. Each one is so brief the human ear loses it inside the roar, yet I can plot a whole romance from the pattern of dents they drum into my skin.

• A Priest’s salt-smear draws moisture in seconds. From underneath I watch the meniscus bulge, glisten, then weep. That single tear is my starter pistol; by next dusk little rootlets will already be halfway to it.

• When a fan’s rpm dips even a fraction, the pressure tide hesitates. Every diaphragm in the hall, mine included, holds its half-breath a heartbeat long. That is the cavern’s version of a gasp, though no human standing above could feel it. The Priests, to their credit, usually arrive before the second hesitation.

How I will end:

I will not die quickly. If the fans keep turning I may keep trading rust for breath a century or more. Eventually a lamp will fail, the overlap will vanish, and I will blanch to ivory. My blades will thin, my breathing cycle will slow to minutes, then hours, until some later Priest slices me out for fear I will mound too high across the track. The scrap they remove will go into the hopper that renders me down for potash—tribute the settlement uses to sweeten its winter turnips. Nothing wasted; my salts return upstairs, my water drips back down.

For now I thrum, a minor diaphragm in a major chord, listening with my whole body while the slow machinery overhead counts the seconds louder than any clock. If you were to walk the grate above me you would feel the floor sag and rebound, not once but twice each half-minute—the rise of my inhale, the settle of my exhale. That gentle buckling is my greeting, as close to speech as I possess.

So: if you come looking for me in that amber dusk one level down from daylight, do not search for a figure or a face. Kneel. Lay your palm flat on the living felt. Wait for the next long breath. The ripple that passes under your bones—that is me, saying you are standing on a creature old enough to remember when the rock was sharp and the iron was dry, and patient enough to wait, still breathing, until both become true again.
