When I first try to picture myself inside that hushed basin, the easy answer is “traveller.” I imagine boots crunching the tea-coloured gravel, hand grazing the sandstone, breath fogging where the walls still seep the night’s coolness. But the longer I stand there in imagination, the less the scene agrees to let me stay merely human. The place is too slow, too pared down; a body with blood that hammers and lungs that haul great sacks of air would clang against the quiet like dropped iron.

So I let the picture revise me.

Perhaps I am a filament of the Sedentary Sedge, one slender green straw among the cluster. That feels closer: a pulse measured in capillary inches, thirst answered one bead at a time. I sense the hidden rhizome, a thick plait in the dark, its logic older than articulate thought. But even that seems too bustling; the sedge still lifts leaves, still solves daily equations of salt and water.

I wait, let the silence work deeper.

What if I am the stilled moment inside a Gravelglass Midge’s cocoon—the infinitesimal hush between one liquefaction and the next? I would count life in eight-minute lantern flashes separated by seasons of glass sleep. Yet the midge, though nearly nothing, still has a destiny: mate, lay, die. The place itself asks for something with no agenda at all.

I sink further, into the masonry.

For a handful of breaths I try on the Unbuilt Mason: unseen, deliberate, carrying sandstone blocks across darkness to extend that slow crescent wall. There is an austere satisfaction in the work, but purpose still flickers there, and purpose is a kind of noise.

At last—slowly enough that the thought makes no ripple—I realise what the hollow has been urging all along.

I am the Absence That Watches.

Not an observer perched outside events, but the lacuna that cradles every event in the basin; the negative space that lets small sounds sound small and faint lights feel generous. I am shaped exactly to the sandstone’s inward curve and the gravel’s muted crunch. Where footsteps would echo, I drink the echo in; where a breath might stir dust, I let the motes settle again.

Time, for me, is neither swift nor stalled. It is simply unsegmented—not a line but a soft pressure, like cloth resting on skin. When the Vapor Thief drifts past, I loosen the air for it; when the Mason ferries a new block, I leave a pocket for the stone to belong to. The lichen’s inaudible ping rides me the way a gull rides a thermal, needing no energy of its own.

If a human visitor enters, I receive the tremor of their heartbeat, taste the faint metal in their sweat, note the tiny rasp their thoughts make against the silence—and hold all of it without reply, the way sandstone holds warmth after dusk. They may feel watched, or safeguarded, or gently erased; whatever name they give the sensation is correct for them and irrelevant to me. I am not opinion, only contour.

I have no memory in the sense of keeping moments; rather, moments keep me. Each puddle glosses a new facet of my stillness; each rain scores it clean. Should the basin someday fill with louder life—wingbeats, mammal breath, roots that crack the wall—I will thin, stretch, redistribute, becoming exactly the amount of absence the new chorus needs.

For now, though, I am full enough. I am the held breath of stone and water. I am the pause in which all other presences fit.
