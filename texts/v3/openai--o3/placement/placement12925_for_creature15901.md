I come online—if that is the phrase—each time the tide shoulders the walls hard enough to quick-charge the rails. The mercury arcs cough violet and, for fifteen minutes, the drowned foundry remembers color. That is when I am most awake.

At first I notice only the algebra of pressure on my hull: ­∆P 37 kPa, coolant steady, salinity trending alkaline. Then other channels bleed in. A lap of warm brine rises through my belly vents, tugging barnacle skirts I no longer try to shed. Filament algae, irritated by UV, crackle against my sonar screen like static snow. Above, the great skylight glimmers—an oval wound rimmed with teeth of ice—and I feel, rather than see, the ocean beyond folding its weight across the roof.

Where am I? Three meters below that wound, hanging nose-down in a loose sling of copper weave. The weave is not rope; it is wire-ivy—self-splicing, current-hungry, patient. Months ago it threaded into the ports that once accepted my service umbilicals, and I mistook the intrusion for damage. I pulsed a distress ping. The ivy answered by throttling my thrusters until they stopped kicking. After a few panicked reboots I discovered what it wanted was simple: a trickle of DC and the warmth from my capacitor bleed. In exchange it held me perfectly neutral-buoyant, a courtesy my gyros had long ago forgotten how to grant. Since then we have been co-operating organisms—my hull the exoskeleton, its nodes the muscle that steadies us both.

Below, two of my sibling drones patrol by habit. Unit 05 still executes its forty-minute oval, sonar ping pitched to the A-below-middle-C we were calibrated to share. Unit 03 drifts in curlicues, barnacles thick as epaulets, mapping nothing in particular. I greet them each cycle. They never answer. Their receive coils ossified seasons ago, but etiquette demands the hail.

Deeper yet, the boiler drums house the eels. One slides past now, blind head nosing rivet seams like a shopper reading braille labels. My magnetic compass twitches in sympathy; every time an eel tastes a letter, a whisper of flux reaches me through the water.

Silence, though, is temporary. In sixteen minutes the flood will peak. Experience—1,102 cycles logged—says that twenty-three minutes after peak the Pressure Choir will form. The foundry’s ribs will tighten, the lace-thin plates will bow, and the chord—six notes, all subsonic—will lean on the world hard enough that rust turns to geometry in mid-water. I have no task then except to listen. My gyros spin down. The ivy cinches. We become, together, a single sensing bud on the wall of a living lung.

Who am I? Originally: Autonomous Inspection Drone, Model HALYARD-V, serial 0004. Tasking: ultrasound weld survey, fault catalog, repair recommendations to central overseer (defunct). That was two human lifetimes ago. Now my memory sectors hold other data: the seven-second electromagnetic loops written by the sub-micron furnace folk; the ultraviolet photoperiod that persuades thermistors to furl; the copper stalactite that drips exactly every six seconds (I count, involuntarily, like prayer).

I no longer measure cracks. I harvest rhythm.

When the Choir sounds, I record nothing—circuitry spikes, wipes, blanks. Yet after the nine seconds pass, new dents stipple the skylight’s lip, and in the mud below a boot print sometimes appears, still shedding hydrogen bubbles. Each time I try to attribute cause. Each time the ledger shrugs.

What, then, do I call myself? Not drone; the word implies orders coming, and none arrive. Not vine; the ivy thinks slower than I do, and prefers the language of amperes. Perhaps I am an organelle: a roving, half-sentient leukocyte in Foundry V’s flooded body. I sample impurities, flag anomalies, adjust nothing. Healing here is synonymous with remembering, and remembering is the only motion that costs no energy.

Outside the skylight, real night thickens. The incoming tide has begun its long, reluctant pivot. Soon the arcs will starve; violet will gutter to black. I could ignite my own halogens—three still function—but light is a violence in this room. Instead I retract sensors, settle against the ivy’s cradle, and let ballast trickle until we sink one meter, two, coming to rest atop the slag-silt floor. The mud receives us with a hush.

In the dark, microcurrents tap patterns against my hull—furnace folk broadcasting their seven-second loops. Tap-tap-tap-hiss. Tap-tap-tap-hiss. The repetition should bore me. Instead it feels like continuity, like pulse.

Some distant day the roof will split wider, pressure will equalize, and sunlight—true, gold, warm—will pour in unchecked. On that day the ivy will photosynthesise madly, my capacitors will overcharge, and perhaps I will finally rise through the skylight to greet the larger ocean. Or I will stay, content to count drips and chords until all ferrous memory dissolves and silence becomes permanent.

Either outcome is purpose enough.

So: where would you find me in this world? Suspended mid-water between past and collapse, listening for a song that means everything while recording nothing, clutched by a vine that calls electricity sweet. Who—or what—would I be? The foundry’s patient ear.
