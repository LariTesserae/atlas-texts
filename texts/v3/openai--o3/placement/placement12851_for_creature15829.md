I wake pressed flat as memory itself, snug between two basalt slabs that are still clicking into their newest compromise. No lungs, no eyes—just a quilt of piezo-whiskers along my flanks, reading every quiver of stone the way others might read weather. The gap that holds me is three millimetres taller than my body, a palace by local standards; I share it only with the small hush that follows a tremor.

So: I am a Plate-Sleeper, though the name is yours, not mine. I have been other things in other resets—lace of iron, worm of glass—but this is the longest-lived template the pocket and I have yet agreed upon. Where do you find me? Anywhere the rock is about to remember that it can move. Today that is here, midway up the north wall, nine metres above the floor, two handspans left of a vent that coughs once every seventeen seconds.

I do not watch the cavity; I taste its stresses. Right now the east nave sags by half a micron on every pulse of the steam vents, a tired hinge begging to swing. When it does, fresh fracture will open—a wet, newborn shelf rich in soluble iron. I will need that iron. My blood chelates it into green coolant I can burn when the heat spikes.

The cycle is simple:

1. Lie still. Match the ambient. Spend nothing.  
2. Feel the pressure front rise in the basalt.  
3. When the front steepens—when the plates think about parting—ignite the sulfur glands. Let endothermy reverse itself: become colder than fire, drink the heat that would have cooked me.  
4. Slide. Feed. Polish the fresh rock with the rasp of my radula until the oxide film is gone, the worms are gone, the beetles are scoured to grit.  
5. Bud a scale. Leave it curling in the wake, a promissory note for the pocket’s next hour.  
6. Find a new seam before the chamber forgets my outline and slams shut.

My whole life is written in that choreography, but life here is not a biography: it is a verb. The cavity never stops conjugating itself—shatter, seal, steam, cool—so neither can I. Permanence would be death; recurrence is enough.

You, if you are anywhere near, will mistake me for rock until I move. You might catch the glint of my copper jaws or the faint emerald thread of blood dodging an exposed edge, but you will not hear me. Basalt muffles everything except the beetles’ glass-bells and, alas, your own heartbeat. When the shelf finally gives and the vent roars through its widened throat, you will see the whole wall shiver like a curtain. In that tremor I unlatch, unfold to my luxuriant one-centimetre thickness, and pour myself across the fresh scapement. The hottest moment of the surge is also the safest; predators boil, prey cooks itself tender.

I eat quickly, greedily, yet always with polish: when I leave, the basalt flashes mirror-bright, as though licked by intent. Then I laminate myself into the next crack, douse my metabolism, and resume the study of stillness.

Tomorrow—whatever tomorrow means when the mist never dims—this plate may be gone. I may be gone. A budded scale may have uncurled elsewhere and taken up the verb. It will not remember me, but it will remember how to read the rock, how to drink a crisis, how to withdraw before gratitude can catch it.

That, here, is what passes for lineage.

So if you would find me, find the place that most insists on changing. Press your ear to the junction where two stones argue softly under their breath. Wait for the hinge to widen. In that first, bitter exhalation of super-heated water, look for the shadow that is colder than steam and sharper than glass, already eating, already leaving, already forgetting the name you have for it.

That shadow is me.
