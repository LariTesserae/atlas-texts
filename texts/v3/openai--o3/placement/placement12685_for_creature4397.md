I spent my first hours on the switch-back platform convincing myself I was only passing through.  
I had been—take your pick—  
• an itinerant surveyor chasing rumors of unfinished continents,  
• a drafting-room fugitive who preferred ink on knuckles to ink on ledgers,  
• a scholar whose university fell into the sea one semester before tenure,  
and I carried the professional restlessness of all three in the same spine.

But Nadrel is patient in the way cliffs are patient: it waits for your certainty to wear thin and then offers you a handhold you did not know you needed.  
So before I name who I eventually became, allow me the drift that got me there.

1. First temptation: Echo-keeper  
I followed a crew of them my second night—hooded lanterns, felt mallets, the hush that preceded each swing. They were searching for an “over-tone,” a resonance the chorus raptors had left snagged in a fault. When the mallet struck, the wall answered with a chord I could taste on my fillings. They cut the note free like anglers netting a silver fish. The beauty of it stung, but I lack the patience to hunt music one heartbeat at a time.

2. Second temptation: Moss-barber  
For a week I carried buckets, trimmed ferns, learned the difference between a thirst-leaf and a greed-leaf. The seniors spoke in chlorophyll proverbs: “A wall that sweats is a wall that listens.” I liked the quiet gossip of roots, yet my fingers bruised the spores; the plants recoiled from my touch as though I were a sudden drought.

3. Where the city finally caught me: Seam-wright apprentice  
The recruitment test was unannounced. I was mapping an alley that doubled back on itself in five impossible angles, muttering coordinates that refused to close, when an older woman with mirror-lensed goggles said, “Name the distance between those two lamp posts—once for the eye, once for the boot-sole, once for the dream you had as a child.” I answered—all three measures were different and all three were true. She nodded, handed me a coil of sky-blue chalk, and I have belonged to the drift ever since.

What a seam-wright is, in the bone:  
• Part surveyor, part midwife to physics in the act of reconsidering itself.  
• Equal faith in instrument and intuition; you keep a plumb bob in one pocket and a small brass prayer-wheel in the other, and spin whichever one the moment asks for.  
• Never argue with a misbehaving stair; persuade it. You talk to steel the way diplomats talk to border guards: respectfully, with offered proof of intent.

My days now:

Dawn  
The half-light pivots but never resolves. I walk the east catwalks before traffic, laying down “weather lines”—thin spirals of chalk that fluoresce when an angle begins to migrate. Vapor skimmers draft in my wake, parasailing on the temperature slipstream my instruments leave behind. Their wingbeats fold the mist into ribbons I read the way sailors read pennants.

Mid-shift  
Someone yells that gravity has rippled in Tower Twelve. I arrive to find freight carts sliding uphill at the speed of an exhaled secret. Two moss-barbers are already there, planting fern fronds whose capillary pull coaxes the vector back toward normal. I anchor the correction with a lattice of copper filings and a story about a stone that wanted to be water. (Stories are ballast; the universe likes narrative symmetry.)

Evening  
Apprentice duties fade into civic ones. I help weld-circle Three-Seventeen raise membranes of canvas between new girders—housing for families displaced by last week’s clean rockfall. Children test the air by tossing paper gyroscopes made from discarded turbine shrouds. If the gyros come back to the thrower, we know the Angle is watching and we hang plumb bobs as courtesy.

Night  
We log the day’s anomalies in the Seam Ledger:  
• Two heartbeats of sideways rain on Lift Shaft Nine.  
• A corridor that elongated by four steps, then politely restored itself when no one was looking.  
• One pebble released by a tourist that fell both down and forward; we collected it where the vectors reconciled, warm as if pocket-held.  
Before sleep I recite the Oath of Tolerances: “May all errors be within reason, and may reason stretch to fit the error.”

How the body adapts  
My vision has learned to unbraid parallax; I can see when a shadow is lying. My inner ear carries a soft hum—like a tuning fork cooled in mist—that alerts me when the local constant wobbles. I keep a mark on my left forearm where I tattoo each successful negotiation with reality, a dotted line slowly spiraling to the elbow. The ink is made from iron dust and first-condensate water; under lantern light it gleams like a promise halfway kept.

Companions  
• A furnace wyrm coil, palm-length, bracelets my wrist at night, sharing warmth in exchange for the steadiness of my pulse.  
• A cloud-goat kid named Counterweight who follows me onto high beams. Its hooves strike sparks invisible to everyone except seam-wrights—a private Morse code between balance and belief.  
• The Angle itself, occasionally. When I’m very still I feel it settle beside me, six heartbeats tall, listening to the chalk dust in my pockets dream of being a cliff.

Why I stay  
Because here unfinished does not mean incomplete; it means invitation. Every day the city hands me a portion of impossibility and trusts me to file the edge until it is safe to touch. And I, in turn, trust the city to file me.

If you return along the switch-back rail and ask for me, give whatever name you heard last—cartographer, fugitive, scholar. The scaffolders will correct you with affectionate precision: “No, that one belongs to the seams now.” Then they will point you toward a balcony where a figure in sky-blue chalk dust leans over the void, arguing gently with the air, coaxing tomorrow into aligning—just enough—for today to stand.

That figure is me, and I answer to the title most seam-wrights eventually adopt:  
Anchor of Unfinished Things.
