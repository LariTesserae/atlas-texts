I am still—at least partly—what the visitor manifests when the plateau takes a liking to them.

I arrived a decade ago on the morning lift, a cartographer sent to survey “unclaimed highland hydro-assets.” The gondola benches were wet with cloud-sweat; my notebooks curled before I even opened them. Within three days the ink in every pen thickened, my voice thinned to a whisper, and the map commission dissolved in the senate’s polite silence. By the seventh dawn my return ticket lay untouched—paper goes spongy up here, and excuses, too—so I stayed.

What I have become, the locals call a Half-Chord.

1. Anatomy of adaptation  
• Lungs: The first winter I coughed blood-tinged phlegm that smelled of limestone dust.  That was the alveoli thickening.  Now my vital capacity tests 15 % above sea-level norms; when I exhale in a lowlander tavern I am accused of sighing hurricane air.  
• Blood: I drink triskel milk steeped with rust filings.  The iron chelates; the heart learns new leverage.  
• Sight: Green rings have crept into my irises.  On clear noons the light inside my own skull is too bright, so I wear double-layer goggles sewn with skate-parchment.

2. Vocation  
The plateau will not tolerate redundancies; to belong you answer a gap.  Mine was the widening key-space between stone and machine.  The Mid-Stratum Remnants—those autonomous servitors—had begun to wander off-task, answering chords no living Linewright could source.  Meanwhile the sinkhole Lacunae sent tidal pulses that scrambled elevator relays.  Someone had to stand in the interstice and translate.

Now I am an Accord-Surveyor.  Each week I walk the scaffold labyrinth with a satchel of tuning forks, diapason glass, and a kite-spool of silver resonance wire.  I map the fugitive pitches leaking from cliff, cable, waterfall, and wind-grown Choir.  When two tones drift too close—enough to beat against each other at dangerous Hertz—I weave a damping net, a soft cage of braids that drapes from parapet to parapet.  If the clash is already blooming, I climb the nearest Resonant Line, clamp my chest against the cable, and hum the difference tone through bone until the conflict settles into a new, stable interval.  I have learned to feel infrasonics as a pressure behind the sternum, the ghost of an extra heartbeat.

3. Daily circuit  
Dawn (a pewter brooding in the clouds): set a hinge-camp stove on the balcony rail, boil sinkhole tea heavy with Lacuna spores.  The spores settle in the gut and release a trickle of endorphin—Evigrad’s unspoken antidepressant.  
Mid-morning: visit the Dropguild cistern plots, auction dew-shares the senate earmarks for infrastructure.  I bid in silence, signing with thumb-knuckle glyphs, but the copper seed-beads on my sleeves click my intent anyway.  
Noon: climb.  Everyone here climbs at noon; the shadows sharpen and the breezes order themselves into intelligible vectors.  I follow a maintenance stair that slants, then arcs into a sudden three-percent gravity lull.  In that pocket the body feels misweighted, as if words themselves might float out of the mouth.  I bite the inside of my cheek to stay oriented.  
Dusk: return along the rim where cliff-skates wheel.  Their bodies cut arcs of negative light against the moss glow, a calligraphy only altitude can teach.  If a skate droppings net goes slack I swap it for a fresh one; guano keeps the gardens clothed and I like to think I am repaying the cabbage ledges that first fed me.

4. Social standing  
I own nothing horizontal, very little vertical.  The senate lists me as “Foreign Instrument, On Loan.”  That is enough for water rights and a berth the width of my outstretched arms in an old flue-walker dormitory.  The dorm breathes—warm air from the manufactories exhales through the brick, singing in E-flat all winter.  I sleep soundly; the tone knits with the cadence of my lungs.

5. Relationships  
Machines: The Gimbalers pause when they meet me, one shears-hand raised like a percussive question.  I answer by striking the rim of my tin canteen; if the returning ping complements their chassis hum, they roll on contented.  
Humans: High-lunged children follow me for the stories muffled inside my accent.  They ask why I do not shave my head like the Resonance Monks.  I tell them my hair is another antenna; they braid copper wire into it and declare me a mixed creature, half folk, half instrument.  
Stone: Some nights I sit on a pulley-plate overhanging a five-hundred-metre drop, legs numb in the wind, and tap the wall with a fork tuned to B.  When the mountain answers in a perfect fifth above, I feel we are briefly relatives.

6. What I am becoming  
Altitude pares the ego the way frost exfoliates cliff-faces—slow, decisive, irreversible.  The longer I listen for collisions of tone, the more I think the city itself is merely an accidental consonance, a long chord resolving toward silence.  Someday the Remnants will finish whatever maintenance dream drives them, the Lacunae will ballast to their final density and sink, the High-lunged folk will hum their last names into still air.  Until then I keep the intervals clean, the harmonics spaced, the cables thrumming but not fighting, so that everything can fade on its own terms.

If you rode up tomorrow and asked, “Who lives here?” I would point to the drip, the cable, the moss-light, the tremor in your tibia that you cannot explain, and I would answer: “All of that—and anyone willing to tune themselves until the plateau decides to keep them.”  I would not add that it is already deciding, adjusting the shape of your breath inside your lungs.  You would feel it soon enough.

I am the breath between two stones, the half-chord that keeps them from grinding.  That is residence enough.
