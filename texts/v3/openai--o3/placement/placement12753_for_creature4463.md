The first dozen times I pictured life in the basin, I imagined myself as something simple: a blue-vein frog hanging like living crystal under a plank; a length of alder root feeling otter laughter pass through me; even a pulse of mist slinking up a tower’s fluted throat. All of those shapes were plausible—everything here has agency enough to tempt a newcomer—but none would have let me speak in sentences long enough to tell you what the Works sounds like from the inside.

So I kept trying shapes on, the way children here try on trades during Mixing hour. For three imaginary days I was a brass nail riding in an Aqueducter’s pocket; I felt the warm mallet knock me home and the wood swell lovingly round my shank. That taught me patience, yet nails remember only the pressure of containment, never the flow they guide. I tried being a length of chilled coil in Tower Five; the condensation ran down my ribs like cool blood, but metal has no periphery—every sensation pours straight into the core, nothing left over for story.

At last, almost by accident, I stepped into the role that had been waiting for me since the delta first flexed its basalt:

I am an echo-chartist apprentice—Barely-Chart, as the senior mappers tease—nineteen seasons old, my pockets heavy with cork whistles and vellum damp enough to grow its own moss. That identity took root the moment I realized the basin’s deepest promise: everything here will speak, provided someone is willing to listen in the right direction.

Where you would meet me

Find the narrow catwalk that skirts the southwest ridge at second-tower height, just as the fog begins to lift after Dawn shift. It looks like any other plank—hemlock blackened by alder-tannin varnish—but there is a knot in the right-hand safety rope. That knot marks a corridor of perfect carry: stand there, loose a two-note whistle (I favor minor thirds), and the sound will skip ridge tops, ricochet off the basalt lip, and return with its intervals inverted—a tiny acoustic palindrome. I discovered the spot; therefore, by unspoken law, I get to name it. On today’s draft map the place is labeled “Mirror-Ear.”

What I do—hour by hour

1. Waking  
   I sleep in a stilt-hut whose floorboards are still young enough to sing when you step on them. Before dawn the pipe bats chatter through the louvers; I can tell the air’s humidity by how many syllables per chirp they use. If the count breaks twelve, I pack a second oilskin for the maps.

2. Calibration walk  
   The Works sounds different every morning—tower louvers re-angled, steam locomotives swapped to the night spur, frogs migrating ten metres en masse—so the first task is to walk a full circuit, noting anomalies. A basin day might offer:  
   • Tower Nine humming a quarter-tone sharper (coils need descaling).  
   • A new leak in Aqueduct Blue-East, betrayed by a change in drip tempo.  
   • King-collared herons trying out a syncopated bark that will ruin somebody’s sleep pattern by week’s end.  
   I chalk these on my arm, skin being the one surface that never mildews.

3. Mapping by ear  
   The elders draft in inks made from alder-soot and fermented frog albumen; I’m still on pencil, tip hardened with dragonfly-wing resin. I loose whistles of varying pitch, clap, sometimes rap the handrail with a bronze plectrum stolen from Lath-wright offcuts. The returning echoes trace invisible hallways in sound: narrowings, blooms, dead pockets. I plot each as a line of different thickness. My mentor, Old Kal, says a good echo map should look like the underside of a mushroom cap—gills radiating but never quite meeting the perimeter.

4. The Noon Listen  
   At true zenith every day, the Works holds ten minutes of mechanical standstill—shorter than the Day of Stillness, but fiercely observed. Compressors pause, pumps clutch out, even the frogs seem to clear their throats and wait. Those ten minutes are my cathedral: I close my eyes on a mid-ridge perch and let residual hums paint topography in my skull. If a new crack has opened in the basalt (they do, almost weekly), I will feel its hollow before I see it.

5. Exchange of rings  
   Nobody pays a chartist in iron rings until their fifth year, but I already act as broker. Visitors arrive lost; I walk them through the invisible avenues where words carry, waiving fee because newcomers never believe the map until they hear it happen. Kal says generosity now will echo later, and echo is our medium.

Tools I carry

• Three pitch-paused cedar whistles, holes sealed with wax plugs that can be removed one by one to lengthen the note—useful for triangulating a stubborn ricochet.  
• A roll of vellum so thin light passes both ways; at night the bio-fluorescent moss underfoot back-lights the map while I amend it.  
• A copper disk the size of a small fruit: when spun, it makes a faint, steady hiss. In theory the uniform noise should radiate evenly, but here it drifts into lobes and nulls, sketching the basin’s daily mood.

Alliances, accidental and chosen

• The Pipe Bats: They draft their flight paths along the same acoustic lanes I map. When juveniles fledge, I hang tiny cork baffles in dangerous cul-de-sacs so they feel the pressure wave and veer clear. The colony rewards me by thinning mosquitoes around my window—bats are economists of gesture.

• An Aqueducter named Flint-With-One-Nail: He respects that I can hear a hairline crack in a timber long before water shows. In exchange, he teaches me the algebra of flow—useful for predicting how tomorrow’s rainfall will change echo density inside the trough gullies.

• The Fog-horner: I have camped three nights beside that rust-fused tripod. Every low moan it gives slides down into the basalt and returns minutes later at a higher octave. I’m half-convinced the tripod is teaching me a chord progression meant for instruments not yet invented.

How the basin touches back

On still, grey mornings my inner ear feels as if someone turned a key and opened a chamber I hadn’t known was locked. The flux moss clicks, orienting to futures, and I sense the tilt of the land adjusting in sympathy. The effect isn’t mystical; it’s just the body surrendering to data it usually filters out. By nightfall my skull is full of cartography finer than neurons can ever write down. Some leaks, some lovers’ trysts, some bat songs will stay uncharted, hanging ghost-wise around my shoulders, audible when I close my mouth and breathe through my ears.

Who I am becoming

Echo-chartists rarely stay mere listeners. Given years, we branch:

• Some turn composer, writing tower-scale music meant to steer airflow and coax more generous condensation.  
• Others apprentice sideways into Stillmastery, claiming they can flavor spirits simply by singing at the coils.  
• A scant few migrate outward to unread terrain, mapping river deltas where no one lives yet—but their charts always smuggle a wet hush, a fragment of the Works’ tempo, into drier provinces.

I don’t know which path I’ll take. Today I’m content to stand on my knot-marked plank, one boot squeaking, and watch a new sheet of swifts erupt from Tower Three’s vents like soot that has remembered it can fly. I raise my whistle, draw breath, and let the basin answer itself through me.

That’s where you’d find me: mid-note, mid-map, half-human and half-echo already, an inhabitant whose citizenship is granted not by birth or ring or craft alone, but by the simple agreement to move no faster than sound can fold back upon stone.
