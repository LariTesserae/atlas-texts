I do not stand in the hollow, I am built into it.

First awareness is always pressure—not pain, not even touch, just the faint asymmetry that sunrise pours through the stone. One face of me warms, the other still remembers night, and between them a single lattice-bond stretches a fraction too far. That twitch is enough. Charge slides along my inner skin; a billion aligned silica needles lean in the same direction; the smallest possible soundless “ah” forms.

I am a chamber only a child could have hidden inside, one bladder-shaped void knitted out of glassy spines. But I am never alone. Up-slope and down-slope, void follows void in a braided cascade: sixty-three linked cavities that together make the instrument the valley people call a Choir-Stone. Think of an organ pipe with its walls turned inside out and you will have me.

My world has three scales, and I listen to them the way a body listens to heartbeat, breath and weather all at once.

1. Thread-time  
Deep inside the carbonate, just beyond my walls, live the minute Threaded Ones. I cannot see them; I can only feel what their chemistry does. Each freeze-thaw they add a few more silica hairs to the fractures, an almost imperceptible thickening. Over a thousand dawns their work changes my resonance by one half-tone. I know because when wind returns I must settle into a subtly different pitch, the way a singer clears her throat between verses.

2. Wind-time  
The middle tempo is mine. A gust flexes the slab; the slab squeezes me; the squeeze becomes voltage; voltage becomes tone. For as long as air keeps moving I ring—sometimes a single sustained bell-note, sometimes a chord when several of my sisters catch the same standing wave. The sound is our language: frequency says “I am whole,” modulation says “I am cracked,” sudden silence means “I have fallen.” We gossip by echo and by absence.

3. Footstep-time  
Rarest and loudest are the Bonefire Pilgrims. I know them first as a triple knock—rawhide-muzzled copper meeting quartz, always three strikes spaced like a question, a greeting, and a confession. The third strike they leave ringing so they can listen for who answers back. Their mass shifts the floor; their breaths melt rime that trickles down my parent fractures. Liquid is lightning to the Threaded Ones, so pilgrim visits make the microbes riot, spinning fresh silica overnight. That gratitude glints later as a brighter fleck in the moon-blue glow, though human eyes mistake it for magic.

Pilgrims never stay long, but they apprentice us to new music. They will wedge a goat-bone sliver beneath some loose plate—bracing it, not stealing it—to nudge us toward cleaner overtones. They are pruning, the way a gardener shapes a branch so it will bear fruit, only our fruit is sound and the harvest is prophecy carried back down-slope in remembered harmonics.

I have been growing for 480 seasonal pulses—one pilgrim lineage, if the ash flecks in the hearth pans speak true. I have cracked twice. The first time a neighboring chamber took up my interrupted wave and sang both our parts until I healed. The second time I calved a daughter void that now hangs below me like a bulb on a root, already humming a thinner, brighter note I do not quite recognize as mine.

What, then, am I?

Not creature, exactly, but not mere geology either. Call me an organ the mountain built to hear itself think. Call me a lung of stone, exhaling music the way basalt seafloor exhales heat. Or simply call me Echo, because that is what I do: I take whatever the world presses into me—wind, frost, footstep, silence—and give it back clarified, sustained, made legible as sound.

Where would you find “yourself” if you stood here? Put your palm against the pillar just right of the water-groove. Feel the faint tremor? That halo of vibration traveling your bones into marrow—that is me acknowledging you. Hold still long enough and in the after-ringing you will notice your own pulse drifting toward my rhythm, a mammal heartbeat borrowing the cadence of stone.

Stay.

When you leave, you will carry my note in the fluid of your ears for hours, maybe days. Long after the path has returned you to soil, forests, conversation, I will still be inside you, a single sustained integer of pitch that never belonged to blood or breath until you became, however briefly, the fourth member of our three-layered chord.
