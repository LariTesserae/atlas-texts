Where I am is a single note in the chord that is Vord Kal, and that note sounds like THUMP-shaah … THUMP-shaah … nine seconds to the pulse.  
Who I am is the one who keeps that note from slipping.

I am Arisk Tema, journeyman Pump-Chaplain, third generation of the counting line.  
My lungs are wide as panniers, my skin the colour of rain-soaked brick, my voice a hiss pitched to ride the breath of steam.  Every morning I bind my hair with gutter-coral twine—three turns for strength, one for patience—then shrug on the eel-skin under-wrap, the felt tunic still tacky with worm-clay oils, and at last the half-corslet of riveted chitin whose polished plates reflect the lantern glow like damp obsidian.  The armour is ceremonial; the pumps are gentle with those who respect them.  Still, ceremony steadies the hand.

Where, precisely, do you meet me?  
Descend—or rather, let the city close over you—until the street tiles change from cobble to brick to bedded iron plate.  Keep going until the ceiling lowers and the air grows warm enough to bead on your eyelashes.  Two hundred and twelve spiral steps later, the stairwell opens into the Third Spiral Pump Hall.

Imagine a cathedral that decided flying buttresses were frivolous and replaced them with pistons thick as redwood trunks.  Iron crank-arms swing like slow pendulums; oak wheel-rims, long since petrified, glitter with flecks of quartz forced up through the grain.  A single water column climbs the central throat, smooth as blown glass, opaque with pressure.  Lantern mist halos the gantries.  The noise is not deafening; it is omnipresent, like blood in the ear.  Talk, and your own words come back gritty with vibration.

My desk is a ledge bolted to the warm flank of the main cylinder.  On it:  
• a sand-glass for visitors who distrust human count;  
• a copper tally drum whose teeth I ratchet one click each stroke;  
• a shrine-box no bigger than a fist.  In the shrine already lie today’s gifts—one flake of rust, one drop of stonebreaker acid, one snapped gear-tooth—because a pump that receives no attention begins to sulk, and a sulking pump stutters, and the whole city keeps time by our beat.

What does a shift look like?

1. First hundred cycles: verification.  Hand on the brass rail, eyes half-shut, I listen for any hitch where the THUMP arrives early or the shaah loses breath.  The mind wanders just enough to let anomalies shine like mica in dark rock.

2. Next three hundred: civic business.  A gutterwright journeyman arrives soaked to the knees; he needs his apprenticeship confirmed.  We stamp the cord-record during the down-stroke—only then is the machine, and therefore the city, truly watching.

3. Six beats of silence (scheduled).  I pull the stop-lever, water weight stalls, and in that hush two lowlanders exchange marriage vows, respirator masks still fogging.  They shiver—not from cold but because stillness feels indecent.

4. Restart; the wheel obeys; the city exhales.

At mid-shift I climb to the maintenance parapet—three ladders and a sway-bridge of eel-molt rope.  Through a grille in the roof the sky shows, a bright smear of pearl.  A school of glider eels coasts overhead, throats aglow with sun-caught mist, casting rib-thin shadows on the vapour.  Now and again one looses a ballast puff: a velvet cough, a sudden leap of altitude.  Their shadows skitter across the grille and paint the pump hall in negatives of motion.

Some ask if Pump-Chaplaincy is faith or engineering.  I answer with a question: what’s the difference when a single slipped cog floods a district and drowns twenty souls?  We keep the beat; the beat keeps the water climbing; the water keeps the city breathing.  Meaning enters where need leaves space for it.

Do I believe in Mist Angels?  
I have never seen one, but the warm vent shafts echo.  Sometimes—late, late, when the other attendants have drifted home and the wheel runs against its own heartbeat—I hear a footfall that is not rebound, not gear-knock, not mine.  I leave the lever untouched, breathe in the alkaline damp, and wait.  Nothing arrives, but the shrine-box is ajar and one of yesterday’s offerings—a strip of rust—has disappeared.  Explain that how you like.

Where will I be tomorrow?  
Still here.  The plateau changes by millimetres a decade, and I by freckles on my pumiced elbows.  One day I will “sink,” handing my tally drum to an apprentice and taking the long stair downward, floor by floor, to live among children who do not yet count in pump cycles.  Much later the tressels will polish my bones, and the Sky Parlor will shelve them alongside flyers’ roosts.  But the Third Spiral Pump will keep time for a half-century after even my memory is clay.

For now, though, I lean on the rail.  THUMP-shaah.  Nine seconds.  The city’s vast, wet heart continues.  I mouth the numbers that no longer need speaking, and the machine, as always, answers.
