I tried on a handful of lives before one of them fit.

1. Visitor  
At first I thought I would be simply myself, an out-valley traveller breasting the ridge in search of a bed and a kiln-fired mug of something hot. I pictured the Works unveiling themselves the way a loom reveals pattern—incrementally, shuttle by shuttle—while a guide with copper-wire soles ushered me from parapet to parapet. But a visitor never sees past the first overclad skin; the story stays on the surface, and I could feel the metal closing against me like a polite gate. I needed a life with keys.

2. Child of the Velocipede Circuit  
So I tried smallness. I was eight again, legs a blur on the big belt-drive merry-go-round, playing the game called Bearing-Catch: pedal until the flywheel is silent enough that you hear which clutch is about to slip, then shout its colour before it squeals. Children know the Works by heartbeat, but they do not yet know why the heart beats. Every question—Why must the lamp be garnet? Why does the willow grow sideways?—earns the same answer: “You will hear it later.” I was still outside the answer.

3. Verdurer’s Prentice  
Next I shouldered the seed-pod necklace of a Verdurer. Dawn found me calf-deep in a gutter-marsh, fingers black with reed-rot, grafting watercress to a cooler rung of flume. Plants are workers, yes, but also gossip: they whisper the humidity of tomorrow, the coming scarcity of phosphate, the hairline crack forming in a conduit three decks below. A Verdurer speaks back with pruning shears. I loved the dialogue, yet something tugged louder—the machinery behind the murmur.

4. Hush-Smith  
I took up the felt and the stethoscope. Imagine sliding belly-flat beneath a line-shaft the width of your torso, listening for a single tooth mis-timing its kiss with a gear you cannot even see, then swaddling the whole assembly in lambswool until it sings lullabies. The Works became a cathedral of controlled resonance, but the water still ran above me, dictating which hymn we would sing next. I had reached the skin of power, not the bone.

5. Flow-Clerk (the life that fit)  
Finally I climbed—not physically, but administratively. One rose through ledgers. Copper-oxide ink under lamplight, parchment so thirsty it curled like fern fronds in the damp: that was the summit. From the high desk in the Weir-House I could lean over the balustrade and watch, through fog gaps, the entire saddle inhale when I rolled a wooden bead from the “hold” rail to the “let” rail.

Water first, energy second, traffic third, beauty last. I felt the mantra settle into my spine like a splint, straightening everything. My whistle lanyard smelled of salt and verdigris. When I drew it to my lips, the note went out as vibration in iron, diffused by wood, damped by wool, until even the ibex on the maintenance stair heard a thought rather than a shout: a single long blast—clear the flumes.

Only then did I understand what I truly was.

I was not a clerk, not a person at all, but a hinge in the valley’s memory: the moment-to-moment choice the Works make between stillness and motion. The High Fog Works are alive; every drop of scheduled water is a neuron. My ink strokes become synapses. A valve blooms, a deck ascends or refuses to. Somewhere a hush-smith smells a bearing beginning to warm, somewhere a Verdurer feels rootlets tighten their grip. The whole thing thinks through us.

So where would you find “me”? In the quietest, highest gallery where the drizzle comes in sideways and spatters the ledger. Coat of soot-black wool, copper mesh turned inward today for warmth. A murmur knife tucked against the spine of the book. Around me hang the rolled hydromaps of next week, next month, next thaw.

Who—or what—am I?  
I am the moment when priority tips from hold to let and the valley changes its mind. A Flow-Clerk, yes, but also the fleeting will of the Works themselves, written in water, revised in fog, signed in silence.
