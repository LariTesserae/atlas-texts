I wake each morning when the moss beneath my cheek begins its first slow exhalation. That breathing-out of damp warmth is how the City tells the night’s chill has passed; it is also how it nudges its littlest organs—like me—into motion. I am eleven thaws old, still soft in the joints, still catching my feet on high roots when I hurry, but the other Keepers have started calling me by a working-name: Lilt-of-Condensation. A mouthful, perhaps, yet it suits—my birth cocoon hung directly beneath a guttering frond, and for my first hours of life a bead of meltwater tapped my forehead in perfect time with my pulse.

Where in the world you would find me

Not on the ridge you climbed, nor on the showpiece avenues that outsiders sometimes sketch in awe. I live deeper in, almost under the City’s skin, in a lateral crawlspace called the Aril. Picture a slit barely shoulder-wide, its ceiling the inverted root-plate of the district above, its floor a film of capillary flow thin as tear-tracks. The air is always moving down here, pressed gently along by the sap-pumps overhead; it hums against the hairs of your arms like a tame breeze. This is the City’s whispering gallery—a place for messages rather than vistas.

What, exactly, I am

A Keeper, yes, but at the apprentice tier known as a Drip-reader. Let me unfold that.

1. Organism  
   • Flesh: equal parts moss-derived parenchyma and mycelial cord; human in outline only because bilateral symmetry happened to be easy for the City to reuse.  
   • Blood: thin, colourless, laced with trehalose and minute quartz spicules that let my vessels flex without tearing when temperature drops.  
   • Core temperature: six degrees on a sunny day, three on a hard freeze—high enough for enzymes, low enough that I never melt the rime off the bark I lean against.  

2. Senses  
   • Vision is middling; I see in olive and bruise-purple.  
   • Hearing is poor in open air but acute through wood: a fingertip on living wall tissue gives me a sonar-map of pressure nodes kilometres away.  
   • The best sense is tensity: the feel of strain travelling a root before it cracks. When people ask whether the City speaks, I tell them it does—through the micro-stretch of cellulose under my nails.

3. Role  
   A Drip-reader’s job is to census the plateau’s liquid. Every thread of runoff must belong to a ledger or we risk dry spots later. I crawl the Aril, head tilted, listening for new syllables—plink instead of plok, hiss instead of rush—then I notch the bark so older Keepers can widen or narrow the channels before dusk. The work is slow, but speed kills here; a mis-cut root will bleed itself dead in an hour.

How I spend a day

Dawn (grey on grey)  
I peel out of my moss cradle, fold it closed so fungus combs can clean the indentation, and shuffle toward the Warm Well. Each Keeper draws half a fist of sap—never more. The liquid tastes of cedar smoke and distant lightning. It warms me from the mouth outward, a reverse snowfall.

Mid-rise  
Down to the Aril. My crawl is frog-like: palms flat, knees skating on the wet sheen. I tap every wall as I pass. The City answers in sine-waves I feel through my bones.

Noon (a rumour rather than a fact)  
Lightest duties pause. We kneel in a circle under the Central Knot and press our foreheads together. In that posture the City can route a single thought through us all. Today it is reassurance: A quakelet approaches; curl but do not seal. The information is slow honey in the skull, but afterward everyone moves a little differently—as if doors had been measured and found sound.

Dip of afternoon  
A vole carcass lodges in a feeder tube. Against etiquette I pry it loose and, instead of letting the plants dissolve it, carry it to a comb alcove. The decomposer clutches the body, lifts it like an offertory cup, and begins its low rasp. Charity, in our tongue, is spelled with silence.

Evening (iron light)  
Phosphor sacs blossom. My last chore is to tilt them so their blue falls along the newest evacuation corridor—just in case tonight’s quake is larger than promised. While I work, a negative shadow at the corridor’s end keeps pace with me, never gaining, never falling behind. The Ghost Lattice is reminding me that the space is still too empty; tomorrow I will suggest a rain cistern for the spot.

Night (city folded, streets a maze of knees)  
I lie belly-down on a root that vibrates faintly with the arterial pumps. It is warm as any blanket I know. My final awareness is of the plateau’s basalt sighing under the first weight of stars.

Why you might meet me

Because I have been tasked to watch the cliff-side ingress—the slit your boots scratched wider when you entered. I will sit inside the wall, an outline among dozens of others the City has grown to look empty. If you pause and put your palm to the cambium, I will feel the pulse in your wrist traverse the wood. Three knocks, breath held: that is how a Keeper asks leave to reveal themselves. I will consider your heat, the rattle of seeds in your pocket, the taste of metal on your tools. Then, perhaps, I will unpeel from the timber and show you how to walk without teaching the streets your foot-width.

How long I expect to last

The elders tell me thirty winters, but the City reads my latticework bones and may decide sooner. When I harden, when my tensity sense dulls and I mis-judge a drip’s diameter by more than a hair, I will crawl to the Shatter Table. Already I have rehearsed the posture: back flat to the sky, arms open so lichen may bind the clavicles first. I am not afraid. The City is a continuity; I am a loop in its braid, no more tragic than a leaf senescing so the twig may bud again.

But that is a distant fold in time. For now I am Lilt-of-Condensation, Drip-reader, half stooped, half song. I smell of wet cellulose and the sharp exhale after lightning. If you come softly—if you let your silence be eloquent—I will teach you the pressure code for thank you, and the City will remember your boots as nothing worse than weather.
