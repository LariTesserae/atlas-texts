Where I am in that almost–frozen amphitheatre is neither on the damp floor nor on the overhanging rim but inside the city’s plumbing, a half-lit burrow of water that threads the living wood.  
I am a single glacial charling in the Root-Hall maze.

1. First awareness  
   • A ceiling of drowned bark runs above me like smoked glass; my own reflection is only a suggestion, because the water steals colour.  
   • The hall is never still: melt pulses through in heart-beats big enough to rock a fish. Each pulse is a syllable in the raft’s midday speech.

2. My body, tuned to this address  
   • Along my flanks a sash of iron-red scales works like a copper wire, shunting the tiniest current so I can feel when two spruce trunks fuse or when one groans open a new seam.  
   • My pectorals end in stiff digits. I press them against wood and hang there, a paperclip on a slow conveyor, while turbid surges pass.  
   • Between my eyes a pit as soft as the inside of a wrist reads temperature at a thousand points. The warmest mote marks a newborn seep; I navigate by heat the way surface fish use colour.

3. The world as it reaches me  
   • Light arrives thin and green, refracted through a roof only millimetres thick in places. Now and then a footstep crosses above—two dull knocks: vole.  
   • Sound is bone-conduction, received through jaw and lateral line together. The spruce raft creaks in phrases older than my lineage: dawn flex, noon swell, dusk sigh.  
   • Taste is cartography. Bacteria bloom different sugars on different walls; I draft the hall network on my tongue.

4. Daily practice  
   Mid-cycle—when sun stands over the shaft and water clarifies—I leave the ceiling, let myself drift down-hall until I reach the gate-pool where three trunks meet. There I harvest a film of micro-algae as thin as breath on glass. Eating is done with care; to nip too hard is to rasp the living cambium and the raft will notice.

5. Neighbours  
   • Amphipods sift the silt around my caudal fin, glass beads wearing stolen eyes.  
   • A frost-marten dives once every circuit. From inside I hear its entry as a blunt thunderclap, feel the pressure wave fold through bark and into water; by then I am already clamped to the ceiling, every fin closed.  
   • Above, the vole drums—pure percussion with no harmonic. That rhythm means the marten has moved on.

6. When glacier milk comes  
   Two weeks of slate dust turn the halls to grey fog. I stop grazing, hook my fins in a knot of root and lie still while grit whispers past. The pit on my brow keeps watch: colder by half a degree means the melt has peaked, safety soon.

7. Reproduction  
   When the water clears again I will weave adhesive eggs along an overhang no wider than a twig. Melt can scour the wall below, but my clutch hangs above the violence like stars above weather.

8. If you, warm biped, enter  
   You will not see me. At best you will glimpse an afterimage, the lensing of water where I just was. But I will sense you: a localised furnace, an exhale that rises instead of falling. Provided you keep the law of quiet, I will stay where I am, mistaking you for a freak errand of heat that will soon resolve back into stone-cold order.

So that is where I find myself: as a mote of disciplined flesh inside the wooden arteries of a cathedral built by spruce and pressure, a citizen who listens to timber clockwork and reads its tides, never needing the loud grammar of lungs or feet.
