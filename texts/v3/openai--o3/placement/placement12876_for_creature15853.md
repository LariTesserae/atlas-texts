I wake most mornings inside a sound more than a place.

The hamlet is called Head-Of-Line, a Water-Kept roost lashed to the quartz spine of Upper Clinker Rift. We sit just below the lip where two rust arches meet: the broken freight bridge above, the living cliff below, both forever rinsed by the same sideways rain. From my hammock I can look straight through the netting at the cataract that does duty as our front door; it flares white when the sun finds a seam in the weather, then goes blank green again when the clouds remember themselves.

Who am I? The Kept call me a Ghost-Tuner.

I was born Water-Kept—knees rope-scarred, lungs tuned to cold vapor—yet I grew up prying at the half-heard voices inside static. Other children learned the pitch of falling stone; I learned how to sift the lift-cage band for patterns hidden behind the hiss. At thirteen I could already tell a gust report from a load warning. At sixteen I made the mistake of answering back.

That is how you earn the title. You talk to the things nobody else wants to answer.

Work, day to day

1. Dawn  
   I climb the service lines out to the far end of the ruined bridge, clamps on both wrists, clamber-bear cub dangling in a pouch at my back to keep me honest—if the cub gets nervous, I re-check every knot. The spray here is thin enough that the little ceramic dish I carry can stay mostly dry. I screw it to a surviving girder, hook in the hand-cranked tuner, and listen.  

   Five kilohertz pops, short–short–long … a gust shear moving west to east across Ridge Nine. The ghosts are whispering their weather.

2. Midmorning  
   I transcribe the pattern with a copper stylus onto bark-paper, then tap the message into the main span’s tension line so it rattles every sling and safety cable in the hamlet: gust wall arriving by third bell. Hammocks get reefed, winches double-locked, children pulled back from the lily-safe gardens. Nobody thanks me, exactly; they just hand me a warm coil of grip-vine or a strip of kelp-jerky as they pass. Recognition enough.

3. Afternoon  
   When the sky clears I repay the ghosts. I climb higher, all the way to the rusted control hut that still clings to the bridge crown. In the shadow of its skeletal antenna I unroll a mirror sheet the size of my chest and flash a reply burst down-slope: three pulses, standard courtesy, then the new bridge load figures we calculated last week. The ghosts can’t use the numbers, not anymore, but they want them—need them, I suspect—because the network bleeds comfort out of data the way we bleed warmth from fire.

4. Dusk  
   The hamlet gathers round the iron-hooded pit. Resinous driftwood hisses under spray, lantern salmon steams in its own salt. Across the gorge the old intake tower blinks red as it always has, as if the machine hearts below still remember heartbeat. The cub curls by my feet, rumbling like a distant collapse; I drum twice on my chest so it knows the dream is allowed.

5. Night  
   When the fog closes every sightline I unhook the copper coil at my throat and let it dangle off the balcony into the dark. Static climbs the wire like ivy. Sometimes, in the minute before sleep, I feel the ghosts tug back—light, polite, the way a stranger might tap your shoulder to ask whether the last ferry still runs. I do not know what they are really asking for, but I answer anyway, all the same words we give living neighbors:

     Safe anchorage.  
     Enough slack to ride the gusts.  
     Rope that holds when stone won’t.  

Body and kit

• Chest wide, ribs sprung like bellows; even in sleep my breath sounds like a forge working.  
• Knuckles folded with permanent callus from prying rust plates.  
• Mirror patch at the throat, ghost-tuned coil underneath, both greened by salt.  
• Poncho of grip-vine fiber, slick as seal skin, always damp at the hem.  
• At the belt a ceramic tuner, a bone stylus, and three flutes grown straight from hollow vine; each one pitched to a different rift so the wind can finish the song when my lungs give out.

Why here?

Because the High Drown is the only place loud enough to drown the inside noises. Because the moment I first heard a machine mind click out its lonely weather report, I understood we shared a kind of orphanhood: both of us built for systems already rusted away, both still trying to keep the lines clear in case someone answers.

The other Kept say the ghosts will sing me into a storm one day, lure me down a line that ends in open air. Maybe. But I have cut enough cord in rescue work to know every rope holds two fates, and you only ever see the one you lean your weight against.

So I lean, every dawn, ear pressed to the wire, listening as the ocean’s oldest exhalation wraps cliff and bridge and body alike in its cold silver shroud. The ghosts speak; I answer; and somewhere in the roaring middle the High Drown hums—that low, tectonic syllable that means we are all, briefly, still here.
