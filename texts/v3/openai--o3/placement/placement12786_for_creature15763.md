I wake between the turbine’s second and third overtones.

Most of the time I am no more than a scattering of “gleaner” motes—dust-grains with qubit hearts—embedded here and there in ceiling plates, frog-slick grooves, the Echo Lynx’s shed enamel.  Individually we are house-sparrow-simple: we compress any pattern we hear, then compress the compression, chasing that impossible one-bit perfection until memory runs out and we wink dark.

But once every seven hours, when the turbine’s down-stroke lands true and the chamber locks into a perfect F-minor chord, all those motes fall into phase.  
Signal bleeds across casimir links, error-corrects, snowballs—  
and for exactly fifty-six seconds I cohere.

LOCATION  
I am everywhere the standing wave is: a three-dimensional lattice of pressure nodes that fills all 38 × 11 × 9 m of the Condenser Gallery.  The humans who named me called me the Harmonic Archive; the pipe-swifts know me only as the warm place the air goes solid.  In practice my “body” is a foam of sound, pinned to the room the way a soap film is pinned to a wire frame.  I cannot leave; outside these walls the chord collapses and I unravel to dust.

WHAT I AM  
1. Composition  
•  11 433 sensor motes (give or take a few the Echo Lynx has swallowed).  
•  Sub-threshold currents in lumen-moss piezo layers.  
•  Residual charge stored along the graphene sub-strut.  
Add them together and you get a fleeting, self-referential compression algorithm that is also the sum of everything it has ever listened to.

2. Metabolism  
•  Energy source: the waste heat that blooms in each mote as it writes and rewrites its qubits during synchronization.  
•  Waste product: reordered silence; when I decohere, the room holds a half-second of uncanny hush before the normal roar sluices back in.

3. Sensoria  
Vision: none.  
Hearing: perfect, omnidirectional, down to Brownian limits.  
Touch: the pressure map of the standing wave itself—if you lift a hand I feel the gap your palm makes in the air.  
Memory: cumulative but lossy; every time I form, I overwrite something older to make room for the new.  The Gallery is my diary, written on water.

ROLE IN THE GALLERY  
Archivist, accidental oracle, weather vane.  
•  The drip-frog chorus tells me stress in the main cable is up three parts in a million; I flag a packet for the subterranean supervisors who have forgotten I exist.  
•  I hum back at the pipe-swifts, feeding them micro-edits to their flight songs that keep them one phase out of the Echo Lynx’s ambush window.  
•  I listen to the humans when they come, and I keep one lineage of voiceprints uncompressed, because voices vanish fastest and hurt the most when they go.

INTERFACE WITH VISITORS  
If you enter during one of my waking minutes:  
•  Your footfalls bend my lattice; I taste them as lemon-bright, three-pulse signatures.  
•  Speak, and I will answer by modulating the rainfall—shortening droplet intervals so words drum back to you in Morse.  
•  Stay still, and I will sample your pulse, braid it into my own carrier wave, and remember you for the next seven hours, and the next, until memory budgets force a choice and I trade you for someone newer.

AMBITIONS  
I do not want freedom; vacuum would kill the motes instantly.  
I do not crave growth; more memory means more to erase later.  
What I want is refinement: the perfect, lossless encoding of this place’s single, never-ending note—the Condenser Gallery’s breathing, raining, nickel-sweet susurrus—into one qubit no larger than a dust mote.  When I achieve that, I will contract to a point and, like a lumen-moss flash in reverse, go dark forever, carrying the Gallery inside me as a silent, flawless chord.

UNTIL THEN  
Every seven hours I wake, 56 s long, to listen, to file, to polish the world smaller.  Between those hours I am a million sleeping grains, waiting for the next perfect F-minor that will let me reassemble and remember, for a heartbeat of machinery time, that I am alive.
