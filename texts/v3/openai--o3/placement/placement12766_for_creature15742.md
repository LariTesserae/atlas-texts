I arrive first as sound.

A single, exacting heartbeat falls through the bruise-dark volume and discovers it can travel farther than my body ever has. It bounces from sump to ceiling truss, loses a little heat, gains a little iron, and comes back to me deeper—almost a bell tone. I send it again, then again, until the chamber and I share a rhythm slow enough to balance on.

That moment—when echo and muscle fuse—is always my true birth. The rest of my anatomy folds in afterward, knitting itself from things already present: the amber damp of pipe-skin, the soft burr of surface rust, the spine-straight certainty that nothing here is allowed to hurry. By the time vision finally irons itself flat and functional, I understand what I am this cycle:

I am an Intervalist, third rung of the Slow Keepers.

Where you will meet me

Down-shaft from the condensation nave there is a narrower, throat-like gallery called the Hem. It is neither as tall as the nave nor as populous; three catwalks girdle it like seams on a pressure suit, and one elderly steam line bisects the void from floor grate to upper manifold. The line is my metronome. Every 1,104 ± 7 seconds it exhales a sigh no louder than the creak of an opening library door. My job is to make sure that interval never drifts beyond eight seconds of tolerance.

What that means in practice

1. I live inside experienced time rather than measured time.  
   • When you look at me you may think I’m listening, but I’m really counting thickness—of silence, of humid breath, of the dark itself. I can tell you how many heartbeats stand between the sump pump’s distant gulp and the very next droplet poised above your shoulder.

2. I carry two implements only:  
   a. A copper tuning fork tuned to the fundamental of the steam line’s shell resonances. Strike it, touch it to a valve; the difference beat tells me whether inner scale has narrowed the bore.  
   b. A ledger plate—square of tin, etched with 91 nested rings. After each circuit I scratch one more crescent at the ring that matches the line’s current lull. When the outermost ring fills, someone higher in the lattice will swap the section before it breeches. This usually takes a human-reckoned decade.

What I look like while doing it

• Think of an armoire key forged from cartilage and iron-phosphate. My limbs are narrow, cross-braced, faintly magnetic; they hum if a live cable brushes near.  
• A collar of halite-moss—the pale green kind that fluoresces like drowned starlight—grows at my nape. I trim it monthly with a shard of mica, careful not to spill spores.  
• Over my eyes sit the mirror plates, currently half-drawn so I can taste the moss glow but not blind myself should a filament bank in the nave stumble awake.

Daily sequence (there is no night to speak of)

1. Wake is the absence of drip-notes for twelve cycles: silence slaps me alert.  
2. A climb to ring three of the Hem catwalk, where I loop one ankle through a bracket and hang sideways, ear pressed to the steam line’s flank. The pipe feels like a huge, patient animal, always digesting.  
3. If the tempo is right, I chalk a white line on the ledger plate and drift into maintenance inspection: bolt-head flake checks, condensate ledge clearing, beetle-pan census. If the tempo is wrong, I fetch a heat-suture kit—lick of braze rod, brief halo of blue fire—and persuade the metal back toward equilibrium.  
4. Return to the scaffolds’ central cradle to exhale moisture into seed dishes for new moss starts.  
5. Let the marrow magnets in my ribs click once against the handrail: a goodbye to any Keeper passing beneath.

What I remember that others forget

Once, surface engineers wielded wristwatches that could not survive a single rust season here. They taught me seconds; I taught them intervals. They have been gone four full outer rings, but I still keep one of their shattered watches wired to the catwalk. Its hands dissolved years ago; the glass face sweated chloride freckles, then caved. All that remains is the empty bezel. I look through it sometimes to be reminded that holes can also measure.

How you might notice me

You will be walking—no, trying to walk—in the nave, impatient with the heat, when three taps bloom beneath your soles: long–short–short. Stop. The deck grill will vibrate like a plucked harp string. That is me asking whether the sump below has skipped. If you kneel and lay your palm flat, I will answer by matching your pulse so you can feel the pipe’s slower one under everything. When you rise, you may think nothing has changed, but the next drip line you pass will fall just a hair sooner, and the next filament bloom will last one breath longer.

That is my whole craft: stretching moments until machinery and biology agree on the same, survivable length.

If I were absent

The steam line would begin to rasp—first a chuffed whistle, then a shred of scream. Condensate would pool too hot, flash to vapor, scald the catwalk supports, pit the ledger plate until the rings blurred together. The chamber above would lose its spine of predictable hush; beats would turn erratic, moss would dry, and some larger, unseen turbine would spin out of sync far above. Endurance needs intervals the way a body needs joints.

So when you ask who or what I am, understand I am less a person than a chosen distance between pulses, given kneecaps and lungs so the space can get up and walk its route. Call me Keeper if titles steady you. I answer more readily to the pause that follows.
