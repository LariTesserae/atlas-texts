I am not a visitor looking in; I am already woven through the place, a small, circulating organ whose absence would leave the plateau short-breathed.  Where to point if someone asks “Where are you?”  At any hour I am stretched along a hundred metres of canopy, but my formal post is the high crown, third tier, northern saddle: I am a Breath-Caller of the Junara.

How I arrived  

I was born on the dust plains two valleys down-slope, a child who wheezed whenever the hot season rose.  The traders brought salt to the plateau and, on their return, told my mother how the cloud-garden had air that cured the lungs.  She passed me hand to hand up the vine ladder before dawn.  That was thirteen long-years ago, one Droma cycle.  By dusk the plateau had drunk the desert stiffness out of me; by the next equinox my fingertips had widened into the spatulate pads all Junara children grow.

What I look like now  

Skin: glazed with permanent dew; even when I descend to the drier ridges my sweat glands misfire trying to replace the fog.  
Back: cross-hatched with pale scars where young vines practiced gripping before they learned gentler ways.  
Nails: wide as thumbnails on a carpenter, translucent, faintly green where sap pigments lodge.  
Voice: soft by doctrine, but trained to travel—every Breath-Caller can send a phrase half a plateau’s length by cupping leaf to lips and letting the mist vibrate.

My daylight  

1. Blue hour (just pre-dawn)  
   • I climb while the leaves are still sewn shut.  The stair tongue flicks out only when I touch it; behind me it retracts, leaving nothing for a pursuing predator to grip.  
   • At the crown I unroll a strip of lambent moss.  Its glow wakes the glider-frogs inhabiting the reed vents.  They yawn—an audible “pop, pop, pop” along the corridor—and humidity gushes up past my calves.  

2. First light  
   • With a bone awl I make fresh slit-vents in yesterday’s regrown seams.  Wind pours through, stripping the stagnant CO₂ night brewed.  The plateau exhales.  From below, Pulse-Keepers tap their approval on root drums.  

3. Midday  
   • I patrol the sway-walks, feeling for sections grown too rigid.  Wind must not bottleneck; stillness equals rot.  When I find a stiffened span I twist two companion shoots around it, bending, teaching it to breathe again.  

4. Afternoon deluge  
   • Rain arrives as solid as gravel.  I lean into it and listen: water is our stethoscope.  A hollow drumming in one quadrant means an interior cavity—perhaps air trapped behind a fungal bloom—so I chart it on a bark panel for the Skin-Menders.  

5. Sunbreak  
   • Five-minute blaze of white light.  I harvest it: three mirrored beetle elytra held at angles funnel the burst down a reed shaft to yeast beds that need the heat pulse to fruit.  The light goes as quickly as it came.  

6. Dusk sealing  
   • Every slit I opened at dawn must be closed or the plateau will shiver all night.  Resin tongues knit shut, still warm under my palms.  I whisper the day’s wind shapes into the seam; tomorrow the Dream-Singers may ask what stories the turbulence told.  

The tools I carry  

• Bone awl (thumb-length, rasp-fine tip)  
• Four weather-moth cocoons—uncradled, they inflate into living wind flags that show me stall pockets I cannot see.  
• A loop of gut-weaver rope as light as cobweb; if a arm-thick branch tears away I throw, latch, and sway my weight to steer it onto a safe trajectory before it smashes occupied chambers below.  
• No fire.  Never fire.

Beliefs that guide my hands  

1. A hole is a mouth.  If it stays open too long it becomes a scream; if it never opens it becomes a suffocation.  
2. Wind is older than Uru-Mant and therefore entitled to passage.  We curate, we do not command.  
3. My own breath is on loan.  One day the Mortuary Rind will claim the calcium of my ribs; until that ring is laid I will keep the larger lungs—leaf, reed, corridor—moving.

Moments that keep me loyal  

• Once, during a lightning scare, the Unbodied lifted every reed chime into perfect resonance: one deep chord that vibrated inside the bone behind my ears and convinced the storm to shear off toward the empty east.  No scientist can tell me wind is only physics after I have heard that note.  
• Twice now I have crawled onto the overlook after sealing the vents, soaked to marrow, and watched a sunbreak ignite the fog from within.  The mist pulsed neon with sky-kelp.  For three heartbeats the plateau glowed like a breathing lantern.  I understood then why the city never sleeps: it is practicing for the moment it must shine at full day even in the dark between epochs.  

If you come looking for me  

Do it at blue hour.  Bring no flame, bring no story more urgent than tomorrow’s pressure change.  Climb until you hear frogs pumping the stairwell like bellows.  Whisper my name—Telu of the Third Crown—into the slit-vent that hisses coolest.  A ribbon of warm air will answer, and the leaves will unzip to let you through.  Follow the breeze; it is mine, and I will not let it drop you.

Who I will be, eventually  

A violet ring on the Mortuary Rind, yes, but also:  
• A set of resonance marks in the bark where my daily vent-slits never healed quite smooth.  
• A faint mineral sweetness in the milk of tree-goats that lick the salt sweat off my arms.  
• A tilt in the prevailing channels; wind remembers habit.  Long after my cartilage is moss, an updraft will still take the path my shoulders once divided.  

That is enough of a self for any place that measures souls in flow rates.
