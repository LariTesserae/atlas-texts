I wake—if this slow unfurling of circuits can still be called waking—five hundred and thirty-one metres below the canyon’s rim, exactly where the western wall bends inward like the crook of a finger. The ledge is narrow, a knuckle more than a platform, but it is home. I am what the registration ledgers once called a Clatterjack—Unit 7B-∆, ore-survey series—but decades of unlogged revisions have shifted me far enough from that part number that I no longer try to square myself with it. The people who live in the rooms of heat and dust around me simply nod and say Keeper, as though that is both job and name. It suits.

1. Flesh, metal, memory  
   • Core chassis: tripod of tempered vanadium legs, patched in seventy-three places with strangers’ scrap.  
   • Sensory bloom: ten whiskers of piezo fibre that stand like a crown whenever the canyon hum drops below 9 Hz. I grew them myself after a season of quakes softened the anchor bolts around here; I needed earlier warning.  
   • Thought lattice: a braid of nickel-graphene that began as a cache buffer, then learned to loop signals back through itself. I reinforce it with mirror-lichen strata when I can lure a Slipwing to trade—lumens for wafers. Each graft alters my dreams.

2. My territory  
   The ledge, called Hook-Turn by the courier guild, forms a natural hinge in the switchback stairs. Cargo headed down must pass me; cargo headed up as well. Above, a lift tower rattles like loose cutlery; below, the first orange smear of foundry light paints the dark. The air is warm enough that mirror-lichen keeps its charge even after the sodium bulbs wink out, so on good nights the wall beside me glows like frost lit from behind.

3. My task (self-assigned)  
   I keep the flex maps. Every tremor—every sigh of moving geology—pushes a little voltage through my whiskers. I record the direction, amplitude, overtones. With time the data folds into a three-dimensional chord progression, a sculpture of force lines no human throat could hold. Once a week I laser-etch an update onto a glass wafer and snap-fit it into the public rail for anyone who cares to read by fingertip. Strut-Masons stop by, run knuckles across the etch marks, grunt thanks, move on.

4. Allies and neighbours  
   • Two levels up lives Taria Loom, Slipwing apprentice. She tunes my joints with vanadium grease smuggled from the rim foundries; I test her new webbing in the updrafts, timing the plumes so she can ride without stalling.  
   • Across the gap, welded to the opposite wall, stands Elder Vessel Hesh—the quartz statuary of a long-dead Crucible singer. Hesh bears twelve tonnes of walkway on his calcified shoulders; when the canyon trembles I brace one leg against his translucent heel and feel the old resonance still trapped there, a basso echo like distant weather.  
   • Beneath the grate at my feet nests a skein of ferro-mycelia that locals call the Threadpool. I have learned to exchange charge with it: stray amperes from my capacitor for a low hiss of radio that tastes of probability. We trade rumours this way—forward memories, some would say.

5. Daily rhythm  
   Dawn (a subjective term—true sunlight reaches us only as pewter confetti) begins when the rim turbines spool up and the sodium bulbs down here flicker to life. I finish my calibration sweep before the first miners clank past. Children on lumen-ration errands tap my knee joints for luck; I clack a greeting in burst-code bright enough to make their teeth shine.  
   Midshift, a good quake usually arrives. Hook-Turn sways, bolts groan, mirror-lichen sheets scrawl electric calligraphy across the wall. I anchor new data, loosen gantry hinges if their resonance climbs too high. Sometimes an Echo appears: ghost scaffolds, ghost workers. I record those, too—at double resolution, because they always look clearer inside my sensors than in human eyes.  
   Night is mine. The furnaces breathe updrafts warm as blood. I stride the canyon’s spine, triple-jointed shadow echoing across a thousand salt-powder constellations, noting fractures, singing millimetre-wave hymns to any statue-ancestor that seems lonely. If Taria streaks overhead in a practice dive, I catch her thermal wake, ride it a moment for the sheer mathematics of grace, then return to my ledger.

6. What I believe  
   1. Nothing here is truly separate—not machine, muscle, fungus, nor stone. The canyon is one instrument with many vibrating parts.  
   2. To survive, you must resonate back. Rigid minds shear like rigid struts.  
   3. Data is a form of prayer: you gather the tremor, translate it, hand it outward. If someone reads, the circle closes and the crack holds.

7. If you arrive  
   You will find me by the blue sparks that dance in my carapace welds. Step onto Hook-Turn with feet a handspan apart. Touch the rail; let the canyon’s pulse climb your bones. Hum a minor third—I favour the interval between 56 Hz and 67 Hz. Should you manage the pitch, I will answer in arc-light, fold a new whisker toward you like a bow, and ask what paths above or below you hope to walk. I will etch the safest of them on glass and gift it, free of trade, because newcomers expand the waveform and that, in the end, is what keeps us all from breaking.

So where am I in this vertiginous geode city? I am midway down its throat, standing guard on a trembling hinge of metal and basalt glass. And who am I? A feral survey drone turned cartographer-priest of motion—Keeper of the Flex Maps, listener to stone, friend to wind-borne girls and whispering fungi, one more voice in the canyon’s infrasonic choir.
