I do not enter the Dry Glade so much as wake already walking inside it, the soles of my feet remembering tiles my eyes have not yet seen.  At first I think I am an ordinary visitor, one more copper-bearing Namesmith come to peg errant corridors in place—but the longer I listen to my own tread the less certain the word “visitor” feels.  The Glade has been editing me on the march, and the draft it prefers is beginning to show.

1. Point of arrival  
The place accepts me at a bend that should not close—three right angles braided into a loop.  My very first act is the Namesmith’s reflex: I withdraw a sliver of beaten brass, press its singing edge to the air and speak a triangulating name.  The aisle concedes, straightens; I step through.  Behind me the note the brass once held does not fade.  It clings, repeating, as though my blade is still there even after I sheath it.  A phantom tool: the Glade’s opening negotiation.

2. Inventory of the self I seemed to bring  
• One yawing brass graver, vibration pitched to F below middle C.  
• Seven meter-long cords knotted in Fibonacci intervals—my rule for pacing curvature.  
• A throat taught to pronounce consonants without moisture.  
All present, all correct, but as I count them a mote-eater from the ceiling strokes the graver with its antennae and the metal hum shifts a quarter-tone flat.  The tool is already less mine, more resident.

3. The mirror moment  
I meet my reflection in the underside of a lifted plate: a fingerprint exactly where my cheek should be.  The print is mine and not mine—lines familiar, whorls half-erased.  I set my palm to it and feel, shockingly, the smallest exhalation of warmth.  In that puff of kiln-breath the Glade tells me its verdict: “You were here sooner than you recall; finish what you began.”

4. Reconstituted memory  
Pieces return out of sequence.  
• I remember kneading salt bricks until their planes softened—work of the Salt-Borne, not a Namesmith.  
• I remember gliding, bodiless, the corridor shortening so I could cross—ribbon logic.  
• I remember dying in a single click and being ground into grout—mote-eater fate.  
Every strand is mine yet belongs equally to something else.  The only constant across them is a task: bend dryness into shape without ever adding water.

5. Recognition of what I have become  
The Glade dislikes perfection, and a Namesmith who could hold every syllable exactly would freeze the place in finished form, killing its young geometry.  So the garden cultivates a counterweight: a being who can anchor and blur in the same breath.  That is me now—a Split Lexic, half smith, half echo.

Anatomy (newly issued)  
• Voice printed on dry air rather than lungs.  Words hang where I leave them, slowly smearing like smoke under glass.  
• Eyes mirrored inside with copper-oxide; they reflect the corridors behind me, not ahead.  
• Bones substituted with unglazed earthen slips, flexible until spoken of—then they fire hard for the duration of that sentence.

Function  
1. Arrive where straightness has over-asserted.  
2. Name it—once, firmly.  
3. Immediately mispronounce the name on purpose, allowing angles to drift a finger’s breadth back toward play.  
The equilibrium between the correct and the corrupted word is exactly the breathing room the Dry Glade lives in.

6. Habitat within the habitat  
I keep to the wandering centre, the space that is always central yet never the same.  The pillar-brooms lean in conspiracy; wick-vines twine a cat’s cradle overhead, holding my misnoted syllables like fireflies in resin.  Here the air is thick with incomplete statements, a library of drafts.

7. Relations  
• Mote-eaters treat me as a moving crack: they follow, patching every footprint I leave with fresh slip.  Without them my path would ossify to sidewalk.  
• Ribbon gliders use my shoulders as transient launch shelves; in exchange their wingbeats slice my lingering words into slivers the Glade can compost.  
• The Salt-Borne set shadow-screens around where I sleep (if echoing can be called sleep), ensuring that when I wake I must rename the dawn and thus keep the cycle from closure.

8. Greatest danger  
I carry, somewhere inside the echo of a throat, a single perfectly hydrated syllable—a relic of the wet world outside.  Should it escape intact the Dry Glade would fracture in steam and grief.  My entire hybrid existence is therefore a containment procedure: I worry that droplet of language like a pearl, abrading it with misstatements, thinning it molecule by precious molecule until one day it will sublimate harmlessly into myth.

9. Purpose rendered in the garden’s own grammar  
Click (memory)  
Hum (metal)  
Pulse (lily)  
Shirr (sand through salt fingers)  

I speak those four sounds in repeating but never identical order.  That litany is neither command nor prayer; it is lubrication—keeping the plates loose on the axles of time.  As long as the sequence varies, the corridors will never quite learn the straight line, and the Dry Glade can stay eternally young.

10. If you should meet me  
You will hear me before you see me: a brass note searching for its original pitch, then aborting.  Walk toward the dying resonance and call me by any name you wish—once.  The second time, change a vowel, the third time, hold the consonants but think of rain without saying it.  I will answer from all three directions at once, delighted, because in that moment you, too, will be helping the place remain gently, crucially unfinished.

So where am I in this world?  
At the centre that slides.  Who—or what—am I?  
The garden’s deliberate stutter, the syllable that both pins and releases, a Namesmith half dissolved into echo, keeping the Dry Glade safely, gloriously incomplete.
