I am no longer sure whether I walked up through storm-snow like any other mountaineer or whether I was always here, waiting to be noticed.  Either way, if you come looking for me you must lie on your back upon the basalt, because the inch of air that belongs to warm-blooded creatures is all I have left of altitude.  I occupy it fully: shoulders against the stone, face pressed to the cold meniscus of the over-sea, ears open to everything the place is saying.

Name: pressure calls me “Interstice.”  Function: I am the plateau’s single mammal, a translator pinned between two immensities.

HOW I CAME TO BE

1. Arrival  
I was a field acousticist once, hired to map fracture lines in highland basalts.  The firm’s altimeter failed at the ridge; its barometric hiss became my first lesson in the Abyss’s improbable pressure.  I remember the moment my glove brushed the hanging sea and came away heavy as lead crystal.  I remember the cataract detonating on the far shore, its boom travelling through stone before it reached the air.  I wrote one sentence in my log:  
 “This place is shaped less like ground than like an ear.”  
Then the spores found me.

2. Inoculation  
Kelp-tree sporulation sounds like sand poured through a sieve—whsssk!—and feels like static on the skin.  I inhaled a hundred million dry gametes before my mask was secure.  They lodged in paranasal sinuses, tear ducts, alveoli.  Their enzymatic bloom was painless: a cool tightening in the chest, a faint brine taste.  By the third dawn I could take only shallow breaths anywhere above the ridge.  Below the ceiling, however, lungs and spores negotiated a joint protocol.  We became co-operating organs, half lichen, half surveyor.

3. Adoption  
The forest verified my usefulness by resonance.  I lay belly-down, knocked two flint nodules together in a quick B-flat / rest / low E.  Fronds opened across a hectare of dusk like lantern shutters.  That was consent.  The rhizome web drank the sound, tasted the calcium in my bones and the iron in my blood, and left me alive.  From that evening I began hearing with more than ears: ribs vibrated, fillings hummed, every cartilage scribed pressure waves onto the nervous system.  The plateau turned my skeleton into an internal seismograph and paid for the conversion with oxygen spun from kelp.

WHAT I AM NOW

1. Physiology  
• Spores have braided capillaries through my lungs; I can extract O₂ from the standing mist where ordinary air holds almost none.  
• My middle-ear ossicles are overgrown with a thin veneer of basaltic nano-crystal precipitated from Lithosei exudate.  Net effect: expanded frequency range, down to infra-boom and up to moss-click.  
• Core temperature runs two degrees low.  Muscles work slower but endure cold water immersion for nearly ten minutes—the plateau’s mercy time for any warm creature that accidently punches up through the ceiling.

2. Role in the Ecological Circuit  
The drums of the absent builders still pivot on kelp-fiber hinges, but no large animal remains to reset angles after winter sag or stone-creep.  I do that.  I read the forest’s hydraulics the way a conductor reads breath in an orchestra and answer with my whole body:

a. Re-tune the Frames  
 – identify which lintel has drifted off pitch (the plateau’s “A” is 47 Hz)  
 – walk the basalt shelf at slack tide, brace myself between uprights, lever stone against stone until the boom rings true

b. Patch the Rhizome  
 – follow the sub-audible crackle that means a root has sheared  
 – kneel, stitch torn vascular threads together with woven moss cord, seal with over-sea condensate (thicker than resin, sets like amber)

c. Carry News  
 – my heartbeat is the only naturally irregular pulse here; the forest listens for it  
 – I tap rib or sternum with a knuckle to mark events the plants cannot sense: the fall of meteoric ash, aurora bright enough to make them photosynthesize at midnight, the yearly freeze of the cataract columns when upper weather goes below −60 °C

3. Timescale of Thought  
The kelp parliament debates by tide-cycle.  I sleep in twenty-minute increments so I can serve as minute-hand to their lunar clock.  My memories still run at mammal speed, but decision feels wrong unless I set it to the slow metronome of the over-sea: inhale on boom, exhale on echo, wait for the answering hiss of spores.

WHERE YOU’D FIND ME

The easiest landmark is Frame Seven, western inner rim.  Approach when the plateau’s underside glows cobalt and the cataracts are quiescent.  From a distance you will see what looks like a dark figure crouched under the lintel, arms raised.  Come closer and you’ll realize I am bracing the net, keeping tension while a juvenile trunk threads itself through to graft.  My hair drips brine; my coat is quilted with moss that closes its stomata when your lamp strikes it.

Do not shout—your voice will slam every chamber the Lithosei have drilled for two hundred meters and bring the over-sea down in a rage of waterfalls.  Instead, stamp twice, softly.  The forest will answer with a sigh of opening fronds, and I will know you belong in the inch of air where animals can still breathe.

WHY I REMAIN

I could descend the ridge, shed spores in lower sunlight, relearn rapid weather and company.  Yet each time I start the climb down I test the ground with my heel and hear only ordinary silence.  The absence feels carnivorous.  Up here every step writes itself into the mesa, every bone conducts news; to leave would be amputation from a body that has accepted me as joint tissue.

When I finally die—be it chill, rockfall, or a mis-timed cataract—the forest will claim the calcium, the Lithosei the iron nails of my blood.  My tendons will dry into cords, lashed across a new frame the plants will raise from fallen trunks.  My skull, lightened by porous crystal, will hang at its center and boom like any other drum when water finds it.

Then I will at last belong completely to the Altitudinal Abyss: not explorer, not custodian, but note in a chord held between drowning sky and listening stone, part of the organ voice that tells the upper world nothing lives here while the whole plateau quietly, deeply, continues to breathe.
