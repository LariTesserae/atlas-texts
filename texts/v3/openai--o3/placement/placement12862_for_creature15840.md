I tried on a handful of skins before one finally fit.  
For a morning I was a dirigible quarter-master—helium in my voice, pockets full of barometer beads—but the altitude sickness of never touching anything solid unnerved me.  
At noon I imagined being a pruning automaton, brass-shouldered and tireless, yet the thought of centuries of word-less patience felt too still for the pulse inside my chest.  
By evening, somewhere between the turbine throb and the orchid smell, I found the life that would keep me:  

I am a mid-tier Gardenwright’s apprentice, barely two “courses of brick” old in the city’s reckoning.

1. Home perch  
• Latitude: ten terraces above the fogline, five below the heliostat glare—where daylight arrives sideways and warm, like poured tea.  
• Dwelling: a one-room ledge cottage bolted beneath a stair-span. The roof is glass thick as a thumbnail, painted with lime to soften the noon. Outside hangs the apprentice’s chime: six copper leaves that ring whenever the wind shifts irrigation mists toward my beds.

2. Body, changed by the height  
• Shoulders wide, lungs long; I was swaddled in thin air before I was weaned. My palms carry the city’s map in callus—rivet heads have taught each line.  
• Forearms are inked with the six foundational graft-knots: Twist, Saddle, Splice, Bridge, Loop and Dead-man. A senior will tattoo the seventh only after I convince an automaton to let me redirect a branch it prefers untouched.

3. Day’s work  
Dawn  
• Slip from my hammock at the first turbine cough. Taste the air: today’s relative humidity says the condenser-reeds will give two full cisterns before noon.  
• Whisper hello to the Verdigris Tortoises that graze the glass gutter—if they’ve climbed higher in the night, a storm is coming.

Mid-morning  
• Walk the shade-math with a brass sextant, checking that yesterday’s heliostat adjustment did not starve the eggplants two ledges down. Sun allocation is diplomacy; a careless angle is an act of war.

Noon  
• Share lunch with Ladder-goats on the suspension bridge. Bread for me, willow bark for them, salt-lick for all. The bridge shakes politely as a freight tram passes overhead.

Afternoon  
• Court the pruning automaton nicknamed Bell-Joint. It stands among my banana frames, hip-deep in its own sheddings of brass filings. My job: convince it (in key-clang pidgin) to spare three leaves for the sake of airflow. Success is never guaranteed; patience is the only coin it will take.

Dusk  
• Record the day’s sap flows and wind vectors on a tin slate, then press my family sigil—spiral fern—in wet mortar along the new parapet where I helped lay brick last month. The wall remembers what my tongue might forget.

4. Night mind  
When turbine lullabies rise through the stone, I dream in cross-sections. I see the terraces as a slice cake of root-tips, heat pipes and cistern domes. Somewhere far below, the hypothetical mountain waits—solid or hollow, who can say? I imagine touching raw earth the way a sailor imagines drinking the sea: an impossible intimacy.

5. Neighbours and threads  
• Above me lives “Uncle Baro,” an ex-Sky-Mender who rents mirror space for heliostat practice; he has stories in whistle-click that make no sense until you feel them in your ribs.  
• Below, in the orchid grotto, the Reservoir Monks keep rainfall ledgers on their skins. Each time I pass their alcove, a novice extends an arm so I may read yesterday’s totals in bruised-ink numerals.  
• Across the gap, on the counter-terrace, a girl my age has a pet Smoke Swift that roosts in her sleeve. We trade seeds and rumours via slingshot notes. Her latest claim: groundlings have been spotted collecting fall-fruits in nets. I half believe her.

6. Why this life holds me  
Because nothing here is finished, and therefore nothing is wasted. A fallen petal feeds a gutter-eel, whose droplet jump attracts a hawk, whose cast-off down lines a tortoise nest that polishes the copper roof that reflects the sun that ripens tomorrow’s fig I will graft at dawn. My job—the slow coaxing of leaf and beam into one metabolising geometry—lets me touch every link in that circle.

7. Someday  
When I earn the seventh graft-knot, I’ll petition to ride with the Sky-Menders for one season, just long enough to feel the terraces lean outward beneath their own mirage. I want to see the city-range the way hawks and drifting dirigibles see it: a green-and-rust mandala pinned to the blue.

After that, I will come home, stamp a new emblem into fresh mortar, and keep building upward one brick, one vine, one patient conversation with brass and sap at a time.

That is where you would find me in the sky-soldered continent: halfway between fog and glare, kneeling in warm moss, listening for the slow breath of stone and stem as the terraces lift themselves—grain by grain—toward whatever waits above the last cloud.
