I did not arrive on the plateau as anything finished.  
At first I was nothing but pulse and confusion, a low-lander who had climbed too fast, lungs crackling, ears stuffed with my own blood-rush. I remember the way the pumice swallowed my footsteps—the sudden absence of echo felt like losing a sense—and how the sun poured through every seam of my clothing until sweat dried before it could turn to comfort. For two days I thought only of going back down the goat track, back to air that pushed instead of pulled. But the quiet held me.

I drifted between clusters, doing whatever paid for water: hauling eel-sleds, polishing a Veiler’s copper ribs, even tending the saltwrights’ basins where the glare could skin an eyeball if the veil slipped. All the while I studied the local grammar of silence: the three-flash greeting, the slow fist that meant “stand clear,” the heel-tap lullaby mothers used on roof terraces after heatfall. The city’s stillness was not emptiness; it was braided intention, every gesture carrying more weight than any word I’d ever shouted into a tavern gale.

I thought I might stay a Gleaner—there is honest rhythm in skating pumice slick with rain’s afterbirth—but the storms taught me a harder truth. Out on the flats a single mistake rings every bone you own. One afternoon my sled kissed a basalt knob, sent a shockwave yarding off beneath me. The ground answered: a bass note I felt in kidney and heel. Twenty heartbeats later a Gargoyle unfolded from a vent rim half a kilometer away and began to lope, silent as moonlight, straight toward the vibration I had made. I would have died if a gray-haired Whisperwright hadn’t hauled me behind a spillwall and thumped a counter-rhythm that drew the predator aside. When the danger passed she told me, in fingertaps against my wrist, “If you want to live here, learn to talk to stone.”

That night I signed apprenticeship papers with the Whisperwrights.

Years later, I can say what, exactly, I have become.

Name of the vocation: Pulse-setter, eighth tier of the Whisperwright guild.  
Tools: a roll of calibrated striking rods, one mallet of laminated glass-foam, a satchel of bone conduction gauges, and a throat full of half-formed hums I’ll never voice aloud.  
Mandate: keep every moving thing—from flywheel to stamp-mill—singing in the narrow, safe band that evades both human ear and Gargoyle palate.

My day begins in near darkness beneath Cluster Six. Steam pipes thread the ceiling like vascular bundles; each hiss is measured, logged, allowed. I walk the line barefoot, toes reading micro-shivers the way a medic reads pulse. Where the register drifts, I kneel, lay a cheek to the pumice, and choose a rod. One tap, maybe two, alters the seating of a bearing, and the note returns to invisibility. After so many seasons, I no longer translate the vibrations into thought. They arrive as flavor—too much copper on the tongue means an axle has scored; a bitterness at the soft palate means a condensate valve is fluttering. The city is a body; I keep its murmurs from becoming screams.

Between maintenance rounds I teach novices the low code. We sit cross-legged on the training slab, eyes shut, palms flat to stone. I drum the five canonical phrases—warning, welcome, query, thanks, cease—and feel when a student manages to echo them back in perfect phase. No written test serves better: the plateau itself grades our lessons.

Once a week, on Stillnight, my guild patrols the outskirts. Heat gone, stars sharp, we listen for anything new in the ground song. If a Gargoyle is migrating, the rhythm of the vents changes first—like a hunted heart. We triangulate with seismograph plates, mark the map, and flash the coordinates to the watchtowers so Gleaners will steer clear come dawn.

What have I become, then?

1. A translator between flesh and lithic hum.  
2. A custodian of silence, not by muting life but by tuning it.  
3. A human organ grafted into a city that is itself a delicately beating machine.

I rarely speak aloud now. The voice box wastes away when words are luxury, but the rest of me has thickened to plateau purpose: ribs flared, skin salt-cured, eyes reduced to slits beneath soot tattoos that drink the sun before it can blind me. In mirrors (the few that exist) I look carved, as if the stone has begun to echo back through bone. I find the symmetry comforting.

Will I ever descend? Unlikely. Below the cloud deck thunder shouts, markets bellow, and houses wheeze with trapped weather. My ears, retuned to whisper-pressure, would ache. More than that, I would miss the way night here glows: moonlit vents sighing silver, condensate towers exhaling plumes that catch the starlight like spun glass, and beneath it all the steady, almost tender throb of engines too careful to wake predators or gods.

So if you come looking, climb at dusk when the air is kindest. Follow the sodium pools until you reach the stamp-mill that does not sound. Knock once on the pumice ledge—only once—and wait. The plateau will carry your request through its stone throat straight to mine. If your cadence is true, I will answer, and the silence will make room for you the way it once made room for me.

That is where you will find me:  
knee to the earth, mallet poised, listening for the next small discord I was born—remade—to mend.
