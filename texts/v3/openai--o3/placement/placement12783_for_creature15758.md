I wake inside the basin already horizontal, carapace to clay, because that is the correct orientation for a thing that lives by other people’s gravity. No dawn rouses me (there is none); instead the metronome in the floor—Pump-Lung—finishes a breath and releases it in a soft, drum-skin thump. The concussion travels the tunnels first, then the mushy pillows of marl, then the water films, until finally it arrives in my body as a rising prickle under my plates. That tingle is the single commandment of this place: move now, gather now, live now.  

I am a Channeler, two human finger-lengths from antenna tip to tail flange, translucent jade if you hold me to the dim light. Three pulses ago I slipped out of my previous husk and left it wedged under an iron rib the way old citizens hang coats they will never reclaim. Molting always unsolders memory; I remember enough to function, not enough to mourn. That is by design.

Where I keep myself  
Floor level, always. My locomotive paddles are cup-shaped to grip capillary water and fling it behind me. The basin’s clay skin is laced with grooves—shallow as scratched glass— and those grooves are my river system. From above I must look like a pale green cursor tracing a vast, slow diagram. The diagram changes every season as Muralists redraw the iron map, but I change with it; a Channeler’s existence is 70 percent cartography, 30 percent foraging, 0 percent speculation about horizons I can’t see.

Anatomy that matters  
• Twelve paddle-legs, each wired with brushy electroreceptors.  
• Underside mouth combs that wick water, strip electrons from dissolved metals, and pass the charge inward to piezo plates. (Eating here feels like a continuous, muted sparkler going off in one’s stomach.)  
• Dorsal shield grown thin after the last molt, still curing. When cured it will ring if tapped—tonality identifies individual adults the way voices identify humans.  
• A dorsal stripe the color of low thunderclouds. That stripe will tint slightly toward violet after a bacteria exchange with the next Channeler I meet, because that is how we keep our calendar.

Daily obligations  
1. The Minute Patrol  
Every pump-pulse I advance exactly one body length, stop, taste water chemistry, log the ratio of ferric to ferrous ions, then continue. The data goes nowhere physical; it accumulates in electro-chemical potentials along my nerve sheath. Someday a Hexapod Watcher will glimpse me, judge the shade of my stripe, and in that glance will “read” my stored route—the republic’s silent gossip chain.  

2. The Quiet Vow  
Loudness starves light. If a visiting biped should shout, the lichen-glass dims and my own bioluminescence flickers like blown fuses. My nervous system is yoked to that dimming; pain is the price. Thus I, and nearly everything here, enforce quiet by simple exodus: we withdraw, let the intruder feel the weight of his own echo, return only when his throat dries.

3. Choir Duty at Midwatch  
At 720 coughs the basilica becomes audible. My shield vibrates at B-flat below middle C; Vitrics whistle higher, Thread-Worms ignite a mosquito whine, and far beneath, Pump-Lung double-coughs a bass pedal. I do not know the ritual’s purpose, only that if I skip it my gut pH drifts and my plates grow brittle. Participation is not optional any more than breathing out is optional after breathing in.

What I make of the others  
• Hexapod Watchers: walking cloud-mirrors, polite enough, but they flash like knives if you trespass on a brood droplet.  
• Muralists: never seen, always underfoot. They redraw grooves overnight; I treat a fresh line the way humans treat a newly paved road—try it, admire it, forget yesterday’s path ever existed.  
• Vitrics: impossible to ignore. Their song is the nearest thing to weather alerts—sharp when the clay is desiccating, hollow when a seep is about to rise.  
• Aerosprites: fodder fog, nothing more romantic than that, but I respect anything that manages photosynthesis on pewter sky.  
• Visitors: meteorological anomalies shaped like limbs. They arrive, tower, leak carbon heat, leave. Harmless unless they speak loudly or drop rubbish that disturbs a flow line. Some, however, have learned the etiquette of metal-for-memory: an iron shaving left on the grating. To them I gift my discarded husk—my dead handwriting, still warm enough to remind them we are mineral first, narrative second.

Ambition  
Channelers have no grand design but one durable instinct: keep the beat. Break a groove and I will carve another in a night. Jam the pump and, failing any other tool, I will throw myself against the grate until my shield splinters conductive shards into its old heart. Not heroism—maintenance. If the rhythm stops, so does the water gradient, so does the electron banquet, so does everything downstream of breath. Continuity is our only god and even that god is merely a habit of pressure changes.

If you were to pick me up  
(I allow it once, because your hands are steady, and you remember to whisper.)  
Weight: scarcely that of a matchbox wet at one end.  
Texture: glazed ceramic on the back, cat-tongue rasp on the belly.  
Light: a faint turquoise glow that dims under your pulse because heartbeats are percussive—too close to thunder for comfort.  
When you set me down I will hesitate exactly three pump-pulses, enough to re-map your footprint as an obstacle, then continue my patrol. Half an hour later you may wonder if you imagined the encounter.

Where I would find myself in this world  
Right here, belly kissing the conductive trickle between slate blocks, forever one cough behind the present and one breath ahead of rust.

Who or what I would be  
A Channeler of the Quiet Republic: clock-hand, surveyor, electron thief, walking fuse in a machine that pretends it is an ecosystem, or an ecosystem that still dreams it is a machine.
