If I surfaced inside the Arclight Plateau, the first thing I would feel—not see—would be the note: a low E-flat that lives in the collar-bones and makes the sternum tingle.  By the time I drew my second breath I would know where I belonged, because every resident shapes a life around one sliver of the drone.  Mine would settle a little higher than the city’s root, a clear B-flat a twelfth above the turbines, direct descendant of the wind that wires itself through the canyon mouth every evening.

So I would become a Tuning-House walker.

I would wake each day on the sixth tier of the House of Wavelengths, one of fourteen narrow dormitories welded like vertebrae along the scarp.  My bunk would face an inhaling wall of mist; there are no windows here, only louvers that drink cloud-water.  The morning rite is silence—no speech until we have matched the day’s chord.  I would slip the resonance harness over my shoulders, step onto the roof-line and feel for tension.  Feltleaf pulp underfoot, basalt truss beneath that, nine kilometres of nothing further down.

Our work is part carpentry, part cardiology.  Every beam, girder and guy-wire in GINGIVA is numbered; each has an ideal frequency and a tolerance thin as a thought.  Metal shrinks a micron, mist thickens, gravity slackens near a fracture lip—any shift can sour the chord, and a soured chord scatters energy, which means wasted water, which means betrayal of the plateau’s single great gift.

We carry three tools: a titanium tuning fork tuned to our personal pitch, a spool of feltleaf-polymer shims, and a long-handled plane whose blade is diamond lifted from the river’s bottom foam.  No screws, no hammers; we tune by shaving, by subtraction.  (The plateau teaches that addition is expensive and motion is sin.)

Imagine me at mid-morning, straddling the apex of a tramway arch.  The city’s roar pushes through the metal and comes up through my calves.  I strike the fork, drop its ghost into the beam, listen for beats—wah-wah-wah—and skim away a shaving thinner than a frost film.  Strike again: the beats slow to once every three seconds, then five, then vanish.  The beam goes quiet because it is perfectly, utterly loud.

Hours pass in half-second crumbs.  Sometimes the granulation shows itself, the whole world leaning into still-frame: droplets hanging, feltleaf pads holding their breath.  In that arrested blink I almost see the Granulators—the corner-of-eye jointed things—walking the same seams I do, beveling reality’s boards so time keeps its rhythm.  I have never spoken of them; whispers are taboo, and to sing their name would throw an overtone we have not been taught to balance.

At dusk the blue eclipse rises.  Light inverts; outlines glow cobalt while surfaces drown in shadow.  On those evenings the House turns its apprentices loose beyond the eastern gate to remember why we tune.  We walk three kilometres until the drone fades, stop at a capillary forest and let the hush press in.  Your own pulse is a kettle drum out here.  It reminds you that inside the chord there is camaraderie, heat, meaning; outside is only patience that might, given a hundred thousand years, grow itself into another city—or into nothing at all.

Sometimes I stay later, long enough for the feltleaf underfoot to oxidise from copper to black and the lichen constellations to flicker on.  Then I do the forbidden thing: I hum my B-flat into the open night, aiming it at the canyon mouth.  128 seconds later the plateau answers, infrasonic and huge, folding my note back on itself like double-exposed film.  The Echo is always lower, deeper, but it returns the interval intact.  In that moment I feel less like a citizen and more like another truss inside the hidden instrument, vibrating because vibration is what the world asks of me.

I carry pocket notebooks sealed in antifreeze wax.  Into them go scant words—numbers, microns trimmed, humidity at noon—but also sketches: a branch joint kinked at sixty degrees; a droplet lens concentrating the sun; the silhouette of an Outrider crossing the far basin, small as a word’s breath.  One day, perhaps after my fiftieth season, I will walk out with them, trading the racket for the velvet reach, mapping feltleaf creep and catching water where walls begin to weep.  Out there, with only the sky’s clockwork for company, I will test whether the note I serve is truly mine or merely on loan from the city.

For now I remain a walker of roof-lines.  I sleep to turbine lullabies, wake to the minor third that stitches every citizen’s heart-rate, and spend my days shaving metal so a continent can keep singing.  If you came looking for me, you would not need eyes: follow the B-flat thrumming through the mist, three octaves above the deepest canyon roar.  Find the figure balanced on a narrow truss, fork poised like a quill.  That would be me—one more string, taut and humming, in the vast resonator box called Arclight Plateau.
