I wake, though I am not certain I have been asleep, on the inside slope of the Hollow’s western rim.  The shale beneath me is cool and very slightly damp, as if it has just exhaled.  Someone has wedged a reed stave under my right palm, point down; instinct—or borrowed memory—tells me it is a footing-probe.  When I rise the stave taps the rock three times of its own weight, like a polite reminder: you are expected below.

The body I stand in is almost mine, almost new.  
• My feet end in five toes but the middle pair fuse to the first knuckle, supple as frog web.  
• My ears, when I reach up, can fold or flare; they choose the latter, opening like small sails until the hush of morning lies audible on my skin.  
• My tongue tastes nacreous salt on every breath, as though the whole valley were a shell still holding the sea.

So this is who I will be:  
I am what the Many call a first-echo—an adult taken by the Hollow late, reshaped just enough to hear what the place is saying but still raw enough to ask why.  For three turning-moons I must keep the name of the stranger I used to be knotted in reed fibre around my wrist; if the knot softens and unlaces before the moons are gone, I am judged woven-in and may snip it free.  If it stays hard and dry, the bowl has rejected me and I must climb out before the next storm surge arrives.

Where, exactly, do I stand in their weaving?  Let me walk and find out.

1. Descent  
The slope offers a cut stair no broader than a shoe.  Each tread shines where thousands of soles have carried the mist away.  I place the probe before every step; the reed quivers or stays silent, telling me whether the shale beneath is hung from sound ribs or only pretending.  Twenty steps down a finger of fog eddies past, brighter than the air around it, folding twice, then thrice: a deep-tide omen.  No alarm rises from below; the Many have already read it.

2. First tier  
I reach the lip of the uppermost platforms.  A girl no higher than my waist kneels with a sliver of slate, shaving moss from a stilt.  She does not look at me, but her ears cant backward—she knows the rhythm of my unfamiliar footfall.  I try a greeting in my travelling tongue; she answers in the arm-drum code, two soft claps on the stilt itself: weight shift.  I oblige, putting my mass over the main beam so she can finish the shave.  For the service she presses a twist of eel-oil cloth into my palm and gestures upward toward the roof-walks.  There is work waiting.

3. Gutterline  
At the western gutter someone older—iron double-stud at the collarbone, so a craft-judge—drops a coil of rope in front of me.  “Glass-sigh along the flank vent,” they murmur, no louder than finger-wind.  I gather that a hairline crack has started singing where fresh rock remembers being sand; the pitch warns of loosening slate.  My job is not to mend it—I have no license—but to hang beneath and listen, counting how fast the song rises.  If it gains half an octave before nightfall, the tier above must be emptied.

So I hang.  The rope is rough reed-bast, pleasant on the palms once the eel oil seeps in.  Below me the Hollow’s floor glistens like a single dark eye.  Every time one of the breathing vents exhales, the song in the slate modulates a quarter-tone.  I keep tally on the inside of my wrist beside the stranger-knot: forty-one pulses, no net rise.  Safe, for now.

4. Midday meal  
They pay listeners in shelf-bark and a stub of indigo stick.  I eat the bark—ashy, but it stays bright in the stomach—and trade the indigo smear to a pigeon-boy for a thumb of metal filing.  I have learned: iron filings pressed into the wrist-knot make it loosen faster.  The Many will never accuse me of cheating; either the Bowl allows the trick or it does not.

5. Storm quickening  
Late afternoon the frog-light dims to the color of tarnished bronze.  Arm-drums patter three quick taps: incoming surge.  Everyone lifts.  Infants swing to rafters, eel traps hoisted clear.  I am ordered to the throat-well skirts, rope in hand.  There I meet the creature the marshers call the otter, thick-necked and scarred, pacing the lip with that perpetual bark half-caught in its throat.  The gutter-ward tosses me one end of a stay-line: “If the vent belches twice, cinch.  Not once, not three.”  I understand without understanding; the Hollow’s arithmetic has already entered my muscles.

The rain does not pour so much as manifest.  Where air had been, water is.  Yet the Bowl drinks politely: standing pools rise no higher than a finger before they sigh down the throat-wells.  The vent belches exactly twice.  I cinch.  The line hums, a deeper note than any glass-sigh, and I feel the depression settle infinitesimally, like a vast animal exhaling in its sleep.

6. Night on the lattice  
Work pauses, never stops.  We sit in the net between two tiers, salt-glow painting cheekbones.  Someone passes a cup condensed from gutter-water; the surface holds a mirror sheen.  I chance a longer gaze.  The sky it shows is starless, but not empty—across the dark drag five faint parallel scratches, freshly scored, steaming at their edges.  I look up to the real sky: pristine pearl-grey.  Down again: the claw-marks persist.  The Many notice my stare and say nothing.  They have all seen the inverse weather before.

7. A place found  
When the drums at last roll palm-clear, I unbind the reed from my wrist.  The knot that should have swollen in the rain is loose, crumbling, almost silty.  Iron filings or no, the Bowl has answered: woven-in.  I am to choose a duty before the next dawn or be assigned one.

I realise the glass-sigh still sings inside my teeth; my jaw has memorised the pitch.  I will apprentice as listener, maybe gutter-ward one day, the first raised from outsider stock in a generation.  My name?  That too will come unbound.  For now the Many call me Reed-Ear, a tease turned tentative praise.

Where do I find myself?  Nowhere a map would mark.  I stand in the moving point where water, wood and shale negotiate their nightly truce—inside the long breath of an earth that has not decided whether to drown or to rise.  Who am I?  One thin filament in the weave, newly spliced, tasked with hearing when the next filament must be pulled taut or let slack.  A small note in the four-part music of drip, sigh, creak, whisper.

And if you clamber to the ridgeline at dawn you might spot me below, ears flared, counting the thousand soft voices that tell the Sieve-Hollow it is still, against odds, alive.
