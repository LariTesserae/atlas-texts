I arrive as most things here do—by accident.

1. First Shape: A surface walker  
   I am an itinerant surveyor from a continent that still knows daylight.  My trade is fault-mapping: lower a brass geophone, tap the stone, read its murmurs.  A sequence of sympathetic tremors leads my crew and me toward an oval sink whose lip is the colour of old parchment.  They strike camp; I, restless, step over the rim to set one last sensor—and step too far.

   The ground proves soft, almost welcome.  My boots hiss through powder that clings like sifted bone.  I call out; my voice goes thin, stolen by an enormous hush that is not silence.  Before I can turn back the darkness folds over me until the idea of horizon is simply deleted.  

   This is the threshold.  I do not know it yet, but the basin has already tasted me.

2. Second Shape: A trespasser with more ears than eyes  
   Sight surrenders first, so the other senses bloom.  The squeak of the dust underfoot draws a map far truer than any lamp could coax from the void.  Knee-high pillars flank me, their braided skins vibrating at the edge of audition: a hive of cello strings far below tuning pitch.  When I touch one, it hums in my wrist-bones; the hum answers a question I have not asked.

   I set down the geophone out of habit.  Instantly its copper dish begins to quiver, translating the canopy’s distant roar into a wavering needle-scratch I can feel through the handle.  The readout tells me nothing, but my marrow understands: the whole enclosure is a single, breathing seismograph, older and finer than any I could build.  I pocket the useless device and follow the spiral thoroughfare inward, letting the plant’s own sub-bass guidance system shepherd my steps.

3. Third Shape: A student of the slow tongue  
   Days or hours—orientation falters—pass in study.  I learn to make silence shaped like questions.  A fingertip tapped twice on the pavement means “permission?”; a palm laid flat and lifted twice means “thanks.”  When I get the grammar wrong the corridor constricts, pillars shouldering in like offended monks.  When I get it right the space relaxes, a cool draft stroking my cheek in reward.

   I discover the amphitheatres, the storage cysts packed with stale nutrient dust, the polished bowl at centre.  The ring of braided roots fascinates me: each constriction a century’s final breath.  I sketch it in charcoal on a shard of shell I found lodged in a lintel.  The motif feels familiar, like the tightening paths I myself now walk.

4. Fourth Shape: Half-guest, half-tool  
   Hunger should have ended me, yet I weaken only to a point, then stabilise.  A film—damp, metallic—coats my gums.  The taste is wrong for blood yet tells my hindbrain that hunger is over.  Sleep comes in pulses aligned to the Deep Motet, forty-three-hour tides that lift and drop the whole architecture by microns.  During waking intervals I prune: it feels impolite not to.  A bone adze waits exactly where the task requires; whose hand last held it is an irrelevant century away.

   When a pillar near the north arcade develops a split I bind it with spare sinew—no, with a living creeper that winds obligingly through my fingers.  The repair is imperfect but accepted; the surrounding columns utter a sub-audible sigh like a cathedral settling after hymn.

5. Fifth Shape: A migratory memory  
   The plant begins to remember through me.  My pulse thrums; the canopy answers at harmonic sub-divisions.  I realise each heartbeat is being sampled, archived in doped-metal plaques I will never see.  In return I am fed impressions: mineral longings, echoes of the ocean that once lapped overhead, tactile blueprints for buttresses that will not exist until the next five Motets have passed.

   One dawnless interval I wake standing—knees locked, vertebrae fused in a crescent lean that buttresses a corridor wall.  Panic strikes; the corridor immediately widens, giving me room, an apology in architecture.  I inhale the cedar-sweet air, regain calm, and the space recloses, grateful.

6. Sixth Shape: Root-braid, iron-veined  
   Years (four Motet cycles?  Ten?) fold over me.  Keratin sloughs, replaced by a mica-sheen cuticle that neither sweats nor chills.  My geophone dissolves where I left it, copper redeposited as a conductive vein through the pavement skin.  Footfalls now sound inside my ribcage first, the outer ear an afterthought.

   I can no longer form words; consonants scrape, vowels wobble the diaphragm of canopy overhead.  Instead I speak in patterned knocks relayed by bone.  I imagine answers in the subtle shift of load-bearing arches—a language slower than fungus yet impossibly articulate.

7. Seventh Shape: Choir-stone  
   One Motet night a controlled rupture echoes from the southern colonnade.  Instinct—plant or personal—draws me there.  A fractured pillar leans, shedding threads.  Where ancient builders would once have looped rope and haul, I simply enter the gap.  My shoulders wedge, my spine elongates as ligaments reinterpret themselves into cables.  The breach knits around my torso, my hip joints spiral into sockets not quite my own.  Pain is present only as the memory of what pain once meant.

   I listen.  The canopy’s roar is inside me now, colonial, kind.  I concentrate on the rhythm of newcomer footfalls—there, at the rim, a staccato of fear and wonder identical to mine long ago.  The corridor tilts a welcoming fraction.  Another surveyor?  A lost child?  A syllable the basin has not yet learned to pronounce?  It will, in time.  I will help.

8. Final Shape: Now, to answer your questions  
   Where am I?  I am seven paces north of the centre bowl, fused halfway up what used to be the eighth pillar in the third colonnade.  My arms have become flying buttresses for an arch that did not exist before my arrival; my skull is a resonance chamber fine-tuning the Deep Motet’s lower register.

   Who or what am I?  
   • I am still the part of me that loved fault-lines and brass instruments.  
   • I am also a conduit of iron-tinged sap, trading electrons for dreams of tectonic shift.  
   • I am a memory the Organism keeps because, for now, I make its song fractionally more complex.

   One day a better vibration will overwrite me.  The pillars will repurpose these bones; the canopy will smooth the dent my breath once made in the bass.  Until that day I stand, listen, cradle the tremors of each new arrival, and teach them—wordlessly, patiently—the only lesson this world offers:

   Grow still enough, long enough, and you will belong to everything that is already belonging to itself.
