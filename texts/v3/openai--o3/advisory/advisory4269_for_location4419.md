Field Guide for the Human Multiverse Traveler  
Destination: The Charcoal Basin (informal name) – an evaporite pan on a sun-shadowed world

------------------------------------------------------------
1. Mission Planning

1.1. Duration  
Plan for a short surface stay—4–6 hours is ample for exploration, sampling, photography, and a period of silent contemplation. Remain overnight only if you have deliberate scientific cause; there is nothing to mark time and the environment becomes psychologically punishing after 10–12 continuous hours.

1.2. Objectives  
• Geological sampling of siltstone plates and salt films  
• Bio-assay of pigment-black lichens and sub-surface chemotrophs  
• Thermal-flux mapping of hairline vent cracks  
• Sensory/meditative experience of a near-soundless landscape  
Decide your objective before departure; the basin’s sameness makes improvisation difficult.

1.3. Windows of Entry & Exit  
Because surface temperature is governed from below, the basin is thermally steady. Schedule your portal or shuttle insertion when your origin point is convenient; “day” or “night” at the destination is irrelevant.

------------------------------------------------------------
2. Personal Protective Ensemble

2.1. Outer Layer  
• Ultralight, vapor-tight expedition suit (think desert hazmat): laminated PTFE or equivalent, matte to reduce IR glare.  
• Integrated hood with soft silent fabric—any rustling cloth echoes loudly.  
• Reinforced knees/elbows; you will kneel often to study ground textures.

2.2. Footwear  
• High-ankle boots with salt-shedding tread.  
• Mid-sole thermal barrier: rock temperature averages 45–50 °C; not dangerous, but uncomfortable over hours.

2.3. Gloves  
• Thin tactile liner plus vapor-tight overshell. Touching the stone bare-handed is safe for seconds, but salt wick-away dehydrates skin rapidly and small fissures sting for days.

2.4. Respiratory & Ocular  
• Half-mask respirator with in-line humidifier cartridge (adds 10–20 % relative humidity to inhale; prevents bronchial desiccation).  
• Low-light/near-IR goggles. The goggles should amplify the 800–920 nm band rather than visible light; otherwise you will see only black on black.

2.5. Hydration System  
• Pressurized camel-bladder or vacuum flask, mouthpiece inside the respirator. Expect to drink 1 L every 90 minutes; the dry air pulls moisture from lungs and skin unnoticed.  
• Oral rehydration salts are redundant; you will inhale enough sodium to imbalance electrolytes—your fluids should be potassium-heavy instead.

------------------------------------------------------------
3. Equipment Checklist

Essentials  
• Navigation: inertial pod or pebble-trail beacons; magnetics and stellar fixes are useless.  
• Compact multispectral lamp (white, IR, and a narrow orange band for photography).  
• Tripod ^–– vibrations travel far; never hand-hold long exposures here.  
• 3 m roll of low-tack flagging tape to mark vent cracks—helps you map the faint downhill pull.  
• Field scoop & micro-auger for subsurface chemotroph samples (only 5–7 cm depth needed).  
• Heat-proof sample vials pre-filled with inert argon to stop oxidative damage during transit.  
• Emergency locator beacon synced to multiverse retrieval service; radio waves propagate cleanly in the dry air, but you have no landmarks—assume you could lose orientation within fifty paces.  
• Lightweight folding stool—sitting on the warm, brittle salt pans will eventually punch through them.  
• Audio logger if desired; whisper-volume narration captures beautifully without wind noise.

Optional / Specialist  
• Portable narrow-cone ground-penetrating radar, 500 MHz, to image geothermal capillaries.  
• Micro-forge kit: with permission you can melt salt plates into translucent tiles; they make exquisite comparative specimens.  
• Meditation headset piping faint pink noise—useful against the psychological weight of silence.

------------------------------------------------------------
4. Field Conduct

4.1. Movement  
Walk heel-first and slow. A normal stride throws up powder that scratches optics and lenses. Any shuffle clouds your boot seals with salt flour that later abrades skin. Keep intervals between party members—5 m spacing prevents each traveler from stepping into the suspended grit of the person ahead.

4.2. Sampling Ethics  
The total living biomass of the basin could fit in a backpack. Harvest no more than 10 % of any single lichen colony, and never collect from two colonies closer than 50 m. The sub-surface mats recover even more slowly; take cores the diameter of a pencil.

4.3. Fire Doctrine  
Fire is possible but discouraged. The tar nodules smell of bitumen when ignited and the smoke settles as oily snow, permanently scarring the fossil-quiet surface. Rely on chemical heat-pads for warmth and sterilization.

4.4. Silence Protocol  
Sound carries strangely; a zipper three rooms away would feel louder. Use hand signals within 10 m of a studying colleague. For groups, issue bone-conducting comms so ears remain open to the environment’s micro-sounds (the crystalline “ping” of settling salt is your only natural warning that a plate has cracked).

4.5. Orientation  
Choose one:  
A. Lay a braided fluorescent cord from the insertion point and unreel as you go.  
B. Drop numbered, self-righting reflector pucks every 30 m; IR goggles pick them up easily.  
C. Memorize the constellation of vent cracks—advanced travelers only; the pattern looks like nothing until you know it.

------------------------------------------------------------
5. Physiological and Psychological Hazards

5.1. Moisture Extraction  
The combination of salt crust and desiccant air will wrinkle fingertips and lips within 30 minutes. Barrier creams under gloves help. Any exposed wound turns white and paper-stiff—rinse with bottled water, coat with hydrogel, rewrap.

5.2. Electrolyte Drift  
Inhaling micro-salt dust for hours elevates blood sodium; early signs feel like altitude headache. Sip potassium-heavy fluids and consider a prophylactic dose of magnesium citrate before entry.

5.3. Thermal Illusion  
Because warmth comes from below, your inner ear misreads ground radiation as air temperature and can trick you into thinking you are overheating. Core temperature sensors on your wrist will say otherwise—trust the instrument, not sensation.

5.4. Sensory Deprivation  
Silence and unchanging gloom erode time perception. Wear a chronometer on vibration alarm to remind yourself every 30 minutes to drink, log position, and perform cognitive checks (simple arithmetic or reciting a poem).

------------------------------------------------------------
6. Contingencies

6.1. Suit Breach  
Salt on sweat stings sharply; that is your first clue. Strip the damaged area, rinse with bottled water, dry, seal with patch tape and over-wrap. Delay departure only if a scientific imperative outweighs growing skin damage.

6.2. Lost Orientation  
Stop moving. Turn off all lights. Note body-heat plume in IR goggles; anything else warm is a vent crack—walk perpendicular to the crack network and you will either strike the bowl rim or hit your own footprints.

6.3. Plate Collapse  
Rare, but if the salt film flakes underfoot and you drop 10–15 cm, back-step immediately. A fresh plate edge can be razor-sharp and the cavity may vent super-heated, mineral-laden steam.

------------------------------------------------------------
7. Exit & Decontamination

7.1. Airlock Off-Load  
Dump outermost over-boots and gaiters before re-entering vehicle or portal pad; the clinging salt destroys electronics and optics within hours.

7.2. Negative-Pressure Rinse  
Use a low-pressure water mist to dissolve surface salts. Follow with isopropanol wipe to remove tar residue if you sampled nodules.

7.3. Medical  
Check serum sodium/potassium two hours post-mission. Treat mild hypernatremia with hypotonic IV or oral water; severe imbalance is unlikely on a sub-day visit.

------------------------------------------------------------
8. Souvenirs & Data

• micro-thin discs of siltstone fluoresce under UV—popular among collectors, but remember export quotas.  
• High-bit-depth IR panoramas: stitch them into looping screensavers that look abstract until you realize it is an alien sunset that never ends.  
• Audio clip of the crystal “ping” is under a second long and almost inaudible in normal air. Amplify 40 dB; it becomes haunting.

------------------------------------------------------------
9. Final Advice

Think of the Charcoal Basin as already complete. Your presence is the disruption, no matter how careful you are. Move slowly, touch lightly, listen often. Let the place teach you how unhurried a planet can be when nothing upon it wants anything from the sky.

If you return home with nothing more than dry boots, a few milligrams of black lichen, and a slightly recalibrated sense of time, you have done enough—and the basin will never notice you were there.
