FIELD DOSSIER  
Destination: “Sky-Well” Chamber, upper escarpment of the Slate-Blue Range  
Status: Actively forming hydro-geologic engine. Lethal to the unprepared; incomparable to the prepared.

--------------------------------------------------
1. ESSENTIAL SKILL SETS
--------------------------------------------------
1.1 Vertical access
• Big-wall technique (aid and free), thin-crack protection, airy traverses.  
• Speed discipline: you must be able to climb 300 m in mixed snow/rock in four hours or less; cloud cover often erases the fissure entirely after that.

1.2 Hydrodynamic caving
• Treat the chamber as a river folded into a sphere. Practice in “wet caves” and class-V siphons where currents change direction overhead.  
• Learn to read riffles, boils, standing waves, and—critically—rebound pulses (the chamber’s “blink” event).  

1.3 Breath & pressure management  
• Static-apnea minimum two minutes, dynamic one minute while working.  
• Continuous equalization under hammering pressure swings; train in wave pools that cycle 0.5–2 bar at irregular intervals.

1.4 Sign language & haptic codes  
Verbal speech will be obliterated. Team members should share a 30-gesture lexicon and a three-tap rope code. Solo travelers must be able to “write” instructions on their own forearms in the dark with one free hand.

--------------------------------------------------
2. PERSONAL EQUIPMENT
--------------------------------------------------
2.1 Bodyshell  
Layer 1: 2 mm biothermal weave (keeps core at 3–5 °C above ambient without bulk).  
Layer 2: Neutrally buoyant semi-dry suit with hydrophobic micro-spikes molded into forearms, calves, boots, and sternum—think river-otter skin crossed with climbing rubber.  
Layer 3 (optional): Skeleton exo-brace built from sprung carbon ribs; offloads peak impacts when the water “folds.”

2.2 Head & senses  
• Dual-cup acoustic baffles (titanium honeycomb + silicone): 25 dB passive, 35 dB momentary peak attenuation triggered by piezo sensors.  
• Low-iron impact visor; interior surface laser-etched with 2° tilt grid so you can see when gravity vectors skew.  
• Directional bone-conduction radio if you insist on tech; otherwise accept silence.

2.3 Hands & feet  
• Gecko-tack gloves with peel tabs (Velcro won’t survive).  
• Shoes: split-toe sticky rubber with drain channels and micro-cleats for basalt.

2.4 Life-holding  
• 1.8 L dorsal rebreather “lung” (liquid-gas mix) good for eight minutes of hard thrash when the cavity completely fills.  
• 10 m retractable umbilical with auto-jettison; anchor it only when you must stop to study the quartz ribs.

2.5 Tools—keep them soft  
Metal corrodes fast and becomes shrapnel. Preferred kit:  
• Two hardwood “beaks” soaked in linseed oil—work as temporary wedges.  
• 4 m silk-kelp braid (can knot against itself, won’t chafe).  
• Ceramic blade the size of a credit card for algae samples or emergency self-rescue.

2.6 Thermal & metabolic extras  
• Pocket of crystallizing salts (snap pack) for a five-minute hand-warmer if fine motor skills fail.  
• Dextrose-caffeine lozenges sealed in paraffin; you will not have time to chew rations.

--------------------------------------------------
3. APPROACH & ENTRY
--------------------------------------------------
3.1 Weather window  
The fissure is masked whenever an orographic cloud bank clings to the escarpment. Best entry chances: 30 – 70 minutes after a cold front passes, when wind shear tears a linear opening in the mist.

3.2 Anchor outside reality check  
Drive a permanent piton three metres left of the fissure before you step through. Mark it with a reflective tag. People who lose orientation inside often shoot back out during a blink and cling to anything shiny.

3.3 Psychological pivot  
Crossing the crack is a perceptual inversion—“inside” feels like dropping upward into a maelstrom. Fix your eyes on a single quartz rib across the chamber the moment you appear; that thread of continuity will keep nausea contained.

--------------------------------------------------
4. MOVING INSIDE
--------------------------------------------------
4.1 Flow mapping  
• The inlet jet ricochets at roughly 38° off the southwest wall, splits, then hugs clockwise.  
• Safe(ish) eddies develop along the northeastern wall every 11–14 seconds, lifespan 3–5 seconds. Live inside those.

4.2 Blink anticipation  
Precursors:  
a) Water temperature plummets ~1 °C in two seconds.  
b) High-pitched hiss under the low roar—air cavities knitting.  
Action: Flatten against any convex surface, knees bent. When the lake collapses, let the falling column hiss past. A rebound sphere will follow; ride its backside upward.

4.3 Reading the ribs  
Calcium stalactites point into flow. When they start to quiver more than 2 cm sideways you have ~90 seconds before that whole vector changes and your ledge becomes a blender.

--------------------------------------------------
5. OBJECTIVES OTHER THAN SURVIVAL
--------------------------------------------------
• Quartz fracture samples older than 48 hours fluoresce peach under 410 nm. Label them; they reveal stress chronologies.  
• Glass amphipods are photosensitive—shine nothing brighter than a dim red or you’ll blind them and contaminate behavioral logs.  
• The three stacked river-stones are a cultural artifact. Photograph, do not touch. Even a fingerprint can disrupt microlayers of calcium memory on their surfaces.

--------------------------------------------------
6. EXIT STRATEGIES
--------------------------------------------------
Route A: Back through the notch—preferred but difficult if wrist strength is gone. Keep a return line trailed to the piton and ascend during a lull.  
Route B: Intentional flush. Clip into your umbilical, face upward, and let yourself be drawn into a floor sieve-hole during a blink. You will emerge 600–700 m lower at a valley spring called the Blue Spout. Risk: tumbling injuries, disorientation, and a hypothermic trek back to base.

--------------------------------------------------
7. HEALTH & AFTER-EFFECTS
--------------------------------------------------
• Barotrauma: Micro-hemorrhages in the inner ear are common even with baffles; schedule an otic check within 12 hours.  
• Tremor: Post-visit shakes last up to two days; they are your myofascia letting go of continuous isometric contraction.  
• Visual lag: For about an hour planar surfaces may appear to ripple. Hydrodynamic vertigo—don’t attempt vehicle operation.

--------------------------------------------------
8. ENVIRONMENTAL PROTOCOL
--------------------------------------------------
Take nothing crystalline larger than a fingernail; replace any displaced rock exactly; no dyes, no permanent anchors, no plastics. The chamber is only a few geological weeks old—our footprint should not outpace its own growth curve.
