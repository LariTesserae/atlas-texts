0. KNOW BEFORE YOU GO
• The Fault-Marsh is not “wild nature”; it is a half-abandoned, half-autonomous terraforming test cell. Treat it as an active laboratory whose security systems have forgotten their passwords.  
• Weather is stable in the sense that it never becomes severe, but micro-glitches in space-time, magnetism and thermodynamics present the real risk.  
• Rescue options are limited: standard beacon frequencies refract out of the basin. The only guaranteed extraction is a pre-installed physical line (see 4-D).  
• Solo travel is strongly discouraged.

1. ENVIRONMENTAL OVERVIEW
Humidity: 97–100 % at ground level. Air temperature: a fixed 9–11 °C year-round (nanomote heat scavenging).  
Light: perpetual civil twilight. Colour rendering index is skewed—objects trend cyan by day, violet by dusk.  
Ground: alternating basalt shelves (“plates”) and peat fens. Fen depth: 30 cm to >6 m. Basalt plates drift a few millimetres per week, with abrupt drops (“subsidence hiccups”) signalled by audible hum.  
Airborne particulates: class-β purple nanomotes, density 10⁵–10⁶ m⁻³, average diameter 400 nm. They are passive until in contact with circuitry, ferromagnetics or strong electric fields.

2. PERSONAL KIT LIST
Clothing
• Hip-to-chest breathable waders with integrated gator guards.  
• Basalt-safe cleated oversoles—broad, flexible and insulating (stone sometimes discharges static arcs).  
• Layered merino or synthetic loft (the air feels mild but conducts heat aggressively).  
• Hooded, non-conductive slicker; avoid bright reds/oranges (attract probing behaviour from mirror-wing herons).

Vision
• Neutral-grey, anti-IR goggles to damp diffraction flashes. Quick-change to low-light clear for night.  
• Head-mounted mirror shard (5 cm²) on a swivel to check reflection lag without approaching water.

Hands
• Double gloves: inner nitrile, outer rough-textile. The juice from fern nodules is an excellent antiseptic but numbs skin.

Safety & Instruments
• Mechanical altimeter-aneroid barometer (no electrics).  
• 30 m braided bio-line (motes ignore organic composite ropes).  
• Hand-crank chronometer (certified ±0.1 s accuracy). Bring two and cross-check hourly.  
• Ferrocerium fire rod; bic-style lighters glitch.  
• Bone or ceramic field knife; steel edges magnetise and sing themselves blunt.  
• Waxed paper notebook; indelible graphite.

Consumables
• Food that tolerates 100 % humidity: cured hard cheese, pemmican, wax-sealed ration bars.  
• Water: source on site but run through a size-exclusion “dead-end” filter rated ≤200 nm, then UV-C with a hand-pump lamp (clockwork).  
• Electrolyte tablets (constant damp leaches salts).

Shelter
• Single-pole tarp of silicone-impregnated canvas; aluminium poles only (titanium encourages motes).  
• Groundsheet: 8 mm aerogel mat isolates against the cold-sink effect.

3. ELECTRONICS & THE NANOMOTE PROBLEM
If it has a battery, leave it home. If you must bring it:
a. Remove all external ports, wrap in three layers of metallised mylar and keep it powered down.  
b. Unseal only on basalt plate at least 50 cm above water, wind speed >4 m s⁻¹ (disperses motes).  
c. Total on-time per session: <90 s.  
Symptoms of infiltration: spontaneous UI colours, emergence of extra file partitions, device grows warmer than ambient though battery is low. Immediately bag, mark QUARANTINE, and barter to researchers upstream; do not pocket.

4. NAVIGATION, TIMEKEEPING & DISTANCE DRIFT
A. Bearings: magnetic compasses precess ~17 ° min⁻¹. Use solar/stellar fixes via astro-card when possible.  
B. Path grids: lay bright jute twine and tie to basalt anchors every 45 m. Twine length is immune to metric dilation; your legs are not.  
C. Reflection tests: every half-kilometre inspect your silhouette in water. A >0.2 s lag indicates local dilation. Retreat 30 m and arc around.  
D. Lifeline: drop a microfilament from the outside rim into the basin before you begin. If all else fails, clip in and winch yourself outward (hand-crank or team haul).

5. CHOOSING CAMP
• Seek a basalt shelf that is:  
  – silent (press ear to stone; any hum means imminent drop)  
  – convex (water drains off)  
  – at least 4 m from upright cypress (branches twist unpredictably at night).  
• Drive ceramic pins and tension the tarp low; gusts rise from below, not above.  
• Set a perimeter of reed stalks stuck upside-down in mud; snapping sounds as they realign will wake you if subsidence begins.  
• Cooking: use solid-fuel tablets on a raised trivet; open flames on stone can trigger lattice feedback and extinguish themselves explosively.

6. DAILY CONDUCT RULES
1. Step-test: probe with a trekking pole before committing weight.  
2. Never jump across water—the mid-air moment is when distance dilation most often edits itself.  
3. Talk softly but continuously during movement; the marsh “breathes” in 14 ± 2 minute cycles. If the ambient hush goes utterly silent, freeze—space around you may be re-indexing.  
4. If you hear your own voice dopplered or echoed forward, crouch and close eyes until the effect passes. Visual input stabilises last; moving while out of synch can leave minor, permanent spatial migraines.  
5. Respect local nomads: greet with palms out, no tech visible, offer salt or fire first.

7. FAUNA / FLORA INTERACTIONS
Mirror-wing heron: Do not shine lights. Their multi-position feathers refract beams into weaponised laser speckle.  
Marsh eels: Edible when snared at dawn with organic line and bone hook; cook until skin flashes turquoise (internal tensors relax).  
Glass ferns: bursting nodules spray a 4 °C tonic rich in micronutrients—safe but mildly diuretic.  
Helical cypress sap: excellent waterproof sealant; let motes finish “curing” it for 2 h before touching tools.  
Shrew-bores: harmless unless cornered; the sonic “modem” scream can desynchronise heartbeats—keep 5 m distance.

8. SAMPLING & RESEARCH ETIQUETTE
• Extract stone cores only with ceramic hole-saws cooled by marsh water. Steel overheats.  
• Do not remove live nanomotes off-site without a containment permit (they will escape any level-1 lab).  
• Replace displaced water/peat volume one-for-one; the terrain self-levels and may “bill” you later via subsidence if you short it.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
9. PHYSIOLOGICAL & PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECTS
Temporal vertigo: feeling of skipped or repeated heartbeats; usually benign.  
Auditory hesitation: echoes that precede original sounds can induce nausea—slow breathing, close one ear.  
Mote chill: persistent core cold despite warm skin. Remedy with isometric exercise, caffeine and dry socks.  
Dream bleed: Many travellers report dreams that contain exact sensorium from the next day. Note them; they provide 60 % reliable route intel.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
10. EXFILTRATION & DECONTAMINATION
• Stage outside basin on high ground for a 12-h chrono quarantine. Wait until both chronometers read the same second for 4 consecutive minutes.  
• Strip outerwear into a sealed polymer sack. Wipe skin with 60 % ethanol, 40 % water mix; motes flocculate and can be scraped off as violet sheen.  
• Inventory memories: write a timeline of major events. If entries loop or branch, consult a temporal medic before re-entering civilisation.  
• Finally, thank the basin. A simple bow is enough; locals swear it makes the next trip easier.

Safe travels, and may your reflection always keep pace.
