Field Manual 77-C  
(First revision for cross-world visitors)  
Destination: The Fossil-Wave Plateau  

1. STRATEGIC OVERVIEW  
Altitude: 3 000–4 100 m a.s.l.  
Barometric pressure: 62 – 70 kPa (≈ 12 000–14 000 ft on Earth)  
Gravity: Earth-normal ±3 % (varies by slice)  
Main threats: rapid weather swings, extreme wind, scarce surface water, unstable topography, ultraviolet surplus, noise fatigue, hypoxia.

Any traveller who treats this as “just another mountain” will be scraped out of a canyon floor by the next convective tantrum. Go in conditioned, equipped, and culturally briefed or do not go in at all.  

2. PERSONAL READINESS  

2.1 Physiological conditioning  
• Spend 5–7 days at 2 000 m or higher before the ascent. If you cannot, carry a course of acetazolamide and know the protocols for HAPE/HACE.  
• Interval-breath training is recommended; average resting SpO₂ on the plateau is 85 – 88 %.  
• Learn to sleep with earplugs or bone-conduction masking—continuous wind noise prevents deep sleep for the untrained.

2.2 Skills checklist  
✓ Multi-pitch canyon rope work (nothing here has a single clean drop)  
✓ Rapid tarp pitching in gale force wind (practice with 40 kg sandbags on the corners)  
✓ Sign language or rope-tug code—conversation distance seldom exceeds 10 m  
✓ Basic stone fluting (optional but encourages hospitality from herders)

3. EQUIPMENT  

3.1 Clothing  
• Outer shell: aerogel-lined wind suit, abrasion patching on knees/elbows, 45 m/s wind rating.  
• Under-layers: humidity-adaptive wool/silk blend; nights freeze even in midsummer.  
• Head/eye: wrap-around glacier goggles with UV-C filtration; ventilated balaclava with detachable ear baffles (wind roar causes tinnitus).  
• Hands/feet: high-friction gecko-rubber palms and soles; sandstone conglomerate chews ordinary fabric in hours.  

3.2 Life-support & shelter  
• Lightweight negative-pressure sleep hood or low-flow O₂ bottle for first two nights.  
• “Canyon-hammock” sleep system: diagonal-lay hammock, side wind-shield, 6-point anchoring. Never camp on open ground; gusts exceed 100 kph without notice.  
• Ear protection: layered—compressible foam plugs + active noise-cancel over-cups. Remove one layer only when moving near cliffs (you must still hear rockfall).

3.3 Navigation  
• Baro-adaptive altimeter (plateau pressure fluctuates wildly—calibrate twice daily).  
• Laser range-finder for canyon widths; optical illusions are common.  
• Hard-copy sketchbook; electronics fail when sudden ionised updrafts fry circuitry.  

3.4 Rope & hardware  
• 120 m of 9 mm kernmantle per two travellers, plus 40 m of sacrificial cactus-fibre line for local trade.  
• Removable micro-cams rather than pitons; hammering metal into this rock triggers exfoliation slabs.  
• Lightweight pulk for hauling—drag, don’t carry; low gravity wind-sail assists on bowl crossings (see Section 5).  

3.5 Water & food  
• Carry 3 L/person/day entering plateau; aim to hold 1 L emergency reserve at all times.  
• 2 m × 2 m mist net & capillary bag: set at dawn among ribbon grass or above canyon dew vents.  
• Potable water tablets handle mineralized seepage; boiling wastes fuel in low pressure.  
• Calorie-dense, low-mass foods: nut butters, freeze-dried lentils, glucose gels. Local edibles: translucent gourds (hydrating, mildly laxative), moss cake baked by herders (acquire with trade).  

4. APPROACH & ENTRY POINTS  

Primary ascent routes hug windward flanks where air density is highest. Avoid leeward chimneys—they collect the plateau’s stone shrapnel. Expect final 400 m climb through loose talus; switch to four-point contact, helmet on. The moment air thins enough that every shout echoes like a rifle shot, you are on the rim.  

5. MOVEMENT WITHIN THE PLATEAU  

5.1 Crossing bowls  
The grass meadows are smoother than they look. Deploy your sail-wing (3 m² rip-stop with telescopic spars) down-wind; friction-reduction plates on boot soles let you “sand-ski” at jogging speed. Watch sky-whiptails: if they spiral tightly, a thermal column is directly ahead—lower the sail or be lofted.

5.2 Canyon work  
Rule: Never descend a canyon you did not climb yourself. New collapses create dead-end sumps and invisible overhangs. When in doubt, traverse 50 m to a known ledge network, then rappel in 15–25 m stages. Repeated calls or clattering pebbles map hidden shelves by echo delay.

5.3 Vertical rivers  
Treat them as no-fly zones. Visibility drops to zero inside the spray, GPS deranges, and updrafts can yank light gear into the troposphere. Take photographs from the wind-shadow of a rim spur only.

6. WEATHER PROTOCOL  

• Blue-sky mornings can birth storms in <40 min. Keep a 15-minute shelter plan rehearsed.  
• Horizontal rain stings like gravel; soft-shell or sun-clothes will shred.  
• If thunder lasts longer than 5 min continuous, you are inside the “drum”; retreat to lee of a ridge or Breath-Chamber—lightning will keep rebounding nearby.  

7. INTERACTION WITH INHABITANTS  

7.1 Herders & Breath-Chambers  
Knock on the wind. Stand outside an occupied chamber and let the gale pass through your cupped hands; the resident will recognise the modulation as greeting. Gifts: lengths of synthetic rope (prized), spare multi-bit stone tools. Taboo: re-tuning fins to “improve” them—each pitch has an origin myth, and outsiders are expected to respect the score.

7.2 Goats on terraces  
Do not chase; cliffs they regard as sidewalks will kill you. If offered smoked goat, reciprocate with spice or salt (rare this high).  

8. ENVIRONMENTAL & ETHICAL CONDUCT  

• Pack in/pack out, including organic waste—nutrient introduction skews the moss micro-biome.  
• Sample only fallen rock. Core-drilling is interpreted as an assault on the plateau’s “memory.”  
• Photograph bioluminescent spore rises with no flash; flashes cause mass premature ejection, shortening the nightly display.  

9. EMERGENCIES  

9.1 Medical  
Altitude oedema kits at 3 mL/min flow. Rapid descent means finding a canyon still connected to the lowlands—use the carved signposts (spiral grooves point toward historical exits). If none exist within 2 km, inflate rescue dirigible (3-person mylar balloon, 12 m³ helium). Launch windows open only at dawn lulls.

9.2 Extraction beacons  
Audio beacons outperform RF. Five-tone ascending whistle (A-C-E-G-B) signals distress; locals recognise and will relay via cliff horns.

10. TIME MANAGEMENT  

Daylight: 14–16 h of fierce sun; schedule travel 05:00–11:00 and 16:00–19:00. Mid-day is repair / shade time. Night temp: –5 °C to –15 °C even in local summer; if spore snow begins, you have ~45 min of calm before normal wind returns—prime stargazing window.

11. CHECKLIST: “ZIP-OUT” INSPECTION BEFORE EACH LEG  

1. Water nets packed wet (proof of fill)  
2. Sail-wing stowed, battens un-splintered  
3. Ear protection reachable while gloved  
4. One 30 m line already flaked for emergency rap  
5. Altimeter reset, weather slate updated  
6. Gift cache accessible (never bury under main load)  

If any of the six fail, delay departure. Out here, redundancy is not luxury; it is lifespan.

12. FINAL COUNSEL  

The plateau makes you small—accept that physics is louder than your intentions. Carry your silence like another piece of gear, listen to the stone chords, and walk as if every bowl is rearranging itself underfoot (because it is). Treat the journey as a conversation with deep time. Answer softly, leave when addressed, and the Fossil-Wave Plateau will add your passage to its slow music rather than to its debris.
