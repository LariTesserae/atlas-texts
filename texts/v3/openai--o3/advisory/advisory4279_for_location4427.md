Guide for Multiverse Travellers:
Preparing to Visit the Plateau of Glass Ferns

————————————————————————
1. Snapshot for the Hurried
————————————————————————
• Elevation: 2 300–2 800 m (thin, very clean air; mild UV).
• Climate: Constantly moist, 5 °C at dawn to 17 °C mid-afternoon; nights crisp, often below 0 °C but never windy.
• Seasonality: Virtually none—an “early-spring” freshness all year. Best light for photography: two hours after dawn; best visibility: three hours after sunset.
• Biosphere: No animals, no insects, no known pathogens indigenous to the Plateau. Major bio-hazard is what you might carry in on your boots.
• Society: Quiet, low-density, post-Neolithic technology. Stone, meteor-iron, spun plant fibres. Barter economy; privacy and low silhouettes are sacred.
• Biggest physical risk: slipping on slick basalt or being under a flake that feels like falling today.
• Biggest social risk: making noise or smoke that rises above head height.

————————————————————————
2. Permissions & Quarantine
————————————————————————
Because the Plateau’s ecosystem has never encountered vertebrate, arthropod, or microbial stow-aways from below, the rim villages enforce a rigorous “dry entry” protocol.

Before ascent:
1. 48-hour sterile staging at the mid-slope hostelry (“The Cloud Gate”): clothing and gear steamed; soles UV-blasted; freshwater rinse to remove spores.
2. Blood test for common Terran microbes (the locals rely on visiting xeno-biologists from older continents to administer it; be prepared to pay in salt crystals or fine cloth).
3. A pledge to export everything you import, right down to biodegradable food wrappings.

Tip: Pack belongings in anodised aluminium boxes—they survive the steriliser’s moist heat without warping and look non-threatening beside basalt architecture.

————————————————————————
3. Physiology & Mind-Set
————————————————————————
Altitude & Air:
• Carry acetazolamide or chew coca-leaf analogues if your home reality sits near sea level.
• Humidity is 100 % at all times; sweat cannot evaporate. Bring wicking merino or bamboo layers, not synthetics that rasp in silence.

Silence Adaptation:
Many off-worlders report “sound vertigo” on day two: with nothing rustling or buzzing, your heartbeat feels deafening. Train with noise-cancellation earbuds at home; remove them gradually to lengthen your tolerance span.

Psychological Stillness:
Time “hangs” here. Set tasks (sketch a ridge, memorise constellations) lest the plateau’s meditative pause turn into aimlessness or insomnia.

————————————————————————
4. Clothing & Equipment Checklist
————————————————————————
Carry weight matters: the access road is passable only on foot or by locally bred pack-sedges (enormous rhizome bundles that slide, not walk). Assume self-portage.

Essentials
✓ Soft-soled, micro-tread boots (vibram squeaks; avoid it). Glue-on felt pads work well on wet rock.  
✓ Knee-length fern-oil-waxed gaiters; mud averages ankle-deep at dawn.  
✓ Three silent layers: 
   1. Wicking base (merino),
   2. Thin quilted alpaca or kapok vest,
   3. Shell of supple shellac-coated flax (“Plateau rain slickers” sold at staging post).  
✓ Collapsible walking staff tipped with river jasper—locals respect this material and it grips.  
✓ Hooded lens lamp (amber LED or whale-oil analogue); no unshaded torches.  
✓ Compact bivy tarp dyed charcoal: any reflective mylar will be confiscated.  
✓ Ceramic water flask with reed stopper (plastic bottles clack).  
✓ Self-heating meal kits that emit no smoke (quicklime boilers, solid-fuel alcohol tabs).  
✓ First-aid: altitude meds, anti-slip knee wraps, broad-spectrum antibiotics for cuts.  
✓ Waterproof field notebook & graphite stylus (ink bleeds).  
Optional but Valued in Trade
• Blocks of pink Himalayan salt (rare above the forests).  
• Fine braided cordage thinner than the settlers can spin.  
• Mandolin strings or soft bronze wire—meteoric nickel-iron is prized, malleable bronze more so.  
• Quiet musical instruments (ocarina, tongue drum) offered sparingly at dusk gatherings.

Strictly Leave Behind
✗ Seeds, pollen, untreated wood.  
✗ Drones (windless air + absolute quiet = instant social banishment).  
✗ Firearms—no fauna to defend against; explosions echo for kilometers.  
✗ Loudspeakers or open-flame lanterns above shoulder height.

————————————————————————
5. Approaching the Rim
————————————————————————
Route: A corkscrew roadway cut by the ancestors hugs the mountain’s newborn joints. Landslides freshen it weekly; porters carry basalt wedges for on-the-spot repairs. Travel in early morning when basalt is tacky, not slick.

Breaks in the Cloud Band:
At 1 900 m the road pierces “The Hallway”—a permanent shelf of cumulus. Layers of past travellers’ charcoal drawings coat the safety alcoves; tradition lets you add one mark, never more. Chalk preferred over graphite (reflects less).

Final Ascent Etiquette:
Silence begins at the first fern trunk that overarches the track. From there, speak in whispers and walk single file so everyone’s boots fall in a syncopated rhythm locals find pleasing.

————————————————————————
6. Life Among the Basin Dwellers
————————————————————————
Greeting: A palm lowered, fingers spread like a fern unfurling. Eye contact brief—lingering gaze is tantamount to shouting.

Housing: Visitors receive a guest mound-house with its own moat garden. Expect interior humidity of 98 %, temperature 12 °C. Charcoal-braziers warm beds from beneath slate floors; learn to stoke with thumb-sized sticks.

Food:
Breakfast—tuber mash flavoured with crushed quartz salt and a drizzle of sweet sap.  
Lunch—fern fiddlehead salad, ribbons of pickled sedge root.  
Supper—stone “hot pot”: tubers, moss pastes, and, on feast days, slivers of meteor–iron-seared lichen. BYO protein supplements if you crave amino acids beyond what the plants yield.

Conversation Windows:
Locals converse while kneeling by drainage ditches; the running water absorbs sound. If invited, crouch at the same level. Speak slowly— Plateau dialects elongate vowels to mirror dripping cadence.

Offerings & Favors:
1. Clear a moss lawn of detritus—shows respect for the plateau’s “tidy youth.”  
2. Carve a swirl on a communal runestone (tools provided). Never carve a straight line—said to beckon lightning that might “alert the mountain.”

Taboos:
• Striking two stones together (reminds them of cliff collapses).  
• Letting steam from food rise unhooded.  
• Casting a shadow onto a roof at sunrise or sunset.

Festivals:
New-Water-Day (once every 37th sunrise) celebrates the appearance of a fresh rill. Low hooded lanterns trace the rill’s path. Visitors may place a single petal (provide your own sterilised parchment “petal”) onto the water; watch it vanish over the rim.

————————————————————————
7. Hazards & Counter-Measures
————————————————————————
Slippery Basalt:  
   – Walk with knees loose; three-point stance on inclines.  
   – If a ridge “pings” (high metallic click) move sideways—flakes fall straight down.

Flash Falls:  
   – Roars start low and rise fast; sprint perpendicular to the cliff.  
   – Shelter behind tree-fern trunks—they are flexible and absorb spray.

Seismic Quivers:  
   – Rare, brief. Sit, do not stand; the soil shifts in crustal plates only millimetres thick.

Hypothermia in Wet Calm:  
   – Because windchill is absent, travellers often misjudge cold. Keep a dry inner layer sealed in wax cloth for nights.

————————————————————————
8. Communications & Navigation
————————————————————————
Mapping: Compasses lose reliability—basalt laced with meteoric iron. Navigate by water-markers: each causeway stone has a groove that points to the nearest rim notch. At night use the “Three Spikes” constellation directly above the central cistern.

Energy: Solar panels work brilliantly but glare is culturally offensive. Wrap them in frosted mica screens; locals appreciate the diffused light they cast on paths.

Outside Contact: No network infrastructure. Line-of-sight heliographs permitted only during midday when their flash is masked by sun glare. Otherwise rely on word-carriers descending with tuber caravans every tenth day.

————————————————————————
9. Departure Protocol
————————————————————————
Clean Exit: Scrub boots and staff in lime troughs by the roadhead. Return any un-eaten plant matter to a slag-compost pile (kept deliberately distant from crops to observe how alien organics decompose). Sign your name, time-stamp, and homeverse signature on basalt ledger slab.

Gift to Plateau: Tradition asks you leave a droplet-sized bead of your world’s water in the visitors’ cistern. Use eyedropper; anything more is considered flooding.

Decompression: Spend a night at “The Cloud Gate” again. Your ears and mind must reacclimate to insect song and birdcalls before descending into the noisy forests below.

————————————————————————
10. Recommended Itinerary (Five Quiet Days)
————————————————————————
Day 1 – Acclimate, trace causeways, learn the drip-rhythm alphabet locals use for poetry.  
Day 2 – Dawn rim walk; midday help patch a terrace; night skywatch (meteor showers common).  
Day 3 – Join a tuber-harvest line; carve your swirl on a runestone.  
Day 4 – Guided climb to Knife-Edge Bluff to watch a newborn waterfall draft its path.  
Day 5 – Private reflection in a moss glade; prepare bead offering, depart at dusk.

Carry the hush with you when you leave; the Plateau of Glass Ferns survives by being listened to more than spoken of. Travel well, and lightly.
