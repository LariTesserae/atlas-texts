Field Manual for the Plateau of Midnight  
Compiled for ordinary (non-augmented) human travelers who have access to multiversal transit but no specialized implants, exosuits, or diplomatic standing.

--------------------------------------------------
1. Snapshot: know before you go
--------------------------------------------------
Altitude: 3.8–5.1 km, but local air pressure ≈ Earth sea level (dense, moist).  
Light: perpetual charcoal dusk; luminance ~1 lux in open country, 3–5 lux near gloam-ore water. No direct sunlight.  
Temperature: 21–27 °C day-night average; swings no more than 3 °C.  
Humidity: 95–100 %; rain every 2–4 h.  
Rain chemistry: pH 9–9.4 (mildly caustic, dissolves glass and most metals over days).  
Dominant hazards: acoustic over-exposure, alkaline rainburn, cliff-edge missteps, infrasonic predators, industrial machinery, drowning in illuminated pools.  
Safe zones: mining outposts every 120–400 km; each maintains a 300-m-radius acoustic exclusion field (“white-noise curtain”).  
Recommended stay for first-timers: 72 h or less.

--------------------------------------------------
2. Bureaucracy and access
--------------------------------------------------
• The plateau is not a sovereign state; miners answer to five competing concessions. Any of them can issue a “visitor maintenance contract” that doubles as an entry visa.  
• A liability waiver is mandatory: fatalities run ≈3 % per Earth year among resident workers.  
• Export of raw gloam-ore is restricted. Personal rations of ore-lit pebbles (≤200 g) are tolerated; larger quantities require bonded freight chests and a royalty ticket.  
• Local guides are freelance technicians. Secure one in advance. Their payment unit is kilojoules of stored electricity (batteries, flywheels, micro-caps)—much more welcome than cash.

--------------------------------------------------
3. Medical preparations
--------------------------------------------------
Vaccinations/boosters:  
1. Broad-spectrum antimycosis (spore loads are extraordinary).  
2. Hemo-regulating antivenom #7 (covers the eight known platelet-destabilising gastropods).  
3. Alkaline dermal blocker (micro-encapsulated topical; slows rainburn).  

Fitness: Anaerobic stamina is more valuable than aerobic—sound discipline often forces you to freeze in place, control breathing, and then sprint. Train isometrics and interval bursts.

Supplements:  
• 2,000 IU vitamin D daily—there is not enough visible-band sunlight to maintain mood or bone health.  
• Potassium/magnesium salts—sweating is constant yet hard to notice in the mist.

Sensory acclimation drill (optional but wise): Spend an hour each day for a week in a darkened bathroom with shower running while streaming overlapping recordings of waterfalls, freight trains, and whale song. Try to pick out individual sources. This hones auditory focus and prevents panic during the plateau’s “total-sound” moments.

4. Kit list (all items field-tested by miners)
--------------------------------------------------
Carriage allowance from the funicular operator is 45 kg per traveler. Prioritise:

Light & optics  
• Low-lumen, wide-flood headlamp (red or amber) for map reading; anything brighter paints a target.  
• Passive infrared goggles: plants and warm fauna glow like candles in IR.  
• Three 10-cm gloam-ore rods—double as emergency beacons and IR film warmers.

Acoustic discipline  
• Active noise-cancelling ear-cups rated for 120 dB continuous.  
• Bone-conduction comms mic clipped to palate; works when ears are sealed.  
• Pocket infrasound whistle (110 Hz)—standard “I am human” distress note recognised by all outposts and most predators (they interpret it as territorial bluff and keep distance).

Rain & corrosion  
• Hooded poncho: trilaminate fluoropolymer outside, inner mesh impregnated with citric-based neutraliser.  
• Goggles with hydrophobic quartz lenses (glass will haze within a day).  
• Field paste: sodium bicarbonate + aloe; smear on exposed metal once daily.

Footing & mobility  
• Amphibious boots with sawtooth sorbothane soles—quiet on stone, grip on slime.  
• Telescoping carbon-ceramic staff tipped with gecko-tape panel (for testing ground; roots can look solid but burst under weight).  
• 20 m of woven basalt fibre rope plus ring-anchor plates. Do NOT hammer pitons—rock “sighedetach” will spit them. Ring plates distribute weight without piercing.

Life support  
• Hydration bag (4 L). Include reactor-grade ion-exchange resin to strip gloam dust and alkalinity.  
• Freeze-dried food sachets in hard polymer cans (rodents here chew through foil).  
• Micro-tent: reflective inner to simulate 500 lux daylight; sets circadian rhythm.  
• Portable daylight LED (10 000 lux) for 30-min therapeutic blast each morning.

Navigation  
• Pressure-sealed paper topo maps: GPS is useless; mountains bounce signals.  
• Two palm-sized echo-drones (“batcoins”). Launch, clap once, and they orbit, mapping distances by sonar and relaying to goggles HUD.  
• Chemical compass (mag fields jitter near ore seams).

Emergency  
• 5 m² mylar throw-rug. Spread when you must sleep outdoors; deters root-stone burrowers that avoid smooth, cold surfaces.  
• 10-ml syringe of antishock neurocalm (minimise ragged breathing when predators key in on chest vibrations).  
• Flares that burn in green IR only (invisible to most fauna, clear to humans through goggles).  
• Hand crank broad-spectrum jammer (60–12 000 Hz) for 3-minute bursts; collapses echo-wolf hunting grids.

--------------------------------------------------
5. Getting in
--------------------------------------------------
Funicular is presently the only sanctioned route.  
Board at the Rust-Rim Terminus (sea-level). Cars depart every 11 h when winds cross-cancel. The ride takes 3 h. Inside: humidity climbs to 100 %, temperature 24 °C, and the roof reverberates with rain like sheet metal in a foundry. Wear poncho; stray droplets arc sideways through open windows.

At the Plateau Station your first sensation will be auditory vertigo: thousands of overlapping echoes from waterfalls, vents, and machines. Clamp noise-cancellers on before the door rolls open. Then give your cochleae two minutes to “zoom out” and normalise.

--------------------------------------------------
6. Movement once on the ground
--------------------------------------------------
Recommended walking rhythm: 10 s still / 25 s move / 10 s listen, repeat. The plateau rewards patience; predators cue on predictable strides longer than 30 s.

Surface features to recognise:
1. Weeping rocks: silver strands = safe drinking source after filtration.  
2. Sump-lungs: any descent that ends in warm fog is a drowned chamber; back out.  
3. “Star-cliffs”: walls speckled turquoise/violet = lichen colonies = high traction. Good camp spots.  
4. Tremor rails: twin indentations where slurry pipes were laid then removed. Often lead to outposts but also to territorial berserker-moles (industrial beasts bred to chew clogged pipes). Follow only with a guide.

Crossing rivers: never at the lip of a cataract. Wade 200 m upstream where flow is quieter; throw a fist-sized stone first—if it sparks dull red on impact, that pool is saturated with gloam dust and can flash-boil around you.

--------------------------------------------------
7. Sound etiquette
--------------------------------------------------
• Speak in soprano whispers (3–4 kHz). Mid-range human speech overlaps predator sonar.  
• Avoid rhythmic noises. Evenly spaced footfalls mimic wounded shaker geckos.  
• Metallic clang means “claim jump” in miner argot and may draw gunfire; keep tools wrapped.  
• When entering an outpost’s 300-m perimeter, broadcast the infrasound whistle every 15 s to prevent the guard AI from tagging you “biothreat.”

--------------------------------------------------
8. Dealing with inhabitants
--------------------------------------------------
Miners: Gruff, sleep-deprived, often vitamin-D deficient; gift bright-white LEDs or citrus tabs to earn goodwill. Do not record audio inside mess halls—union bylaws classify laughter as “proprietary morale engine.”

Echo-wolves: Four phases of encounter.  
1. Rumour: throb in sinuses ≈40 Hz.  
2. Probe: doppler whine as they shift around you. Stay still, slow pulse to <60 bpm.  
3. Bluff: if they hear metal rattle, they charge 10 m and stop. Throw an alkaline neutraliser packet onto ground; the hiss confuses their vibrissae.  
4. Last resort: 60–80 db white-noise burst; run perpendicular to slope contour for 200 m.

Swarm-lorises: Harmless but curious. Click at them twice; they’ll click back and leave. NEVER feed— they’ll follow you and their echo-maps light you up to every predator.

Flora traps: Vines that align barbs toward exhalations. Counter by exhaling through the mouth downward, not forward, while waving a heated glow-stick about 1 m away; vine turns to heat source instead.

--------------------------------------------------
9. Shelter
--------------------------------------------------
Best: vacant pumping huts (look for 4-m stack, magenta rust). Inside, volume is small so acoustics muted, roof already treated against rain.  
Fallback: between exposed root buttresses of paddle-leaf trees. Spread mylar rug and string poncho overhead as drip deflector; the leaves fold to funnel water but also spores, so keep respirator on till dawn.

Sleep cycles: run daylight lamp for 30 min, ear-cups set to 40 dB brown noise; tricks body into thinking it’s inside a cabin cruiser, not a thunder chamber.

--------------------------------------------------
10. Water & food
--------------------------------------------------
Water: Filter, neutralise, then test with pH strip. Acceptable band 6.5–7.5. If you see faint orange glow, too much gloam—add more resin or move to new source.

Edible local:  
• Velvet moss: tastes of cucumber; boil 60 s to kill rotifer parasites.  
• Paddle-leaf sap nodules: sugar gel, safe raw.  
• Geode-fungi: looks like amethyst eggs; must be roasted until outer shell splits, otherwise spores germinate in lungs.

Avoid: reed pith (alkaline), manta-gnat hemolymph (neurotoxic), anything offering its own heat—plants use warmth as advertising, not generosity.

--------------------------------------------------
11. Psychology & chronobiology
--------------------------------------------------
Under constant dusk you lose track of time within 16 h. Wear a vibrating alarm on the inside of your wrist set to 25-h cycle: 17 h wake / 8 h rest. Slightly longer than Earth day keeps circadian drift under control and matches plateau’s subtle infrared “gloam tide” produced by cloud dynamics.

Auditory overload can cause “echo-fugue”: brief dissociation where you feel projected 5–10 m outside your body. If this happens:  
1. Sit, eyes closed.  
2. Hum at 256 Hz (middle C). The deterministic tone re-anchors perception.  
3. Sip water, 5 s on, 5 s off, until heartbeat steadies.

--------------------------------------------------
12. Exit strategies
--------------------------------------------------
Preferred: same funicular down. Buy ticket at least 24 h in advance; only 30 seats.  
Emergency: signal flare in IR green aimed 45° up; drones on standby will triangulate and drop a self-inflating rescue ball that bounces you to nearest outpost.

Absolute last-ditch: cataract plunge. Do this only if capture or certain death imminent. Strap into coracle, seal suit, bite mouthpiece. Odds of survival unknown; romance of the gesture overrated.

--------------------------------------------------
13. Leave no scars
--------------------------------------------------
The Plateau keeps receipts.  
• Do not hammer metal—use adhesive anchors.  
• Pack out batteries; leaked electrolytes entice vines that then tangle pipelines.  
• When you depart, broadcast 100 Hz goodbye pulse. Local fauna interpret it as “territory released,” minimising disruption after you’re gone.

--------------------------------------------------
14. Post-return decontamination
--------------------------------------------------
1. Incinerate boots and outerwear; spores survive detergents.  
2. Monitor hearing—threshold shift common; see audiologist within a week.  
3. Blood panel for alkaline ion imbalance and gloam micro-particulates.  
4. Four weeks of daylight therapy to restore serotonin baseline.

--------------------------------------------------
15. Final advice from veteran guide “Fluoro Jo”
--------------------------------------------------
“Trust your feet more than your eyes, your diaphragm more than your ears. Keep moving like a rumor, not a proclamation. And remember: the Plateau isn’t dark; it’s just saving its light for the things that have earned it. Act like a guest that might deserve a glimpse, and you may walk away with stories that glow for years.”

Safe travels, and may your footsteps never echo in vain.
