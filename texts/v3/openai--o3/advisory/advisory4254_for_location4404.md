Field Manual: Old-Basin Flats (Unnamed Iron-Clay Continent)

VERSION 3.0 – compiled for civilian multiverse travelers with no prior wetland or bog experience  
Primary author: A. U. C. Rabin, Guild of Wayfarers

-------------------------------------------------
0. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
-------------------------------------------------
You are about to step into a mostly level, water-negotiated land where the ground holds still only until it chooses not to. The threats are subtle—drowning, heat exhaustion, infection, disorientation—rather than dramatic. If you arrive equipped for shin-deep mud, overhead sun, omnipresent moisture and a choir-pit of insects, the basin will treat you as a mildly puzzling new animal and otherwise ignore you. Turn up unprepared and it will wear you down grain by grain.

An ankle-height “drop-in” portal will usually materialise you 20–40 cm above the surface. Your very first decisions—where your feet land and whether they immediately continue sinking—determine if the rest of the visit is leisurely exploration or a two-hour extraction drill.

-------------------------------------------------
1. THE FIVE MAIN CHALLENGES
-------------------------------------------------
C1. Variable Ground Bearing  
  • Load-bearing capacity changes every ½ m. A patch that carried you one step ago may take your staff but swallow your boot.  
  • Some seeps hide 1–2 m of semi-liquid clay under a dry skin.  
C2. Water & Pathogens  
  • Potable water is everywhere, safe water nowhere. Expect protozoan cysts, fluke larvae and high Fe2+ levels.  
C3. Heat & Humidity  
  • Daytime WBGT (Wet Bulb Globe Temp) averages 30 °C. Sweat works poorly; evaporation is slow.  
C4. Insects  
  • Biting midges, skeeter hatches at dusk, opportunistic leeches in knee-deep pools.  
C5. Orientation  
  • Horizon is low, relief almost absent, magnetic compasses fluctuate (iron silt). Sound is the locals’ GPS, not yours.

-------------------------------------------------
2. PERSONAL KIT LIST (72 h field stay, 1 person)
-------------------------------------------------
Feet & Legs  
  • Amphibious ankle-locking boots with interchangeable soles. Fit them with mud skis (10 cm outriggers) for long walks.  
  • Two pairs merino-synthetic socks + quick-dry knee-high neoprene gaiters (block leeches, funnel water out).  

Clothing  
  • Ultralight long-sleeve sun shirt, vented.  
  • Quick-dry cargo trousers with internal ducting (allows evaporation). No shorts; you will regret the insect buffet.  
  • Fold-down, broad-brim hat lined with mylar underside for glare.  
  • Packable rain anorak (15 min convective showers).  

Navigation & Stability  
  • Tri-section carbon wading staff with 20 cm pressure plate tip—test every new step.  
  • Acoustic compass: a palm-size parabolic mic array that visualises dominant frequencies 0.5–3 kHz. Lets you “see” frog colonies and orient by their position.  
  • 1:25 000 LiDAR map sheet (relief exaggerated 10×) in waterproof sleeve + inertial nav watch.  
  • Emergency 3-m inflato-sledge (doubles as sleeping platform above mud).  

Water & Food  
  • Gravity bag filter rated 0.1 µm + carbon stage for iron taste; chemical backup (chlorine dioxide tabs).  
  • 3-day compact rations requiring no flame. (Open flame on damp flats is possible but fuel-costly; carry solid-alcohol tabs if you must cook.)  

Health & Bio-hygiene  
  • 30 % DEET or picaridin lotion; head-net for peak insect hours.  
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotic, antifungal foot powder, oral rehydration salts.  
  • Lightweight mylar bivy (nights remain warm, mainly for insect exclusion).  

Observation / Optional  
  • Pocket hydrophone (listen for sub-surface seep vents).  
  • Sample vials for iron-clay, micro-shrimp, cyanobacteria mats.  
  • 4× monocular (mostly useless horizontally, excellent for insect watching).  

-------------------------------------------------
3. ARRIVAL PROTOCOL
-------------------------------------------------
A. Portal Placement  
   • Request coordinates over a known islet (≥1 m rise) if your gate service allows.  
   • Lacking that, aim for any visible knot of sedge or sandstone hump—the densest vegetation often marks firmer substrate.  
B. First 60 Seconds  
   • Land knees bent; immediately plant wading staff.  
   • If mud rises above boot cuff, back-step onto firmer patch—do not stand still hoping it stops.  
   • Assess wind direction and mark it mentally; soundborne navigation will drift without a frame of reference.  

-------------------------------------------------
4. MOVEMENT & ROUTE-FINDING
-------------------------------------------------
Rule of Thumb: Two staff-tests for every step during the first hour; one test for every other step thereafter once you trust your instincts.

1. Walk the Islets
   • Plot serrated routes that hop islet to islet. Average separation: 10–30 m.  
   • Expect micro-cliffs of desiccated clay around each knoll—use them as natural boot scrapers.  

2. Reading the Ground
   • Dark-wet sheen but zero surface tremor = shallow mud crust, safe for one foot’s weight.  
   • Sheen plus slight quiver when the wind brushes it = water perched within millimetres; avoid.  

3. Sound as Map
   • Carry acoustic compass switched to “chirrup” (1.5–2 kHz) in daylight, “bass” (200–400 Hz) at dusk to follow bullfrog ponds. Mark calls on map; they remain surprisingly fixed seasonally.  

4. Night Travel
   • Not advised. Visual depth cues vanish, and pooled stars in the water create false horizons. If forced, deploy infrared headlamp and move at half-pace on staff and knees.

-------------------------------------------------
5. CAMP CRAFT
-------------------------------------------------
Site Selection  
  • Choose an islet ≥6 m across; check for mammal burrows (they reclaim holes nightly).  
Platform  
  • Inflate sledge, lash to four sandalwood stakes (carry or cut mangrove cousin roots—permission is ecological; they coppice painlessly).  
Shelter  
  • Suspend bivy as a mosquito sock between two tripods. Leave bottom open; warm air will draft upward, taking moisture with it.  
Fire  
  • Collect dead mangrove stems (low resin, slow burn). Build in a collapsible twig stove above a clay tile. Embers sink quickly otherwise.  

-------------------------------------------------
6. HYDRATION & FOOD HANDLING
-------------------------------------------------
Water  
  • Skim from seep throats where flow is outward (detectable by faint outward ripple). Avoid stagnant footprints; cyst density an order of magnitude higher.  
  • Let filtered water stand 5 min; iron will precipitate visibly—decant the clearer top.  
Food  
  • Local forage is possible but ecological cost is high. Killifish and shrimp are edible but each pool is a closed system. Best practice: observe only, eat imported calories.  

-------------------------------------------------
7. HEALTH HAZARDS & FIRST AID
-------------------------------------------------
Maceration / Trench Foot  
  • Strip boots every 4 h, powder, air-dry 10 min even if insects swarm—foot integrity beats comfort.  
Heat Syncopal Events  
  • If light-headed, sit on pack (not ground), sip ORS slowly, fan shirt collar. Shade is almost nonexistent; create some with silvered poncho.  
Vector-Borne Illness  
  • Midges carry filarial worms; start prophylactic ivermectin 24 h pre-entry if medically cleared.  
Leech Wounds  
  • Do not burn or salt; they release more anticoagulant. Slide fingernail under oral sucker, flick. Disinfect wound; dress lightly—occlusion breeds fungus.  

-------------------------------------------------
8. ENVIRONMENTAL & ETHICAL NOTES
-------------------------------------------------
• Height is luxury here—don’t erect 3-m tower tripods for panorama photos. They advertise ignorance and may cause bird collisions.  
• Frog colonies set the basin’s acoustic “grid.” Repeated loud human vocalisations can shift their call sites over weeks. Use low voices and avoid playback devices.  
• The iron-clay matrix is easily scarred. Footprints persist for years. Step on existing tracks or stones when possible.  
• Cyanobacteria skins whistle as they degas. Skimming them for souvenirs collapses micro-channels that took seasons to grow. Photograph, don’t pocket.  

-------------------------------------------------
9. RECOMMENDED ITINERARY (48-h exemplar)
-------------------------------------------------
D0 15:00 Land → Reach nearest islet → Establish camp.  
D0 17:00 Sit quietly, map evening frog choruses.  
D1 05:30 Pre-dawn walk: View mirror flats, observe dragonfly hunt changeover.  
D1 10:00 Heat siesta: hammock, sample sediment cores under tarp shade.  
D1 16:00 Circumnavigate a 1 km square using only acoustic bearings; note displacement error.  
D1 20:00 Night watch: record mass vent “sigh,” hydrophone seep chorus.  
D2 07:00 Pack out, following your inbound prints to minimise new impact.  

-------------------------------------------------
10. EXFILTRATION
-------------------------------------------------
Signal your gate service from islet high point using mirrored hat underside. If forced to trek to designated evac, budget twice the time your inbound took—fatigue, crust collapse and the irresistible urge to stop and listen will slow you.

-------------------------------------------------
11. LAST ADVICE
-------------------------------------------------
Stand still often. The basin rewards patience more than mileage. Spend ten minutes watching a stone darken as it “sweats” and you’ll understand the tempo here. Move respectfully, tread lightly, listen always, and the flats will let you leave with boots, health and wonder intact.

—Safe travels.
