Field Manual  
Preparing for a Sojourn on the Continental Ocean-World (“The Slow Mosaic”)

1. Basic Fact Sheet
• Medium: 100 % cold salt water; no gaseous phase anywhere in the column.  
• Temperature: 2–4 °C, never varies more than 0.3 °.  
• Gravity: ≈0.85 g (you will feel lighter and drift farther with every kick).  
• Ambient pressure: 45 bar (about the pressure at 450 m in an Earth ocean) but perfectly steady; no thermoclines or surge.  
• Illumination: background darkness; sporadic plant-borne cyan pulses (0.2–3 lux).  
• Life: exclusively photosynthesising flora; no macro-fauna, no pathogens detected to date.  
• Hazards: disorientation, hypothermia, psychological deceleration (“the Lull”), and getting physically embedded in slow-moving vegetation mats.

2. Body Logistics
a. Respiration  
The water carries ample dissolved O₂ but not enough for passive skin uptake. Choose one:  
   • Gill-mask (semi-permeable artificial lamellae, battery-assisted flow pump). Excellent for trips under three weeks; silent; no bubbles to scar the mood.  
   • Closed-circuit rebreather with scrubber matrix rated to 50 bar. Brings its own O₂; useful if you distrust local chemistry but noisier and heavier.  
SCUBA in the ordinary sense (open circuit) is discouraged: exhaust bubbles never rise and will cling to ceilings, spoiling the site and possibly confusing future visitors.

b. Thermoregulation  
3 mm neoprene is not enough. Wear a shell-type electrically heated dive-suit (or, if you favour low tech, layered wool under a loose drysuit). Continuous 2 °C water will sap you in under 40 min otherwise. Keep batteries in redundant, internally worn packs; external packs cool, lose current, and die.

c. Pressure Acclimation  
45 bar is high but static; after the initial 20-minute compression stage in your portal capsule, your body stops noticing. On exit, schedule a staged 25-minute decompression in the same capsule; nitrogen loading is mild because you are not exerting yourself hard down there.

3. Mobility & Navigation
• Fins: long, soft-blade freedive fins work best in the low gravity; you will travel ~1 m per kick.  
• Tethers: always trail a 1 mm dyneema guideline from your origin beacon—vision often collapses between light pulses.  
• Mapping beacons: place small ultrasonic pingers at every major bowl lip; the stone transmits well and batteries last decades.  
• Vertical movement: risers seldom exceed 8 cm—perfect stair height for fin tips. Resist the urge to leap; you will float annoyingly before settling, stirring silt that can take hours to resettle.

4. Light Discipline
Bring light but use it sparingly. A 10-lumen cyan LED head-pearl preserves night vision and does not shock the flora. White beams bleach pigments and interrupt spore-timers (the only “weather” there), so keep any full-spectrum lamps hooded and point them at the floor.

5. Personal Timekeeping
All watches fail: hands corrode, LCDs cloud, smart-glass blacks out. The recommended solution is a sealed mechanical metronome on your chestplate whose audible clicks you can count for rough intervals. The pulse of the plants is impossible to schedule—it is their clock, not yours.

6. Psychological Hygiene
The Lull: After 8–12 real-time hours most visitors report a “descent of intention”: speech slows, fin strokes stretch, goals feel impolite. Counter-measures:
• Predetermine a task list on a waterproof slate.  
• Set alarm-vibrations every 30 min; force yourself to note position and objective.  
• Pair up: conversation over bone-conduction comms (high-clarity, low-bitrate) keeps minds anchored.

7. Interaction Protocols
• Do not cut, harvest, or clear plants—each frond is structurally knitting one platform to the next.  
• If you dislodge a basalt block (unlikely), reseat it; the dovetail joints will accept gentle hand pressure.  
• Spores resemble opalescent smoke. Do NOT breathe them unfiltered; they are harmless biologically but will clog gill-lamellae and foul sensors. Engage visor shutters until the cloud settles.

8. Camp & Sleep
Best practice is to sleep topside in your arrival capsule. If you must overnight on site:
• Clear an 8 m² patch down to bare stone (brush only; no blades).  
• Pitch an inflatable dome filled with oxygenated fresh water at 35 °C; this warms you and prevents “cold soak” nerve ache.  
• Anchor with micro-pitons in mortar seams. Over centuries the stone moves, but not during your nap.

9. Food & Waste
Nothing edible grows here and nothing will scavenge after you. All supplies in, all detritus out. Pack high-calorie gels you can squeeze through your regulator port. For waste, use double-sealed pouches and keep them on a separate harness ring; retrieval capsules have burn units.

10. Emergency Procedures
Lost line: Surface is impossibly distant; ascent is lethal. Stop, anchor to stone, cut every second light on your suit, wait for partner search.  
Suit breach: Pressure is friend, not enemy—seal with quick-curing epoxy patch tape; the water will not “rush” in.  
Heat failure: Abort immediately; shiver response is nearly absent in 2 °C after the first hour and you will not feel yourself fading.

11. Souvenirs & Ethics
The chisels, pegs, and driftwood dowels are heritage items. Removing them fractures the only record of a civilization whose history is already erased by silt. Photogrammetry, not pocketing, please.

12. Suggested Kit List (72-hour sortie, single traveler)
• Portal compression capsule with programmable portal beacon  
• Heated drysuit + spare underlayer  
• Dual gill-mask modules, 12-hour each  
• Backup 3-L O₂ bailout bottle, integrated to rebreather mouthpiece  
• Two 30 m dyneema guide spools  
• Five ultrasonic mapping pingers  
• Cyan head-pearl (10 lm) + hooded 100 lm work-lamp  
• Bone-conduction comm set, 72-h battery  
• Waterproof slate & grease pencils  
• Compact epoxy patch kit, duct tape, para-aramid line  
• 9 × 400 kcal gel packs; 3 L fresh water  
• Waste pouches, bio-lock tablets  
• Inflatable 2-person thermodome, 35 °C heater coil  
• Mechanical chest metronome  
• Personal retrieval beacon (sonic + magnetic)

13. Departure Debrief
Schedule at least 12 h of topside “fast world” re-entrainment. Loud music, hot food, and conversation are recommended. Many travelers keep hearing the hush for up to a day; this is normal. If, after 48 h, you still feel compelled to do everything slowly, seek chrono-psych therapy—sometimes the Lull lingers.

If you follow the above, the Slow Mosaic will accept you as a brief, polite disturbance. Kick gently, speak softly, and leave no turbulence in the water behind you. The ocean has been waiting a long time; it will still be waiting, perfectly arranged, after you are gone.
