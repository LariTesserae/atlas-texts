Field Guide: Preparing for the Under-Slope Geode  
(Provisional Multiverse Registry: L-873/“The Cavern That Isn’t Finished Yet”)

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
1. Risk & reward snapshot
• Threat level: Moderate (Class 2). No single element is lethal on its own; cumulative surprises can escalate quickly.  
• Scientific value: Very high—unique mineralogenesis; relativistic micro-weather; novel trophic web.  
• Tourist appeal: Sublime visuals, gentle sensory distortions, low noise.  
• Recommended party size: 2-3. Solo entry possible but unwise; anything larger alters the cavern climate and attracts thread-wolves.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
2. Personal kit (per traveller)
Clothing:
• Base layer: synthetic or silk—wicks but keeps warmth in 8 °C drizzle.  
• Mid-layer: thin fleece. Bulky coats snag on gypsum fins.  
• Shell: breathable rain jacket with hood (fine plaster dust + dripping condensate).  
• Bottoms: soft hiking pants; avoid stiff fabric that scrapes crystal film into powder.  
• Footwear: flexible approach shoes, non-aggressive tread (deep lugs tear the floor).  
• Gloves: nitrile liner + thin leather outer. Bare hands abrade gypsum; sweat collapses prisms.

Eye & lung protection:
• Wrap-around goggles with anti-fog coating. Cavern brightness can equal full daylight and micro-shards ride the air currents.  
• Lightweight N95 mask. Not strictly necessary for health, but breathe out too much CO₂ and water globes will flock to your face.

Navigation & safety:
• 40 m spool of 3 mm fluorescent paracord—anchor outside, unreel in. Cavern edits doorways; cord does not care.  
• Headlamp with red filter. White beams perturb photo-moss circadian cycle; red is tolerated.  
• Two tiny chemical heat packs. If gravity lulls and water drifts upward you may cool rapidly.  
• Collapsible climbing wedge or air bladder to brace the entry slit on exit.  
• Analog watch on lanyard (digital displays sometimes freeze when local time dilates).  
• Waterproof field notebook + pencil; audio recorders work, but replay outside can scramble timestamps.

Electronics:
• Camera with manual aperture and physical shutter (mirrorless auto-exposure loops).  
• All devices in condensation-proof pouches; silica gel reloads every hour.

First aid & counter-fauna:
• Standard kit plus veterinary-grade saline: gutter-bat tongue licks can irritate skin.  
• 30 ml atomizer of 5 % acetic acid. A light mist deters thread-wolves—they dislike the pH shift but fungus threads and prism growth ignore it. Use only if cordon threatened.  
• Small microfiber towel to wick rogue globes from hair, gear, or reference samples.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
3. Timing your visit
Barometric “tides” arrive roughly 11:40 apart. Enter immediately after a warm exhalation cycle—the ceiling is stable, air quite still. Plan to be moving toward exit as the second chill gust begins; debris drop probability triples in that window.

Daylight above drives the light cascade below. Mid-afternoon outside = maximum internal brilliance (spectacular), but fauna feeding frenzy peaks then. Dawn or late dusk give calmer biology and easier photography of slow drips.

Avoid new-moon nights: slit sometimes re-opens onto the cliff variant mentioned in the brief.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
4. Conduct in the chambers

General rules:
1. Speak softly. Cavern auto-anagrams loud speech; past 80 dB the “translations” can start manifesting aurally for everyone.  
2. Do not touch floating globes bare-handed unless sampling. Each burst raises local humidity and erodes time-tension; three in quick succession can stall gravity for minutes.  
3. Stay luminous but not hot. Body temperature differences of >12 °C above air cause dew halos that lure blink-shrimp swarms. They are harmless but will chew nylon straps.  
4. Keep still a few seconds every five minutes. This resets your vestibular system; when gravity vectors jog, you notice sooner and prevent ankle rotations.  

Working with geology:
• Avoid chipping prisms. Anything detached becomes ground by the flux floor within hours—waste of science. If sampling is mandatory, collect fallen debris only.  
• Laser rangefinders read short by ~1.6 %. Triangulate with weighted string instead.

Interacting with life:
Blink-shrimp: attractive to light. A slow sweeping motion with your gloved hand gently herds them away.  
Gutter-bats: will land for salt. Offer the back of your (gloved) hand—friendlier than having them mine sweat from your eyelids.  
Thread-wolves: treat cord colonies as you would tripwires. Stow any dangling straps; do not twitch when you sense topology shift. If encircled, low steady hum (150–200 Hz) encourages them to unravel.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
5. Research & ethics

Sampling quotas (per international Multiversal Speleology Accord 4.1):
• Mineral: ≤50 g total; only unconsolidated fragments.  
• Water: ≤30 ml, captured in pre-weighed vials.  
• Biological: photo only; no organism removal yet authorized. Genetic drift rate is measured in hours—outside lab culture fails.

Leave no plastics. Thread-wolves incorporate fibers into hunting chains which later confuse comparative studies.

The origami-wren clay array is a protected Cultural Probe. Observe, measure, but do not disturb alignment.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
6. Exiting & post-exit protocol

Route:
• Follow the paracord, confirm physical contact every 5 m.  
• At the slit, wedge or inflate brace before the last traveller squeezes out. Rock often “forgets” the gap once unoccupied.  

Quarantine:
• Shake gear vigorously; invisible gypsum dust continues to crystalize outside, producing razor-thin blades along fabric folds.  
• Expect personal humidity anomalies for ~6 h. Keep electronics off during this period; condensation can recur spontaneously.  
• Language drift wears off after one sleep cycle. If briefing debrief staff, write rather than speak until they confirm semantic integrity.

Health check:
• Mild hypothermia common if stay exceeds 2 h—rewarm with beverages, not open flame (gear may still exhale water).  
• Jawbone itch from inaudible bat sonar fades within minutes. Persistent buzzing warrants ENT examination for micro-crystal deposit.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
7. What to hope for, what to fear

Possible high points:
• Witnessing a negative stalagmite fully close its fog funnel—looks like beige thunder reversing.  
• Photographing dual water globes colliding, merging, then splitting—rare, publishable.  
• Conversational echo that returns your sentence rearranged into a solution to a problem you hadn’t voiced yet (document carefully).

Possible hazards:
• Gravity stall + water clone surge = sudden buoyancy of loose items (phones, knives) that then fall unpredictably.  
• Thread-wolf snares across exit slit—feel with trekking pole first.  
• Door relocating topside. Carry 15 m static line so a quick rappel is possible if you emerge on the cliff variant.

–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––
8. Departure mindset

Respect that the place is, literally, unfinished. Walk as though inside a sketch still being inked. Every careless scuff, loud laugh, or unwatched tool becomes part of the final draft.

Leave with pockets of unearned dew and sentences that explain more than they should; consider these souvenirs payment for having intruded on a room practicing how to be real.

Safe travels, and keep your shadows faint and orderly.
