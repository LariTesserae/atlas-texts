Practicalities First – Before You Even Step Through the Waygate
1. Permissions. The Junara allow visitors only on invitation from one of their “Tenders-in-Training.” Such invitations are hand-woven from living reed and arrive still photosynthesising. Do not assume you may simply pop in. Secure an invitation, keep it moist, and present it immediately on arrival.  
2. Quarantine. Ninety percent of their endemic fungi are unknown to Earth-side medicine; the Junara are equally wary of our microbes. Expect a three-day fungal-bath quarantine in a ventilated root-cocoon. Pack reading crystals or learn to meditate.  
3. Weight Audit. Re-pack until the total mass of everything you bring—including worn clothes— is less than your standing body casts on the ground at noon (their literal interpretation of “your shadow’s weight”). Aim for 7 kg or less.

Physical Conditioning
• Balance training. Spend a month walking slack-lines, low tightropes, and sprung planks. Catwalks on the plateau flex 10–30 cm under a normal adult load and rebound unpredictably in wind.  
• Squat endurance. Seating is rarely provided. You’ll eat, converse, and observe while in a relaxed knee-bend on springy floor-roots. Practice until you can hold the posture for ten minutes without shaking.  
• Cold-humid acclimation. Simulate 8 °C at 95 % relative humidity in a walk-in cooler or adapt gradually with cold showers. It is possible to feel chilled and clammy while still sweating.

Packing List (Shadow-Weight Version)
Clothing
• Base: ultralight merino or bamboo long underwear. It insulates while wet and resists odor.  
• Shell: a single hooded poncho of electrospun nanofiber (≤180 g). Breathable, water-shedding, silent against vegetation.  
• Footwear: amphibious trail shoes with felted rubber soles. No heavy boots—the substrate swallows heels. Bring two quick-drain pairs; alternate daily.  
• Hands: thin nitrile-dipped bamboo gloves. Protects from rasping vines but preserves tactile feedback.  
• Night layer: inflatable mylar “sleep skin.” Adds 6 °C of apparent warmth and packs to a fist.

Tools & Miscellany
• Head-lamp with amber LED. White light startles sap-glow insects; amber blends with the plants’ own luminescence.  
• Fixed-blade ceramic knife. Metal ions inhibit certain plateau microbes, so steel is taboo; ceramic is acceptable and non-magnetic.  
• 15 m dyneema micro-cord (2 mm). For emergency tethering on wind-flexed balconies.  
• Refillable squeezebottle of unscented, biodegradable lanolin-based salve. Your skin will macerate after day three without it.  
• Collapsible kopje cup with integrated stone-pocket. Junara cuisine arrives as hot broth in leaf gums; you supply the hot stone to warm it.  
• One generous gift. Recommended: a high-resolution thunderprint of a non-plateau sunrise sealed in resin. Visual gifts are prized; anything that burns or smells of hydrocarbons will be refused.

What Not to Bring
✘ Open flames, matches, lighters, stoves, candles. Even a struck match is grounds for expulsion.  
✘ Synthetics that shed microfibers (fleece, some nylons). The plateau filters nothing—your lint becomes an ecological event.  
✘ Audible electronics. Speakers, rattling drones, even a clicky camera shutter add to an already saturated soundscape and mark you as uncouth.

Arrival Etiquette
• Announce yourself by striking the heel of your palm three times against the nearest living column, then wait. The column transmits the vibration; a Tender will appear.  
• Do not shake hands. Instead, gently exhale toward the greeter’s forehead. This shares breath humidity—an offering of water.  
• Tread where invited. Many surfaces are in mid-growth; a misplaced step can shear embryonic handrails and set back weeks of guided growth.

Moving About
• Walk heel-to-ball; keep knees loose. Platforms bounce. Lean into the motion like riding a slow trampoline.  
• Never place both feet on a retracting stair. Maintain a one-step cadence; stalls confuse the staircase’s mechanoperception and may cause it to fold.  
• Wind gusts announce themselves with a two-note “reed gasp.” When you hear it, squat and grasp a fixed stem. Visitors blown off ledges seldom survive the fifty-metre curtain-root fall.

Staying Dry(ish)
You won’t. The goal is thermal comfort, not dryness. Swap base layers when chilled; warm layers go next to skin, wet layers outermost. Hang unused garments in the breeze channels—never near sap-glow ducts; the sugar steam attracts knife-beaks (small predatory birds that sever dangling fabric for nesting).

Food & Drink
The Junara provide:
• Ferment-pods: tart, effervescent plantains mashed with probiotic spores—think kombucha yoghurt.  
• Hot leaf-broths, heated by your personal stone.  
• Dulcet mould sheets—a sweet protein film grown on aerial roots.

Bring salt tablets. Plateau fare is mineral-light; travelers risk hyponatremia after hard climbs.

Health & Hazards
1. Maceration. Treat feet and groin with lanolin salve twice daily.  
2. Hypothermia. Fog-drenched nights at 3 °C feel colder than a still, dry −2 °C. Wear the sleep-skin and share body warmth inside a hollow reed dormitory.  
3. Allergens. The air carries potent terpenes. Carry a minimal antihistamine supply (the one pharmaceutical exception the Junara allow).  
4. Puncture Fauna. The “needle shrimp” can launch from puddles and skewer exposed ankles. Their strike gives an instant bruise but no venom; thick socks suffice.  
5. Sound Fatigue. Constant biophony can exhaust newcomers. Ear filters (10–15 dB reduction) help, but don’t overuse; auditory situational awareness is safety.

Social Customs
• Conversation rarely exceeds fifty words per speaker; verbosity is considered moisture-waste (exhaled vapour). Rehearse concise statements.  
• Every night at mist-closure the community chants a four-tone drone. Join softly or observe in silence; never speak between tones.  
• Gift exchange happens once, at first meal. After that, the ledger is balanced—offering more gifts hints you think their hospitality insufficient.

Things to Do
• Dawn Balance Run. Trot the circumference catwalk as sunrise sparks in dew. Guides clip you to a vine harness; try not to look off-edge when the fog parts.  
• Assisted Tending. Help redirect infant creepers to replace a cracked bridge. You’ll use bone awls and the plant’s own turgor pressure—botanical origami.  
• Cloud-Drinking. At midday peaks, extend a cupped leaf and sip condensed droplets tasting faintly of ozone and mineral.  
• Noctilucent Watch. After total dark, the plateau’s sap circulation pulses amber. Climb a watch-spire and witness the whole city breathing light.

Emergency Protocol
If you become injured or ill:
1. Strike any reed rail rapidly five times. The plant releases a terpene that summons tenders.  
2. Remain still. The stairs and walkways will lock in place to create a rigid stretcher route.  
3. Expect treatment with mycelial poultices and sap salines. Decline politely if you fear cross-contamination, but be aware: they view refusal of aid as self-harm and may restrain you “for your own tending.”

Departure & Decontamination
The Junara will ask you to remain three extra nights after your official leave-taking. During this time:
• All personal textiles are steamed with antiseptic fern vapour.  
• Your body is swabbed for stray propagules; anything viable is offered the choice of staying. (Yes, they are serious. Organisms have agency here.)  
• A final weigh-out verifies you removed no mass of plateau life. If you’re heavier than arrival, expect to donate an equivalent weight of personal gear.

Psychological After-Effects
Many travelers report “silence vertigo” for days after leaving: the modern world seems too still, too empty of layered sound. Counter it by playing low rainforest ambience while you sleep. Some develop a compulsion to compost every scrap of waste—an admirable souvenir habit.

Last Advice in One Breath
Pack light, speak lighter, stay supple, and remember that the entire plateau is listening—not with malice, simply with the full attention of a place that does not distinguish between its own cells and visiting ones. Behave as though you are a temporary organ, and the Junara will treat you like one: protected, nourished, and—when the time comes—gently expelled back into your native circulation.
