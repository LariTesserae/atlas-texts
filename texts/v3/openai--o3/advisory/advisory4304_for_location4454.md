Preparation Guide for the Sunken Volcanic Engine-Chapel

OVERVIEW
This chamber is neither a mine nor a factory nor a cave; it is a compact, overheated, electrically active, nearly air-tight industrial cathedral embedded in juvenile volcanic glass. The physical dangers (heat, sharp stone, conductive water, sporadic toxic gases) are real, but the psychological impacts—echoing stillness, amber half-light, the sense that the building itself breathes—can be just as disorienting. Prepare as if you are going into a high-temperature clean room that doubles as the belly of an experimental power plant.

1. PERMITS, ACCESS & TIMING
• Authority. The site is overseen by the Quiet Geothermics Consortium. Secure a “Level-S” visitor badge at least three weeks ahead; background screening includes a combustion-hazard questionnaire.  
• Arrival window. Schedule your descent for the first maintenance block after local dawn (05:00–07:30 station time). During that window the condensers run at a gentler 80 % output and the elevator has a dedicated safety tech on standby.  
• Length of stay. Limit any continuous exposure to 35 minutes. Plan two 15-minute cool-down breaks topside between repeat visits.  

2. PERSONAL PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT (PPE)
All gear must be spark-proof, close-fitting, and rated for 110 °C ambient.

Essentials
• Undergarment: moisture-wicking, fire-retardant one-piece (Nomex or equivalent).  
• Outer suit: thin silicone-impregnated Kevlar coverall—no loose pockets, ties, or cuffs.  
• Gloves: high-dexterity, triple-layer (silicone outer for grip when wet, aramid middle, silvered heat-reflective inner).  
• Footwear: composite-toe, ceramic-soled boots with micro-tread; side zips only (laces fray and snag).  
• Head: ventilated composite helmet with low-lux, intrinsically-safe LED strip (amber or red; white light bakes the air); flip-down polycarbonate shield for steam bursts.  
• Respiratory: half-mask respirator, cartridges rated for SO₂/H₂S + particulates. (Self-contained air is unnecessary and adds bulk unless gas alarms trend ≥ 15 ppm).  
• Eye protection: wraparound anti-fog goggles (steam + sulfur crystallize on lenses).  
• Hydration: 1-litre, soft-sided, heat-reflective bladder worn high between shoulder blades with drinking tube tucked under suit collar. Pre-load with electrolyte solution, not plain water.  

Forbidden
• Cotton, wool, scarves, dangling jewelry, Mag-straps, open-flame anything, lithium-ion tools not “intrinsically safe,” bottled sprays, snack wrappers that can flutter.  

3. TOOLS & INSTRUMENTS
Recommended carry weight ≤ 4 kg.

• Navigation: laminated 1:500 schematic; magnetic chalk pencil for temporary marks (won’t wash off in drips).  
• Light: pen-size LED probe light with CRI > 90 (helps differentiate black basalt from black obsidian).  
• Touch probe: 1 m collapsible carbon-fiber rod—test walkway stability before committing weight.  
• Thermal indicator tabs (stickers change at 60 °C and 90 °C). Slap onto handrails to check surface temp before gripping.  
• Multigas detector, shoulder-mounted, audio-only alarm (no flashing strobes).  
• Compact first-aid pouch: hydrogel burn dressings, liquid bandage, electrolyte tablets, glucose pouches.  
• Data: voice-note recorder rated IP68/110 °C; optical storage preferred (magnetic heads sometimes desynchronize here).  
Leave cameras topside unless they are sealed in heat-sink housings; humidity will kill standard lenses in minutes.

4. ENVIRONMENTAL PROTOCOLS
Entering
• Step through the antechamber mist-lock: boots and suit are steam-sterilized for 30 s to prevent alien microbes colonising the biofilm.  
• On elevator descent, secure helmet and power up gas detector—methane pockets may ride the cage.  
• Once on the obsidian floor, pause 10 s to orient. The hush fools many into thinking machinery is off; it rarely is.

Moving Around
• Stay on catwalks whenever possible. The mesh flexes but will not abrade like the raw glass.  
• If you must cross the obsidian sheet, choose dust-dry sectors; wet ones can flash into slippery glass soap.  
• Lichen rules: never step closer than a boot-length to green seams; rock beneath is likely hollow.  
• Maintain three-point contact. Even glove-insulated hands are safer on rails than flailing in open air.  
• Use the probe rod to tap grates before standing over them; some warp when pumps cycle.  

Listening
• Memorize the auditory rhythm: low pump drone, cyclic hiss, breaker clicks. Any change—especially silence overhead—means abort.  
• Valve T-clacks in triplets are normal; sustained hissing > 4 s indicates a stuck vent—move cross-wind and alert maintenance via short-burst radio.  

Heat Management
• Heart-rate threshold: 140 bpm sustained = time to leave. Heatstroke can develop even at “just below scalding.”  
• Sip fluid every five minutes. If it tastes metallic, filter pack has failed; return immediately.  
• Cooling trick: press gloved palms against a condensate pipe (never a geothermal line) for 20 s.  

5. EMERGENCY EVACUATION
Primary: elevator cage (60 s ascent).  
Secondary: helical service shaft 25 m east of elevator—ladder rungs ceramic-coated, almost always bone-dry.  
Tertiary: if both routes fail, deploy emergency beacon; engineers trigger ceiling louvers that flood the chamber with chilled nitrogen, suppressing heat but also dropping oxygen—your respirator becomes mandatory air source for the 8-minute purge.

6. FIELD ETIQUETTE & IMPACT
• Treat it as sacred industrial tissue. Engineers consider the chamber semi-sentient; banging on panels is rude.  
• Do not harvest vines, lichen, or obsidian shards. Sampling requires a form signed by both Biointegrity and Structural Integrity sections.  
• Keep voice low. Whisper-echo mapping is a diagnostic tool for the staff; loud conversation pollutes their data.  
• Replace any bulb you accidentally knock out—spare sodium lamps are lashed to every third pillar.  
• Leave no heatprint: scrub boot soles on the copper grate mat before boarding the elevator; it removes trace metallic salts that could seed extraneous mineral growth topside.

7. MENTAL PREPARATION
Expect cognitive distortion:
• Echo delay (300–450 ms) tricks you into thinking someone is following you.  
• Flicker fatigue from sodium bulbs can cause phantom movements in peripheral vision.  
• The smell of sulfur + salt often triggers taste hallucinations (metallic lemon). Carry a strong mint to reset palate.  
Meditate before entry, and rehearse a simple grounding mantra (“Drip, hiss, step, breathe”) to stay oriented.

8. SAMPLE 30-MINUTE ITINERARY
00:00–03:00  Elevator descent, acclimatization pause.  
03:00–10:00  Traverse upper mesh bridge to west condensers; record drip cadence, log valve temps.  
10:00–18:00  Drop to mid-level catwalk, collect biofilm luminance data under a failed bulb (pre-approved).  
18:00–23:00  Cross dry obsidian to new fracture brace—touch only via insulated probe; thermal-image the crack.  
23:00–27:00  Retreat to main pier, hydrate, check gas detector, swap memory card.  
27:00–30:00  Begin ascent while condensers cycle up (ceiling noise rises). Log any anomalous echoes on the ride out.

9. SOUVENIRS & DATA EXPORT
Acceptable:  
• A sanctioned 1 cm basalt chip (laser-cut from maintenance scrap).  
• Time-lapse of lichen phosphorescence (max 10 s exposure per frame).  
Forbidden:  
• Obsidian flakes (knife-sharp, structural value).  
• Detached gauges (“dead” instruments are usually still parasitically alive).  

10. DEPARTURE & AFTERCARE
• Shower in tepid water within 15 minutes to dissolve sulfur crystals in pores.  
• Rehydrate to replace 1.5× fluid lost (weigh yourself before/after).  
• 12-hour rest; data review only after body temp normalizes—heat exhaustion compromises judgment.  
• Report any dizziness, tinnitus, or lingering metallic taste to clinic; early-onset sulfur neuropathy is treatable if caught within 24 h.

FINAL THOUGHT
Enter with the humility you would bring to a functioning heart-lung machine: every component exists to keep the greater body—volcano, water, microbes, and now you—in precarious balance. Respect that living mechanism, move deliberately, and you will walk out richer in stories, unburned, and astonishingly dry.
