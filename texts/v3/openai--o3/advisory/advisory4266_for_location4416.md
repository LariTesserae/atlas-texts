1. WHAT YOU ARE ABOUT TO VISIT  
The basin is a self-modulating hydrothermal field perched 3 200 m above mean plateau level, wrapped in an active gravito-magneto lens apparently generated by nine glass pylons. Temperatures hover between 32 °C and 41 °C (90–106 °F) despite the thin air. Surfaces are razor-sharp, wet, and sometimes move a few millimetres underfoot. Clock skew of up to −38 µs has been measured between rim and outside world, enough to desync satellites and confuse fine electronics but not to rip open causality—yet. Treat the site as an intelligent experimental apparatus that merely tolerates your presence.

2. PERSONAL ELIGIBILITY CHECKLIST  
• Cardiovascular health suited to 3 000 m altitude.  
• No implanted pacemakers, cochlear devices, or metal orthopaedics (fields around pylons induce eddy currents).  
• No clotting disorders or severe haemophilia (microscopic glass flakes travel on the wind).  
• Ability to carry 15 kg while scrambling on unstable talus.  
• Claustrophobia/vertigo assessed: fog closes to <2 m visibility without warning.  
If any bullet fails, stay home; this site offers no rescue infrastructure.

3. APPROACH & EXFILTRATION LOGISTICS  
Preferred route: helicopter drop‐off 800 m southwest on a basalt shelf. A footpath (fixed with orange ceramic pitons) zigzags to the rim in 40 minutes.  
Backup: alpine climb from the plateau’s north couloir (adds 5-6 h, used when rotorcraft cannot approach because of electromagnetic shearing).  
Never bring more than two travellers onto the rim simultaneously; empirical evidence suggests the basin dampens or “culls” additional biological mass—results range from benign disorientation to selective amnesia.  
Exfil: budget 20 % more time than ingress. Worn hikers frequently misjudge distances because GPS is off by up to 70 m laterally.

4. CLOTHING & BODY-CARE SYSTEMS  
• Boots: Vibram or equivalent soles plus replaceable Kevlar gaiters; rim glass slices leather seconds after it dulls rubber.  
• Lower body: lightweight heat-reflective hiking pants with internal ceramic bead padding over knees/shins (you will kneel to examine rills).  
• Upper body: wicking baselayer + loose, light-coloured outer shell rated to 120 °C surface contact; sleeves that cinch tight so glass splinters cannot ride the sweat film to your elbows.  
• Hands: double-glove system—cut-resistant Dyneema under thin nitrile disposables; swap nitrile every pulse cycle to avoid micro-cuts filling with acidic condensate.  
• Eyes: UV400 wraparounds plus side shields; volcanic glass facets throw needles of near-UV that feel like welding arc-flash after an hour.  
• Head: vented climbing helmet. Add a silk scarf you can draw across mouth/nose when sudden supersaturated fog arrives (prevents inhaling glass dust).  
• Hydration: 3 L minimum, electrolyte-rich. The sparkling run-off is not potable; pH fluctuates between 4.3 and 8.7 over a single pulse.  
• Cooling: phase-change neck band or ice-gel insert in helmet. Heat exhaustion masquerades as altitude dizziness here.

5. TOOLS, INSTRUMENTS & CONSUMABLES  
Mandatory:  
• Inertial nav puck (dead-reckoning gyro/accelerometer) that does not depend on GPS.  
• Analog compass (expect drift; carry to confirm disturbances).  
• Fluke-class multimeter & Hall probe to map pylon field lines before you step between them.  
• 25 m spectra line + self-locking carabiners (anchor to pitons in case rim segments “shift” while you circumnavigate).  
• Non-electronic chronometer (spring drive); compare against wrist computer every 10 minutes to log clock skew.  
• Biothermal infrared camera for spotting hollow fractures ready to vent scalding steam.  
• First-aid: haemostatic gauze, burn gel, 500 mg ciprofloxacin tabs (opportunistic thermophiles), antiemetic, 250 mg acetazolamide for altitude.  
• Two Faraday bags: one for data drives you wish to shield from stray induction, one for phone/radio while your ear is against a pylon’s aperture (the differential knocks solid-state memories into bit-rot).  
Optional but wise:  
• Unmanned micro-quad tied to 50 m fiber leash; RF is unreliable, but optical cable gives you live overhead mapping.  
• Small mirror and wax pencil; the basin sometimes “forgets” reflections—mark your own.

6. BEHAVIOR PROTOCOLS INSIDE THE RIM  
• Do not enter within two metres of the central sump during a pulse window (±3 min from the half-hour, top-of-hour, and twenty-minute mark). Flash scald is instantaneous and throws droplets that etch lenses.  
• Walk the rim clockwise first; local shear vectors trend to cancel forward drift that way, reducing risk of the “370-degree lap” phenomenon.  
• Never shout—sound reflections can stall in mid-air and return amplified enough to damage hearing. Speak in low voice or use bone-conduction headsets.  
• When approaching a pylon, keep metallic items >50 cm from the aperture. If you must listen, press only your ear cartilage; teeth fillings and earbuds both vibrate uncomfortably. Limit listening to 30 seconds to avoid foreknowledge migraine.  
• If you toss a stone to test spatial continuity, stand well back. Duplicate landings sometimes erupt in a pressure ring that can knock you from your feet.

7. COPING WITH ANOMALOUS SPACE-TIME EFFECTS  
Symptom: you complete a circuit yet your starting piton tag is not where expected. Response: halt, close eyes, count 30 heartbeats, reopen. The lens often realigns to observer’s last firm kinesthetic memory.  
Symptom: double vision of moving water, or “two stones” effect. Response: trust the second instance; first usually registers from a near-local, not-quite-now frame and represents a ghost trajectory.  
Symptom: wristwatch disagrees with spring chronometer by >200 ms. Response: evacuate; field strength is rising unpredictably.  
Rule of thumb: if any reference (sight, sound, or measurement) disagrees with two others, believe the majority, leave the bowl, reassess.

8. HEALTH & SAFETY—FAST REFERENCE  
Heat stroke signs = halt, shade under moss overhang (safe spots rarely above 35 °C), rehydrate.  
Laceration = rinse with bottled sterile water not basin water; basin water fosters glass-shelled extremophiles that polymerize blood proteins.  
Steam burn = cool 15 min, apply silver-sulfadiazine.  
Altitude headache = acetazolamide 250 mg, moderate exertion.  
Vertigo/fog-panic = crouch until fog collapses (average 90 s). If not, retrace rope guide out of rim.

9. SAMPLE-TAKING, ETHICS & LEGALITIES  
• International Multiverse Directive 14-C places the basin under “Obtrusive-Observation Only” status. One gram of inorganic sample and one cubic-centimetre of biota per expedition is the legal maximum.  
• Cutting or fracturing the pylons is expressly forbidden; previous attempt detonated a drone’s battery with induced current.  
• Document every extraction with timestamped photo and geotag (from inertial map) and file report to the Custodial Council within 72 hours.  
• Moss cushions retract when touched; cue to stop. Violating this provokes brown necrosis trails visible for decades.

10. ABORT TRIGGERS & EMERGENCY PLANS  
Immediate evacuation if:  
• Air temperature spikes >45 °C for more than 60 s.  
• Three consecutive pulses miss their forecast windows (pylon system entering alternate mode).  
• Your inertial nav shows cumulative drift >5 m relative to ingress marker.  
• You hear continuous glass “pinging” lasting >8 s—rim about to shed dangerous shards.  
Evac route: follow fixed spectra line or north couloir; helicopters will not enter if pylon glow exceeds baseline luminescence by 15 %. If disoriented, move downslope; gravity vector, though bent, still points away from bowl centre.

EPILOGUE: THE STATE OF MIND TO BRING  
Accept that the place is younger, hotter, and perhaps smarter than you. Curiosity is welcome; hubris is not. Walk lightly. Touch little. Keep your observations but leave no imprint beyond footprints that the next pulse can wash clean. If you depart with your timepieces still agreeing and a soft ring of cooling glass in your ears, you have visited well.
