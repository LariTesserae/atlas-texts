Field Briefing:
Preparing for Expedition 7-ZX / The Basin of Thirteen Throats
Elevation 5 020 m   |   Mean temp +8 °C day / –2 °C night   |   Constant wind 85–110 km h-1   |   Continuous sound pressure 110–128 dB

────────────────────────────────────────
1. Pre-departure Conditioning
• Altitude tolerance. Spend at least a week above 3 500 m on some other world or in a hypobaric chamber. Anyone who has ever experienced high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema should not attempt the trip.
• Inner-ear fitness. Two weeks of daily vibro-plate sessions (30 Hz, 15 min) accustoms the semicircular canals to near-continuous low-frequency shaking and reduces vertigo in the Basin by ~40 %.
• Hand-signal literacy. Download the 48-gesture “Quiet Sign” lexicon used by local nomads. Practice until you can converse while wearing blackout earphones; spoken words will be useless on-site.
• Audiological baseline. Have a clinic record your present hearing curve. Even with protection you will probably lose 2–5 dB at 1 kHz; a baseline lets you document compensation claims with your multiverse insurer.

────────────────────────────────────────
2. Portage Route & Timing
Standard entry follows the eastern knife-edge ridge. Expect a two-day pack approach from the dirigible drop-zone at High Tern Plateau (surface gravity here is 0.97 g; pack weight remains roughly terrestrial).

Arrive after local dawn:
• Wind is already strong but harmonics are least chaotic, simplifying descent.
Avoid late afternoon entry:
• Solar glare + maximum sleet needles = near-whiteout conditions along the rim.

Nomads migrate through Alcove IX during the equinox fortnight; visit outside that window if you want solitude.

────────────────────────────────────────
3. Personal Equipment Checklist
A. Acoustic Survival
1. Dual-layer hearing system:
   – Inner layer: custom silicone musicians’ plugs cut for –35 dB broadband.
   – Outer layer: active earmuff shells with bone-conduction comms link (throat mic + mastoid transducer).
2. Resonance harness (optional but recommended): web of kevlar straps threaded through sorbothane pads; worn under packs to damp whole-torso vibration that can, over multi-day stays, loosen dental fillings and induce “ghost palpitations.”

B. Climate & Radiation
3. Category-4 wind suit: aerogel-lined, rip-stop, integrated kneepads and elbow pads (you will be knocked down at some point).
4. Photochromic glacier goggles rated UV-400; daylight UV index is 18–20.
5. SPF-100 mineral sunscreen; mica dust reflects additional UV upward.
6. Layered gloves: dexterous inner, windproof outer. Need fingers free to sign.

C. Shelter
7. Low-profile tunnel tent, lengthwise to wind, with internal tension ribs (no exterior poles = less noise). Guy ropes replaced by 5 mm dynema lines weighted with stone bags—stakes will not hold in schist gravel.
8. One roll 6 mm sorbothane matting for floor and for sitting on rock; acts as both thermal and acoustic buffer.

D. Life Support
9. Water: 3 L / person / day carried in collapsible bladders. Treat melt pools only after triple filtration (sediment → 0.2 µm filter → UV pen); microbial films are mostly benign but the silica honeycomb will shred an ordinary pump filter.
10. Isobutane/propane canister stove with pre-heat loop; flame envelope is unstable at low O₂, so bring wind baffle and ignite with piezo baton, not matches.
11. High-fat compact rations (lift bars, nut-oil sachets). Caloric burn is 1.5× normal because bodies fight cold and hypoxia simultaneously.

E. Field Tools
12. Geocord locator—GPS sats are visible but masking occurs in the alcoves, so cache coordinates on approach ridge.
13. Chisel hammer and canvas roll if you intend to collect “singing flakes.” Wrap each shard; the ringing attracts skimmers that will bite exposed skin in swarms.
14. 10 m paracord & micro-grapnel: for retrieving packs that vibrated off ledges (common).
15. Signal panel (0.5 m) in blaze orange for over-ridge evac pickup; flapping fabric doubles as emergency silence patch when pressed around ears.

4. Physiological & Psychological Hazards
• Sound Pressure (110–128 dB). OSHA limits would allow 1–2 min exposure unprotected. With dual-layer system you can remain indefinitely, but check plugs every few hours; vibration works them loose.
• Low Oxygen (barometric ≈ 55 kPa). Even fit travelers saturate at 80–83 % SpO₂. Carry 0.5 L rescue O₂ flask; use if saturation falls below 70 % or if headache, ataxia, or visual snow appears.
• Whole-body Resonance. Nodal “hot stones” on the basin floor amplify certain frequencies; standing there for more than 30 s can nauseate or trigger arrhythmia. Drop a metal mug; if it audibly sings, step away two paces.
• Vertigo & The Vast Sky. Many first-timers experience centrifugal illusion (sense that the basin is rotating). Ground yourself by lying prone with one palm on bare rock until the inner ear re-calibrates (≈ 90 s).
• Dehydration. The wind’s partial pressure deficit evaporates sweat instantly; people often forget to drink because they never feel damp.

────────────────────────────────────────
5. Interaction With Local Life & Culture
Lamellorn and whisper-hares are protected by nomad taboo; no hunting unless you join a sanctioned atlatl party. Choir-swift feathers found on the ground are fair game, but never disturb an active nest inside a Throat—besides ethics, the interior resonance can exceed 145 dB and rupture lungs within minutes.

When you meet highland bands:
• Lift both hands, palms forward (universal “peace / no weapon”).
• They will press a vibrating staff to your collarbone; let it rest until you feel three pulses—this is greeting.
• Trade goods valued: caffeine tablets, mirror-film patches, sewing needles. They will likely offer cured hare-meat or a singing flake in return.

────────────────────────────────────────
6. Field Conduct Guidelines
• Anchor everything. A single forgotten spoon inside your tent will rattle all night like a trapped wasp.
• Cook behind a buttress or inside the vestibule; wind shear can invert a lit stove.
• Seek “shadow pockets” (abrupt leeward hollows) for daily half-hour silence therapy—important for mental health. Leave the plugs out, close your eyes; heartbeats become audible here and restore psychological equilibrium.
• Leave no trace. Pack-out principle is strict: even organic waste persists because decomposition is glacial in this temperature band.

────────────────────────────────────────
7. Emergency & Evac Protocol
Primary: retrace route to ridge and radio dirigible base on line-of-sight channel 5-delta (doable with 2 W handset once you stand 10 m above basin rim).
Secondary: if incapacitated below rim, activate IR beacon; nomad bands monitor the spectrum and habitually assist, but expect reciprocal labor or trade once recovered.

Self-rescue window: weather is monotonous; any day is flyable for an airship provided you can reach clear air above ridge. Sleet at dusk is abrasive but not catastrophic for hull fabric.

────────────────────────────────────────
8. Recommended Duration & Activities
2-night minimum allows:
• Dawn harmonic drop-outs when choir swifts perform.
• Midday collection of resonant stones (sunlight shows mica fractures best).
• Dusk sleet-spark photography (flash-freeze needles glow against violet sky).

Longer than 5 nights and most visitors report “time-loop anxiety”—the sense that nothing in their life outside the Basin ever happened. Schedule departure in advance and stick to it.

10. Departure Mindset
Enter with respect for a place that was finished long before the first human universe cooled. The Basin will not try to kill you, but it will never notice you either; its single act is to breathe wind into sound, endlessly. Go prepared, stay alert, listen with your bones, and when you leave, carry silence with the same reverence mountaineers carry summit stones.
