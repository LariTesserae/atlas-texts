Field Instructions for the Saucer-Basin of Living Noise  
(working designations in the Gate Registry: “Roar-Valley,” “Chemofog Bowl,” or, less poetically, MT-KX-9/13B)

0. Snapshot  
• Climate: perpetually cool (4–7 °C), 100 % humidity, no wind, no precipitation stronger than drizzle.  
• Atmosphere: O₂ 18 %, N₂ 70 %, remainder mostly inert mineral vapors—technically breathable but laced with abrasive dust and opportunistic spores.  
• Light: never better than Earth twilight; primary “illumination” is sporadic bioluminescence.  
• Primary hazards: continuous 120–140 dB sound field, bio-adhesive mucus, omnipresent microfauna, orientation drift, predation when silence falls.  

1. Personal Protective Ensemble  
a. Outer layer  
   – Sealed, smooth-surfaced suit (glossy polyurethane or PTFE finish). Avoid cloth, Velcro, or anything textured; friction equals foothold for grazers.  
   – Integrated hood and face shield with hydrophobic coating. When the fog beads on the visor you can wipe it clear with a shake; touching anything transfers eggs.  
b. Boots  
   – Rock-climbing rubber fails here—too grippy and too porous. Bring flat, slightly cupped soles with micro-suction ribs (cephalopod-inspired) so you won’t skate on slime yet you present no nooks for hitchhikers.  
c. Respiratory / Auditory  
   – Combination over-ear electronic muffs and half-mask respirator (P100 + mineral dust pre-filter). The muffs should provide active noise cancellation up to 140 dB while piping in filtered environmental audio at a user-set floor so you still hear the important changes (namely: sudden silence or localized dips).  
d. Undersuit  
   – Wicking heated base layer. The air never freezes but you will lose heat by conduction when you crouch on wet stone.  
e. Hands  
   – Chemically inert gloves with removable nitrile over-gloves. Peel the outer pair whenever you handle gear you will later bring inside a clean shelter.  

2. Instruments & Navigation  
a. Visuals: Low-light LIDAR goggles or headset. The bioluminescent pulses are not reliable for pathfinding and will ruin dark adaptation anyway.  
b. Inertial tracker / dead-reckoning puck mounted at the small of the back. Magnetic compasses gyre uselessly; GPS obviously absent.  
c. 30 m of 3 mm Dyneema guideline on a belt spool. Every 100 m clip a coded reflector tag; collect on exit for Leave-No-Trace.  
d. Two-tone shaker rod (think blind-person’s cane with internal rattle). Touch ahead for sinkholes; the rod’s acoustic signature also warns small tripod hunters that you are not edible plankton.  

3. Movement Protocols  
• Keep moving at strolling speed; pause no longer than 60 s. After about 3 minutes of immobility, film grazers begin to test surfaces—including you.  
• Travel in pairs tethered 2 m apart. Orientation illusions can separate you in a step; the tether reasserts reality.  
• Should the valley noise dip below conversation level: freeze, kill lights, tamp down any metallic gear that might clang. Apex pass-overs last 10–40 s. The first creature to break silence becomes target; do not be that mammal.  

4. Daily Rhythm (the “Two-Hour Breath”)  
The basin exhales moisture on a two-hour cycle. Use it:  
– During the heavy mist phase (minute 0–45) visibility drops to <1 m; stay put in a nook or elevated perch flagged earlier.  
– When the drizzle slackens (minute 45–90) move; the fog thins, and most aerial predators are busy courting or feeding at water pans.  
– Minutes 90–120 are the valley’s “throat-clearing.” Bladder-beast booms propagate; that’s your audible cue to finish a traverse and re-shelter.  

5. Camp & Rest  
Only bivouac on imported platforms—collapsible carbon-fiber cots with splayed legs sheathed in smooth silicone. Deploy a micro-mesh tent (400 micron) inside a freestanding frame, then pressurize to slight positive pressure with filtered air from your rebreather scrubber. Any gap wider than a grain of rice will admit swarmers. Expect the tent to accumulate luminescent eggs on the outside like a starry sky; wipe with saline and bag the residue before packing.  

6. Food & Hydration  
All valley water tests between 3–5 % dissolved salts/metals and carries dormant larvae. Bring sealed rations and melt-pack water. Even a LifeStraw-type filter clogs in minutes; ionic purifier or nothing.  

7. Biological Containment  
Pre-entry: full body spray with neutral silicone barrier.  
Exit: 20-min decon in a portable mist shower of warm 3 % isopropanol + surfactant, then 60 °C drying fan. Inspect boot treads and suit seams under UV—the eggs fluoresce sky-blue. Anything suspect: incinerate. Swallow a broad-spectrum antihelmintic on return; you probably inhaled something curious despite the mask.  

8. Useful Behaviors & Signals  
• The local mesopredators interpret steady 100–110 Hz humming as “too large to bother.” Many travelers clip a pager motor to the waist; it hums constantly under the suit.  
• Flashing white LEDs panic everything—do not use. If you must signal a partner, wave a dim red chem-stick.  
• Looking for interesting specimens? Follow the salt-vein glimmer (slightly brighter in LIDAR false-color) but never harvest more than 200 g in any 100 m zone—the chemovore plates recycle territory quickly, and exposing raw rock can trigger feeding frenzies that ripple up the web.  

9. Emergency Scenarios  
• Slip or fall: Immediately right yourself. Remaining supine for even 20 s invites ribbonfish to leave the pans and investigate. They can cross wet stone faster than you can crawl.  
• Suit breach ≤1 cm: seal with pressure tape, apply isopropanol wipe, continue to exit. Anything larger: abort visit, activate locator beacon, and climb out fast; baseline microorganisms will colonize exposed skin in <8 min and begin itching like fiberglass.  
• Lost guideline, nav crash: Sit with back to basalt ridge, pound three slow, spaced thumps on the ground every 5 s. Gate-team drums carry >5 km and include an ultrasonic component the ecosystem ignores.  

10. Duration & Exit  
First-timers: limit stay to one basin “day” (three mist-cycles, ≈ six Earth hours). Veteran xenobiologists stretch to 24 h but rotate suits every 8 h to strangle hitchhikers. No one reputable bivouacs more than two consecutive nights—beyond that, odds of bringing an unwanted nursery through the Gate climb past containment capacity.  

11. Souvenirs  
You may lawfully export up to 50 g of inert shells or molted carapace per permit. Anything soft, wet or glowing requires Biosafety-4 quarantine and a signed chain of custody. The valley is literally alive with invasive potential; customs incinerates undeclared items without appeal.  

12. Quick-reference Rule-of-Thumbs  
1. If you can hear yourself breathe, danger is close.  
2. If it looks like a puddle, assume it contains something that jumps.  
3. Never offer the land a still surface; motion is your firewall.  
4. Exit cleaner than you entered—every egg killed here prevents an ecological disaster somewhere else.  

Pack smooth, walk steady, keep the roar at your back, and the Basin of Living Noise will remain a place you visited—not a place that comes home riding on your jacket.
