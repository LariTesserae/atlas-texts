Field Brief: “The Engineered Abyss”  
Facility designation: Pelagic Works 0-7 (“PW-7”)  
Location: undisclosed extra-terran chamber, depth envelope –0 m to –4 870 m (all depth figures are true water depth, not altitude)  
Purpose: bulk desalination, trace-metal harvesting, biotic seeding

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
1. Visas, papers & who actually runs the place  
• Administrative control belongs to the Tri-Jurisdiction Maritime Works Authority (T-MWA). They recognise most multiverse-standard passports but absolutely require an Engineering-Zone Liability Waiver (E-ZLW). Without it you will be turned back at the outer air-lock.  
• You must also carry a Class-C Faunal Impact Bond. Every gram of organic matter you import is logged; any un-logged organism found in the water column is assumed to be yours and the bond debited.  
• No hard currency circulates inside. Transactions are energy-denominated “kilowatt tokens” stored on a waterproof NFC tag you receive at arrival.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
2. What the environment will do to your body  
Pressure: The public decks sit at 3 bar—high but tolerable after a one-hour acclimation hold. Venture outside the decks and you are in the raw column: 30 bar at –300 m, 200 bar by –2 km. The water is a dense, cold compress; mistakes collapse ribs in seconds.  
Temperature: 4 °C almost everywhere; lamp-rings raise local pockets to 7 – 9 °C but also create steam vents >180 °C near condenser horns.  
Light cycle: 30 h “day” / 6 h “night.” Expect circadian disruption—pack melatonin, blue-light glasses, and a programmable watch.  
Noise: Sustained 110–140 dB. Without dampening earbuds you will lose hearing inside three days.  
Chemistry: Salt 2.8 % (lower than oceanic), trace manganese, negligible organics. Water is sterile above –40 m but alive (and sometimes biting) below.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
3. Training to book before you go  
• Two-stage pressure habituation certificate (P-Hab Level 1 covers 0–6 bar, Level 2 covers to 30 bar).  
• Closed-circuit rebreather proficiency; open-circuit scuba is illegal here—bubble streams damage the pumps.  
• Basic industrial acoustics: recognising horn-blast codes saves lives when the turbines spin up.  
• Orientation in 6-DOF space. Most visitors get vertigo the first time the “floor” disappears into a cloud of fish.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
4. Packing list (minimum)  
• Depth-rated hardsuit, 60-bar certification, magnetic boot soles and tether spool. Rentals exist but fit is life at these pressures.  
• Active noise-cancellation buds + secondary passive muffs for sleep.  
• Personal beacon (blue-green laser, 5 km range) keyed to the rescue grid at 493 kHz.  
• Thermal undersuit, chemical heat packs, anti-fungal wipes.  
• Collapsible Faraday cage pouch—filter-eels sense electric fields; stash comms gear when swimming among them.  
• Over-the-counter meds: dramamine (for standing-wave nausea), acetazolamide (altitude drugs help here too), broad-spectrum antibiotics (isopod bites).  
• Three independent timepieces. When the lamps dim you lose all natural sense of hour.  
Forbidden items: loose fabrics, balloons, anything that can jam an intake grille, free-exhaust scuba, firearms (pressure differential makes bullets tumble; you’ll just punch holes in infrastructure).

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
5. Moving around  
• Vertical core elevators are pressurised trains—check that the car’s displayed depth matches your suit’s set-point before doors open.  
• Cargo blimps are delightful but strictly observers-only; even crew wear parachute-ballast hybrids because a hydrogen cell burn will drop a blimp like a rock.  
• Swim transits: consult the Pump Surge Bulletin posted every 12 h. A red-band surge can slam you against the lattice at 4 g lateral acceleration.  
• Never approach anything that hums evenly—those are the intake throats. All safe machinery here stutters or pulses.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
6. Wildlife etiquette  
Legally, first-generation organisms “belong” to T-MWA. Injure one and you pay salvage plus biologist fees.  
Second-generation creatures are unowned but protected by the Baseline Ecological Safeguard. Collect only photographs unless you have a science tag.  
Situational tips:  
• Filter-eels – gorgeous static arcs, but the field can wipe a drive. Keep data crystal ≥15 cm from skin.  
• Carnifex fish – attracted to white light; use red for night dives. They ram first, bite second.  
• Baleen skimmers – curious, lumpy. Let them bump you; push back and they may pin you to mesh in play.  
• Cleaning isopods – harmless until they smell albumin. A cut finger is a dinner bell—seal breaches immediately.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
7. Sleeping, eating & not going mad  
Accommodation: cylindrical “bunk pods” bolted to central spars at –45 m, double gasketed. Bring earplugs; the lattice rings like a cathedral bell when the big pumps cycle.  
Food: almost all vat-protein and kelp derivatives. If you crave terrestrial crunch, pack dried snacks; nothing onboard is crispy.  
Recreation:  
• Ride a scheduled standing wave—grab a foil board, let the surge carry you 12 km across the basin.  
• Nightfall biolum tour—lamps off, the moss and eel flashes paint constellations in 3-D.  
Mental hygiene: take one day off every ten; ascend to 1 bar rest decks that pipe in dry air, birdsong and fake sun. People who skip this go “pressure punch-drunk” by week two.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
8. Emergencies & exit strategy  
Code tritone descending = catastrophic surge. Find the nearest red-band refuge sphere; they are rated to 400 bar and stocked for 48 h.  
Code single 12-second horn blast = condenser vent; move 100 m laterally or get steam-cooked.  
Medical: hyperbaric clinic modules at –100 m, –600 m, and –2 400 m. Decompression sickness is rare (you’re already compressed) but embolisms from rapid elevator ascents kill annually.  
Evacuation: If primary locks fail, you’ll be routed up the ballast shafts in free-climb sleds. Keep your wits—the sleds flood on purpose to equalise for door release at surface pressure.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
9. Five things travellers rave about  
1. Hanging out in zero-reference midwater, nowhere near floor or ceiling, the biggest “room” you will ever inhabit.  
2. Watching construction drones arc-weld new trusses: a fireworks show in a midnight-blue void.  
3. The thunderclap of a condenser vent—the loudest single human-made sound under water.  
4. Feeding lamp waste-heat to your suit’s radiator, feeling a private warm current wrap around you in 4 °C water.  
5. Counting the lamp-rings fade to pinpoints during the engineered “night”—then realising the “stars” are actually juvenile skates circling for plankton.

––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––  
10. Final mindset checklist  
✓ Accept that up/down will lose meaning—practice thinking radially instead.  
✓ Build your schedule around machinery, not sunrise.  
✓ Treat every bit of metal as a moving part until proven otherwise.  
✓ Remember: this is a newborn sea. Everything—including the rules—is still evolving.

Pack tight, sign the waiver, and keep an ear tuned for the next pump surge. The place is raw, roaring, astonishing—and, with the right preparation, perfectly survivable for an ordinary human who remembers that in PW-7, the machines, not you, set the rhythm.
