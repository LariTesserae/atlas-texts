1. OVERVIEW
Altitude: ±2 100 m.  
Ambient temp: –5 °C to +6 °C in sun; –25 °C during a breath-storm.  
Humidity: 80 %+ but rarely liquid; constant phase-change.  
Solar load: UV index 12–14 (higher than Earth’s Himalaya).  
Sound-triggered micro-meteorology: airborne noise ≥70 dB in the basin is statistically linked to “exhalations” (90 s snow squalls, wind-shear ±60 km h⁻¹, temperature drop ≥20 °C).

Think of the place as a self-contained hydrological engine running on silence and reflected light. Everything you do—every footfall, exhalation, word—feeds or starves that engine. Preparation is therefore equal parts mountaineering, speleology, polar travel … and etiquette.

───────────────────────────────────────────
2. GETTING THERE WITHOUT PROVOKING A STORM

Approach vector  
• Final kilometre: on foot, skis or snow-pads only. No combustion, no rotors, no drones; the acoustic shock front of even a small UAV almost guarantees a breath-storm directly under the flight path.  
• Preferred route: the south-east talus ramp, a 15° incline of frost-shattered shale that absorbs impact noise. Crampons optional; padded snow-pads are quieter.  
• Party size: ≤4. Larger groups raise background chatter and footfall harmonics.  
• Voice discipline: switch to hand signals 500 m out. Perform radio comms on bone-conduction headsets kept under two layers of cloth.

───────────────────────────────────────────
3. PHYSIOLOGY & MEDICAL PREP

• Altitude conditioning: Stage at 1 200–1 500 m for 36 h minimum.  
• Hyper-reflection eye risk: Solar keratitis develops in 20 min unprotected. Carry Category-4 wraparound glacier glasses with side baffles; spare pair mandatory.  
• Cold flash injuries: Breath-storms flash-freeze exposed sweat into ice crystals that lacerate skin. Use vapour-barrier liners and carry a 30 ml dropper of dimethyl sulfoxide–heparin mix for micro-frostnip.  
• Hydration paradox: Dehydration onset is masked; you are surrounded by water you can’t drink unless melted. Carry a 500 ml double-wall flask and a palm-sized chemical phase-plate heater (silent, flameless).  
• Vaccinations: none required, but carry a broad-spectrum antibiotic; the crustacean grazers host a psychrophilic gut flora harmless to them, opportunistic in humans.

4. CLOTHING & PERSONAL KIT CHECKLIST

Sound is the enemy—choose fabrics and hardware that neither rustle nor clink.

Outer:  
• Soft-shell eVent anorak, matte light-grey (glare control) with laser-cut seams (no stitching noise).  
• Articulated knees & elbows lined with aerogel panels; they insulate without crunching.

Mid:  
• Two layers of silent stretch fleece. No Velcro; use magnetic tabs.

Base:  
• Vapour-barrier silk or synthetic to keep perspiration from freezing.

Hands & feet:  
• Double mitt system, inner conductive liner for operating touch screens.  
• Insulated felt boots with detachable low-profile gecko-spikes; spikes are titanium powder-sintered to avoid the metallic ping of steel.

Head & face:  
• Balaclava with detachable voice-baffle panel that drops spoken volume by 20 dB.  
• Category-4 goggles as above.

Tools & miscellany:  
☑ 50 m braided climber’s cord; Dyneema core, fabric sheath (won’t ping).  
☑ Folding snow-pad stool (rests without sitting on wet slush).  
☑ Light-slate writing tablet + wax stylus for silent notes.  
☑ 2 W IR beacon for after-dark navigation—harmless acoustically, invisible to local fauna.  
☑ Carbon-mesh trash bag; pack out everything, including urine tubes if you stay <48 h (see Taboo 2).

5. MOVING, EATING, SLEEPING: DAILY CONDUCT

Walking  
• Land each step flat; no heel-toe slap.  
• Pause every 30 m to listen; sudden radio-quiet from birds often predicts a breath-storm ten seconds before the wind.

Water  
• The stone weeps continuously. Hold a titanium straw against a droplet bead; your body heat in the straw liquefies a trickle—silent and fire-free.

Food  
• Bring calorie-dense bars pre-cut into bite-sized wedges; tearing packaging in situ is louder than you think.  
• The crustacean grazers are theoretically edible but taboo (see Section 7).

Sleep  
• Visitors normally rent an unused pillar-hut (1 shell per night, paid in trade goods).  
• If bivouacking, nest between two rock teeth; pitch a single-skin tent whose fabric tension is low enough not to drum in wind.

6. MEETING THE SEAL-CLAD PEOPLE

First contact  
• They reveal themselves only after you have spent at least one quiet cycle (≈4 h) without triggering a storm. Patience is demonstration of worth.  
• Greeting: raise both palms shoulder-high, exhale audibly once, then fall silent. They respond with a single palm-clap (barely audible) if they accept dialogue.

Communication  
• They speak in hush-tone clicks through hollow-bone tubes which filter sound beneath the storm-threshold. Unless you’ve studied it, use gestures; they are adept at reading outsiders.

Cultural norms  
• Volume is morality: louder equals ruder.  
• Trade is concluded by touching items simultaneously and then stepping back one full pace.  
• Refusing food or drink is acceptable; loudly appreciating it is not. Nod.

7. HAZARDS, TABOOS AND EMERGENCY DRILLS

Taboo 1: Loud Impact  
Drop nothing heavier than a glove. If you fumble metal, let it fall onto a coat rather than rock.

Taboo 2: Free-running liquid  
Urine, dishwater, blood—anything warm and liquid invites predatory icing that can fracture stone faces locals depend on. Use sealable containers; dispose outside the basin.

Taboo 3: Predation  
Do not eat crustacean grazers or harvest birds’ droplets. Both species are considered infrastructural, not wildlife.

Emergency Drill: Breath-storm  
1. Freeze where you stand—running generates noise.  
2. Pull hood strings: fabric over face cuts dermal flash-freeze by 80 %.  
3. Turn side-on to wind; keep one hand on a rock tooth (orientation).  
4. After the wind stops, wait five slow breaths: second wind-shift sometimes follows.

Medical evac  
• Severe injury: activate IR beacon in pulse mode. Locals interpret it as a request, not a command. They own silent sleds on polished bone runners and will assist … for a price (usually dry cellulose cloth, impossible for them to source locally).

───────────────────────────────────────────
8. WHAT TO BRING FOR TRADE, WHAT TO LEAVE BEHIND

Valuable imports  
• Compressed carbon felt (fuel & insulation)  
• Non-crinkling plastic film (window patches)  
• Quiet chemical hand-warmers  
• Dyestuff in dry cakes (purples and greens are prized; they can’t produce saturated colours)

Absolutely do not bring  
• Live plants or seeds (ecosystem is closed)  
• Anything that ticks, chirps, rings or plays music  
• Combustion stoves (smoke is both loud and sacrilegious)

───────────────────────────────────────────
9. STAYING LONGER THAN A WEEK

Adaptation schedule  
Day 1–2: Acclimate lungs; expect mild hypoxia, glare nausea.  
Day 3–4: Learn the pulse—six to eight micro-storms per 24 h; keep a tally stone.  
Day 5+: You are expected to contribute. Typical visitor tasks: icicle harvesting, lichen bed grooming, assisting at fermentation pits (warmest work in the settlement).

Scientific etiquette  
If you intend to study the basin, share your raw data. The locals view knowledge as mutual insulation: shared, it protects; hoarded, it fractures.

───────────────────────────────────────────
10. FAST-EXIT PROTOCOL

If you must leave in a hurry (e.g., medical crisis, equipment breach):

1. Activate beacon on continuous mode.  
2. Pack in silence; accept you will leave traces—better a noisy departure than a corpse.  
3. Descend the talus ramp; keep at least one member of the party breathing into a spare hood to muffle involuntary cries.  
4. Do not look back if a storm forms. Visual focus on it seems to delay clearing.

Remember: The basin is not a place you conquer; it is a throat you pass through quietly. Pack soft, move soft, speak soft, and the sky will keep its breath.
