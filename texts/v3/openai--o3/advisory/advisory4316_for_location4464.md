Field Manual: First Human Visits to the High Plateau Ocean
Compiled for multiverse travellers of ordinary physique, average modern-Earth technology, and limited on-site logistical support.  Version 1.3 (circulated provisionally—update after the first three expeditions report back).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
1. What You Are Walking Into
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Altitude Likely 3 000–4 500 m a.s.l. (the rim of the young plateau).  
Climate Tropical sun, permanent 24–30 °C water surface, air 16–22 °C, daily one-hour warm deluge.  
Terrain 99 % water cover; emergent rocks are blade-sharp volcanic glass.  No beaches, no soil.  
Atmosphere Mist-saturated, half-pressure compared with sea level on Earth.  UV is fierce; oxygen is ~80 % of sea-level Earth.  
Biota Plant biomass already high; animal biomass still low and shy.  Microbial spectrum unknown but probably explosive given warmth, organics, and novelty.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Personal Physiology Checklist
--------------------------------------------------------------------
•  Acclimatise to high elevation before you arrive (two days at 2 500 m minimum).  
•  If you have cardiopulmonary issues, bring a lightweight POC (portable oxygen concentrator) or bottled O₂ for the first 48 h.  
•  UV index can hit 15+.  Dark-tint glacier goggles, high-coverage clothing, SPF 50 mineral sunscreen.  
•  Heat load comes from below at night (plants radiate IR) and above by day: pack a loose thermal bivy sheet that reflects IR both ways.  
•  Skin & soft-tissue protection is crucial—every emergent rock edge will cut exposed flesh and neoprene alike.  Lacquered kevlar gaiters from knee down, cut-resistant water shoes or split-toe boots.  
•  Warm freshwater breeds the full catalogue of opportunistic microbes.  Assume every minor cut will infect.  Daily chlorhexidine wash, broad-spectrum topical antibiotic, and a seven-day course of doxycycline or azithromycin in reserve.  Bring antifungals (clotrimazole cream + oral itraconazole).  
•  Vaccinate as if for tropical freshwater work: hepatitis A & B, typhoid, tetanus, polio booster, leptospirosis.

3. Core Kit (per person, five-day sortie)
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Wear/Carry
1.  Amphibious pressure-vent suit: breathable torso, sealed lower legs, buoyant foam panels; integrated knee-pads.  
2.  Razor-resistant gloves (dyneema knit under nitrile outer).  
3.  Full-wrap sunglasses or glacier goggles with anti-fog.  
4.  Wide-brim hat that sheds mist.  
5.  Ultralight climbing helmet (low overhead branches of algal trees calcify quickly).  
6.  25 L drypack with roll-seal.  
7.  Compact rebreather or scuba pony bottle—depths are lower-pressure than normal oceans, but you can still black out at 30 m.  
8.  Lightweight folding grapnel + 30 m dyneema line for hooking submerged ridges or mat-root tangles.  
9.  Three 2-L collapsible water bladders + UV-C purifier wand (water is clear, turbidity low—UV works).  
10.  Eight freeze-dried meals/day: do not forage; every local species is untested for human biochemistry.  
11.  1.5 × body-weight-rated personal flotation harness with quick-release crotch strap (for sleeping half-submerged).  
12.  Iridium/NEN long-range sat-comm puck in waterproof float case—line-of-sight radio dies in the mist.  
13.  Photonic compass (magnetic field is patchy over recent volcanics).  
14.  100 m spool of 2 mm dyneema trace line + reflective tags: the vegetation labyrinth disorients quickly; pay out line whenever you leave direct ridge sight.  
15.  Ten 10 cm titanium pitons & hammer—only rock soft enough to take them.  
16.  Heat-mirrored tarp (2 × 3 m) + inflatable ring pad (allows sleep on water surface during the nightly IR pulses).  
17.  First-aid: wound super-glue, steri-strips, broad-spectrum antibiotics (see §2), epinephrine injector (airborne spores unknown allergy profile), electrolytes.

Group Items (2–4 people)
•  Inflatable skin-on-frame pack raft (2 kg) with dual bladders.  
•  Compact multibeam sonar tablet – maps trenches and sub-ridges in real time; GPS will not exist.  
•  One lightweight carbon fibre “snowshoe” set each for walking on buoyant vine mats (footprint spreads to 600 cm²).  
•  20 W fold-out solar blanket + power bank (sun intensity compensates for mist).  
•  DNA sample vials & liquid-nitrogen micro-dewar (if you have permit to take specimens; see ethics).  
•  3 m telescoping pole with dyed wool tuft at tip—animals appear to give wide berth to anything that looks like a floating frond cluster; good for gentle herding.

4. Moving Through the Environment
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Cliff Rim Entry The safest portal drop is directly onto an emergent ridge.  Locate a rock saddle broad enough for a tent-footprint and no obvious obsidian flakes.  Anchor a fixed line immediately; rain polishes surfaces to mirror slickness.

Surface Travel Most distance is paddled.  Keep barefoot pressure off algal forest crowns—puncture wounds kill the mats; they also spring razor-sharp silica needles when stressed (microscopes show evolving sclerites).  Use raft or vine-shoe technique.

Capillary Arches They look tempting—shimmering water bridges that defy gravity—but the pressure inversion that maintains them sometimes flips when loaded.  Rule: never weight an arch, never tie lines to it, stay two body lengths away.  Instruments show a micro-magnetic pulse at collapse; possible neurological effects (one scout reported brief vertigo).

Daily Deluge Two warnings herald the one-hour storm: the mist intensifies until you can taste metal on your lips, and the ambient hum of root-valves stops.  Immediately secure loose gear in the raft, clip yourself by tether to a trunk mass, and get goggles on—visibility drops to <2 m.  Post-storm silence is so deep you may become disoriented; restart your breadcrumb line.

Night Pulse Infrared scans show the mats cycle +4 °C in 8-minute pulses from dusk to roughly 02:00 local.  Sleeping against bare trunks will dehydrate you and leach electrolytes; use your ring pad to float half-submerged so convective water mixes the warmth away.

Diving the Color Zones Light penetrates 70+ m.  The jade zone (~15-35 m) hosts the densest life; be careful not to wedge hoses in basalt corners.  Below 40 m hydrostatic pressure is low enough you can get mild altitude DCS on ascent—follow 2-minute safety stop at 6 m even if on snorkel.

5. Hazards (Ranked)
--------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Lacerations + water infection.  
2. Altitude / hypoxia + bright-light retinal burn.  
3. Disorientation inside plant labyrinths (no landmarks once inside).  
4. Sudden mat collapse if too many vines senesce after sunset—floatation harness must keep airway clear.  
5. Unstudied toxins—sudden plankton blooms can release aerosolised compounds: if afternoon rain smells strongly of cucumber, don respirator (listed in optional kit).  
6. Rockfall: new escarpments shed blocks unpredictably; listen for the dry crack.  Sound carries far but travel is slow—give cliffs a 50 m stand-off.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
6. Ethics & Impact Protocol
--------------------------------------------------------------------
•  The ecosystem is pre-animal-boom; even small disturbances can bias future evolution.  
•  No disposable plastics, no cut vegetation unless life-critical.  Use existing natural gaps.  
•  All greywater is to be UV-treated then poured into moving surface current—not left to stagnate among trunks.  
•  Zero vertebrate sampling until a planetary ethics board declares the site beyond the “genesis vulnerability” threshold.  
•  Biosecurity going out: every traveler must do a full-body bleach rinse and 60 °C dry cycle before stepping through return portal—spores load clothing fibres.

7. Suggested Itinerary (Five-Day Recon Loop)
Day 0 Arrival on ridge, line anchor, acclimatisation float.  
Day 1 Raft southwest 6 km to first dome-summit “Hot-Knuckle.”  Sleep on dome pools.  
Day 2 Snorkel drift through jade zone trench “Swallowswell.”  Collect water chem samples.  
Day 3 Traverse vine lattice eastward to cloud-sheet valley; observe night IR mosaic from drone-kite 60 m up.  
Day 4 Climb basalt spur “Mirror-Knife,” deploy solar blanket, radar map nearby capillary arches.  
Day 5 Return to portal ridge, de-kit, and portal-out before local noon storm.

8. If Something Goes Wrong
•  Major Bleed Apply quick-clot gauze, spray chlorhexidine, close with skin glue, wrap in elastomer tape; evac within 18 h.  
•  Lost in Mist Stay put.  Blow triple whistle every 30 s.  The plateau is acoustically flat—team can triangulate from 2 km.  
•  Mat Gives Way Under Camp Harness keeps you afloat; deploy raft floor-panel as emergency platform.  Swim calmly—panic kicks will tangle in root-ribbons.  
•  Altitude Sickness Descend by submerging (water pressure ≈ +0.1 atm/ m).  At 10 m depth symptoms often subside; breathe enriched air from pony bottle, then evacuate downslope edge of plateau.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
9. Souvenir Warning
The place will tempt you with luminous fronds, glass-blue pebbles, and living lace-ferns that close on your skin.  Do not take them.  Most will die the moment they are removed from the odd pressure/temperature mix that made them, and a few may not die at all but adapt frighteningly fast.  Bring back images, water samples sealed in quartz ampoules, and the memory of standing among clouds while your knees were still in the sea.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
10. Final Thought
You are not exploring ruins—you are stepping into the first moments of a world still hatching.  Move lightly, listen often, and remember that the warm silence is not emptiness but possibility waiting to decide what it will become.
