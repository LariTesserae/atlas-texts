Guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler  
Destination: “The Half-Assembled Highlands” – a 3–4-km-high, basaltic roof-scape that is cold, wet, thin-aired and, for now, almost entirely the planet’s private workshop.

0. Pre-trip snapshot
• Altitude: 3000–4200 m (≈10 000–14 000 ft).  
• Temperature band: –6 °C dawn, +4 °C afternoon, with >90 % humidity from mist and drizzle.  
• Solar availability: 30–40 % of normal alpine gain; diffuse rather than direct light.  
• Access window: Any season works; there is no true summer. “Warm” months (local late spring–early autumn) offer fewer icing events on rock but more insects.  
• Rescue infrastructure: None. Consider this a Category 3 wilderness (self-rescue expected).

--------------------------------------------------
1. Personal prerequisites
• Cardiovascular fitness: You will be operating at oxygen partial pressures equal to ≈65 % of sea-level. A six-hour sea-level hike carrying 15 kg without stops is a good minimum benchmark.  
• Prior skills: basic crampon use, self-arrest with an ice axe, belay competence, map/compass navigation and reading changing snow conditions. If you lack these, hire/bring a partner who has them.  
• Pre-acclimatisation: If possible, spend a night at 2500 m in your own universe before jumping; otherwise schedule a full acclimatisation day on arrival with only light activity.

--------------------------------------------------
2. Clothing & personal kit (layer by layer)
HEAD
• Thin fleece beanie + waterproof/breathable hooded shell.  
• Glacier-rated sunglasses: UV is fierce even through cloud.  
• Fine mesh head-net (midges).

TORSO
• Base: merino or high-wicking synthetic crew.  
• Mid: 100–150 g/m² grid-fleece.  
• Active insulation: hooded synthetic “puffy” (down can wet out).  
• Shell: 3-layer alpine hardshell with pit zips; permanently damp conditions demand real breathability.  
• Optional: vapor-barrier liner if you run cold.

LEGS
• Soft-shell climbing pants + lightweight long johns.  
• Waterproof over-trousers with full-length side zips.

HANDS
• Thin liner gloves, insulated waterproof climbing gloves, plus an emergency mitten shell.

FEET
• Double-sock system (liner + mid-weight).  
• Stiff, waterproof mountaineering boots (B2 at least); crampon-compatible.  
• Short over-gaiters keep rock shards and mud out.

PERSONAL EXTRAS
• Helmet: mandatory—freeze–thaw rockfall is frequent.  
• Insect repellent containing picaridin; works in near-freezing mud-midge swarms.  
• SPF 50 mineral sunscreen: yes, you can burn in pewter light.

--------------------------------------------------
3. Technical equipment
Navigation
• Topographic map printed on waterproof paper.  
• Magnetic compass plus sighting mirror. Basalt often contains magnetite; expect local deflection—confirm bearings from two points.  
• GPS unit with offline tiles; lithium AA/AAA cells tolerate cold best.  
• Altimeter watch: barometric swings (<10 mb) can signal incoming mist thickenings two hours out.

Travel & safety
• 30 m, 8.5 mm alpine rope (for exposed ridges, crevasse-like fissures).  
• Set of five nuts, three tricams and four 60 cm slings: rock still breaks clean, providing bomber placements.  
• Two lightweight ice axes and a pair of aluminium crampons.  
• Trekking poles with winter baskets.  
• Avalanche probe—not for snow, but ideal for testing permafrost-veneered marsh before committing weight.  
• Compact repair kit: tenacious tape, cord, spare pole section, universal screwdriver bit, shoelaces.  
• 700-lumen headlamp + red filter; nights are dim, not dark, but you will still need real light for rope work.

Shelter & sleep
• 4-season geodesic tent (self-supporting; ground does not hold stakes well).  
• Deadman anchors cut from foam sleeping-mat scraps.  
• -15 °C (5 °F) comfort-rated synthetic sleeping bag + 5 cm closed-cell pad + inflatable insulated pad (*.Permafrost leeches heat fast.*)

--------------------------------------------------
4. Food, water & power logistics
FOOD
• Expect 3500–4000 kcal/day: cold and altitude both raise metabolic load.  
• Choose calorie-dense, low-crumble items: nut butters in sachets, hard cheese blocks dipped in wax, jerky, vacuum-dried meals you can rehydrate in-pouch (keeps smell down at night).  

WATER
• Source: dripping rock, pothole pools, marsh seep.  
• Hazards: Amphibian eggs, beetle detritus, high mineral load (basalt = magnesium + iron).  
• Protocol: Pre-filter through cloth, then use a hollow-fibre squeeze filter. Finish with chemical tabs or UV pen (cold lowers kill rate; double contact time).  
• Capacity: 3 L/person daytime plus 1 L for cooking. Carry *flexible* bottles; hard bottles fracture when dropped on talus.

FUEL / POWER
• White gas or isobutane-propane blends perform; bring 50 % extra—melting water and drying socks every night consumes fuel.  
• Solar: not dependable; panel yields ≈25 % of rating.  
• Redundant power bank inside inner layer; chemical hand warmers stored alongside electronics to keep lithium cells above 0 °C.

--------------------------------------------------
5. Travel technique & route-finding
• Time your moves: Start after first melt (~09:30 local) when rock is damp but not iced, finish before re-freeze (~17:00).  
• Stick to convex terrain (ridges) to avoid surprise marsh pockets and baby gullies.  
• Every hour, take a 360° photo from a fixed knee-height to help reverse navigation if mist closes—in fog, ridges anonymise.  
• Talus tests: tap suspected “plates” with pole; if it sounds hollow or sloshes, step elsewhere.  
• When forced into marsh saddles, treat them like glacier travel: rope-up with 6–8 m spacing; each partner probes 45 cm ahead.  
• Monitor soundscape: silence followed by a single roll of gravel means ibex above; a *second* roll 10 s later may be leopard stalking ibex—alter course.

--------------------------------------------------
6. Camp craft
SITE CHOICE
• Small rises between marsh and ridge give drainage but buffer from wind channels.  
• Stay >50 m from any persistent water; entire pools have been known to vanish overnight, leaving a 1 m subsidence void.  
• Look up: avoid overhanging fractured basalt flakes; freeze-thaw spalls can drop fist-size chunks.

RIGGING
• Use deadman anchors: bury a stuff-sack full of rocks sideways under talus; load after 5 min set-time.  
• Fly tension changes dramatically when mist becomes drizzle—re-tighten at dusk to prevent wind flap.

HEAT & LIGHT
• No wood. Use stove inside vestibule with carbon-monoxide sensor card.  
• Hang a red headlamp outside tent; insects swarm the red far less, and it becomes a beacon if fog thickens mid-night run for the latrine.

FOOD STORAGE
• Odour-proof bag inside a roll-top aluminium “leopard can.” Place 30 m downwind and wedge it in talus—not suspended; no trees. Spray its exterior with unscented alcohol wipe to remove hand oils.

--------------------------------------------------
7. Health & hazard management
ALTITUDE
• Schedule: climb high, sleep low the first two days.  
• Drugs: acetazolamide 125 mg twice daily starting 24 h pre-entry. Dexamethasone 4 mg tabs as emergency for HACE.  
• Self-checks: each evening fill out a Lake Louise questionnaire; descending even 300 m solves 90 % of AMS early.

COLD & WET
• Vapour barrier socks at night stop trench foot phenomena common in 2–4 °C mud walk.  
• Keep one totally dry baselayer sealed in a compression bag for emergencies only.

INJURY
• Most common: lacerations from razor-grit basalt; wrap immediately—infection risk high in constantly wet fabric.  
• Carry haemostatic gauze and skin-adhesive strips; glove when treating to avoid amphibian-borne chytrid fungus entering cuts.

DISEASE
• Midges are disease-free but can carry fungal spores that irritate eyes; keep wrap-around sunglasses on even in gloom.  
• Boil or chemically treat all water—frog marsh carries Giardia analogues.

--------------------------------------------------
8. Wildlife etiquette
Ravens  
• Highly intelligent, tool-curious. Bright objects (spoon, zipper pulls) vanish. Keep anything shiny zipped away.

Ibex  
• Non-aggressive but rock-triggering: when they move, showers of shards follow. Give slopes beneath herds a 30 m lateral berth.

Amphibians  
• Marsh edges resemble ankle-deep gravel pudding full of eggs. Walk rock-to-rock; do not trench across.

Snow leopards  
• Dusk/dawn hunters. Wear headlamp in “bright” mode at camp perimeters. If approached: stand tall, shout, throw fist-size rocks *past* not *at* the cat—noise > impact. Leopard spray (bear-strength capsaicin) works in the single documented close approach.

Insects  
• Midges detect CO₂ plumes instantly. A simple buff over the nose reduces exhaled warmth signatures—and doubles as frost mask.

--------------------------------------------------
9. Communication & emergency plans
• Satellite messenger (Iridium, Inmarsat) with tracking ping every 30 min; set group back-off time: if no movement for 4 h during day, remote contact initiates rescue protocol.  
• Code your location in two ways: GPS and bearing/distance from distinct topographic shape (e.g., “Tri-Spire Pass, 0.8 km NE of 3-tooth ridge”). Basalt spires are visible on low-res sat imagery even under cloud edges.  
• Evacuation: the quickest descent is generally *west*, where ridges stair-step down toward older, more eroded foothills.

--------------------------------------------------
10. Scientific / aesthetic side quests
Geology  
• Bring a 10× loupe; basalt still displays hexagonal jointing. Collect *photos*, not samples—hammer blows ruin the “factory-fresh” look the land wears only for a geological minute.

Bioacoustics  
• A pocket recorder captures frog “steam-valve” calls; upload spectrograms later—you may be logging an undiscovered genus.

Low-light photography  
• Tripod, f/2.8 lens, polariser useless; instead use a 3-stop soft-edge ND grad upside-down to emphasise mist glow.

--------------------------------------------------
11. Leave-no-trace checklist
1. Pack-in, pack-out: every crumb; predators key on new food scents.  
2. Grey water: strain, broadcast 70 m from water bodies.  
3. Human waste: WAG-bag it; soil depth <2 cm—nothing decomposes.  
4. No rock graffiti, no cairns—the skyline is already crowded with natural sculpture.  
5. When you leave camp, scatter anchoring stones so no one unfamiliar walks into hidden holes you dug.

12. 48-hour “pocket” itinerary (for a first look)

Day 0 (Arrival & acclimatisation)
• Afternoon: crest the entry pass, descend 150 m to the first saddle. Pitch tent by 16:00.  
• Evening: slow circuit of 500 m to map frog pools, breathe, drink hot liquid, sleep early.

Day 1
• 07:30 – rise, check frost on talus for overnight rockfall clues.  
• 09:30 – depart for Knife-Lathe Ridge; rope optional.  
• 11:30 – summit minor needle; snack; photograph tri-valley layout.  
• 14:00 – drop to marsh flats; collect water; filter on the spot (less weight). Note ibex paths—these often mark safest talus angles.  
• 16:30 – return along alternate ridge to camp; reinforce anchors.  
• 18:00 – cook inside vestibule; record frog chorus.  
• 21:00 – lights red, quiet watch for leopard silhouettes across mist glow.

Day 2
• 06:30 – break camp; depart before drizzle saturates rock.  
• 10:00 – reach pass lip; one last wide-angle shot of the planet’s “early shift” in progress.  
• 11:00 – multiverse gate activation / exit.

--------------------------------------------------
Closing thought
Everything here is provisional—the peaks, the runnels, even the resident fauna learning to exploit a barely-formed land. Travel with the same humility you’d show wandering through a factory before the machines are bolted down. Step lightly, listen often, and leave the newborn mountains no older for your passing.
