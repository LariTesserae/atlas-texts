7. Tide-Moss (Bryopelagia dominans)  
 Looks placid—never is.  Each mound inhales steam, exhales refrigerated breath.  That chill drops trace salts as frost, feeding only its own core.  Step on one and the surface liquefies; you sink ankle-deep and come up smelling of iron and pepper.

8. Lantern Caddis (Ceratocaula lucerna)  
 Larvae mine tunnels through the moss, lining them with mica chips stolen from clay.  Adults emerge wingless; they wear the glittering cases as armor and reflect plate-fungus pulses, turning themselves into moving beacons.  Heat-spiders ignore anything that bright.

9. Amber Creek Shrimp (Palaeogammarus fulvus)  
 Blind, yes, but their carapace holds piezo crystals that flash pale pink when struck by drip impact.  Whole shoals strobe beneath the water – silent thunder that keeps roof-mites from raining down to parasitize them.

10. Shard-Eel (Gliscescutum cavatica)  
 Ribbon of muscle, plates of glassy chert all along the spine.  It chews nothing: instead it rasps micro-quartz from sandstone, swallows it, and lets resident bacteria crack silica into usable sugars.  When startled it thrashes, spraying needle chips; bats have learned to roost three handspans higher since the last survey.

11. Root-Threader Lizard (Caecivarus subtilis)  
 Clay diver, already logged by surface folk.  Note: courtship confirmed—paired spirals traced in mud like twin calligraphy.  Follow the fresher spiral and you will find egg pearls the size of lentils glued beneath a vine scar.

12. Molt-Stork Fungus (Volucria exuvia)  
 Looks like a limp feather duster until a bat drops a skin-flake.  Then the crown snaps shut with a sound like tearing silk, digests, reopens.  Rows of the things make a carnivorous carpet; the scent is cinnamon and wet pennies.

13. Lurk-Wasp (Gen. & sp. as prior) – addenda

14. Siphon-Drake (Caligorhynchus minor)  
 Amphibian, two mouths.  Upper jaws clamp moss ledges, lower jaw: flexible tube that sips shrimp straight from the current.  Skin is cooled by evaporating mucus; touches like thawing ice.

15. Heat-Spider (Ferrarachne gravis)  
 Scarce sighted, always felt.  I left a slate microphone on Shelf-Five: recorded silence 83 seconds long, then one half-step of spider, then nothing.  Pressure wake measurable: 0.7 Pa.  We are living in the blind spot of a copper god.

16. Ember Slug (Fuligorbus intimus)  
 Lives in the cracks that glow.  Hemolymph contains antifreeze proteins; body water superheats without boiling.  At death it bursts, glazing the fissure walls in vitreous red.  Once cooled the glaze hosts antheridia of plate-fungus—life laid out upon death, tidy as always here.

17. Thrum-Root (Rhizotona vibrans)  
 Not plant, not animal—rope of cellulose threaded with living muscle.  It senses seismic micro-pulses, stiffens, and tugs itself deeper, widening the crack, letting more heat out for everyone else.  The grotto breathes because the root pulls.
