THE DWELLERS OF THE ROOF-SPREAD

1. Names, or the closest thing to them  
Outsiders call them Quietlings, Gap-Folk, Plateau Wives, or—most often—nobody calls them anything at all, because the act of naming requires volume.  Among themselves they exchange patterns of breath and bone-knuckle taps; rendered into text these signals resemble pauses more than words. 2. Lineage and body  
• They look roughly human at first approach: two legs, two arms, a head carried forward like a lantern in fog.  The illusion lasts until the fog thins.  
• Skin: not skin but a three-layer rind of interwoven moss filaments girdled by chitinous veins.  The outer layer desiccates each afternoon, flakes away as greenish dandruff, and grows back by dawn—an adaptation to air so thin that ordinary dermis would crack open.  
• Bones: bone indeed, yet harvested.  Every individual begins life bone-empty, a supple column supported by hydrostatic pressure alone.  Over decades they inlay their own frames with scavenged skeletal fragments—mostly the fossilized remnants of predecessors pried from bedrock outcrops.  You can read a life story in the seams of a Quietling’s shin: trilobite rib, antelope femur, anonymous knucklebone, all mortared together with plant calcium.  Fully mature elders click faintly as they walk, a xylophone heard through mittens.  
• Blood: sapsap—half lymph, half chlorophyll suspension, dark as bruised jade.  It leaks so slowly that wounds lacquer shut before gravity can notice.  
• Breath: indifferent to oxygen.  Alveoli have been replaced by bubble-lined sacs cultivated from the plateau’s spongewood.  These secrete an enzyme capable of cracking water vapour into whatever gas mixture is convenient that afternoon.

3. Coming-into-being  
Sex is a recreation, not a necessity.  True reproduction occurs when two or more adults kneel in one of the dawn pools and dissolve a portion of their wrist rinds.  Spores, shed nightly by the great ferns, drift into the mixture; each spore that cages a drop of Quietling sapsap gains permission to sprout.  Over a season the pool skins over with velvety green.  In mid-winter the skin buckles, ripples once, and walks ashore as a lanky, bewildered juvenile already fluent in the local gravity.

4. Visibility  
You searched, you saw smoke, you saw walls; you did not see people.  The fault is not yours.  To be looked at squeezes them—quite literally—out of shape.  Photons arriving too densely shear the filaments in their rind; in self-defence they relax sideways into the plateau’s many spatial hairlines.  They are present, only thinner.  Look away, and the space left over thickens around them until they can step forward again.  This is why the city’s fires appear untended.  They are being tended in dimensions your binoculars no longer bother to parse.

5. Social organisation  
Hierarchy is mapped onto distance rather than authority.  The one who can move farthest in a single deliberate stride is leader for the morning; at dusk a fresh pleat in time may elevate someone else.  Councils therefore resemble stones dropped into a pond: circles blink in and out as speakers near or recede.  Disputes seldom last long; march away from your opponent and the argument literally cannot follow.

6. Tools and craft  
Metal rots here, so they carve time instead.  
• Knurled Stones: thumb-sized pebbles encoded with overlapping grooves.  Rubbing a sequence of them together produces specific resonance patterns that persuade gravity to even itself out for a few heartbeats—useful when hauling peat blocks.  
• Veil Nets: skeins woven from fern-root fibres soaked in phosphor spores.  At night the nets glow along stress lines, revealing which strands of reality have stretched too thin to bear traffic the next day.  
• Whisper Blades: obsidian edges honed under running water that flows both upslope and down.  The final edge is a standing wave of probability; it cuts by suggesting to stone that it has already been severed.

7. Food  
They eat sunlight, but boredom demands variety.  Meals are communal slurries of moss starch, mineral lace broken from evaporated pools, and the occasional luxury of fish hauled up alive in wicker tubes from warmer air-currents four kilometres below.  The fish arrive gasping, their swim bladders ballooned; the Quietlings open them gently, capture the escaping breath, and trade it among children as toy balloons before releasing the animals, unharmed, into seep-water basins.  (The fish, no longer fish exactly, grow stunted wings of cartilage and spend adulthood skimming twilight thermals.)

8. Belief  
The plateau is ending—slowly, politely, but undeniably.  Cracks propagate through constants, not rock.  The Quietlings consider this neither tragedy nor doom; endings, too, are pollinations.  When the last law fails, their garden will spore outward into whatever comes next.  Preparation takes two forms:  
a) Hoarding silence, for noise will be scarce in the new world;  
b) Teaching the plants the shapes of possible futures by walking elaborate mandalas each morning, feet pressing intentions into compliant moss.

9. Relations with other beings  
They trade with valley caravans once every twelve years, when soundstorm season abates.  Caravaners are required to pad hooves and wagon wheels with felt.  In exchange for obsidian and fermented cloud-milk, the Quietlings offer Veil Nets and small jars of captive stillness—open one beside a fever victim and the illness cools to a halt long enough for healing to overtake it.

10. Death, as far as the word applies  
At about two centuries a Quietling grows heavy with accumulated bone.  Joints stiffen; strides shorten.  One evening they sit by a drip-pool, tuck their feet beneath them, and hum a three-note chord that is mostly pause.  Flesh petrifies in hours.  Within days the spine’s memories leak into surrounding slate like dye into water, adding a faint spiral grain to the rock.  Future juveniles will chisel slivers from that spiral to start scaffolding their own ribs.  The circle closes without fanfare.

11. How to meet them safely  
• Announce yourself by breathing a full lungful, then exhaling through pursed lips in a descending whistle too soft to make your ribs vibrate.  To them this says: I will keep my weight inside my own time.  
• Show your palms.  Let dew collect there.  Water wearing your fingerprint assures them you are not a reflection that wandered off the underside of a cloud.  
• Finish any sentence you start, even in thought.  Unfinished clauses tear edges in the air and can slice passing children.  

If you follow these courtesies, a shape may condense beside you: taller than you noticed, smelling of wet coin and peat fire, eyes lacquer-black and reflecting a sky where constellations hang slightly out of order.  The shape might offer you a knurled stone.  Turn it between thumb and forefinger; as grooves align you’ll feel yourself lighten, ankles tingling, heart floating up behind teeth.  Congratulations—you are being invited to walk with them, one stride, across as much ground as you can imagine.

Do not speak above a murmur.  Do not ask for a name.  Step gently: the plateau is listening, and its people are the plateau, learning what sort of story you intend to leave behind.
