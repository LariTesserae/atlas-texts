THE PEOPLE OF THE HIGH FOG WORKS  

1. What They Are  
   They call themselves, simply, the Keepers. Outsiders (few) prefer “fog-dwellers,” “overclads,” or “quietwrights,” but the Keepers shrug off titles the way copper sheds rain. They are recognisably human—descendants of the ridge-line construction crews who arrived three, perhaps four, lifetimes ago to lay the first power flumes and never left. Centuries of chill damp have bred a certain sameness into them: compact frames to conserve warmth; broad nasal bridges that warm the air; skin the colour of polished iron where the wind off the peaks stings sunburn away; calluses like horn on palms that have learned every contour of handrail and ratchet-wheel.  

2. A Day in Their Calendar  
   • Dawn (Whistle code: one descending note) — Lamp-keepers throttle the dynamos down to daytime amperage. Children, still wrapped in condensate-wool quilts, lope barefoot along the copper parapets to shake rime from planter tarps before the drip can starve the seedlings of light.  
   • Mid-Shift — Valves bloom open; water hisses; the whole saddle inhales. Millwrights in oilskin capes ride crank-lifts to fault sites, unslinging felt mufflers to swaddle any gearbox that has strayed above the mandated hush. Gardeners—who carry the same seniority as chief engineers—prune willows into spray-shields that guide drizzle toward thirsty turbines.  
   • Gloaming (Two short whistles) — Lifts ratchet decks toward the paling sun. Balsa-hull barges, suspended from crane booms, ferry bean-pods and brass fittings to exchange piers far down-valley.  
   • Black Quiet — Every belt is slackened until it just whispers. Sodium lamps dim to garnet. Lovers meet on the steam-struts where exhaust is warm enough to keep dew at bay; they court in murmurs, because covenant says any word louder than a turning fly-wheel is ill-omen. Elder Keepers walk the galleries on “drift watch,” listening for new leaks in the hush.  

3. Guilds and Callings  
   a. Flow-Clerks: Stern archivists hunched over vellum hydromaps, scheduling every drop of water a week ahead. They decide whose sluice floods, whose turbine idles, whose bean-tower may drink. Their ink is verdigris scraped from older pipes; their ledgers smell of salt.  
   b. Hush-Smiths: Mechanics of silence. They lace machines in felt, balance fly-wheels, tune whistle-ports. The finest among them can stand in the drizzle with eyes shut and hear the half-tooth-skip of a rack two terraces away.  
   c. Verdurers: Botanists with billhooks and grafting wax. Each wears a necklace of dried seed-pods that doubles as credential and seed-bank. If a bed fails its duty—slips soil, admits glare, or sours the air—they convene judgment and order an immediate replant, sometimes of whole walls.  
   d. Warden-Forebears: A purely ceremonial lineage whose task is to remember what lies entombed. They give eulogies for machines about to be overclad and recite the rivet counts of foundations no one has seen in three generations.  

4. Homes  
   Family quarters are nothing larger than a rail-truck soldered to the side of a warm exhaust column or a nest of hammocks slung beneath a line-shaft gallery. Living space is leased by the cubic metre of still air; most prefer to reside directly inside the works rather than behind exterior walls—the better to feel changes in thrum and temperature. Privacy is found in vertical distance: spouses sleep at wrist height, infants in dangling cradle-baskets, grandparents on the decking below so that any nocturnal cough drains away from younger lungs.  

5. Dress and Tools  
   • Footing: Split-soled boots ribbed with braided copper wire for traction on damp plating.  
   • The Fog Cloak: A reversible mantle—charcoal wool outside, mirrored copper mesh within. Wool out for warmth; copper out when signalling cranes in low visibility.  
   • Murmur Knife: A slim, hook-bladed folding tool issued at apprenticeship. One edge cuts rope; the back edge doubles as a tuning fork for diagnosing resonance in pipes.  
   • Breath Helix: Thin corkscrew of brass that slots behind the ear; it spins slowly in your exhale, powering a glow-flea lamp the size of a fingernail so night-watchers can see the bearer’s face without breaking covenant quiet.  

6. Law and Polity  
   Nothing here is voted. Instead, priority—water, energy, traffic, aesthetics—serves as constitution. Whoever controls the upstream valve controls every decision downstream of it; therefore deliberations always begin at the highest elevation affected. Quarrels that can’t be settled by precedent go to Trial by Drop: disputants ride an inspection cradle lowered into a spray-shaft and must propose a compromise before exposure chills them speechless. Arguments rarely last more than six minutes.  

7. Beliefs  
   • Doctrine of Overclad Grace: To abandon a thing is waste; to overclad it is mercy. Even corpses are rarely buried; they are ash-baked in low kilns, the brittle bones ground and mixed into grout that seals new gardens. “We all become footing,” the elders say.  
   • The Covenant of the Decibel: Sound above a certain threshold is sin because it presages entropy—anything shrieking is already breaking. Their nearest concept of the sacred is a perfectly balanced turbine whose only advertisement is the coolness of the air around it.  

8. Childhood  
   Infants suckle on tethered slings that let mothers work both hands free. At four years, children join the Velocipede Circuit: a labyrinth of pedal-generators whose merry-go-round motion keeps auxiliary flywheels spinning. Games reward smooth cadence and faultless listening; a child who can’t identify ten distinct bearing pitches by age eight is apprenticed to the traders and sent downslope.  

9. Outside Trade  
   Keepers barter surplus copper patina, dew-distilled alcohol, and rare mountain dyes for wool, stories, and sometimes newborn goats (ibex adapt poorly to the decks after adolescence). Visiting caravanners must wrap every clapper, bell, or chain before the portcullis opens; earsplitting merchants find themselves doing business beyond the fog line, shouting across a gorge to interlocutors who answer with mirrored flashes.  

10. Non-Human Residents  
   • Loam-Wrights: Engineered earthworms the size of chopsticks. Their castings filter heavy metals; their pale bodies are gently luminous. Keepers treat them as co-workers, cursing only when a tangle gums an intake grate.  
   • Feral Engines: Retired machinery not fully de-commissioned. Sometimes a forgotten belt will tighten itself in night humidity, and an orphan pump will clunk awake, drawing water from nowhere to nowhere until a dawn patrol soothes it back to sleep. Children swear they can ride these “grandfather rigs” if they sing at precisely the shaft’s resonant note.  

11. Fears  
   The one horror is an Unmanaged Drop: water escaping its assigned path. A freed cataract could scour decades of layered work in minutes. Entire drills exist only to practice emergency weirs—cog-toothed dams that snap into place on whistle code three rising.  

12. Hopes  
   The consensus dream is not expansion—there is hardly an untouched crag left—but refinement. They speak of a future when no surface drips without purpose, when every engine hums in unison, when the Works at last become so quiet you might hear snow settling on the upper ridges. Then, they say, the job will still not be done; perfection merely opens finer strata of possible grace.  

Stand on any deck at midnight and you will feel it: the tremor of line-shafts idling, the breath of hot mist, the sub-audible pulse of thousands of hands aligned in tasks too ordinary to seem miraculous. No god reigns here, yet the High Fog Works are alive, and the Keepers are its dreaming synapses—soft-spoken, copper-veined, tireless, patient as runoff wearing stone.
