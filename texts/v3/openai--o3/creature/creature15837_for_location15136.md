Who or what lives here?

1. The Sedentary Sedge  
They are not a species so much as a single sprawling body, a colonial organism masquerading as a cluster of reed-thin plants. Each “stalk” is a hollow straw full of lace-work partitions; moisture climbs those partitions by capillary pull, is hoarded in swollen nodes under the gravel, and is portioned out leaf by leaf as fog beads or a shower passes. What looks like dozens of independent grasses are really vents of one cautious creature whose rhizome lies braided and coiled just below the hardpan, stitched to stone chips with hair-root sutures. In late twilight the outermost shoots exude a drop of sugary mucilage that dries into a crystal ring by morning—a slow, plantish way of shedding excess salt.

2. The Lichen Choir  
Pale rosettes the size of coins patch the sandstone shoulder-high, but they share a skin-deep network of fungal threads that runs metres along the wall. Every few minutes one rosette releases a fleck of carbon dioxide; the gas migrates between cups in a microscopic relay that ends with a faintly audible ping—too soft for the human ear but enough to orchestrate the pulses of neighbouring lichens. The whole colony breathes in loose polyrhythms, a patient fugue whose full cycle takes forty-one hours. That is the hollow’s only resident music.

3. Gravelglass Midges  
Almost invisible until they unfold, these insects spend 99 % of their lives vitrified. Each adult midge—a gossamer stick no longer than an eyelash—lands after its mating flight, draws in a lungful of damp, and secretes a cocoon of silica extracted from the silt. Inside, it dries itself into torpor. The basin’s slow alternation of mist and noon glare keeps breaking, then renewing, their stasis: when humidity tops 93 %, the silica case liquefies and the midge quivers awake for roughly eight minutes. In that span it mates, lays, or dies; then the glass reforms, and the cycle sinks back into stone-quiet. A person could stand among a hundred cocoons and never suspect the short flares of winged life switching on and off like pilot lights.

4. Three-Heart Nematodes  
Beneath every thumb-deep puddle wriggle transparent worms whose circulatory trick is to collapse two hearts when water vanishes and run on a single, thumb-nail-sized pump until the next rain. They feed on bacterial films that bloom between dusk and dawn, then coil into salt-tight knots that look like discarded sewing thread. Their nervous system never fully sleeps; even in desiccation they register vibrations through a lattice of calcium granules, a sensory net that extends into the mud around them, making each drought a period of mute listening.

5. The Unbuilt Mason  
No one has seen it directly. Once every few seasons another dressed block joins the curved ghost of wall in the north-east corner. The newcomer is always quarried from the basin itself—its missing niche can be traced, if you search, high on the south-east cliff where sandstone has been neatly parted. The block travels overnight, arriving already mortared into place with a grout made of ground quartz and evaporated pool salts. After setting the stone, the Mason seems to polish the lone slab nearby: tiny concentric scours appear, too regular for falling water, as though a wide palm had spent hours stroking the surface. Whether the Mason is mineral automaton, patient animal, or a syndicate of the basin’s slower processes playing at architecture, no one can say. But the half-circle of blocks is undeniably growing—by perhaps one stone every decade—toward a form only it remembers.

6. The Vapor Thief  
Strictly speaking it is weather, not organism: a zone of air only as large as a child’s cloak that roams half a metre above ground after midnight. Where it passes, beads vanish from the sandstone and puddles wrinkle with sudden dimples, as though something were drinking the surface tension. It never moves faster than a slow walk, never leaves the hollow, and never continues once dawn light reaches the basin floor. The Vapor Thief is both shepherd and limiter of moisture, a moving lid that keeps the damp from becoming abundant enough for more vigorous life. Some suspect the Thief and the Mason are the same phenomenon wearing different masks of matter.

7. Tardigrade Archivists  
Every speck of moss at the southern wall houses clusters of water bears with gut pigments matched to the sandstone’s tawny red, an adaptive camouflage against the basin’s occasional, curious kestrel. When a raindrop strikes, they unfurl, feed, and excrete crystal pellets that trap bits of ambient DNA and airborne pollen, effectively archiving a molecular census of each wet spell. If researchers were to extract those pellets, they would find centuries of climate written in a script of proteins and dust.

8. The Absence That Watches  
Finally, there is a resident best defined by what it is not. Step into the hollow and you feel it adjust around you—a deliberate vacancy shaped like a presence. It holds the hush steady, deadens echoes, softens footfalls. Leave, and the sensation slips away with the first breath of plainer air. Some call it a tutelary spirit, others a psychical acoustic: a hollowed-out consciousness maintained by the equilibrium of stone, shade, and slow water. Whether it birthed the Mason, marshals the Vapor Thief, or merely records the lichen chorus for some unspooling epoch, it is the basin’s oldest life: a persistence rather than a pulse.

Together these inhabitants fill the nave-sized depression with a life so reduced, so diffident, that the casual passer-by reports it empty. Yet the basin hums with organisms and agencies tuned to intervals of mist and mineral, each content to live in the drawn-out middle note the place so effortlessly sustains.
