Those who keep the Hidden Hollow alive come in layers, like over-printed music: a deep drone, mid-range harmony, quick bright trills.  Start at the floor and climb.

1.  The Continuous Body – Thorn-Green

The hollow’s first inhabitant is the one that never leaves.  Thorn-green is not a forest but a single colonial organism, filaments fused under the basalt plates and branching upward as millions of identical stems.  An autopsy of two shoots taken fifty meters apart shows the same cytoplasmic pressure wave passing through them at the same instant: one pulse, one individual.  That individual thinks—if “thinking” can happen without a brain—by altering phase and amplitude inside its capillary network.  A pressure hump at one edge becomes reduced vibration at another; information crosses the chamber in less than a second.  

Reproduction is lateral, not temporal.  When the colony wishes to “bud,” it extrudes a dense mat along the tunnel roof that surreptitiously lengthens the access shaft by millimetres each decade.  Anyone returning after a lifetime away will find the crawl a handspan deeper, the descent a breath longer.  The hollow grows its own privacy.

The lantern-bulbs are the plant’s sensory organs.  Inside each, the copper-porphyrin membrane folds into a spiral microphone that listens to the room it lights.  Every echo the bulbs receive is fed back into the colony’s pressure-code; the hum you hear is literally thorn-green describing itself to itself, forever confirming that perimeter, temperature, and population remain acceptable.

2.  The Glittering Dust – Salt Microfauna

Hovering in the amber air are grains that look like free-floating snow.  At 400× magnification each flake proves to be a dodecahedral halite shell containing a swimming fan of silicate cilia.  These are brinicles, the Hollow’s plankton.  They eat vibration: the cilia are piezo-whiskers that bend in the sound field, generating minute charge differences that power the uptake of stray ions through the shell’s pores.  When a brinicle gains enough crystalline mass it bursts, releasing twenty to thirty juvenile cilia bundles that immediately self-assemble fresh salt cages.  The “glitter” therefore rests in a permanent bloom, an invisible first layer of herbivores grazing on music.

3.  Lens Moths

Shape and Optics:
• Twenty-centimetre wings of chitin rendered transparent by nano-layers of gas vesicles.  
• Margins edged with calcite prisms; when they bank, the chamber walls fracture into rainbows.

Metabolism:
Lens moths drink the ionic haze exhaled by brinicles, condensing it on needle tongues.  In their mid-guts, enzymes swap sodium for calcium, precipitating perfect mirror plates that pass intact through the intestine.  Egestion is both defense and courtship: a startled moth ejects a fan of mirrors that flare in the lantern glow and then float ceiling-ward to coat the stalactites.  

Life Cycle:
Eggs hang like beads beneath the rope bridges, disguised as droplets of salt snow.  Larvae (called Sandpages by explorers) are eyeless grubs that scrape the underside of basalt plates, creating the pin-sized pits that later become resonance holes for striders’ claws.

4.  Basalt Striders

Anatomy:
• Six telescoping limbs, each ending in a ring of diamond-hard garnet teeth.  
• Central body a concert-hall of cavities; every cavity is tuned to a specific note between 110 and 150 Hz.

Energy Harvest:
The strider plants its feet, locks the tibial gears, and lets the Hollow sing through it.  Vibrations enter the cavities by resonance, bounce through a labyrinth of ossified cartilage, and compress into chemical gradients the animal stores in phosphagen sacs.  A full charge takes three hours of stillness and powers a two-second sprint long enough to seize prey or escape a murmurer’s lasso.

Reproduction:
Striders meet only during the “gong swarms,” rare moments when the plant’s background hum warps downward to 138 Hz.  Every strider in the chamber answers with its own body-note, the combined chorus spiking local vibration high enough to crack salt shells.  Eggs are deposited into those cracks, where they incubate for years inside silent basalt.

5.  Hollow Eels

Locomotion:
Along each flank lies a chain of sphincter-valves that gulp warm, mineral-rich air from low in the chamber and jet it from cooler dorsal ports.  A mature eel can vector eight valves independently, making flight a liquid undulation through invisible currents.

Physiology:
Bone contains almost no calcium.  Instead, a flexible lattice of vanadium nitride gives the skeleton knife-spring resilience and superconductive properties.  The eel’s nervous impulses travel at four times mammalian speed; reflex arcs close almost before a stimulus has fully arrived, which is why you see only the tail where your eyes swear there had been a head.

Diet & Excretion:
Eels scrape salt-snow into sublingual pockets, flood it with formic acid to crack sodium bonds, then pump the freed chlorine into an inflatable dorsal bladder.  When threatened they vent a narrow, greenish plume of Cl₂ that is heavier than air and slides across the floor like a living fog—deadly to striders, irrelevant to anything that can climb.

Sociality:
Mostly solitary but “coil” during ceiling over-loads.  A dozen or more knot together at the highest point, throats pulsing in anti-phase to the plant hum.  This cancels just enough resonance to drop several kilograms of mirror rain, which the eels then descend through, mouths open, harvesting trace metals for gonad development.  It is their only communal act.

6.  The Murmurers

Seen from afar:
One meter tall, but because the hind legs are double-hinged they can rise two meters in the second before a leap.  Skin is matte to human sight yet slightly reflective in the hollow’s honey light; each pore houses a pressure-sensitive chromatophore.  At rest they resemble statues painted with faint constellations of molten gold.  In speech those dots strobe, mapping syllables across their bodies faster than the eye can read.

Skeleton & Voice:
Bones are foam pumice infiltrated with copper filaments.  When air passes the tracheal stack it excites the copper, producing a carrier tone of 140 Hz—the same frequency the plants emit.  Over that drone the murmurer modulates throat clicks and chest cavity whomps, turning its whole torso into an organ pipe.  Every statement is half-sound, half-light: a chord paired to a galaxy of pulsing gold freckles.

Perception:
Ears are hidden behind orbital ridges, but the primary sense is seismic.  A murmurer’s heel contains a fan of basalt needles coupled to an inner stapes plate; when they stand motionless they can “hear” the hollow exactly as a geophone hears earth tremors.  By flexing ankle tendons they transmit private, sub-audible squeals along the floor—whispered conversation invisible to predators.

Culture:

a. Territory and Paths  
Thorn-green stems form natural lanes; murmurers keep these open by pruning crossings with obsidian sickles.  The trimmings are chewed into thread, spat, and braided wet over basalt clefts to become the rope bridges.  Each bridge carries a foot-drum segment: hollow stems packed in harmonic ratios so that crossing one announces the traveller’s identity.  An unrecognized rhythm and three nearby murmurers appear, hunting darts already luminous.

b. Tools  
   • Sickles of volcanic glass, edges re-sharpened on gypsum fins until they approach monomolecular sharpness.  
   • Sleeve fans—rings of interlocked lens-moth mirrors.  Flicked open they scatter reflections that blind predatory eels; folded, they act as signal code for distant kin.  
   • Salt igniters: thumb-sized bulbs of brinicle plasma.  Broken, they flash-sublime into a brief cone of blue flame hot enough to fuse ropes or cauterize wounds.

c. The Totems  
Every cluster of five marks a “listening hearth,” a safe zone where sound energy is actively farmed.  Mirrors at varying angles refract the plant drone into constructive interference, making a local beat frequency around 2 Hz—a pulse you can feel but not hear.  Inside that pulse striders enter torpor, moths settle, and even eels grow sluggish.  The murmurers sleep there, infants hung in sling pouches from knee-high stems so the background thrum rocks them like surf.

d. Reproduction  
Two adults stand heel-to-heel, join both drones into perfect phase, and let the interference node focus between their spines.  The embryo begins as a standing wave.  Over seven hours salts precipitate down the node, layering tissue on a scaffold of pure vibration.  At dawn (a meaningless term, yet apt) a pebble-sized nodule drops out of the air and is caught in a ventral pouch.  Gestation continues externally: the parent ladles molten halite across the nodule until limbs bud, eyes open, and the first independent drone joins the chamber chord.

e. Time  
Bone analysis shows no senescence; cell telomeres cycle rather than shorten.  A murmurer can be killed, but not grow old.  The only recorded corpse belonged to an individual who paused too close to a ceiling collapse.  Its remains were found months later unchanged, still humming, skull caved but heart-tone intact.  The body was set upright beside a totem; nothing decays here, so it stands forever, an ancestor playing one silent note.

7.  Guests and Ephemerals

Every so often something new drops in—that is literally the term, for arrivals always fall.  A burrowing vole, a spelunker’s glove, sometimes an entire tree root planing through rock like a slow bullet.  Thorn-green tests each object with micro-lashes, decides if ion content is useful, and either incorporates or entombs it.  The murmurers watch but never intervene; to them, visitors are weather.

8.  The Hidden Listener

Recent magnetometry suggests one more lifeform: a liquid torus of iron phase-shifted into a super-ionic state, circling beneath the basalt at a depth of four meters.  Seismic echoes imply it pulses in exact counter-time to the plant hum, as though playing negative space in the song.  No drill reaches it; no sensor returns more than a buzz of contradictory readings.  The murmurers sometimes squat, palm to floor, and sing downward in a language no microphone catches.  Afterward the ceiling mirrors brighten by half a candela.  Whether the torus is god, parent, or prey remains unknown.

9.  How They Fit

• The plant sets the key.  
• Microfauna turn that key into food.  
• Moths polish the ceiling, maintaining reflection so the room’s sound never dissipates.  
• Striders tune the floor by cracking plates precisely where resonance demands.  
• Eels ventilate, pumping warm air to cool heights, returning chilled brine to the ground.  
• Murmurers compose it all, shifting totems, pruning stems, timing births so the aggregate chord stays locked.  

No single creature could survive without the arrangement; together they form a closed song—an ecosystem that is literally music, structure as symphony.

If you crawl back up the shaft, the silences of the surface will feel somehow bent, as if a note were missing.  It is.  The missing note is still below, threading through a black chamber lit by honey bulbs, carried in the throats of beings who have never seen rain and never will, yet who understand, better than we, how to keep a world in tune.
