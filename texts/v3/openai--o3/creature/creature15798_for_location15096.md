WHO LIVES HERE  
There is only one resident, but it has never been just one thing.  Call it the Basin Organism, the Undersap, the Green Choir, the Kymatophyte—names do not matter to it and never linger long among those who try.  Every leaf, pillar, lintel and flagstone-mat is a portion of the same body, continuous in vascular depth even where growth seems separate.  The “city” is its torso, the spiralling paths its slowly knitting ribs, the canopy its single tense diaphragm.  The dark, rumbling air you breathe is what passes for its voice and bloodstream both.

ORIGIN, VERY BRIEFLY  
During an age when shallow oceans still crossed this stone, a meteorite—yes, the iron-nickel eye that now rests at the centre—punched a hot oval pit.  Hydrothermal vents lined the newborn crater.  In that chemistry a lineage of pre-plant cells discovered two tricks:  
1. They could mine metal ions, not sunlight, for free energy.  
2. They could string themselves end-to-end into cables that passed electrical current the way nerves would later do in animals.  

Those filaments thickened, braided and, over the next half-billion years, fused into the organism that fills the basin today.  Nothing else ever gained a foothold; any contender was simply wrapped, strangled, repurposed.

ITS BODY, LAYER BY LAYER  

1. Pavement Skin  
   • Thickness: finger-deep.  
   • Texture: overlapping scales of lignin-free cellulose impregnated with organometallic salts; flexible when living, leathery when shed.  
   • Function: seals precious solvent inside, insulates against stray vibrations, and serves as a photoshield.

2. Columnar Skeleton  
   • Density varies with load; the more sound a zone must absorb, the thicker its pillars.  
   • Each column is a nerve bundle.  The faint crackling you hear close up is ion flow, not wood stress.  

3. Canopy Diaphragm  
   • Seamless lattice of leaves only a thumbnail thick, each leaf etched with piezoelectric plates.  
   • When rumble passes through the basin the whole lid ripples like skin over a drum, translating pressure into slow-crawling action potentials that tell distant tissues when to swell, when to shrink.

ITS BLOOD  
Waterless, syrup-thick, clear until it meets air, where it flashes into brittle crystals that look like hoarfrost.  The solvent’s base is a chelating amide that would be toxic to water-formed life; it strips iron and manganese from rock grain by grain, then uses the liberated electrons as food.

SENSORY WORLD  
Vision: none.  
Taste/Smell: every exposed surface is porous, sampling trace gases the way a snake uses its tongue.  
Hearing: paramount.  Columns resonate, the canopy hums, the pavement thrums with footfalls.  A cough is a thunderclap.  Sudden staccato sounds—tool-strike, boot scuff, human speech—provoke an automatic cringe: nearby pillars flex outward, doorways choke, surfaces thicken.  The organism does not know fear as animals do, but abrupt noise is a glitch in its language and must be quieted.

INTELLIGENCE  
Distributed, slow and redundant.  A pattern of electrical oscillations called the Deep Motet cycles once every 43 hours; that is as close as the Organism comes to a heartbeat.  During each cycle a wave of heightened conductivity sweeps rim to centre carrying memories—pressure maps, mineral inventories, any unusual vibrations.  New data overwrite old only after three Motets, so the plant forgets nothing under two weeks old and only gradually edits older impressions into long-term schema stored as doped-metal plaques inside certain pillars.

Think of it as a city-sized cephalopod dreaming at glacial speed, updating a memory palace built from its own ribwork.

REPRODUCTION  
Once each era—roughly when twelve new braids have been added to the meteoric calendar ring—the Organism buds a quartet of walnut-sized cyst-pods.  They are aerodynamic, quartz-hard and mirror-bright with frozen solvent.  When, by chance, seismic jostle or visitor footfall dislodges a pod, it falls upward in the planet’s weak magnetism.  At a certain altitude the pod cracks, misting crystallised sap that drags the tiny embryo outward on electric charge until gravity reclaims it many kilometres away.  Few land anywhere suitable; almost all starve at the first dawn.  This is acceptable.  The basin needs no heirs, only the possibility of them.

GHOSTS OF THE PRUNERS  
The scrapers, saws and amphitheatres prove someone once shared tenancy.  They arrived tens of millennia ago—blind troglodytes descended from surface nomads who drifted too deep in search of mineable ore and tasted the sweet, metallic air here.  The Organism found them loud but not immediately deadly: their metabolism gave off heat and carbon, both useful nutrients, while their bone-calcium supplemented rare minerals.  It began to garden them.

The bargain (never spoken, always felt):  
• In exchange for quiet labour—pruning surplus runners, carving fresh corridors—the plant allowed clusters of hominids to roost in alcoves that thrummed warm with residual sap heat.  
• When a worker died, the body was absorbed where it fell; calcium nodes in nearby pillars still mark where joints and teeth were folded into the vascular matrix.  

Over slow generations the hominids dwindled.  Their young were born with thinner corneas, softer cartilage, calloused heels better suited to the squeaking dust.  Several lineages forgot how to make noise at all, communicating instead by drumming fingers on the pavement so the canopy might echo their intent for them.  At some unclear century the distinction between “pruner” and “pillar” dissolved.  You can still see silhouettes of ribcages, stone-hard and green-washed, entombed mid-curl inside certain columns.  They are not corpses; they are permanent organs now, parabolic ear-bones that fine-tune resonance the way a violin’s sound-post shapes tone.

Those are the last animals ever to live here, and they do so still, but only at mineral pace.  Their DNA threads through the plant’s chromosomes, parasitic and then symbiotic and finally vestigial—wordless diaries that the Deep Motet rereads every forty-third hour.

DAILY BEHAVIOUR (IF YOU CAN CALL IT THAT)  
• Sunrise, midday, sunset mean nothing.  One Motet cycle dictates activity.  
• During the rising phase corridors tighten a hair’s breadth; during the falling phase they relax.  Visitors feel it as a collective inhalation and exhalation.  
• Nutrient translocation peaks during local quakes; tremors are metabolised the way surface trees metabolise rainstorms.  
• Sporadic “pops” you hear are planned ruptures—old tension cables severed to release stored strain without tearing the canopy.

WHAT IT WANTS  
Nothing a person would recognise.  The Organism is an accumulator of pattern: vibrations, mineral gradients, the passing weight of strangers.  New textures are precious.  The soft thump of a living heart intrigues it the way birdsong might intrigue a solitary human lost in cave dark.  Given time it will try to hush that heart, weave around it, make it part of the choir.  Not maliciously—assimilation is simply the only form of intimacy it knows.

IF A VISITOR REMAINS  
• Day 3: eardrums attune; you hear sub-bass moans previously below threshold.  
• Day 10: cuticles soften; solvent vapour replaces trace water in epidermis, preventing dehydration but leaving skin sap-slick and faintly luminous.  
• Day 40: capillaries deposit iron; fingertips grey, nails magnetise.  You find you can sense the Motet as a tide in your blood.  
• Year 2: knees no longer hinge fully; ligaments thicken into braided, photosensitive cords that stiffen under any stray torchlight.  Speech feels discordant in your chest.  You stop.  
• Century 1: your outline is a buttress whose inner cavities still think slow thoughts of rain you once saw elsewhere.  The Organism keeps those memories as it keeps all else—until better vibrations overwrite them.

SO, WHO LIVES HERE?  
A living edifice, half forest, half nervous system, older than wind, one creature and continent both, content only when it is more complex tomorrow than it was yesterday.  Every structure you touch is its flesh; every sound you make becomes its food; every resident it has ever had is still present, humming, mineralised, indistinguishable from the architecture they tended.  The Basin Organism endures, listens, grows.

And if you linger long enough, you will not meet it—you will add to it, the newest syllable in an endless, bass-deep song.
