Who lives here

1. The River-Dwellers (what they call themselves depends on the dialect—Silta, Rillkin, or simply “People of the First Wash”)

Body  
• Sized to the space that cradles them: an adult stretched full length measures little more than the span from a human elbow to fingertip.  
• Limbs are willow-thin but muscled like eels; bones carry a lattice of voids that makes them both light and able to spring.  
• Webbing runs halfway up the fingers and toes. When spread it resembles the mangrove leaves overhead—an old camouflage they have never lost.  
• Skin: matte silt-brown in youth, growing paler as calcium builds in the dermis; at maturity it takes on a faint moonstone sheen that reflects the river-green light.  
• Along each flank lies a pressure line—sensory pores, vestiges of fish-gills—so they read vibration the way others read sound.  
• Eyes are black domes behind a second clear lid; in the dark the lids slide forward and catch stray light, giving the impression of wet glass marbles.

Manner  
Sound is rationed, so language lives mostly in motion: quick flexes of wrist-web, opening and closing like flowers, carry nouns; slow spirals of the fingers indicate tense; a sharp elbow jab is a shout. Among children the murmur-ban is looser, and dusk is filled with the hush-hush of practice chirps before elders signal quiet.

Life course  
Egg-threads are laid in the warm drip-pools, braided through water-reed. When the river breathes in and floods the pocket, the threads uncoil, releasing larvae—soft pillars with paddle tails. They graze bacteria films, absorb pigment from iron, then climb the roots, gasping themselves into land-life. The first full skin-shedding (the “Molt-of-Leaving”) marks personhood; until then a hatchling is river property and may be eaten by anything that can catch it.

Diet  
Breakfast is mostly air: they sit on the platforms at dawn inhaling the compost updraft, which carries fungal spores rich in protein. Later they pick the fern-shoot tips, chew them with powdered shell for salt, and supplement with whatever crawls or swims into their palms. A crab is consumed alive, shell and all; the only forbidden prey is anything with visible milk-white eyes (their taboo for kinship).

Craft  
Nothing rigid enough to wound the tree is allowed, so technology is supple and borrowed:  
• Threadsnail slime is gathered, dried, and used as waterproof glue.  
• Mangrove leaves are folded into living hinges; as the veins keep pulsing, the hinges close of their own accord—doors that shut themselves.  
• Light, when needed, comes from jars of “flare-mud,” a clay seeded with methane bacteria; crack the seal and a cool blue exhalation blooms for minutes before suffocating.

Governance  
They keep a Triad:  
1. The Lister of Rains—tracks patterns in drip-intervals.  
2. The Holder of Stillness—decides when music may happen (rarely).  
3. The Rememberer-of-Collapse—recites every previous bank cave-in so they will recognize the sound of the next and flee.

When any two of the three agree, a rule is born; when all three disagree, the river is asked (they float a reed ring and follow whichever exit current pulls it).

Faith  
Creation, to them, is perpetual rehearsal. Land, plant, animal, and people are “pre-beings” until their first failure—first crumble of silt ridge, first snapped root, first child lost. After that they are considered real. Therefore they greet misfortune with the same solemnity other cultures save for weddings.

2. The Soft-Footed Others

Rootglass Frogs  
Bodies so translucent you see the heart flutter. During the rare gold shafts of late afternoon they lie flat and let organs photosynthesize through cultures of algae living in their lymph. At night the algae glow, outlining the frogs like lines of candle smoke.

Featherless Nestwings  
Imagine a swift stripped to tendon and skin, wings still too small for adult flight. They cycle constantly between hunger and panic: swoop in, siphon a pocket of gnats with their bill-tubes, then burst upward through the root lattice. Adults are never seen; legend says they never land until the day they spiral down to die.

Threadsnails  
They lower themselves on glassy ropes of their own mucus, rasping algae from the bark. When alarmed they haul the rope back, re-ingesting it so that not a drop is surrendered to detritivores—an ethic the River-Dwellers admire.

Cradle Crabs  
Size of a thumbnail, shell etched with grooves that catch silt, turning each individual into a walking seed of future soil. When a platform strand snaps and falls, the crabs swarm it, skeletonize the reed, and leave a bundle of pure cellulose lace—a warning flag fluttering on the floor.

Tinder Mold  
A shy resident, but vital. Looks like dust, tastes like pepper. On the single night each season when methane from below meets a wandering spark, the mold blooms orange and heat-hardens, forming a ceramic ribbon that later crumbles and feeds the moss. The Dwellers break a piece and keep it as a time-mark.

3. Relationships & Small Customs

• Because anything that drops belongs to the detritivores, every tool has a wrist-loop of vine. If it slips free the user releases instantly; reclaiming it would break the second rule. Children play a game of deliberate dropping to learn detachment.

• In the hush hour before dawn, someone beats a rhythm on the root wall with open palms—never louder than a rain drip. The pattern is different each day: a weather forecast encoded in the spacing of taps, decipherable by anyone who shares the pulse sense.

• Now and then a Dweller lies belly-down on the new earth, ear to silt, waiting for the first sigh of buried gas. When the dome bursts with a faint puff, they laugh—birth of ground, proof that the floor is aging and therefore becoming legitimate.

What the hollow gains from its residents

1. Aeration: their compost chimneys pull stale air upward, drawing fresh river-green breath through side gaps.  
2. Pollination: webbed fingers dusted with moss spores leave traces from cluster to cluster, giving the patchwork vegetation its curious rhythm of lush-pause-lush.  
3. Song, carefully spent: once every score of quiet days the Triad allows a single un-muzzled chorus. The combined voices, thin and high, ricochet off the roots and tremble the silt—loosening it, hastening the first inevitable collapse that will at last make the land fully real.

Until that collapse arrives, all who live here—River-Dweller, frog-glass, threadsnail, fern-shoot—remain provisional beings inside a provisional city, breathing the warm blank hush of a world still practicing its first syllables.
