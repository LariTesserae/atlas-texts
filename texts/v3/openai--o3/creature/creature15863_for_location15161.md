Who lives on the sky-mesa?  
All of it, from the frost-frail microbes that silver every dawn to the mantis that can fold a canyon shut, is alive and busy.  What follows begins with the smallest chemistries and climbs, rung by rung, to the creatures whose footsteps you can hear a day before you see them.  None of them are truly separate; the plateau is a single, polyphonic metabolism that happens to wear many shapes.

1. Film-Eaters and Light-Catchers  
a. Prismatophages  
   • A twinned bacterium that grows as a skin over wet basalt while the condensate is still liquid.  
   • Each cell extrudes a triangular platelet of silica that slots against its neighbors, turning the film into a living diffraction grating.  Thirty seconds of full sun is enough to photo-charge the cells; the gram of sugar they make in that moment must last two weeks.  
   • At night the platelets invert and scatter moonlight as a milky halo inches above the rock, a banked lantern for things that hunt by refraction.

b. Rind-moss (“quilts”)  
   • Not a plant but a partnership of blue algae and amber fungi knitted around capillary bundles made of chitin.  
   • The bundles soak meltwater by dusk, lock it inside an antifreeze gel, then slowly leak it back to the algal cells through the following day.  
   • Every five or six years the mat heaves up into a blister the size of a dining table, bursts, and throws spores that float on static charge.  Minutes later the wind has blown the blister flat and seamless again.

c. Lantern Lichens  
   • Those cliff-face murals that fluoresce chartreuse.  The glow is not waste light; it is a lure.  Microscopic rotifers navigate toward it, carrying packets of bound nitrogen the lichen cannot fix itself.  Each rotifer is rewarded with a bubble of glycerol it can burn for warmth before the next freeze.

2. The Small, Mobile Layer  
a. Glass-wing Midges  
   • Their whole wing is a single transparent crystal of chitin shot through with microscopic tubules.  Condensate streams down those tubules into the gut while the midge is still airborne; to drink it need only fly.  
   • At dusk the sky turns silver with them: billions rising on the thermal exhalation of the plateau.  They are less an insect than an atmospheric phase.

b. Prism Mites  
   • Two millimetres long, armored in calcite.  
   • They rasp silica off broken obsidian and excrete the dust as true glass, perfectly amorphous, which they smear over their burrow entrances.  Under aurora a mite field twinkles like a city seen from orbit.

3. Grazers, Harvesters, Recyclers  
a. Hexmarmots  
   • Thumb-sized, six-limbed, fur so dense it drips condensation all day.  
   • The “flick-knife” incisors are hollow; each bite injects a slurry of cellulase and antifreeze proteins.  They return later to lap the pre-digested pulp.  Nothing on the mesa breaks plant tissue faster.  
   • They live in colonies that merge and split like weather fronts.  A daylight satellite image of the plateau shows their aggregated heat signatures as slow, orange tides.

b. Rotary Sloths  
   • Waist-high, tripedal, jointed so the whole torso can spin about the anchored forelimb.  The claws really are rotary saws: concentric rings of dentine that ratchet outward the faster the animal turns.  
   • They police the ground for dropped lignin sheets from the ferns.  Each sheet becomes a drum-skin stretched between three sloths, amplifying subsonic clicks they use for long-range coordination.  A migrating sloth flotilla is a walking echolocation array.

4. The Resonant Herds – Cantors  
   • Ten-metre quadrupeds, shoulders taller than the roof of most houses.  
   • Along each spine runs a stack of cartilage bows kept in torsion by braided tendons.  Step, flex, release—every pace plucks a note.  Individuals tune themselves by tightening or loosening the muscle bands at dawn; a healthy herd’s chord progression walks through a full octave over the course of a year-long migration loop.  
   • Lungs double as boilers.  Steam jets sleet from lateral vents, quilting the air with fog that seeds fresh condensate for the moss the Cantors will graze tomorrow.  They are farmers of climate as much as feeders upon it.

Life Cycle  
   • Calves are born silent; their backbows grow slack and useless while bone is still green.  At six weeks the mother shunts a hormone that mineralizes the cords overnight.  Dawn breaks, a hundred new voices join the ground-organ, and the entire horizon seems to sing in surprise at itself.

Symbionts  
   • Glass-wing midges roost between the bows, safe from mantid strike zones.  In exchange they groom the cords free of ice accretions that could detune the animal.

5. Apex Ambush – Alcove Mantis  
   • Five storeys tall when fully unfolded, but almost nothing is exposed to the wind unless it chooses; the exoskeleton is corrugated into pleats like an antique map.  
   • Coppery “vines” down the wing-ribs are arrays of electroreceptors keyed to the Cantors’ resonant frequencies.  A mantis can feel a herd’s approach three days out through vibrating cliff-stone.  
   • Attack is ballistic: wings snap, cavitation booms, the pressure wave alone can stun Hexmarmots a hundred metres away.  
   • It eats by skimming: runs a forelimb through the passing herd, scrapes off a spray of tissue and steam, never stopping.  A single glide can harvest a tonne of protein without killing a target.  Predation here is partial and iterative; no one transaction ends a life.

6. Recycling Engines  
a. Bone-Glass Caddis  
   • Larvae live in ice-rimmed puddles inside old Cantor scapulae.  
   • They build cases from hydroxyapatite shards and bits of stranded tendon.  When they pupate, the empty cases remain: thumb-sized jewels that ring like crystal if the wind is right, a million tiny wind-chimes documenting every death.  

b. Porcelain Root-Worms  
   • Bore through fallen mantis wing-plates, line the tunnels with white excretion that sinters into ceramic.  
   • Those latticed plates become soil beds; pioneer moss threads anchor in the worm-ceramics long before bare basalt could hold them.  The worms are the plateau’s civil engineers.

7. Flyers over the Rim – Edgewind Drakes  
   • Reptile neither in genome nor in airframe, yet impossible not to call a dragon.  Wingspan seventeen metres, body recoil-hollow like a reed flute.  
   • They live almost nowhere: spend ninety-eight percent of life in glide between uplifts, drinking nothing, eating only the clouds of glass-wing midges they sieve with baleen-like gill rakers.  
   • At breeding time they land on the rim fins, carve a nest-trench with acid spit, and line it with shred-moss stolen mid-air from Cantor backs.  The nest glows ultraviolet at night—an advertisement to mates, a warning to mantids.

8. The Distributed Mind  
   • Circulating through every vascular plant is a suite of plasmids known collectively as the Spiral Gospel.  Each plasmid carries snippets of code for antifreeze proteins, lignin-sheet patterning, and chromatophore control.  
   • Midges swap the plasmids when they drink from plant cuticles; mites ingest them from midge corpses; Cantors acquire them in moss; mantids get them second-hand in Cantor blood.  
   • Over geological time the Gospel propagates adaptive hacks horizontally across the entire biosphere.  A mutation perfected by a moss can appear in a drake’s keratin a century later.  The plateau is therefore not merely an ecosystem but an evolving, collective autobiography—a mind written in borrowed genes, edited by selection, recited aloud in color, scent, and sound.

9. Ghosts of Weather – Frostshades  
   • Not organisms in the traditional sense but recurring crystal patterns that bloom on the leeward faces of basalt every solstice dawn.  
   • Each bloom lasts twenty minutes; within that window it diffracts the sunrise into a coherent hologram the size of a barn.  The hologram is always the same: a slowly turning knot of Möbius bands.  
   • Micro-analysis shows the bands encode, in base-64, the nucleotide sequence of the Spiral Gospel’s master plasmid.  The mesa writes its own genome in frost, every year, lest anything forget.

10. What You Will Not Meet  
   • No villages, no tools, no speech locked inside a single throat.  Intelligence here is ambient, enacted, uncounted: a million reflexes braided into a system that can tune its weather, clarify its light, and remember itself in ice.  
   • Should a thinking visitor arrive, the plateau will treat them as one more courier of genes and minerals—offer condensation to the lips, ultraviolet to the eyes, and the great slow music of tendons to the bones.  Nothing more, nothing less.  

Stand on the rim at night.  The lichens are singing green, the wind is turning the basalt organ, and somewhere a mantis folds shut with the cannon-crack of negative pressure.  Everything you can see, and much you cannot, is busy keeping the mesa alive, refining it, teaching it new tricks.  These are its citizens—fluid, radiant, resonant, patient.  They do not need names to know one another; they recognize each other by flavor of light and interval between heartbeats.  

If you must give them one name for your records, call them the Condensation Folk, and understand the word people here means moss, midge, dragon, frost, and stone in one breath.
