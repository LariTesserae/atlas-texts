Who and what thrive on the plateau can be told as a sequence of increasingly complex bodies—first the unseen films that glaze the rock, then the animals that graze upon those films, and finally the people who have learned to graze upon the animals, the films, the rock, the light, and each other’s patience.

1. The First Skins

a. Jade Lichen (mis-named; contains no chlorophyll)  
A slime only two molecules thick coats much of the pumice near the weeping vents. It is iron-oxidizing and ultraviolet-hungry; under a field lens it looks like flocks of tiny green mirrors migrating across the stone. Each dawn the film retracts into sub-pore cavities to avoid the boiling point of its own cytoplasm, then unfurls again at dusk to drink the after-heat. The salt crescents at basin rims are flecked with its corpses—dark little commas that seed the next bloom.

b. Vapor Yeast  
Invisible except when rain falls and turns everything briefly liquid. The yeast blooms into opalescent scum, ferments trace sugars exuded by the lichen, and exudes ethanol strong enough to blister a careless finger. That volatile fringe is what the plateau’s flyers seek.

2. The Crawling and the Swimming

a. Silt-Eels (properly, ferrodermis vermiculi)  
• Length: a handspan  
• Texture: like a bag of wet ball bearings  
• Gait: peristaltic undulations; no real head  
They rasp pumice with a ribbon of magnetite teeth, storing liberated iron as granular nodules along the belly. Every few weeks an eel bursts its own skin, extrudes a fresh, flexible sheath, and leaves the mineral beads behind in the cast-off tube. Those glittering husks are scavenged by children, powdered, and sold to smelters in thumb-sized cloth sachets called rust-rice.

b. Chime Crabs  
Their name matters less than their etiquette. Two rules govern them: never strike without declaring intent (the drummed tremor) and never waste a corpse. If you watch a fight, the victor folds the loser and eats it as neatly as a traveling tailor packs a coat, beginning with the leg joints to silence any accidental shudder. What looks like cruelty is really noise discipline.

3. The Airborne

a. Glasswings  
An adult is a kite of nearly pure chitin, micro-etched to bend light along its span. By local noon a flock disappears into glare; by late afternoon each wing edge fluoresces coppery red where photochemical pigments repair UV damage. Their diet is the vapor yeast’s flash ferment. After a rain they drop, spear the fizzing meniscus with an extendable stylet, and balloon their stomachs so fast that the yeast’s own carbonation jets them skyward again. From a distance it resembles champagne poured in reverse.

b. Meteor Wasps (rare but unforgettable)  
They hatch only during the Long Gleam when geysers blow. Eggs, light as ash, ride the plume, polymerize in mid-air, and fall as silvery darts that stab whatever they hit—roof, Gargoyle hide, human shoulder—planting larvae that mine for calcium in bone or basalt alike. The city pays good jade-lichen paste for the first spotted husk each season; knowing when the wasps have come is a civic defense.

4. Apex: The Gargoyles

Imagine a crouched leopard, strip away the fur, replace the skeleton with reinforced trabecular foam, give it a cormorant’s throat pouch that can balloon with scalding steam, and hinge the jaws not sideways but vertically forward so the mouth becomes a sluice. That is only first sight.

Sensory array  
• Eyes: four, two frontal, two stacked high on the brow for zenith glare detection.  
• Ears: vestigial.  
• Palate: riddled with pit organs that feel micro-vibration and temperature differential across a vent’s lip.  

Behavior  
A Gargoyle owns a vent the way a raptor owns thermals. It lies across the opening, drinks warm condensate, and lets prey approach the water. The kill is a forward-fold of the whole torso; the basalt talons are mostly for braking against stone, not for slashing. It eats by straining slurry—bone, shell, and pumice fragments are spat later in neat pellets that Ledonsk potters collect and fire into ceramic bearings.

5. The Humans of Ledonsk

a. Physiology  
Under the hard sun and rarified air, lungs grow wide; clavicles arch like handles. Infants sleep in shallow brass pans so the moon’s glare will keep their pineal rhythms aligned with the older generation’s—shift workers in condensate towers swear by this. Skin hues trend toward matte umber streaked with nacreous freckles: deposits of transdermal silica taken up from lifelong drinking of mineral-rich water.

Eyes narrow not from disuse but from constant squint against refracted UV. Many adults tattoo semicircular shade bands beneath the lower lashes: soot mixed with eel-oil, a cosmetic visor.

b. Dress  
Fiber is scarce. Garments are platelets of layered paper pressed from boiled chime-crab shell and pumice dust, lacquered with resin imported from below. Flexible when wet, the pieces harden under sun, becoming scale-like armor that breathes. At night whole suits are soaked and re-shaped; closets in Ledonsk look more like aquaria of floating scales than wardrobes.

c. Diet  
Breakfast: vent-water thickened with rust-rice and lichen paste.  
Midshift: a strip of Glasswing jerky (iridescent like petrol film) and a thumb of fermented vapor yeast called fizz-brick.  
Feast days: Gargoyle pellet stew—no actual predator meat, only the bits it found least digestible, simmered until the pumice softens into gnocchi that crunches like sugar glass.

d. Speech & Story  
Tongues are used mostly indoors, for lullabies and oaths whispered against an ear. Public life is semaphore, mirror-flash, and ground-tap. Courting couples sit back to back and talk by drumming heels on the bench: intimacy measured in wavelengths of stone. Written language is columnar; scrolls hang from ceilings so that a reader begins at the top and walks slowly downward, letting the text pass the eyes while the head stays still—another concession to silence.

e. Work Guilds  
• The Veilers: maintain condensation towers, climb with copper wool pads to polish the ribs so nightfall temperature drop is maximized.  
• Saltwrights: score the basins so that salt crescents break clean; patterns are proprietary, sold like loom cards.  
• Whisperwrights: engineers of vibration; tune stamp-mills so the sub-bass hums stay below Gargoyle notice yet above human perception, a narrow acoustic knife-edge.  
• Gleaners: range the plateau after storms, herd the stranded eels into wicker sleighs, and slide them across wet pumice like curling stones toward town.

f. Rituals  
The most solemn is the Stay of the Third Fill. Once a season, when a cluster’s cistern reaches exactly one-third capacity, all work halts. Residents line the ledges, mirrors angled sunward, and for a single silent hour turn the plateau into a dazzle of heliographs—the sky answering itself. Outsiders think it worship; natives call it debt-payment to the glare that keeps them alive.

6. Symbioses & Tensions

Humans keep chime crabs in low troughs under their homes. The crabs chew waste pumice and drop pearls of phosphate that fertilize trays of micro-algae (the nearest thing the city has to a garden). In turn, the steady tremor of domestic crab colonies masks the smaller footfalls of children from prowling Gargoyles.

Glasswings are not hunted with nets—too noisy—but with radiative decoys: a copper vane heated then quenched to emit a spectral line the flyers see as water-rich. When a flock stoops, smartglass louvers snap shut, trapping a few birds by brute daylight removed. The kill is bloodless; the silence is kept.

7. The Almost-People

Miners on the plateau’s shadow side tell of Shiverers: biped forms whose flesh ripples like heat haze. They stand beside the vents only on the coldest nights, arms raised as if warming invisible hands. No tracks remain come morning. Scholars suggest refracted moonlight conjures mirages atop rising steam; Gargoyle scat has yielded phalange bones, however, slender and unmistakably humanoid. The city files the matter under necessary mysteries.

8. Visitors from Below

Caravans ascend through the storm gaps, bringing bitumen bricks, resin, rare cloth. They arrive hoarse; lowland lungs bleed a little in the height-thin air. For every five who come, one chooses not to descend again. The plateau exacts loyalty through clarity—once you have seen a night where even silence glows, cloud-shadow life feels muddy.

9. In Sum

What lives here is anything that can make a bargain with light:

• Films that trade pigment for energy.  
• Eels that trade teeth for iron.  
• Crabs that trade percussion for peace.  
• Flyers that trade transparency for flight.  
• Predators that trade motionlessness for power.  
• People who trade every surplus sound for another hour of water.

Each body, from microbe to municipality, is a lens—bending glare, channeling heat, focusing silence. Taken together, the plateau’s denizens are not a food web so much as a circuitry of brightness, conducting the sun’s excess down through flesh and stone until, drop by patient drop, it becomes drinkable night.
