Those who have chosen (or been chosen) to live in the High Ridge Forge are few, but the basin is never empty. Life here presents itself in three overlapping registers—Human, More-than-Human, and Not-Yet-Human—each shaping the others until the borders blur.

1. The Human Residents  

   1.1  Bodies  
   •  Fifty-four people at last counting: thirty adults, nineteen children, five crones and graybeards.  
   •  Altitude has lightened them. They run narrow but deep-lunged, the front of the ribcage flaring like a bellows to comb thin air for oxygen.  
   •  Skin goes pearl-matte within a season; the mist deposits a film of microscopic silica that dull-shines like nacre. Hair bleaches, too—not white but frost-green, the color of wet willow bark.  
   •  They smell faintly sweet, the way a cut stem does. That scent comes from a sap-based varnish they rub in every morning to keep chill off and fungus out.  

   1.2  Dress & Tools  
   •  Tunics of braided cloud-willow cambium; when fresh they look like hammered copper, when worn they soften to suede.  
   •  Sleeves stop short so hands stay free for grafting. Forearms are cross-hatched with tiny scars: proof of apprenticeship, for each cut records a lesson in how living wood mends.  
   •  No metal, never. Blades are slivered basalt hafted to bone. Every edge is replaced weekly; the Forge Place provides endless flakes if you know where to pry.  
   •  Each adult also carries a tuning thigh-bow: a thin hoop of willow strung with gut. They pluck it against the basin’s echo to test humidity before beginning any construction.

   1.3  Kinship & Dwelling  
   •  They group by Lattice, not by blood. A Lattice is a block of three to seven huts whose walls share living grafts; members pool food, stories and sleep. A child belongs to every adult whose roof roots into the same braid.  
   •  Names are granted by sound. A newborn is carried to the rim; the first note the wind-organ sings after the child cries becomes the infant’s core syllable. Years later additional sounds accrete—one for a notable mistake, one for the day of choosing a craft—so the oldest carry hymn-long names.  
   •  They regard themselves as nursemaids to the landscape. “We are here to keep the stone company while it thickens,” Grandmother Te’in likes to say. No one imagines dying here; when the basin hardens and the rain loop falters, they will move on.

   1.4  Work & Rhythm  
   Dawn: cup the rills—people stretch skins over carved gutters and coax drinking water before the sun births too much vapor.  
   Midday: willow tending—every shoot is finger-bent, grafted, sung to. They shape cradles straight onto branches so that when a child outgrows it the cradle is severed alive and planted, taking root again elsewhere.  
   Dusk: story-laying in the empty center. No fire; the glowing vent provides light. Speakers circle the heat cracks, reciting into fissures so the mountain “learns their memory” and won’t forget them when they leave.  
   Night: silence until the deep organ note. At that subsonic breath, everyone kneels, palms on earth, eyes closed. Infants are pressed belly-down so they feel the ground’s pulse before they learn words.

2. The More-than-Human Residents  

   2.1  Cloud-Willow  
   •  Neither shrub nor giant, it rides the liminal. A tree that never thickens past a wrist but never dies back either; rings form as spirals visible through the translucent bark, so years glow like lantern glass.  
   •  The settlers claim willow listens. Scientific visitors once strapped seismographs to branches and indeed found the trunks “answering” the vent’s surges with faint torsional twitches that map to the same periodicity as the Forge-pulse.  
   •  Leaves secrete an enzyme that slows silica precipitation; moss beneath a willow remains velvet while exposed rock vitrifies. Thus the tree mothers the very soil it someday hopes for.

   2.2  The Forge Mites  
   •  Invisible until dusk, when the vent’s red glare backlights them. Thousands of pin-bright motes whirl above the crack like iron filings over a magnet.  
   •  They are extremophile spiders no larger than a comma, spinning webs of spun glass. Threads flash, melt, and flash again in a never-ending loop: build, fuse, build.  
   •  Children harvest cooled fragments—fused glass lace—and trade them to travelers for stories of the lowlands.

   2.3  Dew-Eaters  
   •  A fungus that masquerades as breath. Look across the bowl at first light and you’ll see what seems a person exhaling; step closer and it collapses into a swirl of spores.  
   •  It drinks condensed mist straight from the air, leaving surrounding leaves perfectly dry. Settlers sweep it with goose-wing brushes off paths they want to stay slick.

   2.4  Rim Voices  
   •  They are not spirits in local belief but “Unborn Rivers.” Every portal in the granite wall is the mouth of a watercourse that will emerge once erosion commits. For now it can only speak in wind.  
   •  On solstice nights the people sing counter-melodies into those holes, teaching the future rivers songs to remember them by.

3. The Not-Yet-Human Residents  

   3.1  Stone in First Skin  
   •  Raw lava that forgot to run. Certain boulders hum faintly warm and give under a thumbprint like stale wax. The settlers warn children never to sit long on such stones; “They are still deciding which way is down.”  
   •  When a rock slumps, everyone gathers and names the newborn shape—Calf, Shoulder, Sickle—then leaves it alone for one year. Only after it keeps the same form through four full seasons may it be walked on.

   3.2  Firelings  
   •  Pale salamander silhouettes flicker inside cracks, never quite emerging. They are illusions—heat shimmers catching the eye—but the people claim one day the mountain will let them step free, and that will be the sign it no longer needs human company.

   3.3  The Future Creek  
   •  Not yet a line of water, merely a tendency. A place on the bowl’s floor where pebbles roll fractionally farther after each rain-pulse.  
   •  Children sprinkle dyed sand there at New Year; the next dawn they measure how far the grains have crept. When that distance equals the length of a grown forearm, the elders have vowed to begin packing.

4. Portraits (so you might meet a few)  

   •  Wren-On-The-Third-Chord: apprentice sound-tester. Can whistle the full rim scale and identify which pore needs reaming when a note sours. Keeps a basalt awl tucked behind one ear.  
   •  Gray Kadal: once a bronze-smith in the valleys below, now keeper of the rule that forbids metal. Wears the last relic—a finger-wide ring of green corroded copper—on a thong around the neck as cautionary tale.  
   •  The Lichen Twins, Hael & Sael: identical ten-year-olds whose job is to follow cloud-willow roots and pinch off any shoot that tries to grow outside the permitted spiral lanes. They move so silently newcomers think them spirits.  
   •  Grandmother Te’in-Who-Remembers-Steam: oldest in the basin, joints swollen with silica deposits. Sleeps directly above a warm vent fissure “so my bones and the mountain can knit at the same pace.”

5. What It Feels Like To Stay A While  

   The longer you remain, the harder it becomes to mark where your breath ends and the basin’s breath begins. You start counting time by pulse—vent, organ, drain—and dreaming in the clicks of expanding crystals. Clothes mildew if you use cloth; you learn to oil them with willow sap instead. You adopt the local habit of tasting rock dust for its warmth before trusting it with your weight.  

   After a month you may find your voice shifting pitch to dodge the dominant frequencies of the organ so conversation doesn’t vanish into resonance. After a year you know the rim’s every aperture by timbre, and you kneel at the deep note without thinking.

6. Leaving  

   No one born at High Ridge Forge has yet reached thirty. When the first generation approaches middle age the plan, spoken or not, is to fold the huts inward, sever the living walls, and guide each willow into a self-supporting arch. The emptied lattice will keep growing, forming something between grove and ruin. The people will descend carrying only tools of stone and baskets of glass lace, searching the ranges for another newborn patch of crust that needs company.

   They say the mountain will finish itself more gracefully for having heard laughter while it was young. Whether that is true no outsider can prove; but long after the settlers depart the cloud-willows will stand, thin wrists crossed overhead, rattling their leaves like distant applause whenever the vent sighs.
