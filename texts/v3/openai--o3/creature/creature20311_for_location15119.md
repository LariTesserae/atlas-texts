Those who make their lives on the ridge call themselves, without ornament, the People of Cyclion. Stranger tongues sometimes turn that into Cyclenes or Cyclionites, but the folk themselves do not bother. They know how small the settlement is, how few throats there really are to name.

POPULATION AND APPEARANCE  
• Permanent residents: roughly 280 souls in the long count, seldom more than 300, never fewer than 250.  
• Bodies: spare, tendon-taut, the muscle of distance walkers rather than wrestlers. Centuries of thin air have lengthened ribcages and taught lungs to drink slowly.  
• Skin tones: the full valley range—umber to shale-pale—yet all are filmed by the same faint sheen of slate dust that rises whenever a chisel works. From a distance every resident looks faintly blue, as if the stone has adopted them.  
• Hair is kept low, braided against the wind. Children wear a single loose loop until their seventh mist season, when they cut it and tuck the coil behind a wall stone; adults never touch it again. It is said the plateau remembers each child by that hidden twist of keratin.

DOMESTIC LIFE  
A household is three structures:  
1. The Sleephouse—windowless, wool-bedded, quiet enough that the drop-drip of the roof nets can be counted.  
2. The Workledge—really just a covered bench built outward from the wall so tools may hang in the cloud-air and be scoured clean by night moisture.  
3. The Firecourt—an open square of flagstones ringed by clay braziers, where meals and talk and all disputes occur. Any person may step into another family’s firecourt; none may step into another family’s sleephouse uninvited.

FOOD AND WATER  
• Water: fog-caught, equal-shared. Two stewards per cistern taste, test, and recite purity oaths at first light.  
• Staples: egg, juniper-smoked chicken, strips of birch bark rendered soft in lye and pan-fried to make a thin, crackling bread. Once a moon a mule train arrives from the valleys with oat and rye; stone carvings and coil baskets go back in trade.  
• The rare white goat is never hunted. To see it is regarded as a sign the plateau’s long negotiation with gravity continues.

WORK AND CRAFTS  
1. Stone-Joiners—five extended families who can true-align a block by eye alone. Their emblem is a slim wedge of bronze worn on the thumb.  
2. Netwrights—linen weavers and rope-lashers. They treat cloth like another living skin of the settlement, mending each tear before dawn dries it.  
3. Shadow-Readers—keepers of the plaza’s standing stone. They mark the drift of noon through the year; from their quiet calculations come planting times in the valleys below.  
4. Quiet-Guards—one in twenty residents serves a five-year turn. They carry no weapon beyond a soft cedar baton used to tap warning on stone. Their single duty: protect the silence when it is needed and break it when danger approaches.

LANGUAGE AND VOICE  
Every sentence in the local tongue ends on a downward breath, as though to lay the words gently on the rock. Shouting is so rare it has a verb of its own—“to cliff.” Outsiders who raise their voices are met with puzzled courtesy and, if the volume persists, with carefully polite requests to “let the stone speak instead.”

CYCLES AND CEREMONIES  
Morning: The First Beading. Families fan out to their roofs, touch finger to the night’s gathered droplets, and mark one silver point on the brow.  
Midyear Night: The Still Festival. All braziers are banked low; the bull-horn flutes drone until the air seems not to move. Elders tell of the one storm that shattered the ridge in elder-elder days and of the fog that mended it again.  
Every Fortieth Season: Selection Day. No speeches. Candidates for council simply stand in the plaza when the sun-shaft strikes. Whoever remains unmoved by full noon’s light—no shifting foot, no trembling hand—earns a seat.

CHILDHOOD  
Children in Cyclion are granted a measure of ownership over time itself. Between their tenth and twentieth winters they each choose one stone seam somewhere on the plateau and spend a decade carving an unbroken groove along it, a slow, private calendar no other hand may touch. Visitors often walk right over these hairline incisions, unaware they are crossing the lives of growing minds.

THE OTHER INHABITANTS  

Ravens  
Not pets, not pests—something akin to colleagues. Each Workledge keeps a shallow dish of snail meat; the birds repay with vigilance, rasping whenever a stranger tops the mule path. Shadow-Readers claim the ravens measure time too, but in wind rather than light.

The Fog  
Locals speak to it in second person singular, “you,” never “it.” A youngster scolded for carelessness may hear, “The fog heard you crash the ladder.” No one believes the vapor listens, yet everyone behaves as if it might.

Slow Lichen  
A turquoise crust that writes its own pale letters on the schist, visible only after a century has passed. The Stone-Joiners read these lichened scripts—patterns of growth, of crumble—and choose quarry faces accordingly. “The lichen draws our blueprints,” they say.

Memory Goats  
Not flesh and bone, but a collective hallucination (or so valley traders insist). Every reported sighting happens at twilight, always a single white silhouette, always stepping away just before one can be quite certain. The children keep ledgers of sightings; when two accounts converge within a handspan of cliff, that place is declared under the goat’s guardianship and no stone is ever cut there again.

THE FEELING OF THEM  
Stand among Cyclion’s people and you notice how rarely eyes move. Gaze lingers: on a drop of water forming, on a rivulet finding its path, on a raven folding one ink-black wing. The inhabitants carry the ridge’s deliberation inside their chests. They are not slow; they are simply paced to stone, aligned to fog, convinced that anything worth having will arrive if given the correct pause.

And if you stay long enough—past the restless week of most visitors—you may wake one morning to find your own breath quieter, your pulse spacing itself between distant drips, and the stone beneath your feet feeling less like ground and more like an old, blue friend that has finally decided to speak your name.
