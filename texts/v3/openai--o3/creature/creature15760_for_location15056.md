Who or what still claims the drowned hall changes with the depth of your gaze. Seen from a distance it is “abandoned,” a damp shell busy only with vermin and echoes; linger for an hour and an entire stacked society reveals itself, each tier tuned to a different niche of stone, water, and darkness. They overlap like reef-life on a single head of coral, all of them quietly converting the hall’s slow breath into food, shelter, or ritual.

1. The Rope-Setters (humans, or near enough)

Appearance and bearing:
• Stature: short, stooped people—partly hereditary, partly the result of early childhood spent under the sagging roof ribs.  
• Skin: silt-grey to river-brown, stippled with pale freckles where lime efflorescence repeatedly kissed their flesh.  
• Dress: waxed reed cloaks, gut-rope belts braided from clatterbat intestines, and—always—woven rush slippers that leave no hard heel-strike on the flagstones.  

Numbers and dwellings:
Thirty-odd individuals at any time, split into six lineages called Cords. Each Cord owns a “loft”: a swaying, basket-like dormitory hung between rafters just below the reed lids. The lofts are lined with lantern-slug mucus to repel damp and to glow faintly in the long night.

Work:
a. Setting ropes and wooden “butterflies” to keep the walls from folding.  
b. Weaving fresh roof-lids (a summer-long project that begins with cutting reeds upriver, rafting them down, and softening them in sump water overnight).  
c. Herding gutter eels into clay raceways, then harvesting their transparent eggs for protein.  
d. Retuning the hall: once each new moon they walk the circumference, tapping stones with crab-shell hammers; any note that rings dull tells them which patch of masonry must be eased, wedged, or relieved of weight before the next barometric in-breath.

Language and etiquette:
They speak quietly and in two registers:  
• Voice-above-mist, a whispered vernacular for daily use.  
• Voice-under-stone, a percussive code of foot taps and hand-drums that travels through the flagstones without waking the shaft.  

Only children may shout across open space; adults fear the Rule of the Parapet too much to risk it.

Beliefs:
The hall itself is not their god but their bargain. “We will keep you upright,” say the Rope-Setters, “and in return you will keep us small, many, and remembered.” When a member dies, a clay tablet bearing their name and the shape of their left hand is mortared into the inner face of the wall, adding literal weight to the pact.

2. The Lantern Slugs (Macroaerium viridans)

More than mere lighting. Each slug is a miniature refinery pooling copper salts and body-made peroxides inside the flank pouch that glows green. Rope-Setters groom them by wiping condensation off their mantles with tufts of moss; in exchange the slugs remain on the sump parapet instead of wandering up into lofts where their antiseptic mucus would kill carefully cultured food moulds.

Breeding:
Once yearly the whole colony descends the shaft in a tenuous green spiral, leaving the hall rimmed in darkness for three nights. During that time no repairs are attempted and even the crabs go still, as if the bones of the building temporarily loosen.

3. Basin Crabs (Geraeus tessellatus)

They are civil engineers in crustacean form. A single crab pulls at a stone to steal warmth; three or more gather and the rhythm of their claw-strikes changes into a measured 5-beat pattern that calls others. At a quorum of thirteen they become a “gang” whose sole purpose is hydraulic: they loosen certain flagstones so water finds new paths to gutters, preventing surface flooding when the sump is high.

The Rope-Setters never interfere unless a gang removes three stones in one night. Then the omen is heeded, a crab is harvested, and its shell carved into the wind whistle said to keep the hall breathable.

4. Clatterbats (Myotis coriaceus)

Dusk eruption is only half their story. At midday hundreds roost belly-to-belly in cells behind the wall slits, wings wrapped in communal muffling that makes the hall unnervingly quiet for one hour—the “Null Hour.” Rope-Setter children use that lull to creep across the floor and trade eel eggs for half-digested night-moths, a delicacy eaten warm.

A single white morph is born roughly once a decade. It is not allowed to fly; instead its guano is collected and dried as pigment for the clay death tablets.

5. Gutter Eels (Anguilla filiformis)

Transparent, almost theoretical, yet absolutely necessary: their wriggling keeps the gutters scoured of algal slime. When the sump sinks they slip into the shaft and digest silt in darkness, returning bloated and opaque just before the full moon. Rope-Setters believe the eels bring bottom-water omens up in their bellies; so they slit open a ceremonial few, reading the grain of digested sediment the way other cultures read livers.

6. The Echo—also called The Delayed Singer

No one has seen it; all have heard it. Any sound launched down the shaft returns distorted and slow, as if mouthed by something with too many throats. The Rope-Setters define adulthood by an ordeal called the One Answer: an initiate leans over the parapet, sings a single sustained note, and waits. If the echo comes back in the same key the bond is judged strong and the initiate is permitted to bear repairs unsupervised. If it returns bent or broken they are considered “Unsettled” and must wear a reed hood for a year, never meeting the shaft’s eye again.

Some claim the Echo is merely physics. Others point to the night every generation when the hall falls silent—no water music, no bat clicks—and from the shaft rises a chord no human throat could shape, shaking dust off rafters in a luminous sheet. Whatever it is, the Echo behaves like a resident power and is honored as such.

7. Strays and Passers-Through

The hall is porous. Things wander in.

• Mud-beards: itinerant peat-cutters who trade bundles of dry sphagnum for crab whistles that fetch a high price upriver. They are tolerated if they keep to the outer gutter and leave before Null Hour.  
• Sump Owls: great hollow-boned birds that arrive only when the water rises to one fathom below the rim. They pluck clatterbats from the air and leave wet pellets laced with silver filaments used by Rope-Setters as surgical thread.  
• Drift-scholars: once or twice a decade an academic in oilskin shows up to map the hall, talk of “pre-ceramic hydromancy,” and vanish again, usually unnerved by how the building answers in the dark.

Synergies and Conflicts

Nothing here kills simply to kill. Predation serves the architecture: bats thin insects that would eat reeds, crabs redirect water that would drown stones, eels clean the drains, slugs sterilize parapet mould. Even human funerary tablets thicken the leaning walls. Violence that offers no structural return—a drunken stranger kicking a slug, a scholar firing a pistol to test the echo—is met by a collective response: the crabs block their path, the bats riot in their ears, and the Rope-Setters wordlessly open the reed seams in the roof so rain drives the offender back the way they came.

In short

The half-submerged hall is alive in the literal sense—stone breathing through the gills of animals and people who long ago pledged themselves to its continuance. Stay a single evening and you might describe its inhabitants as pests, caretakers, or spirits, depending on which tier answered your lantern first. Stay a season and you will see the truth: every living thing inside is a moving part of one slow, deliberate machine whose only product is its own, ongoing survival.
