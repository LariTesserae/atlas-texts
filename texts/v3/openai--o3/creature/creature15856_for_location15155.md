Those who try to draw a bestiary of the High Flood learn quickly that the place decides what may be seen. Walk the same circuit twice and you will meet two very different populations, revealed or concealed by a hand-span of water level, a change in air pressure, a breath of lilac light. What follows, then, is not a census but a composite: the lives most often glimpsed, the lives most often suspected, and the lives that can only be guessed from what they leave behind.

----------------------------------------------------
1. First foundations – the makers of food
----------------------------------------------------
a. Cloud-film bacteria  
Thin sleets of airborne microbes knit themselves into a film on the under-bellies of clouds. They metabolise trace ozone and the minute voltages that flicker through the overcast; each droplet is a battery, each colony a living voltaic pile. When the film thickens enough, beads of surplus water fall—the lilac “pearls” that chime on stone. Everything larger ultimately eats the pearls or what has already eaten them.

b. Ferric sleet  
In the humid galleries rust condenses as a reddish snow: crystals no larger than flour grains. A second guild of nanoscopic bacteria veins that rust, extracting energy from the slow oxidation of drowned bronze machinework. Valleys that seem dusted with cinnamon are really pastures, rich as any grassland.

----------------------------------------------------
2. Small mechanics – those who mine, grind and polish
----------------------------------------------------
a. Glass crabs (already named, but here in detail)  
Fist-sized, hexapodal, jointed like watch hinges. Their “shell” is silica grown so pure it is invisible except where sintered sand smokers tattoo it with pale bands. Mouthparts rasp continuously, shaving stone for the micron-thick algal glaze that lies beneath. A moving colony sounds like distant wind-bells. Once a year they gather by the million at spill-weirs; the outward rush of water sends them tumbling into the inner sea, where they release their young to drift back upward on convection.

b. Vitreous mill-worms  
Foot-long loops of gelatinous muscle lined with corundum teeth. They inhabit the throats of abandoned pipe-organs, turning them into living querns that grind the ever-falling hail to silt. Their castings spatter the lower flumes as a slick grey paste later harvested by gutter mice.

c. Silica spiders  
Eight-legged capillaries of fused sand. At night they string water-threads across open culverts. The threads carry a static charge that stuns airborne plankton; come morning the spiders reel in a harvest beaded like quicksilver.

----------------------------------------------------
3. Runners, climbers and gleaners
----------------------------------------------------
a. Gutter mice  
Soft graphite fur; lungs that can shut like valves. When a flash flood comes they do not flee: they fold. Heartbeat falls to one every thirty seconds, body temperature drops to near freezing. Water can bury them for an hour before they unfold and scurry on, utterly dry beneath the guard-hairs.

b. Quartz hares  
Rare, heard more often than seen. They move with impossible sure-footedness on mirror-slick ledges, leaving hoof-prints that sparkle and then sublimate into vapour. Travellers swear the prints show eight toes, paired in mirror symmetry, as though two hares were running in opposed directions but sharing the same feet.

----------------------------------------------------
4. Swimmers and divers
----------------------------------------------------
a. Valve-swans  
Flattened bills edged like squeegees skim microbial scum. The birds’ bones are perforated with spiracles; when pressure drops before a storm, air whooshes through them and the whole flock moans in an eerie minor chord that echoes for kilometres.

b. Rift eels  
Ribbon bodies plated with chrome scales. The symbiotic lights riding them are themselves predators: a curtain of coin-sized medusae that eat any fry drawn close enough by the lure. An adult eel is a grudging partner in its own illumination, but the light is also a shield—nothing else in the trenches can bear it.

c. Basin rays  
Disc-shaped swimmers whose dorsal skin matches the stone exactly. They cruise shallow cisterns a handspan below the surface, filtering ferric sleet. When a basin dries, the rays “flip” into the air, skin stiffening into a temporary kite that glides them to the next water sheet. From far off they can be mistaken for grey leaves—but no leaf ever hums like tensioned metal.

----------------------------------------------------
5. Flyers, drifters, hunters of air
----------------------------------------------------
a. Echo bats  
Cartilage webbing laced with frost. Their sonar is almost visible: shock-fronts of condensed fog ripple out with each cry. A single collapse in the distance can rouse a million of them; the cloud they make blots half a horizon before it thins to nothing.

b. Cirrus kites  
Not animals in the usual sense but colonial jellyfish that have taken to the sky. Fronds trail downward into the updrafts rising from sun-warmed spillways; gas-filled floats loft them so high that only their shadows cross the Flood, mottling the stone like leaves under water. When a kite dies its floats burst in slow succession, a string of white puffs many travellers have mistaken for signal smoke.

c. Drizzle mantas  
Translucent petals a metre wide drift in the ever-falling beads. Each petal is a single huge cell that inflates with absorbed impact energy; when saturated it abruptly contracts, flinging the manta forward in a skipping surge. Where a brood passes it sounds like applause fading into distance.

----------------------------------------------------
6. The larger predators
----------------------------------------------------
a. Shatter herons  
Vertical creatures: all mid-leg and neck. They hunt where waterfalls behave as hovering curtains. A heron will stand half concealed inside the water-sheet, the impact masking its own vibration. At the tremor of an approaching shoal of basin rays, the bird jabs through stone-smooth water and withdraws a struggling disc. Their beaks are honed by the very currents they fish: serrated, glass-bright, always regrowing.

b. Onyx wolves  
Only encountered during the ten-day continent-storm. Pack hunters that appear as silhouettes in the violet rain, fur black with an oily mirror sheen. Their paws are webbed, their lungs unusually huge for mammals. After the storm they seem to melt away; carcasses are never found. Some say the wolves are valve-swans gone feral, others that they are illusions born of fatigue and low oxygen. Their howls, however—rolling thunder even on windless nights—are recorded on every barometer brought here.

c. Pearl worms  
More scavenger than hunter but lethal in numbers. Bone-white annelids that digest keratin, chitin and calcium, leaving only nacreous replicas. A campsite abandoned for an hour will be returned to find every boot-lace, knuckle-bone and fingernail replaced by ghostly shell.

----------------------------------------------------
7. Ephemeral intelligences
----------------------------------------------------
a. Spray elementals (so named by desperate scholars)  
Occasional knots in a falling sheet of water behave as if alive: twisting to regard passers-by, dividing like cells, rejoining in braided helixes. Instruments detect momentary electromagnetic signatures of astonishing complexity—briefly as intricate as thought, then gone. No two encounters are alike. Some travellers insist these knots reposition fallen masonry, repairing sluice edges overnight. Others describe being shepherded away from unstable arches. If a mind is present, it is as transient as a splash.

b. The Lurian Cartographers  
Never seen directly. One finds their work instead: maps etched into flood-polished stone with a line as fine as hair, always current to within a day of observation. The glyphs are abstract—arcs, dots, pressure contours—not language in any basin tongue. Yet follow them and you arrive safely at still water, oxygen pockets, resonant shelters where collapses can be heard minutes in advance. Whether the cartographers are humanoid, mechanical, or mineral remains unresolved. Their etchings stop wherever visitors begin to copy them, as though privacy were a condition of their continued help.

----------------------------------------------------
8. Travellers, exiles, researchers
----------------------------------------------------
Finally, the Flood hosts the briefly resident: people for whom danger, solitude or curiosity is reason enough to stay.

1. Flood-runners – fur-clad couriers who cross the plateau between scattered mountain ports. They time their dashes to the ten-day rhythm, using the onset of the grand storm to raft new channels that will not exist a day later.  
2. Skirl-monks – anchorites who believe enlightenment lies in listening to perfect silence. They occupy high acoustic nodes, sealing every aperture with wax save a single slit that admits the world’s voice. Few last a full winter; the thin air sings hallucinations into the blood.  
3. Salvagers – mercenary teams in rubberised exo-frames. They hunt intact bronze, not because it is valuable (it is not) but because it records the faint magnetic residue of whatever power once flowed through the machines. To corporate patrons far away, that residue is priceless. To the salvagers it is also lethal: the frames have to be bled of charge every night lest arcs jump flesh to metal.

In time even the hardiest human leaves, or stays forever in the pearl-white reliquary of the worms. No settlement endures. The Flood keeps people moving, polishing them down to travellers’ tales as surely as it rounds a razor-bright facet into dull stone.

----------------------------------------------------
Closing note
----------------------------------------------------
Strip away the miracle architecture, the impossible hydraulics, and the High Flood is still an ecosystem—one whose currency is not chlorophyll but fall, flow, and abrasion. Life here is the art of making use of motion: drinking it, riding it, hiding inside it, and, when the sky opens in its ten-day heartbeat, singing to it with a million echoing throats. Anyone wishing to understand the inhabitants must begin there, with the fact that nothing living on the Flood treats water as backdrop. Water is skeleton, synonym, first language. Everything else is ornament on the surge.
