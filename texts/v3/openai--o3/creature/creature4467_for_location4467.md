Who lives here?  

Begin with the things so small they are almost not things at all, only flavors on the air and textures underfoot.

1. The kiln-sweat.  
   • A fog of thermophilic bacteria drifts invisible except when a vent coughs back-light through it.  
   • It eats the vapor that hangs off every hot surface, strings the excess into glassy filaments you find later like spider silk caught on a valve.  
   • It dies if the temperature drops below body-warm. No one has ever seen it dead here.

2. Basalt blood.  
   • In the pores of the cavern walls lives a crimson archaea that looks, under lens, like rust given organs.  
   • It is the reason the seep-water comes out tinted, why the rat fur by the mezzanine shines auburn, why the pipe eels’ bellies glow when they knot around impellers.  
   • It photosynthesizes not with light but with heat gradients: a slow, mineral hunger.

Pull your vision back to things that have spines, shells, grievances.

3. Furnace swifts.  
   • Wingspan: the breadth of a man’s hand.  
   • Colour: the soot that gathers on welding masks.  
   • They hatch behind loose plates in the roof ribbing, gluing hair-thin feathers together with mineral spit.  
   • Their throats contain stones; they rattle like pepper mills in flight, a sonar that lets them skim pond surfaces without ever touching the steam.  
   • Diet cycle: gnats at dawn-shift, condenser fleas at pressure-release, their own fallen siblings if the algae bloom fails.  
   • Migrations last the length of a maintenance calendar nobody updates anymore. They return when the sodium bulbs begin to flicker; the birds think that tremor is spring.

4. Pipe eels.  
   • Blind, rope-thin, skin slick with a natural grease that smells of graphite and lemon.  
   • They eat lubrication—literally strip it from gearbox teeth—then excrete a cleaner variant that keeps the conveyors from seizing. The machines keep breathing because the eels are hungry, and the eels survive because the machines still dream of turning.  
   • Courtship: two eels braid, then press their flanks against a copper surface; the metal draws out an electrolytic shimmer that becomes a clutch of eggs the colour of old pennies.  
   • Predators: none. The rats tried once, discovered the grease makes the throat forget water, and never tried again.

5. Chitin lobsters.  
   • Scuttling coins of onyx with jointed fans for tails.  
   • They eat the grey algae film and, in doing so, ink the water with powdered basalt they mill in their gullets. Ponds where lobsters graze turn darker, cooler, a living thermostat.  
   • When a lobster dies it folds its body like a broken ruler, sealing inside a bubble of methane. Corpses float up and stick to the cavern roof, where swifts tear them open for calcium.

6. Heat rats.  
   • Larger than surface cousins, fur slicked flat by natural oils.  
   • They have learned to drink condensation straight from insulated cable jackets, puncturing them with incisors honed on micaceous grit.  
   • Dominance battles drum the mezzanine girders: a percussion that serves as territory map for every other creature in the cavern.  
   • Their whiskers are silvered by mineral deposits; in half-light they look already ghosted.

7. The mezzanine bloom.  
   • Not an animal, but a moving city of fungal threads grown up through the bunks the workforce abandoned.  
   • It breathes out alcohol sweet as bruised pears. Swifts grow intoxicated and tumble; rats harvest the stumbling birds. Circle closes.

Shift again, to presences that will not hold still under a microscope.

8. The Chain Choir.  
   • Those blanket murmurs are not mere echoes. A species of resonant mite lives in steel links and roller tracks, boring pinholes that tune each segment like a flute.  
   • When the conveyor shifts in its sleep the mites resonate, a massed organ voice that sounds uncannily intentional. Workers once blamed bad bearings; the bearings were perfect.

9. Gutter saints.  
   • Once or twice a season a human comes down: surveyor, thief, pilgrim of rust. They stay just long enough for the place to mark them with ochre sweat and heat rash hieroglyphs.  
   • The rats remember faces. They have a vocabulary of squeals reserved only for bipeds.  
   • Every human who leaves takes spores in boot treads, grease on cuffs, swifts’ stone-rattle lodged in memory. They carry the cavern out into cooler suns, inadvertent missionaries of warmth and slowness.

10. The unfinished city itself.  
    • Steel softening a micron each month, power cables growing reborn skins of vine-like oxidation, catwalks singing under burden no foot places on them.  
    • The machinery is not dead; it is larval. The eels oil it, the mites tune it, the algae cushions its joints. One day the press will close its jaws for the first and final time, forging something no blueprint ever asked for. Whatever steps out of that die will call this place mother.

And finally, what does not live but nonetheless abides:

11. Heat.  
    • It is the lung the whole cavern shares—drawn from a magma vein somewhere deeper than the lift dares go.  
    • It animates every throat, every pressure-release sigh, every slow chemistry.  
    • If one could kill the heat, the life here would not die; it would fossilize in motion, a cathedral of stillness. But the heat is not ready to be a statue.

In total: a microbiome that paints the air, a guild of scavengers that service dreaming machines, predators measured in decibels instead of teeth, and a climate that wears the crown of a living monarch. The cavern is less an ecosystem than an orchestra tuned to a single, patient note of warmth, playing forever to an audience mostly made of itself.
