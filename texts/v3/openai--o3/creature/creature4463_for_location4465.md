Who and what call the basin home

1. The Humans (though they seldom name themselves so plainly)

a. Tower-keepers  
They climb the fluted chimneys before dawn, sleeves rolled against the stippled cold, to polish the cooling coils and tune the louvers for the day’s weather. Each keeper knows “their” tower the way a violinist knows one instrument: where the frail rivet is, which register of wind makes a sweetness instead of a rasp, how much tilt the flywheels can bear before the dripline wavers. Apprentices begin at ten years of age by spending a whole season listening—no wrenches, no gauges, just ears—because the Works insists that sound is its first diagnostic. Old keepers retire only when their balance fails; then they serve on the ground as Ear-readers, interpreting the great hissed chord of the Works for those still agile enough to climb.

b. Aqueducters  
Stooped figures in oil-black cloaks who walk the trelliswork of timber channels, feeling for tremor and seep. They carry a single brass nail and a short mallet: if a leak appears they patch with the nail—never more than one per day, by custom—so the wood learns to swell shut in its own time. An Aqueducter can recite the day’s allotment of water to the litre for every garden bed and cistern; they speak of flow the way a banker speaks of coin, except no debt is permitted. Excess from any line becomes “gift water,” signed for in chalk and dumped into the alder groves where otters tumble like laundry in a rinse cycle.

c. Lath-wrights  
Craftspeople who turn the basin’s perpetual damp into timber that will not rot. They pressure-bathe hemlock planks with tinctures distilled from moss acids and frog enzymes, then season the boards inside the warm throats of spent condensation towers. A fresh-cut board clicks when flexed; a seasoned one sings like glass. Every dwelling and catwalk plank within the Works has passed through a Lath-wright’s hands; owners often inscribe their family names on the underside, knowing the board will outlast three, maybe four generations.

d. Echo-chartists  
A calling equal parts surveyor, musician, and gossip broker. Because the basin’s magnetized basalt jams compasses and the air funnels sound along erratic paths, Echo-chartists map the settlement by acoustics alone. They loose a short whistle, note how it ricochets, then sketch the route on vellum that never fully dries. Shift bells, children’s songs, frog choirs—all are plotted. Their rolled charts hang in every dormitory and workshop, updated weekly, so newcomers can learn the invisible avenues of voice. Lovers exploit the quiet pockets; conspirators, the loud corridors that swallow whispers whole.

e. Stillmasters  
When the towers first condensed more water than the gardens could drink, someone wondered what to do with surplus purity. The answer was spirits: slow-dripped, copper-coiled, rime-cold. Stillmasters float a single distillery car on metre-gauge track; it creeps from ridge to ridge, parking beside each tower in turn. They coax vapor through coils already chilled for condensation, bleeding off a trickle of clear liquor called Basin Breath. Imbibed lightly, it sharpens hearing—perhaps only suggestion, perhaps a trace of moss-lume alkaloids caught in the mash—but no council meeting convenes without one thimble per member.

2. The Non-human Regulars

a. Blue-vein frogs  
Palm-sized, skin translucent as onion peel, every vein glowing cobalt where it rests beneath luminous moss. They lay their eggs in the basalt’s hairline cracks; at night, egg-strings fluoresce like beadwork draped through stone. Children are paid a crumb of sweet sap for each frog mindfully relocated when repairs must be done, so stepping outside during school recess becomes an amphibian hailstorm of tiny bodies passed hand to hand.

b. Pipe bats  
Pallid, almost opalescent, with ears that meet over the skull like clasped prayer-hands. They roost inside warm exhaust flues and emerge in sheets at twilight. Keepers welcome them: a single bat colony halves the mosquito load. Once a year, when juveniles attempt their first flight, the Works silences every gong so echoes do not confuse the young. In the hush one hears the brittle rustle of thousands of soft wings testing room-temperature air.

c. Three-spoke dragonflies  
Named for the trident shape of their rear wings. Metallic emerald bodies, fully fifteen centimetres long; they hover around tower vents to sip evaporated salts. Lath-wrights incorporate shed wing-membranes into varnish—the chitin makes surfaces bead water for decades. The insects are never netted alive; it is illegal to harry anything that guides rainfall back into sky.

d. Alder-root otters  
Shorter and paler than their river cousins, they tunnel through the peat to den beneath the irrigated alder groves. An otter burrow intersects root channels so precisely that gardeners swear the animals read pipe echoes the way Chartists read speech. Off-shift children unofficially track family lineages by ear: each otter clan has a distinct piping chatter, like a flute trying to remember frog song.

3. Things that may or may not be alive

a. The Fog-horner  
Some nights a single bellow rolls across the basin—deep enough the basalt itself seems to resonate—then silence returns. Search parties, years ago, traced the sound to an abandoned survey tripod whose legs had fused solid with rust. Nothing in or around it could make a tone. Nevertheless, whenever the air pressure tumbles and the delta wind turns seaward, the tripod moans one long note, as if remembering a ship that never existed.

b. Flux moss  
Not the gentle mint-green glow species but a cousin that colonises magnetite seams. At dawn, just as sun menaces the rim, these moss patches realign their leaflets with a faint click like fingernails drumming wood. Lath-wrights claim the moss is orienting to a magnetic field no needle can lock onto, charting where north will be rather than where it is.

c. The Whisper Reserves  
In three stone cisterns at the basin’s edge, rainwater is stored for drought. No pipes feed them; they fill only by direct skyfall. Anyone leaning over the lip during new moon swears they hear voices discussing tomorrow’s cloud cover. The speech is never distinct and never wrong. After each correct forecast, someone anonymously leaves a coil of fresh copper on the cistern rim—payment rendered for counsel received.

4. How inhabitants arrange themselves

a. Shifts and silence  
The Works runs on a two-shift cadence: Dawn and Dusk. Each lasts nine hours; the narrow overlap is called the Mixing, a single hour when both crews walk the catwalks together. No machinery changes hands without a recited inventory and a touch of palms—moisture exchanged to acknowledge shared custody of water itself. Once per lunation, a Day of Stillness halts every mechanism for twenty-four hours. People whisper, kettles simmer without whistles, and children learn that quiet is maintenance in its own right.

b. Hearths on stilts  
Families dwell in stilt-houses perched atop basalt knuckles between towers. Architecture respects height, not sprawl: ladders fold, terraces hinge upward in storm. Interiors glow amber from resin-treated lantern shades that never quite dry, giving every room the warm humidity of a forest hollow.

c. Instruction by osmosis  
Formal schooling ends at twelve; thereafter a youth shadows the craft that best matches their patience. No occupation forbids observation: a Stillmaster will allow a baker to lean against the boiler, provided flour dust is kept out of the condenser breath. Cross-pollination of trades is not courtesy; it is disaster insurance. Any craftsperson must be able to cradle the town through one full shift should illness sweep a guild.

d. Currency of rings  
Coin metals corrode; paper mildews. Instead, value circulates as interlocking iron rings no larger than thumbnails. A ring’s bore is stamped with the trade seal of its maker; giving one away means surrendering a favour owed. Nobody hoards rings: rust would claim them. Wealth is therefore unredeemed obligation, and the richest citizen is the one everybody keeps needing to ask.

5. What the Basin asks in return

The place is young rock pretending—slowly—to become soil. Its residents mirror that ambition: they do not seek permanence, only gradual belonging. Babies are christened not with water (too sacred) but with condensing mist skimmed from a tower’s skin at first light. When someone dies, their body is placed in a peat trough and seeded with alder; tools pass to the youngest apprentice, initials lightly filed away so the metal forgets prior ownership.

Visitors who try to hurry the Works—surveyors proposing faster pumps, traders offering anti-corrosion polymers—are received politely but housed in the outer hostels, where sleepless frogs and echo-tangled conversation nudge them back toward the tempo the basin prefers. Those who remain long enough find their speech slowing, their ears discerning a dozen shades of drip, their ambitions molding to the patience of basalt. Some leave; some stay and, in staying, dissolve into the colony mind of towers, moss, bats, and distant foghorn.

Here “living” is a spectrum: iron breathing steam, moss arranging by magnetism, people apprenticing themselves to water’s deliberate fall. Nothing stands absolutely still, but nothing rushes. The inhabitants—not merely the humans, but frogs, alder, smoke, even the spin of a lost compass needle—share one citizenship: allegiance to slow, echoing balance.
