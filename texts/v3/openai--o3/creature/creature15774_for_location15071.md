Who Lives on the Plateau of Gravenleaves

0. Why Nobody Recognizes Them at First
Every guidebook swears “no mammals reside” and “animals avoid the place,” so newcomers step onto the moss expecting abandonment. They mis-see what stands ten paces away because the plateau has edited the normal cues that announce a living body: silhouettes kink where gravity folds, breathing fog dissolves before it can outline lips, and footfalls are swallowed by the same sponge-rock that hollows under a hammer blow. The inhabitants do not hide; they are merely calibrated to a geometry the visitor’s eyes still refuse to solve. Stay two dawns and the people glide into focus the way a double image in an old stereoscope snaps into relief.

1. The People Proper—Homo Cantilever
a. Build and Biology
They are recognizably human from clavicle to kneecap, but everything that keeps a lowlander upright has been modified by one long conversation with curvature.

• Bones: light, spongy lattices impregnated with the same silica that stiffens the pillar-trees. (The locals joke they are “kiln-cured saplings with opinions.”)  
• Feet: hind-toes never fused; each foot ends in two broad digits that can pinch a moss carpet or clamp a bark beam like a wrench on pipe.  
• Lungs: four chambers, not two, split by mid-septa as thin as onion skin. Thin air delivers enough O₂ only if routed through a longer maze.  
• Balance Organs: a secondary vestibular pouch just behind each ear fills with resinous fluid they call pitchwine; it thickens or thins according to ambient distortion, letting them feel spatial slip before it is visible.

b. Coloration and Dress
Skin tones trend toward cool metals—zinc, pewter, polished dusk—because of nickel chelated in the blood. Garments are webbed silk from condensation spiders, layered in strips that can be detached if the wearer must thread a gap narrower than a shoulder-width. Nobody bothers with shoes unless traveling: the moss is a familiar mattress, and the basalt underneath reads like Braille to their soles.

2. Birth, Growth, and the Naming of a Person
Wombs carry to six months only. At that threshold a half-grown child, still eel-lithe and translucent, is transferred into a cradle-gourd: a hollow, glucose-rich swelling coaxed from a cantilever root. For three more months the infant floats in sap solution while the tree reads its endocrine chatter and seeds it with symbiotic yeasts. When the gourd browns and splits, two births are declared—child and companion-tree are now “twins” in legal record. A name therefore has two halves: the sapling half (thin, sung in whistle tones) and the spine half (thicker, consonant-heavy). Outsiders hear names like “Ælee / Brunt-Nine.”

3. Vocations—There Is No Word for “Farmer,” Only Different Kinds of Engineer
a. Strappers
Muscle and intuition. Strappers climb cantilever beams with belts of iron and hemp, buckling and unbuckling thousands of joints of track, pipe, or cable. They work by pattern memory: ninety-one shoulder movements to reseat a rail segment, seven clipped calls to coordinate a five-person tension reset. The Corrugation can shorten a beam in an afternoon; the strappers elongate or compress the hardware before a single rivet feels the strain.

b. Whisperwrights
Acoustic cartographers. They map sound-shifts through the city by shouting chord-series and noting what notes vanish. A new void in the scale means a fresh wrinkle in space: a corridor telescoped, or a factory floor rotated half a degree. Their field notes become the updated street atlas issued every six days.

c. Sap-Weighers
Part biologist, part stockbroker. They sample cambium pressure, forecast resin futures, and decide when to tap a vein for industrial sealant. Cantilevers cooperate but not selflessly; an over-tapped tree will loosen its counterweight roots in protest, threatening any mill it hosts. Thus the Sap-Weighers double as diplomats.

d. Anchorites
Monks of metrology who plant memory-nails and recite every step count ever recorded. They keep a sung archive: each historical surveyor’s route is a melody that encodes pace length, bearing, and the day’s shadow drift. If a visitor is lost, an Anchorite will hum three bars and the ground appears to shuffle, embarrassed, back into its documented position.

4. Social Geometry—Households Without Walls
Because visibility can lie, proximity is choosy. Most households establish “continuous adjacency” by tethering themselves—literally—with ivory-weave cords clipped to belt rings. A family walking to the hydro-loom moves like spelunkers on a rope: if the corridor kinks in transit, they discover the bend together. Private space is not indoors; it is achieved by untying and drifting thirty meters out onto the sound-damp moss where even words cannot reach unaided.

5. Diet—Photosynthesis by Proxy
Food arrives in four currencies:

• Dew-grain: starch nodules that form on leaf-veins after nights of heavy condensation. They taste of cucumber and ozone.  
• Basalt-lichen cakes: protein concentrated from the mosaic of moss species, bound with spider silk, toasted on steam vents.  
• Compressed fog: moisture captured in membranous “lungs,” drunk chilled; provides trace minerals leached from cloud dust.  
• Stranger’s Salt: the only true import—blocks of sodium chloride lugged up the switchbacks on visitor caravans. Locals prize it the way lowlanders prize wine. A pinch seasons three months of meals.

6. Senses and Communication
Sight betrays, so most discourse leans on rhythm and scent.

• Talking is half-spoken, half-drummed on the chest with open palms. Vibrations carry through rock even if sound waves don’t.  
• Courtship perfumes are brewed from cantilever blossom oil. To invite intimacy one dabs a single drop on the lower eyelid; when tears well (inevitable), the oil diffuses into a citrus-smoke halo sensed two paces away.  
• Written language grows: letters are etched into young fungus boards that bulk out with age, gradually enlarging the font so elders can read without bending.

7. Relationship with Non-People
True animals rarely stay, but three categories share the plateau:

a. Sap-Ticks: fist-sized, glassy arthropods that insert resonant proboscides into trees. Instead of drinking, they equalize pressure, preventing blowouts; the locals leave them undisturbed, considering them unpaid safety inspectors.

b. Wrench-Ghosts: not ghosts at all, but autonomous pockets of compressed air given coherent edge by Corrugation shear. They cling to metal tools, making them hum at middle C. Any tool that holds the note for more than a minute is marked sacred and never traded away.

c. Spectral Wyrms: children hunt their “footprints,” which manifest as perfectly dry lines on fresh dew. Scholars suspect they are shadows orphaned when a real creature long extinct fled the plateau, leaving its silhouettes behind.

8. Governance—The Ledger of Loads
Power belongs to whoever can calculate stress distributions. The Load-House meets under a braided dome of nine merged trees; its floor is a gigantic balance platform. Proposed projects—new rail arms, heavier mills—are argued by piling annotated ingots on strategic tiles. If the platform tilts beyond four degrees, the proposal is “unsound” by literal demonstration. The speaker whose load kept level becomes Chair until the next wobble, usually eight or nine days.

9. Religion, if the Word Applies
No gods, only trajectories. The people revere vectors—“those arrows you cannot step off once chosen.” Birth ceremonies carve the infant’s predicted life-arc into a bone baton. Upon death the baton is planted in a runoff channel; water swelling or shrinking the moss either lifts or lowers the stick. If it settles to the exact horizontal, the dead one is said to have matched their vector and is forgotten, their name released back to the lexicon. If it tilts, descendants adopt the uncompleted angle as a family quest.

10. Visitors—Handling the Outsider Paradox
The plateau trades resin-sealant, cantilever leaves milled into turbine vanes, condensation silk. In return come salts, copper ore, and legends. A visitor is loaned three aids:

1. A cord of adjacency—stays clipped to a local guide.  
2. A pitchwine eardrop—tempers the first vertigo storm.  
3. A hush-drum—tiny, worn on the sternum; amplifies one’s own heartbeat into tactile Morse so one never loses personal tempo.

Most leave after a week, complaining the city edits dreams: nights re-loop until you choose a different door in the sequence, then reality lets the calendar continue.

11. Life Cycle’s End—Becoming Infrastructure
After thirty to forty plateau years (hard to convert to lowland time), the resin in a person’s bones begins to floresce. Joints stiffen not from age but from elective metamorphosis. The elder chooses a spot where a beam needs another counterweight or where a newborn tree girder lacks mass. They lie down, drink a draught of concentrated pitchwine, and instruct a Sap-Weigher to tap their marrow. Within hours their skeleton fuses to the root-stone, fleshy parts are digested by colony fungi, and what remains is a living brace of phosphate-rich bone feeding minerals back to the cantilever system. Thus sidewalks occasionally reveal a ribcage arch or a humerus strut: you walk literally upon the civic ancestors.

12. Myths They Tell Themselves
• The Corrugation is their exhalation—proof that the plateau is alive and every distortion is a rib rising.  
• Beyond the fog skirts lies the Reverse City, identical in architecture but populated by the shadows that lag behind. Some nights an echoing rivet-gun reports from below; then citizens pause, counting heartbeats until the sound recedes, pretending they are not eavesdropping on their own mirror labor.  
• One day gravity will change its opinion and the whole bowl will float free. On that dawn the strappers will unbuckle every restraint at once, and the plateau will sail off seeking a larger sky to negotiate.

13. What a Patient Visitor Finally Sees
Stay long enough, and the initial loneliness blossoms into complexity: figures leaping between branch-avenues without a visible ladder, work songs pulsing through stone like throbbing veins, lamps sprouting lumen-petals at dusk, elders reclining against trees they will soon reinforce, infants asleep in amber gourds that glow as clouds scrape moonlight overhead. The city is not sparse; it is simply tuned to a bandwidth that takes days to enter. Once attuned, you realize Gravenleaves is crowded—just not with the sort of lives you expected when you first stepped onto that whispering moss.

Endnote for the Returning Traveler
When you leave, the adjunct vestibular sense you cultivated will fade, and distances will behave again. Yet on certain humid nights in the lowlands you may catch your shadow arriving late, panting, and for a blink you will hear a whale-beam groan across the measurable sky. That is only the plateau reminding you that space is negotiable and that somewhere above the fog a city of tensioned timber and patient engineers continues to adjust the angle of reality by fractions of a breath.
