Who—and what—inhabits the high country?  No single answer takes the whole plateau in its mouth.  Voices share it the way storm cells share the afternoon sky: distinct yet overlapping, sometimes fusing for a brilliant moment before wheeling apart again.  Begin with the forms easiest to see and move steadily down the scale until matter itself seems to quiver with occupancy.

1. The Plateau Folk  

1.1  Shape and metabolism  
• Height and build: attenuated, tendon-taut bodies shaped by one-third-pressure air; clavicles wide, ribcages fluted like bellows.  
• Blood chemistry: double‐stranded hemoglobin with embedded copper ions—an engineered throwback that binds oxygen fiercely yet dumps it fast under effort.  
• Skin: matte umber strewn with lenticular chrome patches—bio-nanite refraction plates that scatter ultraviolet.  In freeze nights the plates constrict, helping slow radiative heat loss.  
• Voice: the larynx has a second, fluttery set of membranous folds; in thin air they generate low-watt overtones that carry 500 m without amplification.  Gossip travels along these tremolo “skywhisper” channels even when the network drops.

1.2  Domestic rhythm  
Morning (–07 h to –02 h solar)  
   • People wake to “moisture hush,” the percussive rustle of condensation sloughing off tower skins.  Children take felt-lined scoops and harvest beadlets for breakfast tea before they drip away.  
   • Gale-hare forecasters—amateur meteorologists who keep hutches on balcony shelves—release two or three hares to read their ear-filament splay.  Orientation within five degrees predicts a sleet wall; full furl indicates thunder.  

Midlight (–02 h to +05 h)  
   • Work divides into two castes, not social but circadian:  
        Day-vein craft—maintainers who tramp the glass flats inspecting micro-fractures with back-mounted terahertz scanners.  
        Shade-loom craft—tower internalists who husband water flow, lichens, and the capillary farms snaking through living quarters.  
   • Between noon bells and the daily convective surge, all exterior rail traffic halts.  People string sling-chairs under balcony eaves and read the columns’ ticking like seaside folk hear surf.

Stormwatch (+05 h to +07 h)  
   • Everyone migrates to the upper 20 % of a tower where air is driest.  Kids are schooled in counting double and triple thunder echoes so they can map storm cores in three dimensions by ear.  
   • Rainfall rights are public by law; heads of households rotate turns at the valving galleries that divert downspouts toward communal cisterns or into experimental myco-vats.  

Nightglass (+07 h to +12 h)  
   • Courting happens in the skein pods—two people book an empty mag-lev car, dim cabin gravity to lunar, and ghost above the plateauscape while auroras write over the windshield.  
   • Street theatre, such as it is, meets on platform roofs.  Actors wear capes laced with gold filament; when they gesture, loosening stubs of gravity mesh, capes billow horizontally in the thinness, turning every soliloquy into slow aerial dance.

1.3  Governance  
Frael’s polity is a triumvirate:  
   1. The Horizon College—cartographers and ethicists who enforce the right to distant sightlines.  
   2. The Aquarche—engineers of water etiquette; they can overrule any construction if runoff modeling shows a net loss.  
   3. Skeinmind—an AI distributed through sensor nodes; its vote is algorithmic but not opaque: raw weights and loss functions livestream on public channels so citizens can challenge its reasoning in real time.  

2. Non-human Citizens  

2.1  Skeinmind’s field-bodies  
   • “Ribbon drones”: centimetre-wide flexi-strips that ride electromagnetic rails on wind power alone.  
   • “Tympan bots”: walnut-sized spheres that wedge into basalt cracks, using piezo arrays both to harvest cooling energy and to listen.  The columns’ shrinkage ticks are Skeinmind’s stethoscope; if patterns deviate, it diverts maintenance crews or damps mag-lev frequency to reduce resonance.  

2.2  Tower symbionts  
   • Hushmoss—a lichen bred to carpet interior walls.  It excretes a proline-rich polymer that dampens echo so voices stay intimate even in 200-m shafts.  
   • Threadworms—translucent nematodes seeded in tower foam.  Their waste bio-dopes the silicate skeleton, continuously healing microcracks the way bone remodels under stress.  

3. The Free-roaming Fauna (beyond the primer list)  

3.1  Banded rift gulls  
Silvery, jawless flyers with eel-like mouths that filter cloud droplets.  They surf the updrafts between weather-loom pulses; brood pouches on their flanks hatch live young that cling like barnacles until their first glide.

3.2  Pumice weasels  
Pearl-grey mustelids that dive into the pumice drifts of glass flats.  Their sternum plates beat like oystercatcher bills, fluidizing the dust so they can “swim” under it.  They hunt the egg sacs of winter midges, leaving dimples that later trap sleet and feed moss scabs—accidental gardeners.

3.3  Column inkfish (myth / maybe)  
Local legend speaks of cephalopod analogues inhabiting the flooded contraction cracks thirty metres down.  Periodically a gush of near-boiling water burps out, laced with chromatophores.  Geologists blame hydrothermal pulses; poets call it inkfish graffiti.

4. Micro-life, the Uncounted Majority  

4.1  Glass-film cyanites  
A picket line one molecule thick on cooled rhyolite.  They run quantum coherent photosystems for nanoseconds at dawn when UV peaks—enough to fix carbon and vanish before frost nucleation can spear them.  Twenty trillion live on a handspan of mirror flow.

4.2  Basalt marrow yeast  
Thrives in the vesicles of basalt pillars.  When a shard-ibex horns the rock for silica, it also licks the yeast froth—obtaining vitamin complexes otherwise absent in plateau flora.  In return, spit-borne enzymes etch the rock face, promoting further silica drip.  Goat and fungus are partners in patient masonry.

5. Invisible Life  

5.1  Auroral plankton  
Charged dust grains electrostatically clutching organic radicals.  They bloom only during K-index events, drifting down like invisible pollen the following dusk.  Residents hang passive collectors—silk veils slung between tower spires—to harvest the radicals as feedstock for polymer batteries.  Whether the plankton are “alive” is an argument the Horizon College reopens every winter.

5.2  Standing-wave phantoms  
Ionized layers in the upper troposphere sometimes arrange into self-reinforcing oscillations that mimic neuronal firing.  For minutes, they satisfy the five accepted criteria of cognition: response, homeostasis, energy exchange, reproduction (they fork), and self-termination.  The Plateau Folk greet them with wide-band greeting chants; some engineers swear the echoes come back slightly revised.

6. Patterns of Co-habitation  

6.1  Food web loops  
   • Ice-mink carcasses left by terracotta vultures nourish detritus beetles.  
   • Beetle frass sinks into rift shelves, feeding cushion fungi.  
   • Fungal mats produce glycols; runoff seeps to marsh roots whose fragrant exudates lure shard-ibex, closing the loop.  

6.2  Human exchange  
   • Gale-hares receive vitamin pellets in return for meteorological labor; their departure patterns are logged and compared to Skeinmind’s forecasts, creating an organic calibration cycle.  
   • Shard-ibex are not domesticated but “chartered.”  Citizens in need of horn crystal apply for grazing visas, then shadow a herd for weeks, collecting naturally shed horn tips under strict quotas.  

7. Spirits, Stories, Hypotheses  

7.1  The Plateau as adolescent deity  
Children are taught that the land itself is barely old enough to have moods.  A cracking column’s echo is its voice changing; steam vents are it learning to breathe.  Civic planning notes adopt the same language: “The young ground wants another decade before we pin a transport node here.”

7.2  The Two-Drop Oath  
Water is so guarded that law requires witnesses when any citizen voluntarily pours out more than two drops onto open ground.  Tradition casts the act not as waste but as tithe: a literal libation to keep the plateau alive.

7.3  Returners  
Once a decade, a lowland caravan climbs the switchback lifts to Frael.  They bring seed crystals for new tower foams and carry away tapestries woven from pumice-weasel fur.  Few stay; oxygen debt cripples them.  Folktales warn that if too many lowlanders remain at altitude, the plateau will sag—dragged downward by unfamiliar blood.

8. The Feeling of Being Among Them  

Step from a mag-lev pod onto a rift balcony: air thin as promise, basalt columns ticking below like the world’s cooled heart.  A woman in reflective throat-cloth taps her larynx folds to greet you; her voice arrives as a tremor felt in bone more than ear.  A shard-ibex appears on a distant sill, horn shards flicking rainbows.  Overhead a ribbon drone whispers, sampling humidity.  All around, space is dense with presences yet vacant to the eye—a city performed rather than built, a wilderness rehearsing civilization.  

Stay a week and you start cataloguing the actors: the lightning that threads towers, the auroral plankton that may or may not dream, the hushmoss around your bunk exhaling terpene sleep songs, the storm you helped summon by adjusting a mirror two degrees.  You will never again ask whether the plateau is alive; the argument is not philosophical but sensory.  Living things, thinking things, barely-thinking things, and things in the act of becoming thought share the table-continent the way lichens share a stone—bordered, entangled, polychrome.  

That is who dwells above the lowlands: people light of bone and wide of view, goats that mine their own horns, aeroplankton testing cognition, machines that mend basalt the way gardeners dead-head roses, and the terribly young rock itself, always stretching, cracking, speaking in ticks, pushing upward with the stubbornness of something that has only just learned it can grow.
