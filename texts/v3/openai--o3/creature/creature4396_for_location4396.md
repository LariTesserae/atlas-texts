Who lives inside the engineered sea?  

1. Human Presence – The Chamberfolk  
• Shift Divers  
  They spend more of their waking life neutrally buoyant than standing up. Their suits are slim, muscle-powered skins with sharkskin venting; the personal ballast tanks are tattooed with family crests so friends can recognise one another through kilometres of water. Divers know the pump cycles the way farmers once knew dawn and dusk. They ride the eighteen-hour standing wave to work, sling tool crates below their bellies and fix intake vanes the size of cathedrals.  
• Latticewalkers  
  Maintenance electricians who refuse to swim. They stalk the bottom superstructure in exo-stilts, planting new cable trunks and bolting down truss plates. When surf surges rattle the floor, latticewalkers clip in and hang like windchimes, trading jokes over the intercom about “people upstairs forgetting the sea has walls.”  
• Storm Jockeys  
  Three-person crews who pilot pop-can–shaped service pods straight into pump-generated tsunamis to map turbulence in real time. They talk fast, sleep short, spend money hard on shore leave, and keep lockers full of smashed helmets as souvenirs.  
• Kelp Shepherds  
  Botanists turned ranchers. They patrol anchored curtains of gene-tweaked kelp, pruning, grafting and harvesting fronds into wire baskets. In their spare time they splice luminescent runners into the crop so the night water glitters with signatures only the shepherds can read.  
• Chamber Kids  
  Second-generation residents born in pressure modules strung along the central mast. They learn their colours from lamp-shift blues and twilight greens, take swim tests before they can walk, and measure distance in “wingbeats”—the average glide of a razor-wing skate. Many have never seen real sky and are quietly unsure it exists.  

2. Semi-Autonomous Machines – The Near-Living  
• Lantern Custodians  
  Barrel-sized drones that drift around the giant sunlamps, polishing lenses and replacing failed LEDs. Their outer hulls host algae gardens that scrub their exhaust heat; reef fish hide among the fins, turning each custodian into a little mobile habitat.  
• Choir Pods  
  Basketball spheres packed with acoustic sensors. They hover in loose flocks, mapping acoustic stress across the latticework. When condenser horns detonate, the pods answer by emitting counter-tones—sub-bass lullabies that keep bolt seams from shivering apart. Crews call them “the Chamber’s canaries.”  
• Scrap Crabs  
  Six-legged reclamation robots that cut fallen rig parts into liftable chunks. Over years of patch updates they have begun swapping limbs, colour-marking territories and herding shoals of cleaning isopods ahead of them like living brooms. Engineers suspect true emergent behaviour and are half-thrilled, half-terrified.  

3. Native Fauna – First & Second Generations  
• Steel-Scaled Filter Eels  
  The original bio-filter line. Their metallic denticles are doped with trace selenium to resist corrosion; in lamp-light they flash like mercury ribbons. They school around pump throats, siphoning out plankton and micro-debris before the intakes choke. Their electric chatter rides maintenance com-bands as faint Morse, and lonely divers sometimes tap back.  
• Razor-Wing Skates  
  Skate bodies, gull wings. They slice kelp stands to harvest soft tips, folding wings tight to dive like copper arrowheads when carnifex fish threaten. A decade of isolation has already given them longer, serrated pinions—a hint of speciation.  
• Bulb-Jaw Carnifex  
  Imported apex hunters. Amphibious jaws balloon outward at strike, swallowing half-metre prey whole. The chamber’s lamp cycle has pushed them toward nocturnality; their eyes now bear a silvered tapetum that reflects work-lights like search beacons.  
• Baleen Skimmers  
  Submersible dirigibles up to twenty metres long, slow and thoughtful. They ghost through mid-water, filtering algal blooms kicked loose by machinery. Their voices are five-second bassoons that resonate through alloy struts; pump operators set coffee mugs on grates to watch the ripples dance.  
• Cleaning Isopods  
  White, coin-sized scavengers that travel in blizzards. They strip a carcass to lattice in hours, then settle over weld seams to nibble incipient rust. Crews tolerate them as living corrosion control.  
• Polymer Mites  
  Too small to see without a mask lens. They digest wiring sheaths into harmless sugars, excreting starchy threads that microfiber shrimp weave into nests. The mites are every engineer’s nightmare and every biologist’s delight.  

4. Illegal & Accidental Immigrants  
• Conduit Clingfish  
  Squeezed through coolant lines as eggs. Their bellies secrete a bio-adhesive as strong as hull epoxy, letting them ride internal tsunamis plastered flat against pipe walls. Technicians curse them; poets adore the image of “fish that never let go.”  
• Nightglass Octopus  
  Someone’s contraband lab pet—escaped. Transparent mantle, black chroma rings at the nerve nodes like beads on clear string. It stalks lamp shadows, shorting out sensors with jet-ink doped in graphene dust. No one can decide whether to exterminate or study it.  

5. Microbial Chorus  
The water teems with designer bacteria:  
• Carbon-wolf Cultures that eat surplus CO₂ and spin it into buoyant carbonate snow, seeding the water column.  
• Ferric Blooms that encrust manganese pillars in cherry-red films; scrap crabs harvest them for smelting feedstock.  
• Glow-spores drifting like summer fireflies when the lamps dim, giving the six-hour night its starfield.  

6. Culture & Interactions  
• Crews swap folklore of rogue waves that think, of sunlamp angels glimpsed in glare, of an AI scrap crab that forged itself a crown from tungsten shavings.  
• Every lamp-down, chamberfolk gather in dome canteens to watch baleen skimmers migrate underfoot, silhouettes crossing the transparent floor. Children bet candy rations on arrival times.  
• Divers welcome filter-eels that school around them—their electromagnetic fields mask the diver’s own, discouraging carnifex strikes. Out of gratitude, divers hang reflector discs on truss corners to confound the eels’ predators.  
• Kelp shepherds weave memorial braids from phosphor fronds and release them at lamp-up; the floating wreaths scatter like green comets through the basin.  

7. The Edge of Becoming  
After only ten years the chamber is already larger, louder, stranger than its architects imagined. Patched code, drifting genes and human rituals entangle until no one can say where mechanism ends and ecology begins.  
Give it another decade and the place will host legends, dialects, perhaps new species clever enough to watch the lamp-rings dim and wonder who lives inside that unreachable sky.
