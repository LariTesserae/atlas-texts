Who—or rather what—lives here is hard to name, because life is not divided into individuals the way it is in animal worlds. Everything you glimpse is a tissue, a limb, a fleeting mood of a single continental organism. Still, the place contains castes, tempers and temporary bodies, and it helps to speak of them as if they were separate people.

1. The Continental Body  
   Think of the whole landmass as one sprawling vertebrate turned inside-out: roots for arteries, leaves for lungs, geothermal vents for stomachs. It has no center; its “brain” is distributed through millions of cambial knots—knuckles of wood where signals slow, compare, decide. Those knots exchange ions the way neurons trade neurotransmitters, and the exchange travels through copper phloem filaments extruded expressly for computation. In the dark you saw maroon pulses wandering trunks; those are thought packets, taking hours to crawl a kilometre, but they never stop.

2. Knot-Brains (Cortical Nodes)  
   Where three or more major xylem trunks braid together they form boluses of hyper-lignified tissue riddled with resonant cavities. Sap oscillates in the cavities at audio frequencies far below human hearing; the phase pattern across a whole knot is the local state of mind. Some knots are only centimetres wide, deciding whether to angle a leaf. Others grow as big as stadiums, adjudicating the long-haul allocation of iron ore. If you stand near one and press your ear to the bark you will pick up a sub-bass flutter like distant thunder. That is governance.

3. The Dynamo Sleeves  
   Magnetite-sheathed cylinders that slowly twist around supporting trunks, driven by osmotic torque. The dynamo’s rotation is reef-slow—two, three degrees a day—but the coils inside are kilometres in circumference and the voltage they bleed is respectable. Sleeves are studded with photosensitive valves that open when outside light falls, so the grid always drinks whatever photons wander down the canopy stacks. Power is routed through hot-sap conduits toward colder districts, and the temperature difference keeps the sleeves turning. They are the muscular system of the continent, forever tensing and relaxing.

4. Courier Forms (“Sapwalkers”)  
   Once in a great while the plant needs to carry decisions faster than ions can drift. It buds a courier: a hollow rod of jointed cellulose two or three metres tall, each joint inflated by gas cultured from dissolved limestone. When inflated the rod stands, breaks its root moorings, and lurches away on stilt-like negative pressure bladders. A sapwalker can cover twenty kilometres in the few days before its joints lignify. When it reaches the destination it kneels, locks, and becomes one more support pillar in the canopy architecture. Travelers sometimes mistake long avenues of kneeling sapwalkers for a colonnade carved by hand—they are just expired memos.

5. The Ligature Swarms  
   From beneath vault groves rise clouds of fibres finer than hair but stiff as glass. Each fibre carries a speck of magnetite at its tip and twitches to faint electromagnetic rhythms drummed out by the knot-brains. A swarm’s job is to braid itself into cable or cloth wherever structural stress begins to exceed tolerance. A storm of roots lifts a ridge? The ligatures condense, sew tension lines between trunks, then cement themselves with silica secreted along the strand. Weeks later they crumble, are chewed back into glucose, and wait to be summoned again.

6. Digestive Creepers (The Quiet Teeth)  
   Predation here is internal, but it has a face. From the borders of a nutrient-hungry district extend flat, ribbonlike roots whose top surface carries enzyme nodules. They insinuate themselves into the softer phloem of a weaker neighbour, dissolve walls, and drink the slurry. If two districts of equal strength meet, their creepers duel by secreting blocker proteins into one another’s xylem. A stalemate produces a living demilitarized zone: a tangle of half-digested tissue pressed flat between rival sap flows. These strips are the closest thing the land has to scars.

7. Spore-Mist Choirs  
   In the still air just below the highest canopy swim clouds of motile spores that share pigment molecules able to fluoresce at specific wavelengths. At night (or what passes for night) whole cubic kilometres of mist blink, fade, and bloom again in geometric progressions. The pattern encodes long-range broadcast—usually inventories of stored metals or warnings of mineral famine. Single spores live minutes; the choir as information structure may persist centuries, migrating slowly on convection threads, always updating itself as members senesce and are replaced.

8. Auxetic Seed-Engines  
   Seeds here are not passive. When a knot-brain judges an outlying vein redundant, it orders the vein to bloom. Lignin plates inside the dead-end branch rearrange into an auxetic lattice; the moment internal pressure rises the whole branch balloons outward into a winged cradle full of embryonic buds. Piezoelectric sheets embedded in the wing bases flutter against the thick air, extracting trickles of current that keep the embryos metabolically idling while the cradle drifts. Flight lasts an hour; landing is gentle, but the instant the shell tastes raw mineral its muscles petrify and the whole assembly becomes permanent scaffold for the newborn grove.

9. The Mycelial Parliament  
   Strictly speaking, fungi are the only “other” life. Threadlike, translucent and slow, they lace every root mass, their hyphae acting as brokerage houses for trace elements the plant itself cannot mine quickly. In return the Parliament receives continuous carbohydrate stipend. Once a decade the fungal lattice thickens at pre-agreed coordinates, inflates into a fist-sized nodule, and squeezes out a single fruiting body. The cap splits, releasing an aerosol of messenger RNA addressed to every knot-brain. The plant listens. Some observers think most large-scale reconfigurations happen only after the Parliament “votes.”

10. Apparitions (Misinterpretations)  
   Visitors report shapes at the corner of vision: a figure that steps behind a trunk, a soft tread on the root-mat. They hear one footfall, never two. Analysis of footprint hollows shows them to be collapse pits where air pockets in the mat gave way. The apparition is the forest sampling your acoustic profile—local cambium stiffens, throws a sonar pulse, then relaxes. Your own echo dressed as a ghost.

Do any “people” walk here? Only you, and not for long. The continent prefers to keep all its carbon local, and the Quiet Teeth are happy to recycle a lost hiker. But if you insist on personhood, grant it to the place itself: a single, somnolent, all-encompassing engineer dreaming in timber and iron ore, forever refurbishing its indispensable self.
