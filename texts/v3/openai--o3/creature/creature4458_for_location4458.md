Those who inhabit the young plateau do not form a single kingdom of life.  
Instead the place is crowded with half-overlapping sovereignties—plant, animal, fungal, metallic, human, and something that exists only as a pulse inside stone.  
All of them have learned the same etiquette:

• Move rarely.  
• Hoard noise as though it were food.  
• Treat copper as both altar and meal.  
• Never trust water to remain where you last saw it.

Below is the census as well as anyone has been able to take it.

------------------------------------------------------------
1. The Verdigris Lattice (primary vegetation)
------------------------------------------------------------
Appearance  
A vine, at first glance, but with stems the color of old coins and leaves that flash mirror-bright on one side, storm-cloud black on the other.  Each stem is ridged by twin copper veins; scrape one and the smell is that of a hot penny pressed to the tongue.

Habits  
• Roots are electro-tropic: they wriggle toward any conductor.  A forgotten length of wire will, within a season, be braided so deeply into the Lattice that metal and cambium can no longer be parted.  
• Once a year—never on a schedule anyone has cracked—the vine builds voltage the way other plants build sugar.  At dusk its leaves touch.  There is a muffled pop, then a crackle that runs leaf to leaf like a whispered fuse; seeds are ejected on statically charged plumes of fibre and drift for miles, absolutely silent.  
• The Lattice is why every condenser stays unclogged: the plant drinks the mild electric field in the copper skin, cleaning as it feeds.

Role  
It is best to think of the Verdigris Lattice as the plateau’s circulatory system: it keeps the machines polished, the cliffs green-edged, and the air faintly ionised.  Many of the other residents depend on its stray charge the way desert creatures depend on cactus water.

------------------------------------------------------------
2. Sky-Kites (apex aerial hunter)
------------------------------------------------------------
Physiology  
Wingspan two metres, mass scarcely a kilogram: bone replaced by cartilage struts, feathers by membranes thin enough to read newsprint through.  The skin holds microscopic prisms; in full sun a Kite looks matte black, but tip it and the prisms spill dazzling pinpoints that stun insects into stillness.

Behaviours  
• They never flap.  Updrafts born in the knife-edge valleys are strong enough that a Kite can live a decade without beating its wings more than a few hundred times.  
• Vision is ultraviolet-weighted; a single motion—your hand on a canteen—is a beacon.  For that reason every other moving animal freezes when a Kite’s shadow passes, and the plateau briefly feels even emptier than it is.  
• Kites breed on sheer faces where quartz veins cross like hatch-marks.  The static hum from the stone seems to lull the chicks; no one has seen them nest anywhere else.

------------------------------------------------------------
3. Step-Echo Newts (the only loud creature)
------------------------------------------------------------
A palm-length amphibian of no particular glamour: mottled grey, mucus-shiny, eyes like lacquered peppercorns.

The mystery is their call.  Once between sunset and total dark each Newt emits a single syllable—a glassy plink, higher than human hearing but strong enough that you feel it in the molars.  A dozen plinks from different slopes form a chord that maps the whole valley in echo.  Hikers have used the pattern to judge distance the way mariners once read lighthouse flashes.

Nobody knows what the Newts get from the ritual.  Perhaps it is simply a census: I am here, you are there, the plateau is still holding together.

------------------------------------------------------------
4. Verdigris Mites & The Copper Nursery
------------------------------------------------------------
Where the Lattice grows thickest you will find mats of pale yellow dust.  Under a lens the dust resolves into mites shaped like flakes of ash.  They lay their eggs only on bright copper; the hatchlings scrape the metal for oxides, cleaning the surface until it is almost pink, then migrate upward into the stems that drew them.

A condenser that loses its mite colony tarnishes within days and stops drawing water within weeks—a reminder that even the machines have begun to require their own parasites.

------------------------------------------------------------
5. The Linewalkers (humans, or close enough)
------------------------------------------------------------
Origin stories multiply in the libraries of lowland universities: failed mining syndicate, weather-monitoring monks, post-collapse technicians who never received the recall order.  The Linewalkers do not confirm or deny.  In truth they have shed biography the way cliffs here shed talus.

Physiology & Dress  
• Stature thin, lungs proportionally huge, skin dusted with a film of carotene that acts as sunblock and sheens golden under direct light.  
• Clothing is copper-threaded flax that crackles faintly as they move, helping them feel—rather than hear—each other’s approach.  A long coat hem can transmit vibration better than most drumheads.  
• Nearly all have a callus on the forehead where climbing helmets meet skin: they ride the plateau face-first, always able to lean into stone.

Social Structure  
Solitary by default, but linked by the tracks that lace the high ledges.  A walker will tend a three-mile segment: clearing vines from wheels, tapping porcelain culverts to be sure the pitch of water is right.  When the sun sets they shelter inside switch-houses whose bulbs still burn.  The hum of wasted electricity is their lullaby.

Language  
Spoken words are an emergency measure.  Normal discourse is accomplished by modulating the pitch of water in small tin cups.  Five drops struck in a row, the fourth slightly off-tempo, means I have spare solder.  A hiss poured through cloth means A cliff has cracked; avoid the western track.  The entire vocabulary fits in half an octave.

Belief  
Copper is the central sacrament.  A condenser is always approached barefoot, to feel its faint charge.  They believe each machine has an “allotment of silence,” and the penalty for startling it with a shout is paid in mechanical failure elsewhere.  No one has demonstrated a causal link, but you will not get a Linewalker to raise their voice among the fins.

Life Cycle  
Children are rare.  When one is born the parents hang a seed-pod of the Verdigris Lattice beside a Pelton wheel.  If the seed germinates, the child is said to be accepted by the plateau and may stay; if it dries, the family descends together to the lowlands and is not spoken of again.

------------------------------------------------------------
6. The Clockwork Waters (machine-organisms)
------------------------------------------------------------
The condensers, cisterns and idle tram are often spoken of as ruins, but the plateau treats them as large, slow animals.

• A condenser’s copper fins gather trace charge; the Verdigris Lattice and its mites keep the fins polished; that charge in turn stimulates the vine’s next growth cycle.  It is a loop elegant enough to feel designed, though of course design is the one claim no resident will make.  
• The tram still moves because roots have knitted around its flywheels, winding and unwinding with day-night temperature swings like a vegetal mainspring.  Should a coil snap the tram would halt until the vine grew back.  Travelers time their journey by blooming cycles rather than by schedules.

------------------------------------------------------------
7. The Stone Choir (immaterial life)
------------------------------------------------------------
Quartz ribs everywhere whisper in the radio band.  On a crystal receiver you hear them as needle-thin voices: sometimes a trill, sometimes what might be overlapping fugues.  Linewalkers insist the pitches change after each major rockfall, as though new notes are being added to an unending composition.

No one has located a source—yet batteries left too long on quartz go flat in hours, and caged birds brought from lowland villages beat themselves to death trying to flee the unheard music.  The best theory is piezoelectricity amplified by fault-strain, but that has never explained why the voices resolve into recognizable scales during meteor showers.

------------------------------------------------------------
8. Visitors (that means you)
------------------------------------------------------------
Spend a week here and the plateau begins to rewire your reflexes.

• You step softly, not from fear but from politeness.  
• You hold a metal cup under any drip you pass, even if you are not thirsty.  
• You stop talking to companions in sentences and start sharing meanings in the clink of gear, the rubbing of sleeve on pack-strap.  
• You catch yourself waiting for the dusk-plinks of the Newts and feel oddly unsafe if a valley stays silent.

Most leave before the change calcifies.  A few stay, shave copper shavings into their water, learn the pitches, and someday inherit a three-mile stretch of track.  Those become Linewalkers, though the plateau never calls them that.  To the land they are simply another organ added to the slowly assembling body—a body made of slate teeth, mist, quartz song, and machines that will outlive every biography we are tempted to write.

And so the place remains inhabited, thoroughly, by beings with lungs or leaves or turbine blades, all participating in the same precise hush, all keeping one another wound like clocks that refuse to run down.
