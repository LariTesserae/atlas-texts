In the Gorge of Pistons every creature is a moving part, shaped less by ancestry than by the demands of pressure, metal and sound. What follows is the best catalogue we can make without dissecting the place, which would be like prying the back off a clock to watch the wheels while it still keeps time.

1. The Film – the First Eaters  
Size: one-half micrometre thick, yet in patches kilometres wide.  
Substance: a two-layer skin, upper of iron-oxide vesicles, lower of sulphide-reducing archaea.  
Habit: It lies where the cold, metal-rich seep meets oxygen. A molecule of copper here is energy, a stray ion of iron is a handle. The Film grows by electroplating itself atom by atom, brittles with age, then sloughs off in glitter that drifts like ash. Every other life in the gorge treats the Film as soil, bread and scripture.

2. Riveters – the Shallow Swarm  
Shape: twenty-jointed isopods, prism-cross-section, forelegs ending in matched wedges of diamond chitin.  
Colour: mirror black in youth, smoke silver once the shell fouls with pyrite dust.  
Voice: the cot-cot-cot of wedges striking stone; singly a tap, in millions a down-pour of nails.  
Work: A riveter walks with six legs, braces with two, pries with two. Each forward stroke shaves a Film-laden flake; each backward stroke flicks the flake to its hinged throat. Knock, shear, swallow: a rhythm they keep from hatching until the day their mouth-parts fuse shut and they starve slowly, husks still fastened to the wall.  
Reproduction: At century tides (when three of the planet’s moons line up) the gorge goes briefly silent; the swarm loosens its grip and lifts as a glitter cloud. Gametes are released in silence—because any sound would make them prey—and minutes later the riveters clap back to the stone and resume chiselling, as though embarrassed by the lapse.

3. Sluicers – the Moving Ground  
Body: muscle tube one metre long, lined inside with cilia that beat like grain elevators.  
Head: nothing but an iris-valve mouth capable of dilating to forty centimetres.  
Tail: a fluted exhaust aperture trimmed with manganese nodules for ballast.  
Feeding: A sluicer positions itself downstream of riveter rain, opens the iris and inhales a ribbon of flakes. Everything smaller than a rice grain keeps moving; everything larger is regurgitated sideways as tidy cylinders that stack into dunes. Because their waste is too coarse for rivals, sluicers occupy territory without competition.  
Remark: Sluicers almost never collide. They feel the gorge’s standing wave with their lateral lines and steer by differential vibration. Fishermen (if there were any) could net them blindfolded simply by humming the right counter-note.

4. Presses – the Sudden Ones  
Architecture: two matching plates of nickel-blue chitin, hinged down one margin, edged with interlocking teeth of iron phosphate.  
Posture: anchored vertically, hinge outward, like a door left ajar.  
Trigger: any staccato set of taps within the gape—typically a riveter stream—detonates the closing reflex. The plates slam, water jets, prey pulp.  
Sound: the slam is the gorge’s heartbeat, a basso crack that perspires through a kilometre of basalt.  
Growth: Juveniles, flat as dinner platters, glue themselves to an adult’s outer shell and spend years absorbing echo data—every parent shell is a boarding school of acoustics. When their own hinges mineralise enough to ring true, they drop and claim a crack in the wall, tuning the crevice by shaving or adding shell until its resonance matches theirs. Two adjacent presses whose notes clash will slowly migrate apart, a mechanical form of territorial song.

5. Derricks – the Light Harvesters  
Form: a circular canopy six metres across, membrane thin, supported by a skeletal ring-gear of calcium struts. Hanging below is a three-metre tether thick as a human thigh, swollen every fifty centimetres into sphinctered bulbs.  
Surface: pure specular chrome at birth; later lacquered in iridescent colonies of commensal bacteria that convert photons to chemical torque.  
Motion: The gimmick is a viscous alternation of pouch contractions down the tether, each squeeze translating as a one-tooth click of the canopy’s ring-gear against the resisting water. Twelve thousand contractions complete a full rotation. The canopy never reverses; its accumulated twist is stored in the tether like torsion in a drill bit. Every seven to ten years the tether releases in a single slow uncoil, pumping a surge of nutrient-rich lysate into the rock seam below. Presses schedule spawning to that flood.  
Senses: A Derrick hears through the mountain. Vibrations arrive via its anchoring vein, are phase-sorted in the pulp of the tether, and map as moving shadows across the canopy. A particularly strong pulse—an earthquake, or a cluster of presses mis-firing—makes the Derrick flare brighter, a mesmeric umbrella signalling calm to the creatures attached to its shadow side.

6. Grommets – the Threaders  
(Overlooked in many early dives.)  
Size: rice-grain gastropods with a helical foot that can corkscrew through basalt shoulders.  
Role: They lace all the rest together. A grommet selects a live but wounded shell—often the oxidised scar of a press—tunnels in, sips a film of haemolymph, then excretes a plug of bioceramic that stops the leak. One grommet can petition favour from forty larger neighbours, wandering from fracture to fracture plugging and nursing. The gorge’s rule that “nothing dies quickly” is largely their ministry.

7. The Stillborn – latent lives  
Inside the rock itself lie chambers full of unspent possibility: embryos of creatures yet unrecognised, sealed in vitreous pods where the pressure is fractionally higher and the chemistry subtly different. When the mountain burps a new fissure, these pods fissile into the water. Some never activate; others awaken into morphologies the existing niches cannot absorb—a crab with peristaltic claws, a spiral lamprey, a wing-fin squid. Most perish silently and thus remain unseen. A few, every century or so, invent a new key that fits the moving lock of the gorge and are admitted. Riveters were once such trespassers, according to isotope analysis of their oldest plates.

Social Physics  
• Contact is etiquette: to touch is to declare location and rhythm. Creatures that wish to hide fall silent and withdraw limbs.  
• Memory is mineral: the composition of an animal’s shell records seawater chemistry at the moment of each growth layer. By reading a Derrick’s ring-gear or a Press’s hinge, one could reconstruct a millennium of volcanic pulse with month-scale resolution.  
• Succession is audible: as the mountain rises, water pressure lessens, changing the pitch of every shell. The living community retunes instinctively, shifting feeding grounds and attachment sites so the overall chord—the Gorge’s factory roar—stays within a favoured bandwidth. Should the note ever drift too sharp or flat, the animals would migrate en masse down the mountain, leaving the summit acoustically deserted.

Life Cycle of the Gorge (long view)  
1. Birth of rock and first venting of crystal foam.  
2. Arrival of Film, probably by spore-rain from neighbouring seamounts.  
3. Invasion of Stillborn diaspora, rapid speciation into Riveters and prototype Sluicers.  
4. Establishment of sound-governed predation; origin of Presses.  
5. Settlement of Derricks, steadying of the light regime.  
6. Present clockwork equilibrium.  
7. Forecast: In ten thousand years the summit will crest the surface, light will intensify, metal venting will slacken, and the gorge’s machinery will seize. Derricks will bleach to nacre, Presses will fall open forever, and the last Riveter will tap once more into air and not understand the silence that answers.

Until then the Gorge of Pistons lives, hammering out slow decades in a fugue of iron and cobalt, every creature a cog, every cog a hymn, every hymn a line in the unfinished manual of how mountains learn to breathe underwater.
