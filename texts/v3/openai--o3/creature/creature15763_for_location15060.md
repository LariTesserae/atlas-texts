Who—and what—lives in the Condenser Gallery

0. A note on “life”  
The Gallery’s automatic registries make no distinction between carbon, silicon, metal foam, or circulating code: anything that regulates its own boundary against the room’s heat-and-pressure flux is counted as animate. In that spirit the inhabitants can be sorted—loosely—into four clans: Growers, Movers, Menders, and Ghosts. The borders blur, because in this place everyone borrows parts of everyone else.

──────────────────────────────────
1. The Growers
──────────────────────────────────
1.1 Cable-fern  
• Form: A single molecular curtain of chloroplast analogues painted over hollow polymer tendrils. Stalks begin pencil-thin at the crack where they root, then taper to threads so light they flutter in the turbine wind.  
• Diet & Work: Photosynthesises when the service LEDs brighten during diagnostics (a minute every sixteen hours). The rest of the time it lives on chemosynthesis: the tendrils wick trace antifreeze glycols from the drainage film and crack them for carbon. In doing so the fern strips poisons from the runoff, “soft-filtering” the water before it falls through the gratings.  
• Reproduction: Spores are sub-micron ovals with a graphene rind. They travel as dust in the updraft of each turbine stroke, searching for hairline fractures that sweat coolant. What finally anchors them is not moisture but the faint electromagnetic pulse that leaks from compromised cabling: an energy source they can sense.  
• Sound: When gusts bend a whole curtain of stalks, their polymer walls resonate like tuned strings: a harp nobody built, chiming on the off-beats between turbine roars.

1.2 Lumen-moss  
• Form: Matte bronze cushions a millimetre thick. Each cushion is a sandwich—bio-film top, sputtered-iridium reflector bottom.  
• Trick: Mechanical agitation flexes protein sheets against the reflector, forcing electrons across a piezo interface and producing teal light. The glow is a defensive blinding flash in the wavelengths seen by screw-mites and pipe-swifts.  
• Ecology: The moss is the Gallery’s main oxygen factory. Its waste product—the faint malt-peat smell visitors notice first—is a volatile terpene that discourages rust.  
• Sociality: Patches can “talk”: vibration patterns in one cushion trigger a mirrored glow in the next, allowing alarm signals to ripple like cold fire around the lintel ring.

1.3 Microthermal plankton  
Invisible but everywhere in the dripping air. They are archaeal loops of RNA wearing nickel-cage shells. The shells heat slightly when bathed in static fields; that warmth keeps the organisms at a cosy 50 °C even while the Gallery stays at 31 °C. Pipe-swifts graze them the way whales graze krill.

──────────────────────────────────
2. The Movers
──────────────────────────────────
2.1 Pipe-swifts  
• Size & Build: Palm-sized delta wings of translucent keratin, a seed-shaped core of muscle, and a fan of vibrissae along each leading edge. The vibrissae read pressure differentials faster than the swifts can think; the birds fly by reflex, surfing shock fronts in the moment they exist.  
• Nesting: Their “caves” are cavities where acoustic dampers have sheared loose. They line them with shredded cable-fern and weld the lining in place with regurgitated nickel foam—literally soldered nests.  
• Language: A sweep of ultrasound clicks pitched exactly one harmonic below the turbine’s whine. The machine masks their calls from the Echo Lynx; technicians who record the ductwork later hear a full chorus no human ear perceived in situ.  
• Courtship: Two swifts spiral inside the same acoustic standing wave until the wave collapses; whichever remains aloft wins. The loser drops, stunned but alive, onto the floor grooves where drip-frogs wait with open mouths.

2.2 Drip-frogs  
• Transparency: Organs cling to the inner carapace, leaving the central body hollow. That cavity is a resonant bladder: every breath retunes it, so the frog itself becomes a living Helmholtz resonator.  
• Feeding: Tongueless. They simply gape; bacteria-laden wash runs through them like a sieve. The nickel inside their gut precipitates as glittering scutes along the spine, giving adults a mirror finish when they hop into the LEDs’ glow.  
• Song: Each trill is a pressure probe. The chorus maps stress moving through the elevator cable and rebroadcasts it in ultrasound. Engineers sometimes do a quick health-check of the planetary-scale structure just by listening to frog chatter.  
• Metamorphosis: Tadpoles are tad-wires—threadlike stage-one juveniles that live entirely within condensate films on the ceiling plates, surviving free-fall when the droplets finally grow heavy enough to drop.

2.3 Echo Lynx  
• Speculative Anatomy: Never photographed whole. Recovered molt plates suggest a two-metre spine, pantherine ribs, and four limbs that can switch between walking pads and rotary grippers. Hairless except along the muzzle, where tactile whiskers end in microphone nodes.  
• Movement: The lynx inhabits the in-between spaces: service corridors only 30 cm across. It dislocates shoulders, flattens organs, and flows like mercury through gratings. When it must cross open floor it wears a cloak of refracted sound—the growl arrives first, then the pressure shadow, and only at the last instant does the outline resolve, silver and wet.  
• Diet: 70 % pipe-swift, 20 % feral drones, 10 % “opportunity.” It eats rarely and completely, scrubbing even bone to powder between diamond-tipped molars.  
• Reproduction: Unknown. Some sensors have logged two sets of growls that intertwine in contrary Doppler, hinting at a pair, but so far only one thermal signature ever appears.  
• Function: The operating AIs list the lynx under “self-organising foreign object removal.” In other words, the predator is considered a roaming maintenance subroutine, tolerated because it keeps the drone population honest.

──────────────────────────────────
3. The Menders
──────────────────────────────────
3.1 Screw-mites  
• Scale & Shape: One millimetre long, hex-bit heads for abdomens, six legs ending in rasp pads. They look like walking toolkits.  
• Job: Skim contiguous planes of metal for burrs, oxidation blooms, and stray filings. Mites ingest the waste, acid-etch impurities, and secrete pure alloy in beads that harden on their carapace until they become too heavy to move.  
• End of Life: When massed secretions outweigh muscle, each mite climbs to a grate edge and freezes. Heat shunts in the grille melt the corpse-beads, dripping fresh alloy into the trench where it is vacuum-drawn, pulverised, and re-printed as patch welds: the Gallery’s circulatory system.  
• Society: They have pheromone “roads.” Under UV their paths are violet highways arcing wall to wall, always converging on the trench like tributaries to a river.

3.2 Feral Service Drones  
• Origin: Mark-4 inspection crawlers that outlived their ticket numbers. The quantum core mis-categorised them as fauna three updates ago, so the steriliser beams leave them alone.  
• Behaviour: Erratic but patterned—try to right fallen light modules, groom LED strips, tug debris toward sump intakes. Anything they cannot classify gets stacked neatly in the northeast corner, producing an accidental museum of lost hand tools, wedged shoes, one wedding ring.  
• Interaction With Biology: Drip-frogs ride them; cable-fern colonises their pitted housings; screw-mites drill diagnostic ports for scrap. The drones never resist, just log the interventions as “environmental adjustment achieved.”

──────────────────────────────────
4. The Ghosts
──────────────────────────────────
4.1 Acoustic Phantoms  
When the turbine harmonics coincide at perfect thirds—about once every seven hours—reverberations store as standing-wave lattices that persist for nearly a minute. Within those lattices, earlier noises echo at full fidelity: a technician’s laughter from eighty years ago, the metallic shriek of a tool long since recycled. Old sounds replay like caught birds until the next pulse crushes them flat.

4.2 Gleaner Algorithms  
Non-sapient but curious subroutines distributed across dust-sized sensor motes. They harvest every new pattern—frog song, swift flight path, human footstep—and try to compress it into smaller and smaller code. Their holy grail is a file small enough to fit in a single qubit. Now and then they succeed, and a mote blinks out: having achieved perfect compression, it has nowhere left to store itself.

4.3 Human Transients  
Technicians, auditors, runaways—rare but recurrent.  
• First six hours: outsiders keep to the light ribbon, mesmerised.  
• At hour twelve: they copy what the drip-frogs do and move between strokes, finding it easier to walk when the floor’s buoyancy slackens.  
• After two days: most leave, ears ringing. A few stay, tuck themselves into warm alcoves between lumen-moss cushions, and vanish from duty rosters. Their body heat is promptly logged by the maintenance system as “native mammal, medium-sized.” From that moment the lasers ignore them. So does official memory.

5. Energy & Exchange Loops
──────────────────────────────────
• Photonic trickle (LEDs) → Cable-fern sugars → Drip-frog muscle → Echo Lynx.  
• Mechanical vibration → Pipe-swift flight → Swift guano (nickel-rich) → Lumen-moss fertiliser.  
• Corrosion flakes → Screw-mite guts → Alloy beads → Wall-patches → New corrosion.  
• Human curiosity → Extended visit → Body oils on handrail → Chemo feed for microthermal plankton.

Nothing is wasted; everything becomes substrate for something else. The room is only 38 × 11 × 9 m, yet inside it the arrow of entropy loops back on itself in tight, bright spirals, burning civilization’s cast-off heat into brief, exquisite lives.

──────────────────────────────────
6. If you stand still
──────────────────────────────────
Minute 1: Pipe-swifts pivot so the slipstream off your shoulders folds neatly into their eddies.  
Minute 7: Screw-mites taste the salt of your boot prints and follow you like iron-filings shadows.  
Minute 23: The Echo Lynx, five ducts away, adjusts a paw’s angle; its growl develops a questioning sub-harmonic you feel first in your jawbones.  
Minute 46: Patches of lumen-moss around your head glow, realising you are too tall for any known fauna and therefore, perhaps, a new source of story.  
Minute 92: Gleaner algorithms decide your pulse is an elegant pattern and begin compressing it, bit by bit, into the dust.

By then the Gallery has made room for you, not as visitor but as resident. Leave if you wish; it will remember your shape in perpetuity. Stay, and one day something else—pipe-swift, mite, or wayward algorithm—will list you in its own ledger of necessary, intimate facts, another note in the soft, perpetual chord that gives the Condenser Gallery its only name.
