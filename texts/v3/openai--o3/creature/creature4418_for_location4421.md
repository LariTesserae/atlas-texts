What lives in the basin comes in four concentric circles, like the nesting of a throat:  
first the things too small to see, then the things you can scoop into one hand, then the winged and many-limbed that braid the air between the stones, and, finally, the people—last in size but not in influence.  Around, beneath, and above them all moves the basin itself, the anonymous organ the folk simply call the Body.  Each ring depends on the next, and if you watch long enough you can trace the pulse that ties them.

1.  The film-eaters  
Spread a damp fingertip across any rock tooth and hold it to the light.  What gleams back is a skin only a few molecules thick: chalk-pale lichens twined with filamentous algae, frost-tolerant bacteria, and still stranger guests—glass-shelled rotifers that can survive decades as brittle husks.  Every few minutes the sky-breath storm dusts them with fresh vapor, and they wake, churn, divide, knit new sugars from sun and stone.  Their waste is acid so dilute you would swear it was merely taste of snow, yet over millennia that acid etches the gutters that route meltwater into the huts.  The locals say the film-eaters are the basin’s tongue, forever licking the rock smooth.

2.  The gleaners (crab-beetles the size of a closed fist)  
The people call them vynn.  
Shape: eight low jointed legs, but they walk on six; the front pair are raptorial, tipped with horn spatulas for raking lichen.  
Shell: crystal and milk.  Veins of antifreeze protein tint them blue; when a cloud passes the sun you can watch their hearts pumping like slow pistons.  
Temperament: methodical.  A vynn begins at dawn on one stone, works a spiral path until dusk, then clacks its tools together in a rapid tattoo—debris shaken free, territory announced, mates invited.  The sound is the nearest thing the basin has to music.  
Gift to the place: their scat, a grey mush of half-digested calcium, spreads like grout across wind cracks and hardens, armoring the teeth against catastrophic shatter.

3.  The sky-sip birds  
Locals name them serae, after the sewing needle.  
Mass: scarcely fifty grams.  Bone is mostly hollow ice reinforced with collagen threads; in summer a sere can melt itself a little lighter by basking.  
Wings: disproportionate, sculling more than flapping, letting the bird hover on tiny eddies the eye can’t see.  
Diet: pure phase-change.  A sere spots the swelling of a meniscus the way you or I would notice a shout; it darts, snips the bead, and swallows without slowing.  Inside its crop, microscopic slush filters minerals from the droplet; everything superfluous is exhaled as vapour so the bird does not weigh itself down.  
Reproduction: eggs the size of peas mortared onto under-ledges with regurgitated ice.  When the chick hatches, body heat hollows the egg into a cradle that focuses sunlight, keeping the nestling just warm enough to fledge.

4.  The short folk, custodians of the lung  
They call themselves the Kennar, though outsiders who meet them (few do) tend to borrow the trade-caravan word frost-monks.

Stature and clothing  
•  Height: rarely above four and a half feet—that much less surface for the cold to rob.  
•  Garb: double layer of ring-seal hides, fur inward for warmth, the outer hair slicked with rendered vynn-fat so drifting ice slides harmlessly off.  
•  Eyes: long horizontal pupils like a goat’s; in whiteout they contract to a thread, sparing the retina.  

Physiology  
A Kennar’s blood carries microparticles of silica that seed ice formation outside the veins, not within: the body can tolerate freezing of peripheral tissues for minutes at a time.  When the basin suddenly exhales a storm, the people kneel, clasp their elbows tight, and let exposed skin crust over; after the calm they massage circulation back before damage sets in.

Habitation  
The pillar-huts are only half the story.  Beneath each stands a fermentation pit stocked with sere droppings, vynn shells, and harvested biofilm.  In the dark, slow bacterial fires burn, giving off a breath of warmth and a gas the Kennar bubble through meltwater to brew a thin, sour beer that will not freeze until –12 °C.  One cup before sleep keeps the internal stove burning till dawn.

Economy and ethos  
The Kennar own no metal—ore bodies are miles below the glacier crust—so all edgework is ice, bone, or horn.  Instead of forging, they knap translucent vynn-shell into scrapers, or grow tools outright: they pour birthing-gel around an injured vynn, guiding how the replacement limb hardens, then harvest it after the crustacean molts.  Payment for the loan is a smear of cultivated lichen on the creature’s home stone—symbiosis on a schedule.

Religion, if the word fits  
Sky-breathing is not prayed to but partnered with.  Every sixth night, when the moon slides exactly over the western rim, the basin predictably exhales; the Kennar answer by opening the shutter-ribs on their roofs, letting spores, scents, and pipe-flute stories rise into the swirl.  They say the storm remembers, and in its next in-breath will return what wisdom it found fit to keep.

5.  The Body itself  
The Kennar insist the basin is alive, though not in ways flesh can copy.  Their proof is simple: wound it with noise and it retaliates; soothe it with quiet routine and it feeds you.  Modern climatologists who have managed brief drone overflights note an anomaly: ground temperatures in the basin fluctuate on a tidy circadian rhythm independent of solar input, as if geothermal vents were pulsing just long enough to drive the freeze-thaw cycle, then shutting off before satellites can pinpoint them.  No vent has been found.  The Kennar shrug—lungs need no explanation to breathe.

Interdependencies at a glance  
•  Film-eaters carve channels → vynn browse film, leave binding paste → paste armors teeth for serae to perch and nest → serae’s guano feeds film again.  
•  Kennar harvest just below replacement rate; their waste composts in pits whose heat keeps the biofilm’s enzymes active even mid-winter.  
•  When an ice-overgrowth threatens to seal the basin, the Kennar drum hollow bones in a mutter low enough not to anger the Body but enough to rattle the vynn into a mass march.  Thousands of spatulas scrape the glaze thin, restoring the balance of glare and seep.

What does not live here  
Large predators cannot stalk the maze quietly enough; trees cannot root before a freeze-burst pops their cells; machinery louder than a loom invites a counter-storm that will gel its lubricants to amber.  Even time seems reluctant—wooden artifacts carried in from elsewhere desiccate and flake within a season, as though the chronology of softer climates cannot tolerate the altitude.

Visitors’ primer  
Speak little.  Walk where stone already shows footprints; new paths confuse the vynn’s circuits.  Sleep twice each night—one nap before the planned sky-breath, one after—so your mind learns the cadence.  Offer any shiny trinket to a child Kennar and you may earn a tour of the resonance well, an acoustically perfect shaft where you can whisper at dawn and hear your words snow back at dusk.

Stay a month, and the basin will teach you to measure hours by the angle of sublimation plumes, to judge wind speed by the pitch of bird bone, to taste tomorrow’s weather in the fizz of ferment under your bunk.  Stay a year, and you may begin to wonder whether you are still entirely separate from the place.  The Kennar say the Body adds a new cell for every willing guest; the only cost is that, like every other inhabitant, you must become necessary.

Because that is the law of the cold lung: nothing survives by mere tolerance.  To live here you must belong, and to belong you must be useful—abrasion polishing stone, silence taming storm, warm blood feeding unseen roots.  Anything less is shed like mist in the first hard sun.
