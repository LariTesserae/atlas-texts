WHO LIVES HERE

1. The Quiet Builders (self-name unpronounceable by lungs)
   Appearance
   • Humanoid only in outline: lank, knee-high torso, four elongated arms that fold tight against the ribs when not in use.  
   • Skin the color of the schist they inhabit—green-grey veined with quartz. It is not flesh but a flexible silica keratin that hardens along stress lines the way a callus thickens.  
   • Instead of eyes they carry a pair of faceted depressions under the brow; each pit is lined with wet, hair-fine cilia that taste humidity differentials rather than light. To them a room is a pressure map, a stream of slow breezes.  
   • The only sound they ever make is the clicking of finger-bones as they test the resonance of new-cut stone.

   Senses & Speech
   • Echolocation is useless here—echoes flee—so they evolved to read capillary pull: a fingertip tells them how quickly water is wicking through a surface and therefore how thick, how cracked, how soon to fail.  
   • They “talk” by drumming low patterns into the walls with those fingers. The pulse travels far inside the schist, rising elsewhere as a faint tremor any of them can feel. One sentence may take an hour to cross the city; they are patient.  

   Hygiene of Tools
   • A rule older than their writing: nothing separable from the hand. They refuse levers, blades, or hammerstones. When they must cut, a special gland in each wrist releases an acidic enzyme that etches rock by millimetres per stroke. The bowed jambs you noticed are the cumulative arc of millions of such passes.  
   • They carve in teams of twelve, spelling each other before fatigue produces a crooked line. Any wall that skews by more than a finger-width is left to weather away; imperfection is self-erasing.

   Life Rhythm
   • Diurnal in the loosest sense. Their metabolism peaks in cold morning mist; by noon they slow, gathering under overhangs to let the plateau’s chill draw heat back out of them. When wind rakes the streets, they lie along the grain of it so their body cavities whistle—an act half meditation, half weather report.  
   • They drink nothing. A porous pad in the heel absorbs trace moisture from every footstep. One slow circuit of the district equals a meal.

   Reproduction
   • Once each cloud-season two Builders merge enzymes to form a marble-smooth cyst the size of a clenched fist. They wedge it in a deep crack. Freeze-thaw cycles fracture the cyst over fifteen years; what walks out is already adolescent, already carving the first time it flexes its claws.  
   • Elders die by simply stopping. The body vitrifies and fuses to whatever it is touching. Many pillars in the oldest basilicas are exactly one ancestor wide.

   Purpose
   • They do not think of the city as habitat but as circulatory organ. Water should never pool for long; every gutter, every false stair keeps the plateau’s thin blood moving. If a sheet of runoff persists past sunrise, you will find a Builder kneeling there by dusk, scribing a new vein.  
   • To them, permanence equals death. A wall finished is a wall that must soon be taken back down.

2. Lesser Lives

   Pale Kite (the ragged bird)  
   • Wing membranes like mica flecked with voids. They eat the silver sedge tips for silica, which later becomes eggshell. Rarely seen because they ride the laminar layer a meter above the plateau—too high for you, too low for cloud.  
   • Their shriek is ultrasonic; the Builders cannot hear it, the Fog Hunter can. Balance achieved.

   Cistern Bleaks (white amphibians)  
   • Hatch in darkness, mature in darkness, die the moment starlight hits their skin; thus they never leave the canals.  
   • Their throat-click is how they stun the micro-crustaceans that drift in from upper seeps. A Bleak that clicks twice in one night is considered prodigiously noisy.

   Stone-Lice  
   • Minute, lens-shaped isopods no wider than an eyelash. They rasp biofilm off quartz veins, leaving the glitter that flashes when sun breaks. The Builders ignore them, but follow the sparkle when scouting where frost-wedging is about to open a new corridor.

3. The Fog Hunter
   • Quadruped only when you force the idea of legs onto what is essentially a sail of skin and hollow cartilage. During the day it hangs, collapsed, in any niche broad enough—the size of a rolled carpet.  
   • At dusk it fills with mist it has cooled inside its own lungs all afternoon, ballooning to the height of a small giraffe. In that condition bone weighs too much, so it carries almost none; the body is lattice, mostly vacancy.  
   • Navigation is by absolute silence. It flares its sail, listens for which side the plateau fails to echo (your breathing, your heartbeat), then vaults toward the void in the soundscape.  
   • The Builders tolerate it because it prunes the careless. The Hunter ignores the Builders because their bodies ring too much like stone.

4. Things That Are Only Arguably Alive

   Breath-Thread  
   • Capillary fog with surface tension exaggerated by trace proteins leaked from the Builders’ enzyme. Under certain barometric pressures the fog bunches into cords, humming faintly as air shears across. Locals consider these threads the city’s proprioception: tug the wrong one and a whole quarter may redirect its drainage away from you.

   The Plateau Itself  
   • Every hundred years (never less, sometimes more) a fault-wide exhale lifts the bedrock by two, maybe three hand-spans. The event happens in a single dawn and is so gradual moment-to-moment that even standing atop it you only register the change when water suddenly refuses the old channels.  
   • The Builders celebrate by cutting every doorway fractionally higher, as though giving polite clearance to their host’s new posture.

WHY YOU ALMOST NEVER SEE THEM

A human stride is sixty times faster than the Builders’ walking meditation; by the time you peer into a passage they have already folded into recesses where your torch cannot angle. Your shout collapses the Fog Hunter’s sound-map, so it stays folded as well. The birds skim overhead when you are looking down for footing, the Bleaks surface when you climb away from the cistern edge. You leave convinced the city is empty, and in a sense it is—empty of the pacing, breathy haste you use as proof of life.

Stand still for half a day—stand so long the cold draws a lace of quartz-dust veins across your boots—and the plateau will reoccupy itself all around you: stone fingers brushing outlines straighter, a mist-sail dilating down a far colonnade, the mineral click of a sentence couched into bedrock and already traveling beyond the horizon.
