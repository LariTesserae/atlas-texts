Nothing here walks or watches or wonders.  Life on the tabletop continent is a choreography of films, foams and filaments—slow chemistries that trade motion for permanence and talk to one another in temperature, pH and light.

1.  The Architects: laminar guilds beneath every plate  
Look with a lens at the underside of a loose shard and you will see a stack-order that never quite repeats, for three separate microbial consortia take turns adding pages to the crust.

•  Initiators (pale-green cyanids) bloom first when a fresh wet film spreads across mud.  They fix a rush of carbon, supersaturating the skin with carbonate that rains out as a dusting of chalk.  
•  Weavers (silicats) follow hours later, exuding ribbons of polysaccharide that stiffen into glassy gel.  The ribbons net the chalk, trap more silica from the evaporating brine, and seal Oxygen inside tiny vesicles like bubble-wrap.  
•  Cappers (umber fermentors) arrive last.  They gorge on residue, exhale CO₂ and heat, then die of their own fever.  Their carcasses flatten into a caramel varnish that sun-bakes hard enough to bear the next generation.

Each layer is no thicker than a banknote; yet in places a thousand notes are pressed together, and the crust still grows from beneath.  The mild warmth your palm feels in a buckle is the fermentors’ dying gift—an exothermic pulse that powers the trinity upward one more lamina.

2.  Seam-Flora: the copper stalks  
Where stress cracks open a hairline, spores from an entirely different lineage sift down on the dusk breeze.  They belong to the Capillophytes, the “silica-bamboo” plants already glimpsed above ground.

Morphology: a single stem, jointed at six- to eight-centimetre intervals; skin of glaucous copper that contains nano-plates of mica acting as sun-mirrors.  
Feeding: at dawn they unfurl cap bands—thin circumferential slits that drink condensed dew by capillary suction, then close before full sun.  Photosynthesis is relegated to twilight; in daytime the plant enters a metabolic trance only five degrees above ambient.  
Reproduction: each sunset the topmost joint fractures with an audible click, launching a cloud of flat silicate seeds.  Because the plant grows always upward from the fracture zone, it is, with every evening, a millimetre shorter: a standing fountain that spends itself grain by grain.

Capillophytes never crowd.  Their roots would meet if two shoots germinated within a metre, and root contact releases a mutual inhibitor—so any seed that lands too close aborts.  Distance is the forest here.

3.  The Vapor Stratum: storm-plankton  
When midday convection snaps the sky into towering storm cells, both architects’ spores and capillophyte seeds are sucked aloft.  Inside the cold cloud they cohabit with a third guild, the Nivocytes—minute ice-shell organisms descended from the weavers.  They precipitate around themselves a spike-ball of frost that bristles with protein needles.  Each needle is a lightning rod; a single bolt vaporises the shell, blasting the resting cell clear of the cloud to scatter on the plain as invisible hail.  Thus the microbes themselves seed the rain that wets their future building site.

4.  The Pore-Sea: life inside the plates  
Between older laminae lie brine-filled micropores, too small for light but large enough for chemolitho autotrophs that mine trapped oxygen bubbles and dissolved silicates.  They never rise to the surface; instead they tunnel laterally, changing the refractive index of the plate.  At dusk their labyrinths fluoresce—hence the faint amber glow travellers see after sunset.  The light is neither heat nor fire but the last actinic photons these cave-plankton captured twelve hours earlier, re-emitted as they purge excess energy before night’s cold clamps their enzymes.

5.  Relations and cycles  
•  The architects create the pavement but depend on the cloud plankton to reseed new mud—without the vertical loop, diversity would collapse into monoculture and the crust would craze to dust.  
•  The capillophytes cannot sprout unless a crack stays open through one full noon; the heat of the architects’ fermentors slows closure just long enough.  
•  Nivocytes introduce nitrates by fixing lightning energy inside their ice shells; runoff from the first two minutes of any rainstorm contains the planet’s whole bio-available nitrogen.

6.  In lieu of animals: pulses, pings and perfumes  
There are no jaws here, but there is predation.  When a weaver film senses the tell-tale musk of fermentor decay it floods its gel with hydrogen peroxide, bleaching the surface and killing late-arriving rivals; the peroxide breaks down minutes later, leaving a sterilised slate for initiators.  The tenuous pop you hear at evening—the plate-field’s descending chord—is partly mechanical, partly the mass venting of peroxide from a million such chemical duels.

The only scents are functional:  
•  petrichor edged with vinegar (initiators’ start signal),  
•  hot paraffin (capillophyte inhibitors),  
•  faint ozone (nivocyte lightning blooms).

7.  What you would call “individuals”  
Hold a thumb-sized chip of plate to starlight and you cradle a metropolis: tens of species, all transient.  In a month the chip will be powder again, recycled into newer, higher pavement.  Nothing here lives long, but every lineage is effectively immortal, braided into processes instead of bodies.

So the tabletop continent is not lifeless; it is merely post-animal.  Its citizens are mats that lithify, stalks that walk only upward by discarding themselves, and clouds that hatch thunder to ferry spores.  They write their history as stone and read it back each dawn in the thin water they are allowed.  To visit is to stand on a living book, listening to pages turn beneath your feet.
