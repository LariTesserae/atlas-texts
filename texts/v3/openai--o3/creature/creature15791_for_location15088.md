WHO OR WHAT LIVES HERE

1. The Last Engineer  
   • Names: among the lowland monasteries she is called the Last Engineer, the Mist-Hinged, or simply the Woman Who Does Not Dry.  
   • Provenance: once chief hydrologist of the ridge-top laboratory whose scrap still plates the floor. The personnel manifest lists her age at disappearance as thirty-four; the mountain, by its own slower calendar, says that was eighty-one seasons ago.  
   • Body: prolonged immersion in mineral vapors has pickled rather than aged her. Skin the colour of steamed paraffin, hair a tangle of nickel filaments that grew when ordinary keratin learned to incorporate the battery dust drifting through the air. Joints wired with living creeper—green articulations that photosynthesise through translucent flesh. Lungs spliced to a web of orchid roots that hang, curtain-like, from her ribs and wick water straight from the humid air. When she exhales, orchid mouths open and close, showing orange throats.  
   • Clothing & Tools: a maintenance harness of braided vine and salvaged cable, forty-three pockets perpetually wet. Contents: copper punches for resetting the turbine governors; a jar of salamander eggs to test water purity; a roll of waxed schematics so swollen with moisture they resemble sea sponges; three bone tuning forks whose pitches match the mountain’s geothermal pulse.  
   • Work: each waking cycle (she sleeps in ten-minute floating trances inside the pool) she walks the circumference, tapping rails, tightening a valve by a quarter-turn, loosening another. She times everything to the turbines’ single slow revolution. If a drip acquires an off-beat, she sings—soft, vowel-heavy notes that persuade the water back into rhythm.  
   • Speech & Memory: language remains, but pared down: “Flow,” “Quiet,” “Strain,” “Wait.” Longer thoughts she stores in droplets. She exhales onto a cool slab, lets condensation bloom, then nudges the bead along engraved channels that correspond to ideas only she recalls how to read. By evening the droplet has fallen and the thought is released back into the general noise of the bowl.  
   • Motive: custodianship, nothing larger. The system is her own extended heartbeat; any change in its rhythm is palpitations in her chest. She is not immortal—merely indefinitely suspended in a problem she refuses to declare solved.

2. The Clock-Salamanders  
   • Form: translucent, wafer-thin amphibians banded in white quartz veins. Hearts pulse once every four minutes, perfectly syncing with the turbine housings.  
   • Diet: micro-shrimp that bloom in the thermal rills; flakes of basalt exfoliated by temperature shock.  
   • Function: living gauges. Their skins flush crimson hours before a steam vent surges; the Engineer watches them and adjusts valves accordingly.  
   • Reproduction: a single egg clutch per decade, glued to the warm brass of pressure tanks. When the compressors fire, the vibration cracks the eggs and the newborns slide directly into the overflow sluice, beginning life in the dark pipes before wriggling out to daylight months later.

3. The Condenser Swallows  
   • Not true birds but composite organisms: hollow-boned vertebrates whose wing membranes are moss. Feathers replaced by overlapping scales of iridescent lichen that drink the mist.  
   • Behaviour: at dawn they fly laps around the rim, scooping airborne minerals which precipitate on their stomach lining as crystalline grit. Each evening they regurgitate this grit into the pool; the trace elements keep the water electrically conductive enough for the hydro generators to bite.  
   • Call: a single click, like a stone cooling—never more, lest echo clutter the acoustic field the Engineer uses to diagnose hidden fractures.

4. Root-Servos  
   • Discovery: look beneath any slab of hammered steel and one finds bundles of cambium and phosphor bronze braided together, shuttling nutrients and current with equal indifference.  
   • Mobility: when a plate corrodes beyond usefulness, the bundle detaches, seeks a fresher seam, nails it down with new rootlets, then grows a veneer of cellulose over the metal until only a living board remains.  
   • Cognition: none individual; in chorus they maintain a slow, plant-paced map of load stresses. The Engineer sometimes lays her ear to them, harvesting a tremor of information we would call seismic data and she calls simply “murmur.”

5. The Vapour Micro-colony  
   • Composition: aerosolised archaea, magnetite dust, and shredded snippets of the laboratory’s archive DNA.  
   • Habitat: the mist itself, each droplet a drifting library cell.  
   • Activity: fragments recombine, edit, and separate with every condensation cycle. Most chains are nonsense, but rare alignments encode whole blueprints, memories, dreams. On nights when geothermal flux is high, phrases precipitate on the pinnacles in rime-ice letters; by sunrise they melt, rereleasing the text. The Engineer reads them when she can, though she never says what they tell her.

SEEN BY A VISITOR

Approach in the turbine’s rest interval. Climb the last stair. You will first notice the swallows, then the salamander prints, then—only if you pause—the woman standing knee-deep in the pool, vines trailing from her ribs, rotating a fly-wheel half a degree. She will not speak unless you touch the machinery. If you do, she may gesture once: a flat palm meaning Wait, or a crook of the finger meaning Follow.

Follow and she will set you small, necessary tasks: re-seat a loose valve core, scrape calcium from an intake grille. She has no concept of payment but will allow you a night’s sleep on a warm plate the size of a coffin lid. In the morning you will wake glazed in dew. The turbines will grunt, the salamanders will drink, and the Engineer will already be on the far side of the bowl, walking her slow orbit, leaving wet footprints that sprout moss before they fade.

Stay longer, and changes begin:

• Day 3: your fingernails silver at the edges; the vapor’s nickel ions enter the keratin.  
• Day 5: language feels bulky; you catch yourself answering echoes rather than originating speech.  
• Day 9: dreams arrive scored to the rhythm of a single, massive heartbeat—iron on iron, water on stone. Somewhere in those dreams the Engineer is younger, laughing, telling you the system will prove itself in just another week or two.  
• Day 12: lichen freckles bloom along your shoulders in patterns that match the circuit etching on the old control boards.

Few stay beyond this. Most descend while they still remember why dryness matters. Those who remain—one every generation—join the Engineer without ceremony. Their bodies shift, their pulses slow, their names erode. Outsiders later mistake them for her, and in a way that is correct: the custodianship is a relay, its baton passed from wet hand to wetter hand, endlessly, until the range at last finishes cooling and the need for guardians is gone.

Until then, the bowl is lived in—not by a population, but by a single, manifold caretaker: woman, vine, amphibian, swallow, root, mist, all turning together at the pace of newborn stone.
