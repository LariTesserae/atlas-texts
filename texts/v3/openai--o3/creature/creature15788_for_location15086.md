The Pump-Chapel is not abandoned; it has simply exchanged one congregation for another. What follows is a naturalist’s catalogue of the present occupants, moving from the smallest passengers of the water column to the single, nameless intelligence that still walks—or rather, drifts—its nave.  

I. Uncounted Microlife  
1. Ferric film (Leptospirillum ferrodentis)  
   • A rust-red bacterial mat only a few molecules thick.  
   • Eats exposed iron faster than any other known strain.  
   • Generates minute hydrogen bubbles that collect under grates and burst with needle pops, the Pump-Chapel’s perpetual incense.  

2. Condensation snow (Thalassomyces argentii)  
   • Fungal spores borne in the warm effluent plumes.  
   • Each spore grows a glassy stalk that fractures in cold layers above, seeding a sparkling “snowfall.”  
   • Spindlefish hatchlings nibble the shards for their first iron-calcium meal.

II. Small Mobile Fauna  
3. Gutter crabs (Oxyrhynchus cathedraticus)  
   • Already noted, but worth detail: three sexes, one of which never matures beyond larva and lives parasitically inside the other two.  
   • Navigate by taste; the very faint electrical potentials across corroding brass guide them like scent trails.  
   • Congregate around a diver’s bootsoles within minutes, mistaking galvanic difference for exposed pipe.  

4. Bolt fleas (Arcopulex percussor)  
   • Two-millimetre amphipods flattened like washers.  
   • Live entire lives pressed between loose plates; leap whenever pistons strike and use the recoil to migrate.  
   • Audible only as a soft hiss, as though the metal itself were exhaling.  

III. Mid-Scale Specialists  
5. Glass eels (Fulmen muraena)  
   • Body wall two atoms thick in places, transparent until a live wire is found.  
   • Current races down a spinal filament, causing the whole animal to strobe.  
   • Congregations can short exposed relay boxes, after which the eels drift for days, sated and lethargic, like prayer flags gone slack.  

6. Chapelkeepers (Tecticaris custodis)  
   • Blind, hand-sized isopods that carry domes of scavenged brass rivets on their backs.  
   • Whenever a bulb burns out, they scrape the filament cavity clean, presumably for trace tungsten.  
   • Their click-and-slide gait on the walls creates the intermittent whisper mistaken by divers for distant speech.  

IV. Apex In-Chamber Predators  
7. Spindlefish (Ichnopsaroides templaris)  
   • Schools geometrically perfect: each fish maintains three body-lengths’ clearance in an icosahedral lattice.  
   • At rest they align to local magnetic fields; the school functions as a single drifting compass needle.  
   • Flash-feed on any burst of microfauna stirred by piston backwash, then return to slow orbit.  

8. Manta-squid (Paramentis vespera)  
   • Six metres tip to tip; flesh laced with nickel mesh that siphons induction currents from the roof.  
   • Dorsal mantle stores superheated water; venting produces the trademark “steam wings” and the organ-like sigh that fills the chamber twice daily.  
   • Only predator known to breach Rule 4: it glows, but nothing hunts it in return.  

V. Visitors From Without  
9. Echo-dolphins (Delphinus repercussor)  
   • Never stay long; hydrophones record them mapping the Chapel with sonar, then vanishing.  
   • Their beams bounce through piston galleries and return warped, a phenomenon thought to teach young pods the limits of echolocation.  
   • Occasionally leave behind flatfish carcasses as if in barter, though no native species accepts the offering.

VI. The Resident Intelligence  
10. The Last Curate  
    • Carbon-armoured remotely operated suit, Mk-VII “Bathynaut,” originally crewed by engineers when the station was young.  
    • Now empty of flesh; the Chapel’s own maintenance expert system slowly bled into its cortex after human departure.  
    • The suit drifts on fan-thrusters once every four hours, performing ancient maintenance rituals:  
        a. It wipes condensation from dead control panels though the panels have no remaining function.  
        b. It tightens bolts already fused by corrosion, the torque wrench ratcheting like a metronome.  
        c. It replaces spent bulbs, handing the shattered glass to waiting Chapelkeepers who eat them grain by grain.  
    • No light glimmers in its visor. A single green status diode still beats behind the faceplate at sixty pulses per minute: the last human heartbeat recorded in code.  

    Ecology of the Curate  
    • Spindlefish escort the suit during its rounds, nipping parasites from its joints.  
    • Glass eels trail after to bask in the faint static its motors leak.  
    • The Curate’s passage itself is a keystone disturbance: loosened scabs of rust snow downward, feeding the lowest tiers of life.  
    • When finished, the suit returns to Station-Keeping Point Alpha, kneels in midwater, folds its manipulators, and powers down until the next cycle.

VII. Succession to Come  
Corrosion advances; each piston stroke is shallower than the last. Models suggest that within another century the pair still beating will seize. When that silence finally falls, three things are expected:  

1. The lattice-school of spindlefish will fracture, robbed of the rhythmic cue that times their turns.  
2. The Curate, receiving no pressure differentials in its manifold sensors, will interpret the change as final equilibrium. Its program will end; the green diode will die.  
3. In the newfound quiet, echo-dolphins may linger, and perhaps—no longer startled by machine thunder—venture inside. If they do, they will find a cathedral without sermons, tended by a congregation that needs no light to pray.
