WHO (AND WHAT) CALLS THE LEDGE HOME

I. THE QUIET-FOOT PEOPLE  
Locals down-valley lump them all together as “hut-keepers,” but the beings who raise those corbelled shelters are a braided handful of lineages, not a single tribe.

1. Stone-Rafters  
• Shape and stature: Human-tall yet unnervingly narrow, as if mountain air has planed their breadth away. Knucklebones show even when they smile.  
• Skin and scent: Skin the grey-blue of wet schist, flecked with mica–their elders say they are born that colour, though in truth they rub powdered rock into the pores of newborns so predators mistake them for outcrop. A faint smell of crushed thyme follows them; it keeps midges from the eyes.  
• Livelihood: They farm the ledge the way sailors farm a deck—tying, splicing, reefing life into the stone. With hooked antler awls they tease moss cushions apart, tuck in spores of new species, then lash the patch shut with root fiber so the next gale cannot peel it.  
• Seasons: They appear for seventy-two sunrises on either side of the summer solstice and again for only nine after the equinox, keeping an agreement with the mist leopard none will explain.  
• Speech and silence: Voices pitched so low they ride below the wind’s shriek; listeners press wrist bones to each other’s throats to feel the words as vibration.  
• Custom most travelers notice: Every night they unlace the hide roofs of their huts and fold them back so starlight can touch the hearth ash. “Stone eats night,” they say, and believe the practice keeps the mountain from hungering for larger meals.

2. Vapor-Kin  
• Origin: Not born but condensed. When an evening updraft is rich with spores of sundew and mineral charge, a kind of velvet frost forms against the cliff, coalescing into knee-high figures shaped like bare willow wands wrapped in fog.  
• Lifespan: Two, maybe three days—longer if drizzle continues.  
• Behavior: They drift through the dwarf birches collecting beads of exuded water in thumb-thick bladders of chilled air. At saturation they return to a fissure behind the huts and release the hoarded droplets in a sound like distant chimes. Some think that ringing is what dams the subterranean rivers from bursting onto the ledge all at once.  
• Intelligence: Debated. They appear to read the posted offerings: take meat, leave stones arranged in prime-number piles; never disturb tools. A conie that strays near them emerges with fur standing on end, as if gently shocked.  
• Death: When sunfire reaches them they sublimate, leaving behind perfect filigree shells of ice that survive a single breath before shattering.

3. Roof-Sleepers (Sky-Goat Keepers)  
• Build nothing; they use the huts only while mending glider-hooves.  
• Bodies: Barrel-chested, arms as long as legs, shoulders set high so the whole frame resembles a slanted capital A—the architecture of someone who has climbed so long that rest feels unsafe.  
• Language: Whistles that imitate katabatic wind; every sentence ends on a down-spiral so predators do not track it upward.  
• Tool of note: A looped cord of braided sky-goat beard hair. The loop is thrown around a goat’s midsection, the free end anchored to a root. The animal leaps, rappels, grazes—then claws back up the cord when finished. Goat and keeper treat the rope as shared limb.  
• Lore: They claim the ledge is a bridge half-finished by titans: “When the other half appears, we’ll walk to the next world on hooves and harness.” Until then they stay, riding each electric charge as sailors read the sky for omens.

II. CREATURES ALL OTHER LEDGES LACK

1. Rock-Conies (“Tail-Rabbits”)  
Appearance: Rabbit weight, marmot sturdiness, with a furless, grippy tail that forks at the tip like tweezers. The fork locks around lichen sheets; a startled conie can hang suspended, body flapping like a pennant, until danger passes.  
Social order: Nests are stacked in scooped stone bowls, five layers high. The topmost layer sleeps; each lower layer keeps watch for something different—sky, slope, shadow, tremor. Only when all four watches grunt in sequence may the colony feed.  
Mating: Rumbles felt more than heard; partners drum hind feet on schist until the mountain answers with a sympathetic vibration. Conies claim the stone’s reply as wedding blessing.

2. Sky-Goats (“Ridge Gliders”)  
Anatomy: Shoulders wide, hips narrow, body flattened side-to-side like a loaf set on its long edge. Hooves splay into four independent toes; between them stretch membranes that let the animal parachute short distances.  
Feeding range: A vertical lawn no wider than a banner, hidden from eagles by mist. They graze upward, leap outward, glide down, and begin again.  
Defense: The click of toe bones locking before a glide warns eagles that the goat is about to become uncatchable. Even the slate-backs respect a practiced clicker.

3. Glacial Gulls (“Ice-Clad Choir”)  
Plumage: Primary feathers edged in translucent keratin; back-lighting turns a banking flock into a windowed roof of stained glass.  
Song: Not the stereotypic gull scream but a six-note antiphon learned from echoes of the timber horns in the valley. They rehearse against the cliff until they can answer a horn blast before its own echo fades, a duet across kilometres of air.  
Service to the ledge: They raid dragonfly swarms when the insects grow too many and begin stripping sundews—the ledge’s natural gnat-catchers—of prey. The huts go white with droppings, yes, but sundews stay fed.

4. Slate-Back Eagles (“Fog Razors”)  
Vision: Ultraviolet-shifted; they track urine trails left by nervous conies, which glow to their eyes like fresh snow.  
Wing-loading: Low enough that a stoop begun in sunlight can finish in thick cloud where prey thinks itself hidden.  
Rite of passage: A juvenile must seize, kill, and carry a cloud-coated stone of its own body weight to the opposite wall of the gorge, releasing it so the rock’s crash signals the bird’s adulthood to every ear on the ledge.

5. Mist Leopard  
Outline: Forever indistinct; what is known is compiled from aftermath—a tail mark in frost, a single auricular tuft left in a birch crotch, the half-gulped roar that stops wind itself.  
Hypotheses:  
a. A true cat that hunts by refracting light through hollow guard hairs, bending its own appearance.  
b. A polypore fungus infecting discarded goat hides and puppeteering them with pressurized steam.  
c. Mountain lightning given muscle for the span of a charged night.  
Evidence fits any theory and none. What all agree on: when fresh blood darkens the ledge, a hush like air in a vacuum precedes the leopard’s coming.

III. THE TINY, THE PLANTED, THE HALF-REAL

1. Sundew Choirlets  
• Each ruby tentacle ends in a microscopic silica plate that clacks shut at ultrasound frequencies. When slate-back eagles dive, the wind shear makes subaudible keens; sundews clap in answer, a ripple of microscopic applause that rolls the length of the ledge like a tossed pebble parting water.  
• Some Stone-Rafters swear the plant noise confuses eagle echolocation, sparing conies. Others insist it is simply the garden cheering its own drama.

2. Moss Batteries  
• Physiology: Cells rich in pyro-phosphates; capillary action drags trace metals from schist into living mats, essentially wiring each cushion as a slow, damp voltaic pile.  
• Role in the “charged days”: When atmospheric ions surge, the moss stores them, preventing lightning strikes. Walkers feel the hair-raising tingle as the batteries reach capacity; if one were to scrape away the mat at that moment, the discharge could lance straight to the core of the mountain—hence Rule 5, Do Not Dig.

3. Vein-Sprites  
• Particle-sized life—silica skeletons shaped like twelve-pointed stars.  
• Drift in the seeps, emitting femtosecond flashes of pale blue. To the naked eye the water only glimmers; under slow-motion crystal viewers (a Stone-Rafter invention) the ledge becomes a night sky turned upside-down, constellations pouring out of cracks.

IV. PATTERNS OF COEXISTENCE

1. The Annual Day of Hollow Light  
When the sun lifts just enough to send a flat beam across the gulf yet still hides its disc behind the opposing range, every ledge inhabitant—people, conie, goat, gull, eagle—casts a shadow that floats two metres above the rock. No one knows why. Quiet-Foot children chase their own silhouettes, vapor-kin try to hug theirs and dissipate, eagles refuse to fly. By dusk the phenomenon is gone for another turning of the world.

2. Trade Network  
• Conie Lichen → harvested by Stone-Rafters → fed to penned sky-goats during molting → beard hair shed → braided into roof-sleeper harnesses.  
• Goat membrane trimmings → stretched over sapling frames → gifted to Vapor-Kin at birth; the membranes slow evaporation, giving each fog-child an extra half-day of life.  
• Eagle-dropped bones → gnawed by conies for calcium → leave tooth-gouged runes the hut-keepers read as weather auguries.  
Thus the circle holds, each creature an artisan or resource for the next.

3. The One Thing Shared by All  
Every resident, whether birthed, condensed, or blow-borne, seems to agree on music. When wind calms after a charged day, the entire population releases held breath in sound—gull antiphon, conie drum, human throat-song, even the sipping noise of sundews feeding. For three, maybe four heartbeats the noises line-up into a chord as old as schist. Then the katabatic scream returns and the chord is lost, but memory of it keeps dwellers loyal to the ledge.

V. IF YOU MEET THEM

• Bring no brass. The metal draws sparks from moss batteries and unsettles the Vapor-Kin.  
• Accept the first thing offered, even if it is only a pebble. Inside hut etiquette, gifting flows outward like ripples; refuse and you jam the circle.  
• Match breathing to the distant drum of meltwater—three counts in, five out. The Stone-Rafters teach children this rhythm before they teach walking; keep it and you will feel, rather than hear, the ledge warn of rockfall seconds before the first pebble skitters.  
• Leave by dawn on the third day unless explicitly invited; that is when the ledge recalibrates its charge, and any body without established place in the pattern risks being counted as loose stone.

In that way the community of granite, mist, leaf and blood continues: a tapestry stretched across nothing, woven by hands and paws and forces you can taste only as metal on the tongue, held aloft mid-mountain by mutual need and the slow electric heartbeat of water inside stone.
