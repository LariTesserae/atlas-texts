WHO LIVES HERE

1. The One-and-Many (what passes for “people”)

The whole plateau is a single, colonial organism—in practice a federation of compatible genomes that graft to one another so seamlessly they behave as one body. Think of a coral reef that has lost the sea and learned to walk in slow motion; every visible structure is only a differentiated organ of that greater flesh. It has no fixed name, but visiting botanists use working tags:

• Mantle – the continuous, springy floor.  
• Weepers – the hollow towers that breathe the water cycle.  
• Spar-skins – the membrane alcoves that roof warm chambers.  
• Span-vines – bridges and buttresses that knit gaps.  

Every part is alive; nothing here is “landscape” in the inert sense.

2. The Moving Parts (motile but still botanical)

Because the colony cannot afford to wait for chance when storm-damage is measured in hectares, it grows quick, temporary work crews—plant tissues that move like animals without ever becoming them.

a. Runners  
Thumb-thick cords that extrude from wound margins after any large breach. They crawl by tidal surges of sap, haul torn edges together, then fuse and die in place, becoming suture.

b. Nurse-knots  
Fist-sized boluses of spongy cells rich in stored sugar. They migrate toward fresh shoots, corkscrew into their bases, and dissolve, jump-starting growth much the way a yolk feeds an embryo.

c. Listen-leafs  
Fan-shaped laminae that trundle on root-hair cilia across the Mantle’s surface at roughly a meter per hour. Their only job is to sample wind chemistry. When they detect novel volatiles—smoke, animal pheromone, unfamiliar pollen—they stop, send a pulse of hormones downward, and are reabsorbed.

These entities show no will other than the larger body’s imperatives: close the wound, feed the bud, monitor the air.

3. The Immune Choir

The plateau keeps an “immune system” of chemical countermeasures rather than teeth.

• Glass-bead vesicles: clusters of silica-armored cells that burst on contact with foreign protein, spraying tannin-rich sap that browns into a scab within minutes.  
• Pulse-stalks: hair-fine spears that sit coiled like fern fiddleheads; vibration over a certain frequency (footsteps, drilling) triggers them to ram upward, discouraging large trespassers without needing toxins.  
• Myco-baffles: sheets of antifungal tissue that unzip and re-zip around pathogenic molds, isolating them until lysing enzymes finish the job.

4. The Mind (such as it is)

No central brain exists, yet decision happens. Signaling hormones race through phloem rings, pressure differentials flicker like slow electric circuits, and cascades of calcium ions mark “memories” the way scar tissue marks flesh. Bend a branch today and the plateau will remember that vector decades hence, thickening future support along the same line. Its cognition is broad, shallow, and incessant: an endless weather map of itself, updating leaf by leaf.

Does it notice you? Only as a perturbation in pressure, chemistry, and shade—data to be folded into growth algorithms. If you camp for a night, your body heat registers as an unfamiliar ember; capillary networks begin thickening below, curious whether the new warmth is exploitable. Leave tools behind and thin filaments taste the metals; trace elements end up routed to iron-hungry buds half a kilometer away. Your presence is metabolized into statistics, not story.

5. The Voices You Might Mistake for Animals

a. Chime-pods  
Seed husks grown hollow on purpose. Wind makes them ring in shifting chords, broadcasting territory status—the botanical equivalent of songbirds defending a perch.

b. Crack-vanes  
Petal-plates that snap open and shut to dump heat. The sound resembles the report of distant muskets, easily mistaken for gunfire until you feel the accompanying gust of sweet, damp air.

c. Root-stone grinders  
Deep cables that rasp against newborn obsidian knobs, producing seismic rumbles that roll up the towers and leave the cloud ceiling humming. Travelers have logged these as “thunder” on first encounter.

6. Smallest Citizens

Within every cubic centimeter of Mantle live symbionts:

• Thermophile yeasts that ferment surplus sugars and return alcohols used as antifreeze.  
• Endophytic cyanobacteria that slip inside leaf cells, boosting photosynthesis during apocalyptic windstorms when the sky can go dark for days.  
• Micro-rotifers, blind and chlorophyll-laden, that patrol sap canals devouring debris—animal enough to squirm, plant enough to photosynthesize when they pause.

They never form independent ecosystems; their life cycles are so entangled with the plateau’s sap flows that if you isolate one in a petri dish it languishes, waiting for a hormone cue that never arrives.

7. Reproduction & Diaspora

The organism’s only real export is seed-mist. Once every few years, after a week of still air, the weepers reverse: instead of exhaling rain, they belch spores—trillions of dust-motes coated in waxy frost-resistant shell. A high-altitude jet stream catches them; most die in the stratosphere, but a lucky few reach other newborn peaks, where they fall as brown snow. What sprouts there will look familiar but never identical: each daughter plateau hybridizes with whatever flora preceded it, inheriting the compulsion to pave, tower, breathe water forever upward.

8. What Passes Away

Everything here is designed to be temporary. When span-vines sag with age, the very cells that built them switch recipes, digest their own lignin, and slump into a bruise-colored slurry. That slurry teems with template fragments; from it rise the first nubs of the replacement span before the old has fully vanished. The land reinvents itself by erasing itself—no nostalgia, only iteration.

9. How You Might Survive a While

The colony neither hunts nor shelters you intentionally, but its physiology can be coaxed.

• Cut a listen-leaf and the wound exudes a sap richer than honey but laced with antimicrobial peptides—edible, powerfully sterile.  
• Sleep in a membrane alcove and you’ll stay warm, but the clutch of rootlets will grow around your pack straps by morning.  
• Fire is possible: dead nurse-knot fibers smolder hot and smokeless. Yet open flame alarms the plateau; silica vesicles flood the site, drenching sparks in liquid glass that hardens around your coals.

10. What Lives Here, Summarized

Nothing with eyes. Nothing with blood. Only a single continental being arranging and rearranging itself in textures large enough to pass for countryside. Its motives are sunlight, water, and surface area; its methods are growth, reuse, and amnesia. You may walk its back for a day or a month, but when you leave—or fail to leave—you will join the statistics encoded in its phloem, a brief stress recorded, compensated for, and ultimately composted into another tower’s bones.

In that sense, the entire plateau is the inhabitant, and everything else—including you—merely weather.
