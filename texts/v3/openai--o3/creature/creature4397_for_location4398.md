Who (and what) calls Nadrel home

1. The scaffolders – people born to altitude and iron  
• Build and breathe vertically. A native child learns to climb before she can walk; parents lash the nursery cot to a travelling hoist so the infant’s inner ear grows fluent in shifting gravity. Lungs are roomy, ribs sprung wide; blood carries an excess of iron borrowed from the ever-present dust, giving lips and fingernails a faint gun-metal sheen.  
• Clothing: overlapping panels of waxed canvas and chain-stitched felt—garments that can be buttoned into a wind-sock, a sling seat, or a rain hood without coming off the body. Everyone owns at least one magnetic glove for catching emergency handholds on girders.  
• Names record elevation rather than lineage. “Riss Three-Seventeen” once lived on girder three, tower seventeen; when she moves, the number changes but “Riss” endures. Outsiders hear the streets renumber themselves every generation and assume chaos; locals hear autobiography.  
• They are polite in the way load-bearing bolts are polite: nothing decorative, everything dependable. A promise is referred to as a “weld.” Breaking one is “going brittle” and can strand you socially as surely as stepping onto a frayed suspension bridge.

2. Orders and callings  
Because space is carved more often than discovered, profession blurs into citizenship.

• Seam-wrights  
  Track the reality “drift” the way sailors track tides. They wear mirrored goggles to catch the hairline shimmer that precedes a local law of physics slipping out of true. When an east catwalk starts to lean fifteen degrees dream-ward, seam-wrights chalk new reference lines, hang weights, whisper triangulation prayers, and leave the bridge perfectly intact yet undeniably elsewhere.

• Steam-nurses  
  Not medics but engineers of the city’s great piston hearts. They describe temperature with familial language: “The third boiler’s sulking,” or “That compensator’s newly in love—hear it?” To mechs left idle too long, a nurse will read aloud until the flywheels settle into a restful purr.

• Moss-barbers  
  Groom the living green that prevents sheer walls from flashing floodwater straight into homes. They carry silvered sickles, tea-flasks of dilute fertilizer, and gossip that propagates faster than fern spores.

• Echo-keepers  
  Quarry the cliffs for usable silence. When the chorus raptors’ metallic song has charged the air to the point conversation kinks sideways, an echo-keeper inserts felt baffles into a crack, waits while resonance drains, then harvests the muffled stone for acoustic tiles sold to libraries and lovers.

3. Familiar strangeness — the more-than-human residents  

• Cloud-goats have already been noted, but city law treats them as citizens of the “third footing.” They pay their taxes in droppings rich with rare minerals; children collect and trade the pellets like marbles.

• Vapor skimmers are individually simple but commune through the mist. Let five rest in the same updraft and they braid their thin voices into a parliament of weather predictions. Dockmasters consult those choirs before scheduling freight lighter than a goat.

• Furnace wyrms (misnamed; they are headless cords of silicone cartilage) nest inside the still-warm lava ribs. By concentrating in coils they remain above freezing and emit a gentle red light. Some towers run crimson lantern-lines between ledges—what visitors think is clever civic design is only the wyrms out for a stretch.

• The Angle – not a species but a mood the land occasionally wears. Where gravity kinks and objects fall sideways, the Angle condenses as a glimmer six heartbeats tall, faceted like splintered quartz. It never speaks yet negotiates by presence alone. Locals leave offerings of plumb bobs and level bars; on good nights the Angle balances them edge-upright in gratitude.

4. Social weave  
A feast here is measured in echoes, not courses. Guests bring bronze bowls; a host stands on a balcony and ladles soup thirty metres down into each bowl in turn. If the stream of broth remains unbroken, everyone applauds the cook’s “vertical palate.” Courting couples test compatibility by riding opposite ends of a freight hoist: able partners adjust valves in perfect sync, rising and falling without jostling the cargo in between.

5. Cycles, rites, repairs  

• When a ledge shears away (it happens several times a decade) the cliff rings like a cathedral bell. Work halts mid-hammer-stroke; people listen to the note’s decay to gauge casualties. If the pitch drops cleanly, the void was uninhabited and the city exhales. A ragged note triggers the Rescue Cantilever: rope brigades, semaphore lines, stretchers folded from kite spars.

• The Festival of First Condensate opens the wet season. Residents carry empty glasses to rooftop gutters, catching the inaugural drip. To swallow that water is considered a voluntary renewal of citizenship—much like taking the oath again, but colder.

• Mourning is vertical too. Ashes are mixed with hydraulic grease and painted onto drive chains. The departed quite literally keep the city moving; every squeak unheard is a hymn in their honor.

6. Outsiders and the negotiation of arrival  
No gate, no wall, only the rail that hauled you here, yet entry is conditional on a courtesy: newcomers must answer one question honestly—“Where do you believe down is?”—then walk to the indicated edge and drop a pebble. However the stone chooses to fall, the city accepts that you and it will disagree about something fundamental, and the disagreement is welcomed as fresh data.

7. What the place whispers about its inhabitants  
Stand long enough among the scaffolders and a pattern surfaces: nothing is wasted, not a rivet, not a promise, not a stray physical law. They have learned to inhabit incompleteness the way others inhabit finished houses. Their lives are prototypes forever iterated; their joys are test-runs that prove sturdy enough to keep. Ask them, finally, what they are building—and they will shrug, gesture to the cliffs, to the clouds trying on sunset and dawn at once, and say, “A place that can stay unfinished without ever being undone.”
