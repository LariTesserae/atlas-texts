LIFE, IN ORDER OF HOW LIKELY YOU ARE TO NOTICE IT

1. The Flooring  
   a. River-glass Algae – A single-celled film that plates every wetted surface. Under magnification each cell looks like a trilobed coin of pale jade. At noon they refract light downward into the feldspar, warming the rock from within and powering capillary convection that keeps the puddles oxygen-rich. They are the unthanked engineers of the hollow; without them the water would go septic in a day.  
   b. Suede Silt – Not inert dust but a democracy of spore-seeds, cysts and molted skins, all electrostatically interlocked. Most of the other organisms spend one phase of life as a grain of this “soil,” waiting for some angle of sunlight or taste of chemistry to tell them which body to build next.

2. The Water That Crawls  
   a. Hemistomata (“half-mouths”) – Transparent arthropods 200–400 µm long. From the side they resemble punctuation marks, a comma with jointed legs. They carry a wedge of vacuum inside their thorax; when threatened they invert themselves, releasing the bubble and snapping backward the width of a finger-nail. A school of hemistomata in panic looks like effervescent champagne.  
   b. Clock-Shrimps – An instar of the hemistomata, reached once every 43 days when the moonless night aligns with high humidity. They fuse into radial wheels of a dozen individuals, legs outward, intestines braided into a single peristaltic ring. The wheel rolls across the puddle, skimming biofilm and redistributing nutrients. At dawn the wheel falls apart into identical, solitary commas again.

3. The Air That Seethes  
   a. Vapor-Gnats – Winged hexapods smaller than an eyelash. They do not beat their wings; instead they ride amplitudes of evaporative lift, trimming tilt with microscopic vanes. Their hemolymph is supersaturated with water; they exhale mist as ballast, making each ascent a visible exclamation mark of vapor. They converse chemophonically, releasing molecules that crackle faintly when they oxidize—only other gnats can hear the static.  
   b. Thread-Darters – The “motes” you glimpse only because something else avoids them. They look like living wishbones of glass, bodies drawn by surface tension rather than flesh. Their wings are Möbius strips only one molecular layer thick; each half-twist refracts light into the ultraviolet, which blinds most predators made of matter but lights a runway for predators made of light.

4. The Light That Hunts  
   a. Photofeather Fry – The surface-skimming fish. Anatomically they are brine-shrimp slender, but each body is written in standing photon waves locked by the hoops’ ultraviolet spark. They paddle with interference fringes that behave like fins. Inside their “guts” real glucose molecules shuttle, phagocytosed from prey that had mass a moment earlier. When a cloud blots the sun the fry collapse into droplets of sugar water, which the coma-shrimps devour.  
   b. Mirror-Pike – One per several hectares, seldom seen. You detect them when your shadow folds wrong on the ground. A pike is a moving pocket where local spacetime is minutely concave; light falls toward it, in-falling photons acquiring the same refractive index and thus the same partial mass. The pike’s “jaws” are just two regions where the curvature steepens. Anything—mote, fry, unlucky gnat—crossing the gradient is torn red-shift from itself and stored as heat. The pike vents waste entropy as a cool breeze: the only breath of wind the hollow ever feels.

5. The Green That Pretends to Be Motionless  
   a. Spiral Fens (the helicoid ferns) – Each frond is a double Archimedean spiral that coils centripetally at dawn, centrifuging pooled water into its core where desiccation cannot reach. By noon the spiral unwinds, and nanoscale stomata detonate the stored water into superheated fog. The blast lofts spores that parasail on vapor-gnat highways.  
   b. Cardinal Turf (scarlet moss) – Red only in daylight. At night the pigment disassembles into colorless dendrimers that trap cations from evaporated puddles. Come sunrise, the molecules reassemble, bright crimson again, charged with minerals the moss will parcel out to the algae film in exchange for fixed carbon.

6. The Night That Glows  
   a. Lattice Mycelium – A fungus you never see until the sun is wholly gone. Its hyphae build geodesic domes of emerald light, centimeters across, mapping the stress lines in the feldspar. Where erosion threatens to crack the rock, the mycelium pulses infrared, coaxing feldspar ions to migrate and anneal the fault. The artifacts do maintenance on a macro scale; the fungus handles hairline repair.  
   b. Dream-Eels – An after-form of mirror-pike that manifests only in infrared. They slip through the fungus’ domes, dining on excess heat. At dawn they molt into nothing but a line of colder-than-ambient air that sags into a puddle and becomes… possibly a hemistomate egg. The cycle is tight; life here seldom wastes a nucleus.

7. The Custodians (hardest to believe, easiest to prove)  
   a. Feldspar Blocks (there are three, though you have found only one) – They are not pumps but bodies. Their metabolisms run on band-gap differentials: shade to sun, hot to less hot. The ring of levitating water is simply their way of breathing—exhaling to cool, inhaling as condensation. Inside, each block cultures a dendritic brain of titanium atoms spacing themselves at prime-numbered nanometers. The brain speaks only in fluid-mechanic modulations of the local humidity, which the spiral fens answer by adjusting spore release.  
   b. The Doorframe – A gestalt organism composed of eighteen minerals, a fossilized enzyme and the shadows of anyone who has ever walked through it at dawn. When enough compatible shadows accumulate (estimated recurrence interval: 418 years) the lintel opens onto the place the builders went, inviting new genomes to immigrate. The hollow spends the intervening centuries training candidates: teaching silence, the value of closed cycles, the art of living in light without noise.

WHAT DOES NOT LIVE HERE (ANYMORE)

• The Makers themselves: bipedal, abiogenetic metallovores who printed the hoops and then printed themselves a better universe somewhere else.  
• Terrestrial weeds: grasses, filamentous algae, anything that tries to grow by linear conquest. The local rules delete them after one generation; their atoms dissolve into the suede silt.  
• Echoes: Speak aloud and the air simply does not return your words. Vibrations are eaten by lattice mycelium, digested into silence calories.

IF YOU LINGER

Stay through a sunset. You will see the photofeather fry school one last time, quicksilver nerves playing across the stone. The mirror-pike will open its throat and swallow the dusk, leaving the ground fractionally brighter than the horizon. Then the entire basin will exhale—a geyser of fog no higher than your knee, scented like petrichor and cardamom. In that breath every organism exchanges signals: census completed, debts cleared, ledger rolled shut until morning.

Step outside the basin afterward and ordinary night will feel clumsy, overarticulated. You will hear crickets, wind, maybe your own pulse in your ears, and you may wish those sounds away. That is the hollow’s last and subtlest resident, carried out in you: a quiet that knows it is not an absence but a species in its own right, looking for empty rooms in louder worlds where it might one day take root.
