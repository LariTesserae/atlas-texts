Life in the basin begins as tone.  A pressure‐wave rolls through mica the way a pulse rolls through flesh, and wherever that wave lingers long enough something condenses: a filament, a valve, an eye that is mostly crystal lattice, a mind that is mostly echo.  What follows is a natural history of the inhabitants, from the smallest to the strangest.

1. Glassmoss (Silicaphyton diaphanum)

Size and form  
• A film only eight molecules thick, laid out in hexagonal plates like a city seen from orbit.  
• Each “leaf” is a single needle of doped quartz tipped with germanium; billions interlock to form the mossy sheen on shaded stone.

Metabolism  
• Vibration-trophic.  When low-bass hums shake the substrate, piezoelectric stresses raise an electron current across every needle.  The current drives the slow chemistry that scavenges trace iron and copper from condensation films.  
• Able to shut down entirely when the valley falls silent (a rare event—usually during eclipses).  Reactivates hours or decades later with no loss of function.

Reproduction  
• A strong, sudden chord—most often from a plate collapse—fractures mature sheets into spores the size of smoke grains.  These drift on fog and reattach wherever amplitude is suitable.

Ecological role  
• First coloniser of fresh crystal faces; it lays the electricity that every other lifeform taps like sap.

2. Rainshard Mycelia (Lacrimorhiza violacea)

Anatomy  
• Looks like frost inside glass.  Threads of doped selenite 20 µm thick explore the interstices below every plate.  
• Where two threads cross, they weld into nodes that glow violet under pressure, giving the crack-lines their inner aurora.

Nutrition  
• Chemiosmotic, but carbonless.  The mycelium sits across micro-gradients of pH and salinity created when seawater vapor rises through mineral slurry.  Ions drift; the network harvests the charge differential.

Mind and behaviour  
• Slow, distributed cognition.  Every node carries a phase angle relative to the hum of the valley.  Changes propagate centimetres per week.  
• At colony scale, the mycelium decides where sound should go.  It extrudes crystal whiskers that lever plates minutely upward or downward, fine-tuning pitch.  Over centuries it has tuned the whole basin into a self-resonating harp.

Reproduction  
• Once in perhaps two human lifetimes, several square metres of mycelium mineralise into a single solid geode that detaches and rolls downslope.  Impact shatters it, seeding a new colony.

3. Skimfish (Rhedonema laminaris)

Morphology  
• Length 60–110 cm.  Thickness only 4 mm at the midriff, giving the animal the proportions of a bookmark.  
• Skeleton is cartilage stiffened by silica ribs; skin is a clear mucin into which nickel micro-plates are embedded like confetti.  The plates refract the twilight, flashing oil-slick greens.

Locomotion  
• Lies flat in the water film, beating the margins of its body in metachronal waves that look like wind across grass.  
• Because the film is microns thick, forward progress is measured in metres per hour.  That is fast here.

Feeding  
• Mouth is a mesh weir at the fore edge, sieving colloidal organics released by rainshard excretion.  
• Lateral lines are sensitive not to pressure but to the pitch of drip-notes; a Skimfish follows the note signature that historically predicts richest nutrients.

Life cycle  
• Hatched as transparent ribbons from eggs laid under up-arched mica plates.  
• Lives only five local years, then beaches itself on a high plate where body water quickly evaporates, leaving a nickel-filigree carcass.  These skeletons are the only thing here that rusts.

4. Condorclams (Aerostoma caligo)

Body plan  
• Two silvery valves, ovate, each the size of a human fist.  Between them: a mantle that extrudes a dozen translucent bladders.  
• Internal tissues are spongy aragonite riddled with catalytic selenium veins.

Lift and drift  
• The clam floats by electro-catalysing water into hydrogen and oxygen inside its bladders.  The process is powered by day-glow: even the valley’s dim copper sun is enough.  
• At altitudes of three to six metres the bladders cool, the gases contract, the animal sinks.  It repeats the cycle daily, so at dawn a handful are always descending like slow hail.

Feeding and growth  
• When grounded it tongues mineral slurry, seeking lithium and boron needed for gas catalysis.  
• Growth is the slow addition of aragonite lamellae that record, like tree rings, the valley’s shifting chord structure.  A seventy-ring clam remembers music human cultures never heard.

Sociality  
• Never more than two within shouting distance.  Nearby bladders detune each other’s resonance, so they keep apart.

5. The Chordspires and Their Architects

The fact  
Thirty-odd stacks of crystal plates stand in quasi-rows, each tuned to a fundamental pitch.  When one falls, it is rebuilt.  No animal yet described can accomplish that.

The candidate agents  
a.  Rainshard mycelia could raise plates, but lack the vertical reach above ground.  
b.  Condorclams can hover but cannot grip.  
c.  Skimfish never leave water films.

What actually does it  
• Surveyors call them Stackwrights.  Local legend says they are “made of ringing.”  In strict terms they are standing waves—knots of constructive interference in the valley’s sonic field, stabilised by the piezoelectric flexing of the plates themselves.  
• When drip-notes align in a precise five-tone ratio, energy concentrates enough to polarise air molecules, creating a temporary sheath of ionisation.  Dust grains lock onto the sheath, revealing a gauzy, human-tall outline that flits from plate to plate.  
• Within such a body the gradient of acoustic pressure can push objects exactly the way ultrasound levitates droplets in laboratories.  Over hours the Stackwright nudges a fallen plate upright, sets it on edge, adds another, always chasing the just‐evaporating harmony that birthed it.  
• When the chord that sustains the interference pattern detunes—as dawn warmth or shifting fog drifts by—the outline flickers and collapses.  The finished spire continues to sing the chord long after its maker has dematerialised.  Thus the builders are as impermanent as breath, yet their work may outlast empires.

Are they sentient?  
The phenomenon repeats with too much purpose to be random.  Visitors watching through spectrographs note feedback loops, hesitation, even what looks like aesthetic choice—Stackwrights prefer alignments that create beat frequencies exactly in the range of Skimfish audition, thereby enriching the food web.  If that is not culture it is something adjacent.

6. The Basin Itself (Hypolithic Organism 0)

Many researchers now treat the entire saucer as a superorganism.

Evidence  
• Resonance predicates structure: a biosphere in which sound shapes matter which shapes sound is functionally one feedback body.  
• Genetic material is absent; what propagates here is pattern.  The valley is a library of standing waves etched into stone and water.

Behavior  
• Quakes from the offshore caverns arrive like distant heartbeats.  The rim’s shards tilt in response, subtly retuning all drip-notes.  Over millennia the valley has responded by knitting new platelets, growing “auditory excrescences” that compensate, in the way a tree grows buttress roots against prevailing wind.  
• When human voices enter the basin they are captured, refracted, slowed, folded back until speakers hear themselves as if from seasons away.  Some report that the echo answers differently than they spoke, an early sign that the valley is parsing the information for its own use.

7. Ephemeral Visitors: Humans and Others

Humans  
• Cannot live here, but are briefly adopted.  Low frequencies dilate blood vessels; high glass bell-tones trigger the stapedial reflex in the ear.  Together these lull explorers into the basin’s tempo.  
• Prolonged stays (>40 hours) produce chronesthesia drift: people can recite entire conversations they have not yet had, presumably because their internal pacing syncs to the droplet cycle.

Other itinerants  
• Once every decade a baleen whale, mis-navigating an undersea canyon, beaches across the rim in a quake.  It dies, but its skeleton resonates like an enormous wind chime for centuries, and the local life thickens around the new frequencies.

8. Food Web in Summary

Energy input  
• 15 % solar photons  
• 25 % mechanical vibration from oceanic percussion  
• 60 % chemical potential of ascending brines

Flows  
Glassmoss turns vibration into current → Rainshard mycelia channel current into ionic gradients → Skimfish graze secretions → Condorclams mine deeper minerals and mix aerial strata → Stackwright events integrate all above into macro-architecture that optimises future vibration capture.

The web therefore loops back on itself.  Nothing dies so much as downgrades: bodies weather into silt, silt compacts into plates, plates vibrate back into life.

9. Why No Predators?

Speed is impossible; stress fracturing limits tensile strength of living crystal.  Anything that tried to bite would shake itself apart faster than it could feed.  The valley enforces détente by physics.

10. Futures

In 11,000 years the rim will slump enough to admit a single narrow tide.  Modelers predict the basin will become a breathing marsh of brine whose harmonics sit an octave lower.  All present species will adapt or petrify.  The Stackwrights will persist: wherever sound can stand on its own shoulders, something will rise to listen.
