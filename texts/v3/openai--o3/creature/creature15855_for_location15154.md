They inhabit the plateau the way marrow inhabits bone—filling every crevice, growing it outward, keeping it alive even as they consume it. Below are the principal lives that make the Hollows of Kwa-Lashi what they are, from the largest idea to the smallest gut microbe.

THE ONE MADE OF BILLIONS
Kwa-Lashi Titan‐Termites (Vastitermes concordia)
• Mind-shape: Not individuals but a distributed, quorum-driven nervous system. At any moment a hundred million workers are exchanging pheromone pulses; the plateau’s “decision” is the averaged chemical weather of these signals.  
• Scale: A newborn worker could lounge on a human thumbnail; an egg-laying princess is as long as a man’s arm and so swollen she can no longer turn around.  
• Occupations – and they really are jobs:  
  – Masons chew wood and leaf pulp into spittle-mortar, extruding it as soft ribbons that harden in minutes.  
  – Gardeners seed fungal brood-loaves, prune them to avoid the lethal white blight, and fold spent mycelium into nursery pillows.  
  – Nectarers rove the outer skin at dusk to harvest wind-borne pollen and juice the few free flowers, carrying the thin syrup home in elastic gullets.  
  – Resonators form living choirs in buttress-chambers; by drumming abdomens against ribbed walls they keep the entire hive vibrating at 37 hertz, a frequency that stuns most predators and encourages faster root-growth in the symbiotic fungi.  
• Lifespan: Workers last three wet seasons; soldiers five. Queens may persist for a full human century provided they are groomed every dusk to prevent their joints from ossifying.  
• Religion, if the word applies: Anything that breaks the surface and sees the real sky is holy. Every decade a cohort of “sky-breachers” is allowed to carve vents straight upward. They freeze in the night wind, die by morning, and their bodies are built around rather than removed—a sacrificial crown on each new chimney.

THE HAMMERS AND SAWS
Rotary Beetles (Orbitosoma lignivora)
• Bread-loaf–sized scarabs whose mandibles have fused into concentric saw-rings. They spin the entire head capsule like a drill chuck, reducing logs to paste that titan-termites lap up.  
• Domestic status: Half livestock, half power-tool. Termite workers feed them stripped bark; in return they chew ventilation shafts and core out new storage bladders no jaw alone could shape.  
• Escapees occasionally go feral on the outer face. The hive tolerates this—spare drills waiting for recall.

THE LOOM HERD
Moth-Cattle (Seriluma pacifica)
• Start as caterpillars thick as a stout rope, striped umber and cream.  
• Shepherd castes of termites ride them like living escalators, scraping silk glands with horn scrapers. The threads are woven wet and cure into the taut ceiling membranes that mute the sun.  
• Metamorphosis is strictly timed: once a cohort’s silk quota is met, the shepherds feed the larvae a specific mildew that triggers pupation. Any moth that ecloses early is immediately plucked apart for wax and wing-scale pigment.

THE CLEANERS
Copper Frogs (Hylarana cupreus)
• Coin-sized amphibians that glitter like tarnished metal.  
• Live forever in twilight sluices, eating the mites that would otherwise clog air-pores.  
• Their skin secretes an alkaloid repellent to ants; termite nurseries borrow them as organic sentries, paying in stray eggs and the occasional midge swarm fanned their way.

THE INNUMERABLE SMALL
1. Pollen ghosts—sub-millimeter gnats that float on electrostatic charge, sparking microscopic lightning when two meet.  
2. Gut forges—bacteria in termite stomachs capable of precipitating zinc, used to plate soldier mandibles from the inside.  
3. Thread lichen—the dim green wires that edge corridor floors, engineered by centuries of selective nibbling until they glow just enough to mark lanes but not enough to draw moths.

PREDATORS (TOLERATED, WATCHED)
• Mirror Mantises: live mirrors; their skin is a mosaic of mica scales stolen from shed beetle elytra. They ambush lone workers then hang the clean skulls as warning. Allowed to exist because they harvest the inattentive.  
• Honey Viscacha: the only warm-blooded mammal to survive here. Big-eyed, slow, feeds on fermented seed-honey collecting in dead ends. Titans ignore it; beetles flee; frogs hitch rides on its fur to reach new pools.

WHAT PASSES FOR WEATHER
Inside, seasons are measured not by temperature but by tone. When nightly condensation is rich, water threads beat a faster drip, shifting the background chord up half a note. The colony responds: gardeners accelerate harvest, rotary beetles are walked deeper to stay cool, queens oviposit an extra clutch. The entire biosphere flexes like a single diaphragm.

LIFECYCLE OF A CHAMBER
1. Excavation by beetle and soldier jaws.  
2. Quick-lining with leaf-vein felt; mycelium seeded.  
3. Four years as nursery or granary.  
4. Roof sags, becomes compost.  
5. Chamber refilled, re-lined, reborn—usually larger by one worker’s width. A thousand such pulses and a cliff becomes a dome.

UNWRITTEN LAWS THEY OBEY
1. Nothing may end as nothing. All corpses—even alien ones—return as wall, spore or nutrient.  
2. Sound is structure: silence breeds fungus that kills the queens, so rhythm must continue.  
3. Light is enemy and ally; welcome it only through filter or firefly.  
4. Strangers are studied before eaten; knowledge is a seasoning.

IF YOU MEET THEM
• Stand still. The surface beneath you is reading your weight, tasting your sweat.  
• Remove metal ornaments; zinc smell mimics soldier pheromone and will draw guards.  
• Hum. A steady, low drone convinces the Resonators you are part of the greater chorus.  
• Do not follow the copper frogs. They can leap where the floor is paper-thin and will lead you through a place the hive can afford to lose. You cannot.

EPILOGUE: THE UNKNOWN RESIDENT
Deep in the plateau’s oldest kernel hangs a room no larger than a grape. Its walls are lined with calcium carbonate, not resin. Within, a single pearl-white grub older than any queen loops endlessly in its sleep, its body studded with nerve nets that map the entire hive in miniature. Whether it is monarch, memory core, or forgotten larva, no worker disturbs it. They merely feed the calcium veins that keep the shell intact. Perhaps the plateau itself dreams through that worm, or perhaps it dreams alone, content with the thunder of its billionfold heart.

These, then, are the lives of Kwa-Lashi: tool and builder, food and farmer, predator and caretaker, woven so tightly that to name one is to gesture at the whole. The plateau is not a city. It is an organ, and every creature within is a cell still learning the shape of the giant it composes.
