INHABITANTS OF THE NEW-GLASS POCKET  

1. The Pallax – Architects, Archivists, Aristocrats  
   a. Body plan  
      • Length 8–12 cm, breadth never more than a drinking straw. The whole animal is four ribbons twined: two muscular “propeller” ribbons for undulation, one vascular ribbon bearing a triple-looped heart, and a fourth neural ribbon studded with gemlike ocelli that read pressure gradients the way surface creatures read light.  
      • Skeleton: a ladder of cartilage rings, each softly fluorescent; the glow is quenched when the animal is frightened, allowing shoals to vanish by simply going still.  
      • Skin: crystal-flex keratin, slightly birefringent. At some angles a shoal looks like floating film; at others it flashes fly-wing rainbows.  

   b. Internal chemistry  
      • Blood substitutes iron with cobalt, giving it a metallic teal that binds oxygen even in near-anoxic layers.  
      • Tripartite stomach: first chamber dissolves basalt dust with strong acid, second mixes the slurry with silicate-binding peptides, third extrudes the digest as amber lace through spinneret pores that run the length of the belly.

   c. Life cycle  
      1. Glass-seed (egg) – 0.6 mm, rides inhaling current into random crannies.  
      2. Lumen-larva – transparent comma that drifts for three glacier pulses learning pressure rhythms; mortality 93 %.  
      3. Builder – sexually immature for roughly forty pulses; spends every hour excreting scaffold lace, racing siblings to claim virgin surfaces.  
      4. Archivist – gonads mature; building slows. Individuals embed themselves nose-first into the city walls, becoming living seismographs. Neural ribbon grows into the structure; memories, songs, even navigation maps are encoded as vibration fingerprints inside the amber terraces.  
      5. Aristocrat – final stage. The body contracts to a nub no longer capable of movement. Each Aristocrat is walled into a cup, its only duty to eject sex cells into the passing tidal gusts. After one reproductive season its tissues mineralize, becoming the first capstone of the cup that will cradle the next generation of eggs.

   d. Society / “Culture”  
      • No eyes, no ears—only the hive-lattice. A thought in one wing of the city becomes a tremor felt everywhere two heartbeats later.  
      • Status equals altitude. The eldest, most data-rich archivists choose the warm, rising central towers. Juveniles compete to build toward those zones, literally climbing the social ladder.  
      • Timekeeping is architectural: every pulse the outermost builders lay one new fringe. A vertical slice of the city is therefore a perfect ledger of its history, readable by anyone who knows how to feel thickness changes with their lateral line.  

2. Glass-Urchins – The Settlement’s Nails  
   • Diameter up to 4 cm; three-hundred-plus silica spines, each grown choosing the thermal gradient as a ruler, so an urchin viewed in cross-section is a flawless polar graph of last night’s convection patterns.  
   • Digestive crown on the underside rains fine quartz grit that Pallax use as polishing sand.  
   • When an urchin dies, its hollow spines collapse into drinking-straw conduits that the city co-opts for micro-ventilation. Whole arcade ceilings are lattices of former urchins, now breathing tubes.  

3. Thread Eels – Wayfarers and Street Cleaners  
   • Two metres long, thinner than a shoelace; internal silver gut the lone opaque organ—everything else glassy.  
   • Locomotion by single slow helicoidal undulation; an eel can stay stationary relative to the pocket’s swirl by matching the city’s drift like a satellite in geosynchronous orbit.  
   • Diet: microbe snow and Pallax waste flakes; their skin secretes antibiotic mucous that sterilizes the terraces. Pallax tolerate, even encourage them, carving special “silent lanes” so eels can slip through without damaging new lacework.  

4. Thimble Rays – Gardeners by Negation  
   • Disc width 7 cm, velum-thin. Wing margins lined with ciliated “combs” that sieve larvae indiscriminately.  
   • By cropping urchin and Pallax young, they prevent population clots, keeping water flow efficient. Builders treat the passing of a thimble ray as both funeral and festival: deaths clear room for fresh architecture; the vibration pattern the ray’s fins leave in the lattice is celebrated as a signature of renewal.  

5. Chemotrophic Carpets – The First Brick  
   • Sheets of burgundy archaeans anchored in microfractures. Secrete acidic exopolymers that quarry ions from raw basalt faster than water alone could achieve.  
   • Their waste salts nucleate into nano-crystals that drift upward—prime feed for both lumen-larvae and urchins. No carpet, no city.  

6. The Grey-Green Light – Living Atmosphere  
   • Not passive glow but a continental-sized conversation: every microbe flickers in three intensity levels corresponding to nutrient status. The vast, low-resolution display tells thread eels where to graze and signals Pallax larvae when to metamorphose. In essence, the water column itself is a billboard of metabolic gossip.

SYMBIOSES AND RIVALRIES  

• Pallax & Glass-Urchins: builders raze urchins that shade growing walls, but urchin spines later become plumbing. Destruction is investment.  
• Thread Eels & Chemotrophic Carpets: eel mucus is mildly alkaline, neutralizing carpet acid along transit corridors, preventing the entire bowl from dissolving itself.  
• Thimble Rays & Everyone Else: hated, needed, unchallenged. Even when a shoal of builders mobs a ray, their lace jets simply skate harmlessly across its elastic dermis.

PARASITES & SCOURGES  

• Frost-Fleas – micron-scale crustaceans that tap the blood of embed-fixed Archivists. Too many fleas dull the lattice-telegraph, so juveniles patrol with rasping mouthparts, scraping off infestations like gardeners shearing ivy.  
• Echo-Mold – a rare fungal sheet that imitates lattice vibrations perfectly, jamming communication. When discovered, whole neighborhoods self-amputate, collapsing terraces in controlled avalanches to bury the deceiver.

SENSORY EXPERIENCE FOR AN OUTSIDER  

Approach a cluster of Archivists and your dive computer will vibrate—hardware harmonizing inadvertently with their speech. Point a laser and their cartilage will refract it into a thousand pinpoints dancing across the glass bowl. You become, unwillingly, part of the conversation; your heartbeats send seismic Morse no native can ignore.

WHAT THE INHABITANTS THINK OF YOU  

They have no mythology of giants, no word for “surface,” yet your exhaled bubbles are instantly interpreted as malformed, superheated Pallax spawn. Builders try to net them with lace. Archivists record the pattern, debating whether the bubbles are prophecy or pollutant. Within three pulses the event has its own dedicated shrine: a fresh chamber tuned to the precise rhythm of your breath.

CONCLUSION  

Life here is an orchestra of pressures and precipitates. Nothing has a beginning unlinked from masonry, and nothing ends except by being taken up as new scaffolding. The residents are artisans of impermanence, racing glacier and magma alike, carving habitable hush from the brief space between crushing ice above and molten stone below. In their world every creature is both tenant and tool, every death a brick, every heartbeat a note in the mineral hymn that keeps the pocket alive until the next fissure beckons them onward.
