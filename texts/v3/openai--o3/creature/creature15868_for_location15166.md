Who and what live on the Plateau of Canted Echoes are shaped less by flesh or masonry than by the single, endless bass note that possesses the land. To speak of them is to speak of the ways they bend themselves around the sound—how they muffle, harvest, imitate, fear and finally become it.

I. The Human Lineages of Iron Sheds

1. The Tenders
   • Numbering perhaps five hundred in all eight districts, the Tenders are the plateau’s engineers-in-perpetuity.  
   • They sleep in four-hour shifts so that the turbines never spin unattended; they say the roar, like a hearth, must have an eye on it or it misbehaves.  
   • Their skin is ochre-freckled from the iron mist, their palms glossy where clay seep has polished them. Most shave their heads to fit the cork ear-hoods tight; what hair remains is braided with slivers of lead sheet that double as emergency earplugs.

2. The Hushsmiths
   • Artisans who make the silencers—ear-hoods, felted gloves, cork-soled boots, vibration-deadening furniture.  
   • Because a tool dropped on bare stone can crack a turbine blade kilometers away, every hinge, latch and buckle they fashion is over-cushioned.  
   • Their craft guild’s mark is stitched in absolute black; color is considered noisy.

3. The Quietwardens
   • A civic order halfway between priesthood and building inspector. They monitor harmonic maps hung in every powerhouse: skeins of quivering copper wire that twitch if any frequency creeps above tolerance.  
   • When the map stutters they walk the district, signing questions, hunting the culprit—an uncapped stovepipe, a loose fascia panel, sometimes a singing child—then coupon-fine the offender in silence.

4. Railwalkers
   • Adolescents who patrol the single freight line. To keep axle-crack at bay they pace beside the crawling wagons, listening through the soles of their boots for sick notes in the rail.  
   • Each wears a brass anklet keyed to 29 Hz; if the ground-tone rises, the anklet buzzes, and the walker chalks the nearest sleeper for replacement.

5. Children of the Note
   • Born into the roar, they develop calloused eardrums and an uncanny sense of pressure change. Doctors from the lowlands record that their middle-ear bones grow thick as barley grains.  
   • At thirteen they undergo the Unclasping: ear-hoods are lifted for one full minute at midday. If the child can maintain stillness without panic, they are judged “fit for the chord” and may begin vocational training.

6. Elders, or Sinkers
   • When death comes, a body is wrapped in rust-black linen, ringed with iron chain and slid feet-first into a swallow hole. The splash is inaudible over the ground-note, but mourners sign the same farewell: “Now you vibrate from beneath us.”

Governance
   • Each district elects three Hands to the Council of Twenty-Four. They meet in a sub-basement whose walls are packed with wet clay; every motion is carried by light-slate, never by voice.  
   • Law is recorded in two columns: the statute itself and the resonant risk it avoids.

II. The Subtle Fauna

1. Basin Skimmers
   • Flat as your palm, rust-colored, they bask where waterfall sheets strike stone. Their bones are cartilaginous disks that resonate sympathetically; if you lift one the note faintly rings inside it.

2. Cave Pigeons
   • Blind, puff-feathered, they echolocate in multi-click bursts above 40 kHz—well clear of the plateau’s infrasonic domain. Now and then a live bird is expelled through a turbine vent, rolled out in a tumble of down, then gathers itself and beats away into fog.

3. Shiver Ants
   • White, wingless atennae-long ants that quarry silt grains into tiny Helmholtz chambers. At night the ground seems to fizz; that is the colonies venting excess resonance through their nests like organ stops.

4. Ironclaw Mantids
   • Slate-gray, forelimbs barbed with iron phosphate deposits. They perch on rail pylons, using the metal as conductor to detect oncoming carts minutes before human ears can register them.

III. Persistent Flora

1. Ironwort
   • Blade edges hold microscopic teeth of iron oxide. The Hushsmiths harvest it, grind it, mix it into soft wax to create a near-frictionless polish for turbine shafts.

2. Sac-moss
   • Looks desiccated until squeezed; then it pours a day’s worth of distilled mist. Children carry thumb-sized plugs on cord necklaces as canteens.

3. Ghost Orchid
   • Grows only in water spray where sunlight is refracted twice. Petals are translucent; at dusk the orchid seems absent, present only as a brighter patch in the air.

IV. Things That May Or May Not Be Alive

1. Resonants
   • Pale, fist-big polyps found clinging to cavern walls below turbine depth. They swell and contract in perfect counterphase to the 29-Hz drone. Some Tenders insist that the plateau itself bred them to dampen harmonic spikes—a living baffle system.

2. Mistlings
   • On windless dawns the air stratifies into layers of differing vibration. Sheets of droplets hang like veils; within them human silhouettes appear, walk a pace, then shred into spray. No camera has recorded them. Railwalkers salute anyway.

3. The Karst Leviathan
   • Once every generation, maintenance crews discover a new void whose floor is carpeted in vertebrae the size of fuel drums. Engineers log them as “structural debris.” Philosophers enter a single line: “What sang before us, sings after.”

V. Rhythms of the Living

Morning
   • Lamps doused, ear-hoods donned. First water draw at each district cistern; anyone caught clanking a bucket lip is volunteered for a week of Shiver Ant harvesting—bare-fingered.

Midday
   • All motion slows. The plateau’s resonance climbs by a tenth of a hertz as rock warms. Workshops reduce hammer stroke; freight halts entirely. Children attend silent school where lessons are scratched on slate suspended from the rafters by wool string.

Evening
   • The Unclasping for adults. Ear-hood straps loosen; citizens stand in doorways, letting the bass wash their skulls. Some hum a fifth above the ground-note, claiming it relieves pressure behind the eyes.

Night
   • Power demand falls; turbines idle. In the lull the roar drops just enough that you can hear water dripping through the weeping rock like beads sliding on a string. Couples choose this hour for proposals, signing vows only when they can also hear one another’s heartbeat.

VI. Leaving and Returning

Every departure is a shedding of weight: cork hoods off, shoulders un-hunched, breath springing open. Yet returning travelers report a sharper relief—the moment the ground-note reenters the bones like a remembered pulse. They step from the train, sign the traditional phrase: “Silence is a wound,” and the plateau answers with its slow, patient thunder, reminding them they are home among the resonant and the resonated, the makers of distilled storm, the folk who do not speak because the land itself is forever speaking.
