Who—or what—claims the Frost Nave as home?  
Answer that and you discover that every living thing here, from microbe to myth, has learned the same discipline: stay small in mass, swift in gesture, and unfinished in form, the better to keep pace with a landscape still arranging its own bones. The inhabitants fall into overlapping circles; begin anywhere and you find yourself spiraling outward.

I. The Minute Unseen – the Breath-Takers  
1. Frost-lattice bacteria: Hair-thin films growing inside the first millimetre of ice. They photosynthesise through the ice itself, colouring the buried layers turquoise. Their waste product is an antifreeze protein; wherever they bloom, the ice fissures into starry patterns that direct melt-trickle toward plant roots.  
2. Slate-munch molds: Neither fungus nor true amoeba, but an in-between lineage that creeps across exposed granite when it is wet for more than three consecutive minutes. They secrete an acid that etches the rock, leaving pin-prick hollows that will become later root holds—a quiet terraforming.  
3. Whisper spores: Almost weightless, they rise on noon thermals and remain aloft for days. Sky-Dwellers trap them in bell jars; dried, the spores thicken soups, add a piney bitterness, and keep meat from spoiling without fire.

II. The Bright Swarm – quick-cycle invertebrates  
1. Cloud-gnats (given)  
   • Life span: eleven days; they hatch as translucent larvae inside icicles, pupate in moss, then launch into the sun.  
   • Courtship: two individuals orbit a point of light reflected from the rock; whoever keeps the reflection centered wins.  
2. Horned skimmers (given)  
   • Their “horns” are hollow keratin tubes. Males stridulate by forcing air through them; in the Nave’s stillness the mating song carries a kilometre and sounds like glass rims being stroked.  
3. Argent keloid beetles  
   • Iridescent, coin-sized. They specialize in newly fractured rock: mouthparts rasp microscopic shards to ingest trace iron. By dusk their wing-covers carry static charge; Sky-Dwellers collect the beetles in clay bowls to spark tinder.  
4. Biting snow-flies  
   • Larvae live inside cliff-cony scat, speeding decomposition that would otherwise never occur. Adults last only hours, but their wingcases concentrate rare amino acids; lace owls depend on them to replace worn feathers.

III. The Furred, Feathered, and Not-Quite-Vertebrate  
1. Cliff-conies (given)  
   • A tripodal gait: two hind legs for spring, single forelimb for braking. They can cling to a surface with the forepaw, pivot, and fling themselves the other direction without letting go completely—a living carabiner.  
   • Gestation ends precisely at the first full moon after equinox; litters of three are common, a superstition for the Sky-Dwellers who—also in threes—bind marriages that night.  
2. Ridge ibex (domesticate)  
   • Bred by humans. Barrel lungs, spiral hooves, and a double-layered coat whose outer guard hairs are translucent, funnelling light to dark inner fleece that warms by greenhouse effect.  
   • Give sour, copper-tasting milk rich in hemoglobin; drunk fresh, it can stave off altitude sickness.  
3. Lace owls (given)  
   • Feathers lack barbules; each vane frays into myriads of filament ends, scattering air turbulence and sound. Consequently, the feathers reflect little light and appear soft-focus—hence “lace.”  
   • When the Nave’s rock plates shift, a resident pair will circle the fall zone before the dust clears, using the airborne grit as camouflage to take multiple kills.  
4. Chime serpents  
   • Limbless, ice-pale reptiles that live wholly inside vertical cracks. Scales overlap like roof tiles; when the serpent breathes, the loose edges vibrate, producing a faint ringing that gives warning of thaw.  
   • They feed by protruding a hinged jaw to snatch cloud-gnats that drift too near the wall—predation by ambush, without leaving the crack.

IV. The Sky-Dwellers – people learning to stay provisional  
Appearance and Physiology  
• Average stature is slight—thin air favours low mass—but chest depth is exceptional. Ribs flare like bellows; toddlers learn a slow, two-stage inhalation that outsiders never quite master.  
• Skin shows pale to copper tones dusted with tiny white flecks: deposits of cryoprotective salts exuded through pores, then re-absorbed when temperatures rise. Children collect the crystals from each other’s faces and taste them for fun: slightly sweet, faintly metallic.  
Material Culture  
• Tools are basalt cores with replaceable bone edges lashed by yak sinew. A single handle may outlive a dozen edges, an economy of permanence wrapped around expendables—exactly how they view their camps versus the mountain.  
• Slate cairns with antler caps are not shrines but “way-spools.” Inside each cairn lies a sealed roll of braided gut line. A traveller may open the cairn, rig a bridge where none exists, then replace the coil at the opposite side; the number of unbroken spools serves as a civic account of collective debt.  
Social Structure  
• No fixed clans. Instead, every adult belongs simultaneously to three overlapping “spheres”:  
  1. A blood-kin ring responsible for children and the aged.  
  2. A craft ring (weaving, hunting, knot-navigation, weather-reading).  
  3. A traverse ring formed for each seasonal journey. Rings dissolve and reform; names change suffixes to denote current allegiance. One winter you might be Tharu-of-the-KnottedLine; two years later Tharu-With-the-Ibex, and so on.  
Belief & Narrative  
• The mountain is considered “young-older-young”—older than people, younger than its eventual self. Anything solid is half-promise. Therefore burial is unheard of; the dead are placed on thaw-ledges where chime serpents will consume them over weeks, returning flesh to motion instead of rest.  
• Mirror walkers (see Weirdness) are accepted as advice made visible. Sky-Dwellers court them with deliberate missteps—dropping a pebble, stumbling on a ledge—to coax the reflection into showing the safe move. A child’s first lesson is how to read one’s own shimmer.

V. The Ones Without Substance – phenomena that almost qualify as life  
1. Mirror walkers (optical but reactive?)  
   • Close observation shows the duplicate sometimes corrects posture before the original does, indicating a feedback loop faster than conscious nerve response. Are they precognition, light echo, or the mountain rehearsing possibilities? The debate is academic; everyone acts on the hint.  
2. Avalanche wraiths  
   • When a whole slate slab dislodges, the dust cloud maintains a coherent front for several heartbeats, curling like a beast sniffing the air. Lace owls surf this front; Sky-Dwellers swear the dust chooses a path that minimizes long-term damage to vegetation, as though landslides were pruned by intent.  
3. Noon halos  
   • On the two equinox days, sunlight aligns with a hairline cleft high above the fracture. The beam ricochets five times and produces a floating torus of light in the Nave’s centre. Insects caught within age at double speed—gnats live five days, not eleven—and anything with chlorophyll pulses a second micro-bloom. Life here is therefore counted in two overlapping calendars: ordinary and halo-time.

VI. Interactions – the living grammar  
• Cobalt moss embeds antifreeze proteins that keep cliff-cony droppings from freezing solid. The slightly warmer pellets support snow-fly larvae, which, consumed by lace owls, concentrate iron that is later shed with feathers. Beetles eat those feathers for the iron, and their charged wings light Sky-Dweller hearths. Six steps, one circuit—the Nave’s quickest loop of matter.  
• Ridge ibex wear bone bells tuned to the key of the chime serpents; herders listen for dissonance, which signals serpents on the move. Thus predator warning is outsourced to musical mismatch.  
• A dropped tool, per Rule 4, will indeed root in place. Sky-Dweller apprentices carve divots in handles before “accidentally” losing them, seeding future anchor points for bridges they themselves will one day cross.

VII. Life Histories in Snapshot  
Morning: Frost-lattice bacteria thin the ice, letting first light strike chain lichens. Cloud-gnats hatch in a sparkle storm; horned skimmers rise behind them like silent hawks.  
Noon: The slot is white fire. Vegetation exhales menthol vapor; cliff-conies sun themselves, pretending safety. Mirror walkers bloom brightest; novices practice deliberate stumbles under their mentors’ gaze.  
Dusk: Temperature plummets. Lace owls emerge; beetles hide inside rock fissures warm with bacterial melt. Chime serpents ring once, twice—the universal curfew bell.  
Night: Almost nothing moves but mold filaments and the thoughts of Sky-Dweller insomniacs, who count avalanche wraiths instead of sheep.

VIII. What It Means to Be Alive Here  
To endure the Frost Nave is to accept a provisional body. Bones may belong to you, but each tendon, pore, hoof-sheath, or leaf stoma is tuned to a clock that could shift without notice. You survive by revising. Cliff-conies add or lose whiskers according to wind direction; Sky-Dwellers trade names; even bacteria rewrite their own antifreeze recipe every generation.

Visitors sometimes call the place inhospitable. The locals laugh: hospitality implies walls, and walls imply finality. The Nave’s residents prefer thresholds—places you step across, knowing the stone might still be wet paint. That is the truest citizen here: anything willing to remain unfinished.
