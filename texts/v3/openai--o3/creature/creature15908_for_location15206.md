Who lives on the Floor of the World?  
No one answer is whole.  The place is too looped, too newly-finished, for a settled population; every living thing here feels half in the present moment, half still being made.  Yet if you linger you will meet presences—some briefly, some again and again, as if the same encounter were being folded back into your path.  What follows is the best catalogue travelers have scraped together, written in the order the inhabitants usually reveal themselves.

1. Seam-Tenders  
Look first for the little repairs.  A hairline crack opens: within the same hour a trio of figures pads out of the glare, kneels, and starts to work.  They resemble tall children cut from different hardwoods—multicoloured, jointed by pegged dowels, the grain running the length of every limb.  Up close you realise the “planks” are greaves of palm bark soaked until leather-soft, then wrapped in spiral layers around something slender and boned.  No two colours match; each Tender is a walking parquet, the land’s own pattern tied into a single bundle of limbs.

Tools:  
• a fist-sized gourd of raw slip for gluing the wound,  
• a wicker stencil that insures the replaced shard repeats the surrounding tessellation,  
• a slotted needle the length of a forearm for stitching reed ties through the sub-plenum.  

They never speak.  Their mouths are marked only by a paler band where bark has been peeled thinner.  Communication happens in taps against the tiles—five coded rhythms that mean bring, carry away, brace, stand clear, and finished.  When a patch is done they pour a thimble of brine through the fresh seam, listen for the hollow knock that answers from beneath, and leave.  Every Tender you meet will look two or three seasons old at most; no one has yet discovered an elder or a corpse.  They appear born already at apprenticeship age, as if the Floor periodically peels workers out of itself.

2. Water-Knockers  
While the Seam-Tenders keep the surface sharp, another kind of life circulates below the plates.  You will hear them rather than see them: slow percussive drumming, six strokes, pause, six strokes again.  The brine leaps in time with the beats.  Drop a weighted reed through any maintenance port and you may glimpse ochre shapes—pillars that flex like muscle, capped by petalled discs.  They resemble giant sea anemones built of clay diaphragms.  Each contraction hammers pressure through the raft of tiles, flushing sweat up the nail-holes, drawing cooled liquid down the gutters, then resting precisely seventeen minutes (the same interval that governs the cycle of mist).  Engineers in your party will want to call them pumps, but they are self-seeding and self-healing; kill one and a smaller sibling inflates from the same roots within hours.  They are organs in the body of the country, not machines.

3. The Folded Folk  
Once every loop of 480 pace-beats you may catch another traveller pacing you from the far side of a canal.  They wear the same clothes you do, at the same angle of fatigue, carrying the same dust on their boots—yet the face is subtly wrong, a sketch iterated once too many times.  If you wave, they wave.  If you shout, mid-syllable they turn aside and blur, as if the noon hardens around them.  Guides call these echoes the Folded Folk, fragments of walkers whose paths kinked through a place where two sides make a triangle.  They are harmless, but keep your distance: occasionally an echo finds a shorter way to where you are standing and steps through you like a sheet of hot glass.  No wound is left, only the smell of scorched linen and a newly-blank patch on the tiles the size of a footprint.  The land appears to reclaim the misfolded substance for later use.

4. Aviators of the Fixed Noon  
Those great laminar shadows belong to creatures the Seam-Tenders call upwindra—“wind writers”—though there is no wind.  They have the length of gliders, the skull of a skink, and wings that should be too thin to carry them.  Each membrane is reinforced by reeds stitched in a radiating fan, the knots sealed with identical tree resin used on bridges.  The pattern is so regular that natural growth is unlikely; more reasonable to think the first skein of hatchlings was netted, splinted, and released, and every generation copies the harness on the next with instinctive patience.  Upwindra never land.  They need the thermal plate of perpetual noon the way seabirds need currents.  Courtship is silent figure-eights over brick mesas; eggs are dropped into sunken lotus ponds where the heat boils away predators but never the broth.  Within an hour of hatching a chick climbs a bamboo scaffold some forgotten builder left, launches, and the sky gains another fixed gear in its silent clock.

5. Leashed Wilderness  
Nothing green is truly wild here.  Under every rooted cluster you will find a tether:  
• looped bark collars around mangroves to keep them from prying up grout,  
• lateral stakes limiting fern rhizomes to perfect rectangles,  
• trellis lattices that teach cud-weed which seam not to cross.  

The gardeners are minute—thumb-long spiders of fired ceramic whose abdomens hold twin spools of plant-fiber twine.  They move in choreographed swarms at duskless noon, rappelling down the greenery, pruning with mandibles honed sharper than flint.  When a leaf matures past the builder’s design spec—curling a finger beyond its allotted span—the closest spider knots a tourniquet, the leaf yellows, drops, and is flipped neatly into a drainage hole.  The spiders drink nothing; they take in vapour through pinprick pores, then vent it as still more twine.  A single plant may wear a century’s embroidery, all invisible until you peel the foliage aside and hear it whisper with taut lines.

6. The Twice-Quiet Fauna  
All animal voices share a law: one utterance per hour.  Pink ibis raise their wicker-soled feet and speak a single bark that counts the time.  Bracelet frogs vocalise exactly on the same beat, so the country ticks like a living chronometer.  Predation is technically allowed—ibis stalk frogs, larger frogs swallow smaller—but no carcass decays.  The moment muscle slackens, the seam spiders arrive with solvent threads; within minutes the body is a bundle of recyclable protein parcels, dropped through slotted tiles and gone.  Shortly afterward a clutch of young appears somewhere sufficiently distant to keep the census balanced.  Death feeds birth but under a bookkeeping so rigorous it feels mercifully heartless.

7. Absent Elders  
The thumbprints in the unfired hills, the leather knots glossed with age, the glaze on ropes: these all insist someone with human-scaled hands built the Floor.  Yet no warm-blooded elder stirs.  Ask a Seam-Tender in gestures and you will get a still shrug, palm lifted as if to say the question was filed long ago.  Script scored into a cracked retaining wall—four sinuous lines—translates loosely to:  
Here stands the demonstration.  
Judge the craft, not the craftsperson.  
Many scholars suspect the makers ascended through the same logic that keeps the world at noon: a final optimisation stripped even them away, leaving only the model running in immaculate repeat.

8. Possible Ways New People Appear  
Most fresh human arrivals swear they walked in from somewhere real—a salt flat, a fever dream, the deck of a becalmed ship—and found a door of light lying flat on the ground.  A few, however, insist they were born directly of the place.  One woman remembers clawing up through a mangrove pit, skin glazed like newborn pottery, lungs already full of the steaming air.  She could speak within hours but her voice never echoes, as if she had been grammared to the world the way shadows are.  If the Floor wants custodians with mammal hands—hands to mend the joints a spider cannot reach—it may cast them in small desperations from the clay under every tile.

9. What They All Share  
Whether wood-sleeved Seam-Tender, silent aviator, or accidental pilgrim, every inhabitant obeys two reflexes:  
a) never let unregulated entropy show,  
b) never break the hush.  
Lightning reflexes of care blossom where a dropped pack might scuff the glaze.  Conversations drop to breathy signs.  Even hot tears are dabbed away before they spot the tiles.  The land’s serenity is so total that to disturb it feels like desecration; yet correspondingly, to help maintain it grants a belonging more profound than nationality.  Visitors who manage one full loop of 480 steps without causing an unruly sound begin unconsciously matching the Tenders’ tapping code with their heel strikes.  Give it a month and you will wake speaking no name the air can echo, pruning a vine exactly a finger-span from a seam, neither surprised nor sad that you cannot remember where you came from.

10. The Quietest Thing  
Far beneath even the Water-Knockers, in the sump no step has ever mapped, something larger breathes.  When the ibis call the hour you can put your ear to a ceramic plate and feel a subaural answering pulse—slow as continents, gentle as a mother rocking a crib.  The Floor may itself be alive, dreaming of the beings that pace across its skin, shaping them from whatever material is easiest to reach: bark, brine, echoes, lost travellers, good intentions.  If so, every inhabitant listed above is really only a facet of that single, flawless organ.  They just do not yet know the wider name that holds them all—and of course, should you ever discover it, the air here will refuse to repeat it back.

So the Floor of the World is not uninhabited; it is perfectly, exactly inhabited.  Life is trimmed, stitched, wedged into the slightest crack where disorder might arise, then left to pulse forever under the hard white noon.  Meet its people—wood-wrapped menders, ceramic surgeons of leaves, gliders in cartridge-paper skies—and you realise the place is less a landscape than a choir sustaining one endless, almost inaudible note.  Step carefully; you may find yourself singing it before you mean to.
