WHO LIVES ON THE HIGH DROWNED TERRACE

(“Lives on” is imprecise; nothing here merely occupies the terrace.  Every creature, cell to whale-sized, is braided into the living mesa the way pigment is braided into marble.  What follows starts small and climbs in scale.)

1. The First Skin – Micro–Settlers
• Lens-algae.  A single-celled film that lies one molecule thick on every sun-facing surface.  Instead of chlorophyll it uses a ruthenium porphyrin that stays photo-active under the dim turquoise light.  To a human diver the film is invisible until a handprint wipes it away; the exposed bark beneath gleams silver for hours before the algae ooze back into place.

• Iron-binders.  Threadlike archaea that stitch flakes of dissolved ferric oxide into self-supporting lace.  They live only where magnetite liners generate weak electric fields, and the lace they knit is what the glass eels actually graze.  Remove the binders and the terrace loses its halo of cold light in weeks.

• Clock-yeast.  Occupies micro-fractures inside the boiler walls.  It buds and dies in precise 73-minute pulses, matching the boilers’ pressure cycle, and the bloom-and-crash pattern releases a metronomic fizz of CO₂ that keeps the gas bladders correctly buoyant.  Faulty yeast timing is the first sign a ridge is ill.

2. The Working Gut – Terrace Symbionts Proper
• Root-moths.  Wingless, finger-length larvae that look like clear cucumbers when at rest.  They crawl inside the peristaltic trunk-ridges, rasp flaking cellulose, then excrete the slurry already inoculated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria.  The terrace pays for the service by letting them pupate in warm boiler chimneys; the adults live six fluttering hours, mate, die, fall as snow.

• Cartographers.  Flatworms the colour of old honey.  They inhabit the space between bark and basement stone, digesting only dying tissue.  Each worm secretes an insulating mucus tunnel that persists for decades, and the aggregate maze forms an ever-updated map of structural weaknesses.  Vault-whales “read” this maze with their electroreceptors, choosing the safest up-drafts without conscious thought.

3. The Middle Bodies – Residents You May Actually See
• Glass eels.  Already noted, but up close the “glass” is an immune membrane that sloughs off parasites the moment they bite.  The eel is both hunter and city: over two hundred commensal shrimp species live on its inner surfaces, venturing out only when the eel sleeps inside a trunk hollow.  No two eels host the same entourage, so they recognise one another by taste.

• Anklet crabs.  Their ring-shaped bodies are not mere armour; they are literal bracelets.  Each generation finds a juvenile trunk-ridge, secures itself, and mineralises into an open loop.  The living crab eventually abandons the ring and grows a new one further along.  Old terrace stretches are paved with thousands of vacant rings, silent testimonies to forgotten pilgrimages.

• Vault-whales.  Leaf-thin, yes, but also leaf-wide: the largest span more than a hectare.  Their cartilaginous jaws are lined with tens of thousands of pressure plates that double as ears; every “clack” is both a feeding snap and an echolocation pulse that lets them hear the slow thunder of boilers from kilometres away.  A lone whale sounds lonely, so neighbouring whales echo the pulse, building an anti-predator chorus that shakes silt from the terrace roof.

4. The Apex Stillness – Things Too Large to Hunt
• Reef-trees (the terrace itself).  Each trunk-ridge is the visible strand of a single organism whose body spreads laterally through the mesa for hundreds of kilometres.  Crown-to-crown encounters provoke neither competition nor merger; the trees simply overlap tissues and share sap.  Botanists argue whether the drowned plateau is one super-organism or a parliament of tens of thousands that long ago voted never to part.

• The Stereolith.  Rare as comets.  Imagine a turtle the size of a town square, shell grown from the same lignin-calcium composite as the terrace.  It drifts a metre above the mesa, anchored by dozens of tendril-feet that plug into the bark.  Once a decade it extrudes a single offspring stone-egg, rolls it along until the shell pattern matches the local trunk-pulse, then abandons it.  The egg hatches only if the terrace itself “accepts” the rhythm; unapproved eggs petrify and are re-absorbed over centuries, so the Stereolith lineage co-evolves with the plateau’s own heartbeat.

5. The Occasional Thinkers – Persons, Perhaps
• Echo-binders.  Octopoid, translucent, seldom bigger than a human head.  They spend whole lives wrapped around magnetite veins, shaping the electric fields to modulate terrace thunder into slow music.  You will hear them before you see them: the ridge-pulse gains unexpected harmonics, a seventh above the boiler drone.  Whether the binders are intelligent or merely reflexive artisans is undecided; tampering with their work produces rapid, targeted blackouts that suggest collective intent.

• Pelagic Archivists.  Two or three sightings per century.  Humanoid silhouettes inside triple-layer diving robes woven from living sheet-leaf.  They never speak, never accept help, never vandalise.  They arrive trailing kite-books of linked membrane, let those books fill with terrace current for a decade or two, then furl them and depart.  Some believe they are surface scholars; others suspect they are terrace-born sapients collecting news from above.  Until one removes a mask, the debate remains open.

6. The Uncounted Multitudes – Death That Is Not Death
Every body, once still, is processed by moss-kelp into fresh organ or architecture.  That digestion lasts just long enough to host entire after-life communities: worm-hermits, filament sawyers, formaldehyde gnats that breed in the preservative chemicals of cooling flesh.  By the time a carcass becomes anonymous terrace, a dozen micro-civilisations have lived, warred and vanished within it.

7. The Terrace Itself – Final Word
Ask a terrace linguist how many inhabitants the plateau owns and she will give you a number: one.  All pulses, growls, migrations and pilgrim-circuits are the movements of a single continental animal wearing a million masks.  Everything smaller than the mesa is an organ, a song, or a dream.  Wander long enough among its steady thunder and you may feel your own heartbeat entrained, your thoughts pacing to its timeless zero-flux pulse, until you cannot decide whether you are still visitor or newly budded cell.

Turn away while you remember how to count yourself as separate.  Those who linger too long emerge speaking in rhythms, not sentences, and keep the terrace’s roar in their lungs like a second breath for the rest of their abbreviated, surface-tossed lives.
