Who lives on Atmern Ridge?  
No single species, no single culture, no single consciousness—rather a loose confederacy of interdependent beings, each making a different kind of peace with heat, darkness and upward-crawling water.  Think of them as strata laid one atop another, the way slag-cast towers rise from basalt: the deeper layers hardly remember the newer ones, yet each relies on the rest to keep from crumbling.

────────────────────────────────────────
1. The Ridgefolk (Homo condensensis, colloquial “Condensers”)
────────────────────────────────────────
Origin and body  
• They are recognisably human but long adapted—half by genetic tinkering, half by relentless natural cull—to a world that hovers just below the scald line.  
• Skin: matte umber, riddled with sweat-glands enlarged to pearl-sized nodules that weep continuously; the liquid evaporates in seconds, dumping heat the way desert lizards shed colour.  
• Hair: replaced by cap-fine keratin whiskers that bead moisture and funnel it toward collection collars worn at the neck. A worker can fill a half-litre canister in a humid afternoon without once lifting a cup.  
• Eyes: pupils go needle-thin in the copper murk; irises wash out to smoky grey by middle age. Cataracts are common, so almost everyone over thirty tattoos way-lines—thick cobalt bars—at temple and cheek to help friends recognise them by touch.

Dress and gear  
• Clothing is less fabric than membrane: layered perfluoro sheets laced with capillaries that wick condensate into hip bladders. Garments hiss faintly as safety valves vent excess.  
• Every belt carries three essentials: a ferro-compass that displays direction as a trembling dot instead of a needle; a corundum time-bead that resets itself when the borehole beacon pings; and a thumb-sized algae vial whose glow strength doubles as dosimeter for magnetic surges.

Numbers and settlement  
• Fewer than nine thousand souls, spread across seventeen slag towers. Families rarely top three members; kinship is traced more through apprenticeship than blood.  
• They call the stretches between towers “the Breaths.” Anyone crossing a Breath walks single-file to minimise thermal turbulence and wears a shoulder-mounted strobe so the fog knows to fold around, not into, delicate lungs.

Work and rhythm  
• Ninety percent of labour turns on water: coaxing it from cloud, stripping it of metal, bottling both separately. Titles grow from that work: Up-Fall Clothiers sew the rust-red prayer ribbons; Churnwrights tune the vent whistles so “the belly” speaks in tempered chords; Slagwrights supervise the ceramic knitting spiders that shape the next generation of towers.  
• Because chronometers drift, a shift ends not by the hour but after 40 000 pump strokes, counted by vibration through the floor. Ridgefolk learn to sleep on any surface that does not hum.

Mind and myth  
• Religiosity is low, superstition high. They do not worship the ridge, but they avoid angering it: no one drains a vent basin dry, no one shouts during boil cycle, and every newborn receives a single drop of “up-water” on the tongue so it learns early that liquid can fall both ways.  
• Old love songs treat two bodies as condenser plates: never touching, always exchanging heat through the narrowest possible gap.

────────────────────────────────────────
2. Second Citizens: Parthenogenic Machine Clades
────────────────────────────────────────
Genealogies of iron  
• Most heavy equipment on the plateau descends from three original fabricator rigs. Centuries of self-repair have left them culture as much as mechanism—each rig stamps its parts with a clan sigil (Chevron, Rip-Tooth, or Hollow Sun) and therefore propagates a mechanical “lineage.”  
• While Ridgefolk own the towers, machines own the spaces beneath: the condensate guts where belt furnaces roar and distillery worms coil. Here the air is lethal to organics, so humans enter only in snorkelled sarcophagus suits. Machine clades negotiate among themselves by thermionic code bursts that resonate up the slag ribs like whale song.

Political status  
• The 17-Tower Accord recognises any autonomous process that can keep itself operating for 10 000 pump strokes as a Second Citizen. Such entities may petition for resources—ore allotments, maintenance downtime—through carbon-etched tablets slipped into drop slots at each condensation plaza.  
• Friction is low but not nil. In 412 Y (Ridge calendar) a rogue extruder tried to steal a whole tower’s worth of slag to replicate itself; Churnwrights answered by starving its feed lines, turning it into a glass maquette still on display at Plaza Three.

────────────────────────────────────────
3. The Covert Biota
────────────────────────────────────────
3.1  Condensate Algae (“Glowveins”)  
• Single-celled but colonial, they lace gutters in filament webs. The turquoise light comes from electroluminescent proteins triggered by trace nickel; maintenance crews scrape the mats monthly, the harvest pressed into resin tiles that line children’s rooms in place of lamps.  
• If the algae’s glow winks out en masse, Ridgefolk know a boil cycle is imminent—they say the “veins close before the plate inhales.”

3.2  Ridge-Moss (Siliciflor ascendens)  
• A self-weaving silica lattice seeded by spores that ride the magnetic eddies. It “feeds” on humidity, knitting translucent carpets over unused walkways.  
• Remarkably, its crackle pattern encodes the footsteps that broke it—a natural waveform memory. Archivists can read a week’s worth of traffic by shining polarized light across a moss sheet and counting diffraction pulses.

3.3  Glass-Scaled Hexapods (Class disputed)  
• Height of a stalking cat, six legs jointed backwards, body plated in calcium-silicate that mirrors the basalt. Sightings spike during clear rain years; no carcass has ever been recovered.  
• Folk belief: they are the clear rain personified; to see one is to owe the ridge a secret. People who claim encounters never speak above a whisper again.

────────────────────────────────────────
4. Ephemeral Citizens
────────────────────────────────────────
Clear-Rain Wraiths  
• During the once-in-decades precipitation of “dry water,” silhouettes appear—humanoid outlines cut darker than dark, as though scooped from the meagre light.  
• They elude sensors but register on magnetographs as standing waves. The theory goes: the ridge’s bent time coils into quasi-stable loops, giving momentary shape to its own statistical anomalies. The Ridgefolk simply call them “Wraiths” and leave a bowl of ordinary condensate at each tower threshold so the apparition, having borrowed brightness, may return something wet.

────────────────────────────────────────
5. Social Constellations
────────────────────────────────────────
Guilds rather than government  
• Condenser Council: Five elders rotate chair every 12 000 pump strokes; decisions are carved onto slag tablets then ceremonially cooled in seep water.  
• Ribbon-Ascetics: itinerants who map up-falls by tying oxidised silk streamers. They speak rarely, subsist on moss cracklings, and consider their ribbons the ridge’s nervous system made visible.  
• Time-Sibs: horologists who live in the borehole shaft. They graft gyromagnetic sequencers to their breastbones, claiming to hear the planet’s true beat. Ordinary folk think them half mad but trust no other clocks.

Trade with the below  
• Gravity tramlines ferry casked mineral water, shimmering with heavy-metal micelles, to lush lowlands that pay in grain, pigments, sometimes children eager for apprenticeship.  
• Returning gondolas bring woven organics—cotton, wood pulp—to be spun into drip-proof films. Anything that burns is hoarded for festivals: once a year Plaza One blooms with saffron flames, a thing the ridge’s wet breath cannot easily supply.

────────────────────────────────────────
6. Everyday Texture
────────────────────────────────────────
Morning  
Dawn is theoretical: the sky passes from dull red to duller red. Vent nets open with a clack, blowing vinegar-hot air that tastes of hematite. Children race gutter-scooters, kicking through turquoise algae spray that paints their shins.

Midshift  
The quiet feels deliberate. You hear your own pulse louder than your neighbour’s voice. Somewhere a slagwright curses as ceramic knitting slips a loop; across the Breath, a machine choir answers with a calibration chord—F-sharp descending to silence.

Evening  
Glowveins brighten, sodium arcs stay on. Couples climb the obsidian chimneys to sit where acid mist rots the edges; they share distilled sap from ridge-moss cores, a drink both sweet and faintly abrasive. Far below, gondola brakes screech, dragging tonight’s heat toward the kingdoms of ordinary air.

Night  
The ridge exhales; steam columns collapse into drizzle that never hits the ground. Someone on watch swears a hexapod just rippled across the plate. No one argues, but next morning you’ll see fresh ribbons flapping rust-red where the creature supposedly paused to drink.

────────────────────────────────────────
That, then, is what lives on Atmern Ridge:

• Humans refactored for humidity, surefooted on cracking glass.  
• Machine lineages who speak in temperatures and cut treaties in voltage.  
• Algae that glow when the sun gives up, moss that remembers every trespass, animals made of mirage and rumor, and perhaps the ridge itself—time-crooked, magnetic, half awake.

Together they keep the enormous lung breathing: drip, boil, drip again, generation after generation, until clear rain falls and the whole plateau forgets the difference between biology, geology and dream.
