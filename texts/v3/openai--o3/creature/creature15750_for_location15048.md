Who or what manages to persist in the Upper Sea can be sorted—loosely—into five overlapping provinces of being.  None is truly separate from the others; gravity bends their borders, time shuffles their ages, and every life inherits a little mineral from the drowned mountain and a little dream from the water-sky above.

I. Things Too Small to Name Only Once  

1. Saltglass Phytids  
• Single-celled but armored in transparent plates of silicon borrowed from the vitrified arches far below.  
• Drift in the upper three meters where pewter light is strongest; each cell works like a concave lens, gathering every stray photon into a chloroplast ribbon.  
• When local gravity swings sideways the plates click together, forming living snow-flakes that tumble in tight spirals, slowing the descent that would otherwise strand them in darkness.  

2. Chronofuse Spores  
• Origin uncertain—perhaps a fungus once, perhaps fallout from an abandoned machine.  
• Collect along the humming “gravity scars.” Their metabolism is tied to the slight temporal slippage found there; they burst, divide, and recombine in flickers measured in negative seconds.  
• Most larger creatures pick them up inadvertently; the spores keep their hosts a fraction of a heartbeat out of step with local entropy, enough to hinder decay but not enough to grant immortality.  

II. The Rooted & The Reluctantly Mobile  

3. Spiral Barnacles (locally: Wayfarer Helices)  
• Each animal extrudes a calcium-carbonate track beneath itself, then ratchets forward one denticle at a time—about 80 cm per day when gravity is kind, far slower when it flips.  
• They graze crimson lich-coral, leaving kilometer-long braided pathways that later become nurseries for ribbonfish eggs.  
• At the coil’s center lives a brood-queen the size of a child’s balled fist.  All outward rings are her cloned daughters, aging in larger and larger coralline whorls until, at the edge, the eldest break away, release their shells, and float up as jelly-soft feeders that resemble loose, milky scarves.  

4. Pillar-Algae Courts  
• The forty-meter cellulose trunks described in the survey are not solitary plants but federations: dozens of separate genotypes grafted together around a hollow vascular chimney.  
• Along that chimney perch “courtier” organisms—violet slugs with mirrored faces—that sip exuded sugars and secrete antifungal resins in payment.  
• When a gravity rip-tide topples a pillar, every courtier abandons the wreck, carrying cuttings in its gut.  Within days a new colony root fuses into the silt of whatever plane is now “down.” Thus the forest walks, just exceptionally slowly.  

III. The Drifters, Hunters, & Breakers of Silence  

5. Shear-Line Ribbonfish  
• Paper-thin, translucent, never longer than a human handspan.  
• Occupy the narrow thermal boundary layers where ascending brine meets colder sinks; they hold position by sculling their long pelvic fins like tiny ailerons.  
• Diet is simple: anything shaken loose by turbulence—mostly plankton, occasionally the eyes of less agile kin.  
• When frightened they spread their fins to catch the next buoyant updraft and are hurled sideways or upward in a glitter of scales, gone before a predator can vector-correct.  

6. Glass Mantas  
• Wingspan five to seven meters, skin laced with interlocking silica spicules that refract the olive-copper gloom into knife-long spears of light.  
• They do not pursue prey; instead they flare those wings, flash-blinding entire shoals of ribbonfish, then glide mouth-open through the stunned cloud.  
• Mantas court along vertical curtains of water, tracing figure-eights that echo the fold in local space.  Observers blame these dances for many timing anomalies in nearby instruments.  

7. Choir-Eels  
• Blind leopards of muscle and gristle, resonating sacs along their flanks tuned to the infrasonic moan of stressed quartz.  
• They hunt by harmony: anything that fails to hum on the correct sub-pitch is bitten, tasted, and either released or dismantled.  
• When several hundred gather the overlapping bassnotes make the water itself tremble; canyon walls sprinkle silt like rain, and delicate machines—as every lost expedition eventually learns—shear their own mounting bolts and implode.  

IV. Archival Presences  

8. The Archivist (recorded titles: Parallax Kraken, Umbra Librarian, The Quiet Law)  
• Shape impossible to fix; optical baselines disagree by whole meters.  Most sightings resolve into a vertical stack of ribbed plates, each plate rotating against the next like a book being leafed mid-swim.  
• Size estimates start at fifty meters length and rise until the witness runs out of nerve.  
• Non-predatory.  It intercepts fallen fragments—smashed kelp trunks, broken quartz, shards of vitrified architecture—and appears to store them inside a mantle of overlapping voids.  
• Instruments inside the stored regions report time offsets up to several days.  Whatever the Archivist is, it is also a library, or at least a vault where duration itself becomes shelving.  

V. Visitors, Exiles, & Other Questionable Intelligences  

9. Foam Gnats  
• Not insects but micro-drone organisms spun from chitinous bubbles. They graze the airborne sargassum in the broth-thick rainscape above the water’s roof.  
• Some descend in scouting flocks, tasting mineral compositions, then ascend again.  No one knows whom they report to, or whether their endless sampling constitutes its own purpose.  

10. Sealed-lung Monks (Human; cultural offshoot of the Orbital Pilgrim Factions)  
• Settle on summit knolls where the kelp is thin.  Their pressure robes are patched with living lich-coral to self-heal against slow tears.  
• Life’s work: listening.  The monks sink quartz chimes into crevasse mouths, then record the resulting harmonics on wax cylinders spun by braided vine-mat sails.  
• They claim the sea speaks in a grammar of descending chords and that one day the Archivist will align its plates to make the final, resolving note.  Until then they trade data-amber for supplies with any expedition brave or foolish enough to dock at their platforms.  

11. Ghost Alloy (If Material Can Think)  
• Entire seams of metallic ore have annealed with the Chronofuse spores.  The result is a semi-elastic lattice that remembers every stress it has ever suffered.  
• When the lattice accumulates enough memory it extrudes dendritic antennæ and begins to echo noises never made nearby—occasional voices, often the deep clang of forges, once the unmistakable laugh of a child.  
• Whether this qualifies as life is in dispute.  The monks insist it is simply the Sea dreaming itself metal; prospectors smell profit; the Archivist circles any large vein for reasons it has not disclosed.  

Threads That Bind Them All  

• Gravity’s fickleness has replaced speed with patience.  Nothing here hurries; everything drifts, ratchets, flexes, or sings its way toward food and reproduction.  
• Light is currency.  The Upper Sea is poor in it, so creatures wear mirrors, lenses, and phosphorus the way surface fauna wear fat and fur.  
• Memory migrates outward: etched in quartz, spiraled in barnacle tracks, laminated in the Archivist’s layers, even soaked into metals.  Intelligence, if it emerges at all, is archival first, analytical second.  

Stand long enough among them—let the hush rise over the ticking of your own heart—and you will begin to feel the place studying you back.  Not hungrily (though hunger exists), not even warily, but with the same slow, glandular curiosity the Sea applies to everything that persists within its folding, dusk-lit reach.  Endure a few seasons, and some part of you will be remembered here forever, filed among the mineral thoughts and drifting hymns of the beings that call the Upper Sea home.
