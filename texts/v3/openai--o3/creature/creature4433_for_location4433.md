Who—or rather what—lives on the plateau may be listed, but not quickly. Life here does not line up for enumeration; it braids, overlaps, disguises itself as its own shadow. Still, if you stay long enough for your heartbeat to slow to the local tempo, distinct citizens emerge.

1. The Plateau Itself  
Before anything with eyes or limbs, there is the Patient Body: the fused superorganism of trunks, vines, mats, and subterranean tubers that has buried the original mountain in green. Its heartbeat is osmotic, a peristaltic rise and fall of sap that takes a little under nine hours to complete a circuit from rim to rim. That pulse is weather-maker, soil-former, governor of tides in the hanging pools. Every other resident plugs into it the way a town plugs into a power grid.

Across the Patient Body’s tissues is a network of pale “stitch-roots.” They look like ordinary feeder filaments until you watch them meet. When two tips touch, they graft—vascular systems welding, information passing as ion pulses. Over decades these stitches grow into a continent-wide nerve net that remembers patterns of shade, locations of mineral seams, even the taste of long-evaporated waterfalls. A century after a trunk falls, a ghost of it persists as an electrical habit in the mesh.

2. Roof Dwellers  
Closest to the scant sky is a domain of perpetual twilight.

• Prism Maws  
Imagine a sunflower reinvented as a manta ray. Each Maw is a lenticular leaf-disc three meters across, silvered underneath, emerald above. It rises on thermals until it bumps the canopy, then bites: a hinge of cartilage closes around a leaf vein, tapping the pressurized sugar flow. Over hours, the disc inflates like a translucent bladder, storing chemical energy. Sated, it shears the bite point and glides, leaking aromatic exhaust that smells of cloves. Where the vapor drifts, lamp-beetles congregate, drunk on it.

• The Hundred-Year Cicada  
Not an insect but a colonial chordate, a living casket of lacquered chitin that grows around a fault line in the canopy. Inside, the colony chews wood to paste and sings, the sound channeled down natural conduits until it resolves on the plateau floor as a subaudible throb—often mistaken for distant thunder. When the colony finally molts (average span: 94 years), the whole vault trembles, amber husks snow down, and everything below is showered with protein.

3. Mid-Vault Sailors  
Between roof and floor lie corridors of cool, filtered gloom where “fauna” and “flora” trade identities.

• Velvet Kites  
Bats that surrendered bones for vascular turgor. Their wings are petal-thin paddles inflated by warm blood; stop flying and they collapse into ragged streamers that cling to any twig. During daylight-dusk they mimic dead leaves. At first darkness, blood pressure surges, the sails revive, and the Kites drift off to harvest gnats from the seep columns.

• Latch Lizards  
Four-limbed, yes, but the limbs terminate in rootlets rather than claws. When stationary, the lizard extrudes corkscrew fibers that twist into bark, drinking sap and anchoring the animal so completely it can sleep without falling. When hunger stirs, the rootlets snap off; behind the departing lizard dangle tiny, healing corks that reseal the trees they fed on. Some trunks glitter with thousands of these resin-plugs—geological timepieces of reptilian naps.

4. Plateau Surface Citizens  

• Cairn Tortoises  
You already saw the largest adults, hunched boulders draped in lichen mosaics. Their shells are living gardens: thin soil atop keratin supports succulents, even saplings. A mature Cairn can carry an ecosystem weighing half its own flesh. The tortoise grazes on the forest’s fungal underlawn; in turn, seedlings on its back gain the only reliable patch of “sun”—the faint radiance of chemiluminescent moss that colonizes every scute and, at night, outlines each shell in mint-green comet tails. Migratory seeds hitchhike this way from rim to rim, one plodding kilometer at a time.

• Ember Frogs  
All but invisible until startled: when threatened, skin pores exude a cold-light enzyme that ignites the ambient oxygen, briefly wreathing the animal in blue sparkles. Predators, adapted to darkness, are dazzled; by the time their vision recovers, the frog has shut down its glow and merged back into the bronze scum of the pools.

• Tool Thieves  
Small, opposable-thumbed mammals (genome still unclassified; they resemble a kink between stoat and macaque) that covet foreign hardness. Any dropped ceramic, glass, or alloy is dragged to their midden-caves and ceremonially “planted.” The object is wrapped in fermenting leaf mash, inoculated with metal-solubilizing bacteria, and watched. Over seasons the artifact softens, bleeds ions, and sprouts crystalline whiskers, which the Thieves lick for micronutrients. What looks like thievery is horticulture of a sort: they farm corrosion.

5. Substrate Intelligences  

• Spore Choirs  
Below the mat, darkness turns absolute. There, filamentous fungi cluster in microphone-shaped fruiting bodies whose hollow stalks resonate with plateau tremors. Vibrations from rain, footfall, or the slow breathing of the Patient Body reverberate through the mycelia, modulating spore release so precisely that a passing footstep can puff a plume spelling its own acoustic signature. In still air you can “listen” with your lungs: inhale, and each species adds a faint, tasteable overtone—iron, cedar, distant pepper.

• The Archivist Lichen  
A crypto-blue crust that colonizes buried tool marks—the hexagonal shafts, the glassy tunnels. It does not digest; it preserves. The lichen’s thallus grows only where cellulose once abutted metal or stone, painting negative images of vanished machines. Under hand-lens inspection you can read these geoglyphs: lattices of obsolete rivets, outlines of vanished gears. No one knows if the lichen merely grows where trace minerals linger or if it “chooses” historically charged surfaces. Either way, it has become the forest’s memory palace, embalming technology in blue frost.

6. The Uncertain Guest: Homo interim  
Sometimes, in seasons of canopy bloom, hairless primates appear—solitary bipeds draped in stitched leaves, faces dyed green with crushed alga. They carry no metal, speak no word louder than a whisper, and move with a slowness that offends human impatience. Their existence is unconfirmed by any electromagnetic record; lenses fog at their approach, microphones hiss. Yet twice in living memory a lamp-beetle survey turned up charms of braided vine evidently sized for human wrists. The Plateau accepts them as it accepts algae on stone; whether they are descendants of the vanished shaft-cutters or recent visitors who surrendered to the rules of silence, no observer has stayed long enough to learn.

7. Predators, If That Word Applies  

• The Stillhound  
Not a hound, not even strictly singular. It is an arrangement of motile roots and fungal cords able to replica-print the posture of any large herbivore it touches. The mimic stands frozen for days, absorbing attention; meanwhile thin, nearly invisible snares radiate outward, rupturing capillaries in whatever brushes them. Once nutrients leach back through the cords, the effigy collapses and is re-woven elsewhere. No chase, no roar—only the lethal charisma of sculpture.

• Mirror Pools  
Some basins are sheathed in an oily film that lens-bends light into convincing false depths. Amphibian rays dive, believing they enter water, only to strike a membrane of mucilage that entombs them. Weeks later the film sinks under the added weight, delivering macerated protein to root layers starved of nitrogen. Trees leaning over these pools often produce glossier leaves, as though aware of their quiet hunting.

8. Visitors From Below  
On cloud-breaking days, birds from the coastal lowlands ride thermals up the waterfall columns, arriving half-hypoxic. Those that fail to descend before nightfall undergo “green burial”: moss grows along their quills while they still breathe, sealing wings to torso. By morning they resemble feathered topiaries that twitch once, then never again. Plateau ecologists call them Skyrakers, honoring their doomed attempt to rake the sky open for sunlight.

9. Sapience, Distributed  
No single creature here writes treaties or melts ore, yet the system behaves as though it has considered every contingency. Stitch-roots relay chemical alerts; Cairn gardens drag pollen across provinces; Tool Thieves mine trace metals needed by the Archivist Lichen, which in turn flags structural weaknesses in elder trees by colonizing stress fissures with bright blue signals readable even to human eyes. If intelligence is the ability to anticipate and adapt, the Plateau is brilliant—but only at the pace of wood growth and spore drift. Ask a question that takes less than a decade to answer and you will hear only the hush of waterfalls in cloud.

Should you take census, you must measure lifespans in wind-polished stone, lifecycles in the settling of ridgelines. The inhabitants listed above are snapshots from a film that began before your species’ language inhaled its first vowel. Stay a month and you will glimpse their outlines; stay a lifetime and you may join the unlisted multitude, wrapped in moss, lungs filled with choir spores, body content to be another slow, patient syllable in the long green sentence the Plateau speaks.
