Who—and what—inhabits the sky-held sea?  Almost everything here is in its first draft; even so, the place already throngs with lives that are adapting to the cradle of steam, stone and dim light.  Follow the shore clockwise from the lowest break in the rim and you will meet them in rough order, from the simplest to the most willful.

1.  The Makers of Breath  
In the shallows, sheets of micro-algae called veilcyan grow so densely that you can scoop them up like scarf-cloth.  They are the primary reason anything larger can exist: veilcyan exhales the little oxygen the basin possesses and secretes waxy bubbles that do not pop but fuse into lentil-sized beads.  These beads drift upward, giving every midday its faint pearlescent haze.  Larvae of almost every animal graze on them.  When veilcyan dies it sinks in emerald snow that feeds vents and worms alike, tying sky to water to stone in a single ribbon of carbon.

2.  The Glass-Wing Ray (Aetheroptera lucens)  
A hand-span across at birth, a dinner-table wide at adulthood, the ray’s cartilaginous “wings” are membranes so thin you can see the heart stutter beneath.  Capillaries end in pores that can purge water and take in air; within six strokes the ray lightens enough to breach the surface, ride a humidity updraft, then fold and re-enter like a dropped handkerchief.  

Social life: Loose flotillas of thirty or forty.  They communicate by momentary opacities: a wing flashes cloudy, stays so for three heartbeats, clears—every pattern means something different.  Youngsters practice stories this way at dusk, filling the mist with flickering sentences no outsider can parse.

Ecological role: Essential pollinators.  Each time a ray re-enters the water it brings aerosolized spores that cling to its ventral ribs.  Only spores that experience pressure differentials germinate, so many cliff-mosses depend entirely on these brief flights.

3.  Spiral Eels (Archithermis helicon)  
Picture a length of ivory ribbon curled round and round a heat vent until it becomes a living auger.  At the center is a mouth like a radial iris that drinks dissolved metals; a paired tail at the outermost coil feels temperature changes and flicks to hold station.  Every vent has exactly one mature eel—juveniles that arrive late either perish or scout for nascent fissures and so spread the colony map.   

Curiosities:  
•  They vibrate at infrasonic frequencies that loosen nearby basalt, enlarging the vent in slow millimetres.  The people mine such eel-softened stone first.  
•  Their skins precipitate aragonite scales; when dried these thin plates turn to mother-of-pearl—the closest thing the settlement has to coinage.

4.  Drip-Foots (provisional name given by rim-children)  
Those cat-sized, heart-shod shadows are neither mammal nor reptile but something half-way: warm-blooded yet egg-laying, skin sleek like otter fur until wet, when it displays a film of ultra-fine feathers.  The inverted-heart footprint is produced by two joined middle toes; the outer toes articulate independently and can pinch vine, so the creature seems to carry four thumbed hands where legs should be.

Habits: Tremendously curious but color-blind; they judge the world by gloss and texture.  A freshly knapped obsidian scraper left unattended will almost certainly vanish within minutes, spirited into a drip-foot midden of shiny trophies.  Their midden-piles are valued by human knappers who sometimes trade smoked fern-frog for lost chisels.

Domestication prospects: Adults loathe confinement, but pairs about to nest have been known to accept food offerings and move their clutch closer to human dwelling beams, presumably for shared predator vigilance.  The arrangement is called “roof-cousining.”

5.  The Vine-Engines  
Vines as thick as a wrestler’s torso hang from the inner caldera wall.  They look vegetable; in truth the central cores are colonial animals—muscle-ropes—that have coaxed symbiotic creepers to armor them in living bark.  When wind tilts them they can contract, drawing sap upward in a visible shudder.  That pulsed flow powers dangling leaf-pumps that filter trace elements straight from basin water.  

Each engine ends in a root-brain: a puckered disk of nerve tissue pressed flat against rock.  These disks sense vibration and, if cut, pinch shut like a stingray mouth.  The locals respect the engines, harvesting only the outer bark, never the core, and leaving an offering shard of salt for every meter skinned.

6.  The Cloudlets  
Some lives never touch ground.  Within the hot fog roam airborne colonial bacterial mats lighter than the moist air they inhabit.  At night you identify them by the faint ultraviolet glow of fixation enzymes at their surface.  They shed stringers that drift down like spider-silk, seeding new mats.  Glass-wing rays sometimes collide with them and emerge filmed in glowing lace; children believe this is how the rays learn their flashing language.

7.  The Basin People  
Population: 620 give or take the last birthing tide.  They call themselves Pai’cho, “we who stay afloat.”  A more intimate autonym used only during story-ritual is Ulloil, mouth-shape of the hush that surrounds them.

Physique: Short, buoyant builds; bones infused with microscopic gas vesicles captured in infancy by drinking boiled bladder-fern tea.  Adults can float motionless for an hour with lungs empty, which allows deep trance listening to the caldera’s tectonic whispers.

Language: Mostly vowels breathed through the side of the mouth so consonants don’t echo off the water.  Outsiders would hear a chain of sighs and open-throated question-tones.  Names elongate across years: a child is “Ei,” the same person at twenty might be “Eiosi,” at elderhood “Ei-so-u-laha,” each syllable added during civic feats.

Dwelling pattern  
•  Rim shelves: chiselled bunk alcoves, obsidian mirror-panes angled to catch the rare sun-shafts for skin-warming.  
•  Waterline baskets: vine-plait spheres half filled with pumice, so floor level just kisses the surface.  Household tasks are done knee-deep; babies learn to crawl by paddling.

Subsistence  
•  Fishing rays with hair-nets woven from engine-vine bast; no barbs, the catch is milked of wing-fat then released.  Wing-fat ferments into a cheese that never molds and is traded for meteor iron.  
•  Gathering hot-vent salts precipitated by spiral eels; these flavor every meal, give bones their lightness, and serve in ritual pigment.  
•  Algaebeer from veilcyan pressed through bladder-fern leaves, drunk warm and luminous.

Craft  
Obsidian remains cornerstone, but the pride of Pai’cho artisans is negative-weight jewelry: pouches of dried cloudlet mats sealed in mother-of-pearl scale.  Wearers hover a finger’s width lighter, a fashionable swagger.

Kinship & law  
A person belongs first to a Current—clusters of three to seven households moored by braided vine-rope.  Currents rearrange annually on the night when elders recount the origin route of the massif through the mantle, a tale so long it forces dawn into attendance.  During the telling, rope ties are cut and re-tied; marriages, apprenticeships and alliances physically realign with the motions of barges.

Religion  
No gods, only Bearings—six cardinal inclinations defined by phenomena rather than direction:  
•  Upflow (toward densest clouds)  
•  Downfall (toward deepest echo)  
•  Warmth (nearest vent)  
•  Coolth (where meltwater seeps)  
•  Still (centre of hush)  
•  Stir (where hiss grows loud)  
An action gains fortune when aligned to its right Bearing.  For instance, childbirth should occur in Still, stone quarry in Stir.

Rites of Memory  
Because written matter rots, a life’s deeds are neither carved nor sung but floated.  On the night a person dies, their Current weaves a little bladder-fern balloon.  Inside are tiny tokens (a bit of scale, a thumb-sized tool, a shred of hammock) and a breath from each mourner.  Released just above the steam layer, the balloon will bob for months, visible occasionally as a pale bead.  When at last it bursts, whatever lessons the life held are said to return to common air.

8.  The Substrate Moths  
In tunnels where stone was quarried too hastily, damp cracks bloom with miniature crystals.  Snails eat these; moths hunt the snails, rasping them open with garnet-tipped proboscides.  The moths’ wings are flakes of basalt that grew along protein templates.  When a moth dies, its wings do not decay; they break free, identical to any other shard of volcanic glass.  Quarrymen keep one eye on every tool pile lest a wing in disguise take flight and set powdery eggs in new crevices.

9.  Unseen but Suspected—The Great Overflow  
Oral tables make room for a creature none have seen directly: a being tremendous enough to stir the sea in one breath.  Signs are wakes that appear on still days, sailing-barge moorings found snapped, and the periodic absence of rays, as if some titanic shadow has passed beneath.  Scholars argue it may not be single but plural—a pod of thermal leviathans that has not yet breached evolutionary horizon.  Children of the Pai’cho are taught to greet any large, slow wave with the formal salutation: arm slanted Upflow, head bent Downfall— a courtesy extended to whatever future intelligence might be listening.

10.  What Lies in the Stone  
The massif is young, yet geodes already pepper the walls—hollows full of water older than the basin itself.  Within some of these pockets drift eyeless, millimetric beings called adelphs: transparent ellipsoids whose only organ is a ring-shaped chromosome visible like an inked halo.  They divide once per century.  If liberated into open water they die immediately, so quarrymen keep newly split geodes sealed and lower them intact to the scholars’ barge, where adelphs are watched as living memories of the planet’s deeper mantle seas.

Toward Tomorrow  
Life in the cloud-held ocean is improvisation.  Each season the water warms a little, rim rises a little, vents open or close; each change births a niche and something fills it.  Already a few glass-wing rays have been found with stiffer scapular arches, hinting at true powered flight.  Spiral eels at the lowest vents grow so large the people chatter of building an eel-ridden elevator—rope harness about the beast, basalt counterweight in a buoyant corridor, raising freight into the rim caves with living muscle.  Even written word may someday win its struggle: artisans experiment with sapphire slurry boiled onto obsidian tablets, a glaze that laughs at mildew.  Should that succeed, the Pai’cho will at last fix stories in a medium slower to fade than memory—though some elders mutter that forgetting is what keeps the Currents supple.

For now, though, the basin remains poised on the lip of possibility, inhabited by things tentative, luminous, half imagined but wholly real: rays that write with light, vines that pump like hearts, mammals that steal reflections, people who rearrange their city to the rhythm of a spoken map.  All of them are fledgling notes in a long, slow composition that the massif itself is still learning to hum.
