They are easiest to miss at first, because the grotto’s native colors—bruise-violet ore, iron oxide, soot-green fronds—have already been woven into everything they wear.

1. What you mistake for an extra rib of basalt leaning against a column is often a resident at rest.  Their coats are pieced together from furnace felt, boiler-scale leather, and the peeled bark of cave-palms; when the wearer stands still the cloth slackens and glosses over with condensation until it looks like another wet stone.  A practiced stillness is their first defense: in a hall where every surface sweats, motion is glare.

2. Skin tones run the gamut of copper slag: black glass, fired clay, bright ingot-orange in the heat of work, cooling to liver brown in the off-shift.  Colour is helped along by the inhaled minerals that dust every lung.  Each birth cohort darkens differently, depending on which seam of ore was quarried most in the decade their alveoli first toughened.  Grandparents can name your batch-year by the undertone in your palms.

3. The eyes: pupils fat and soft as overripe figs, irises reduced to knife-thin rings that catch the cavern’s violet.  Children begin wide-eyed; by adolescence the pigment has retracted until their gaze is almost lightless.  They see warmth more than brightness.  A hand pressed to a pipe, a leaf flexing open, a stranger whose heart drums a degree hotter—these flare for them the way lanterns would for surface dwellers.

4. Hair exists, but hardly in its old job description.  The locals long ago traded ordinary follicles for something halfway to rootlets.  Stand close and you’ll see the strands are hollow, capillary bores that wick sweat upward.  At shift’s end they drape their braids over drip-troughs, squeeze, and harvest the salt.  Infants are shaved smooth until their first personal crop of wick-hair pushes through; the day is toasted with a thimble of brine.

5. Clothing is grown more than sewn.  They cultivate two chief textiles:

   • Feltvine: a liana that extrudes continuous ribbons of pith coated in wax.  Split, beaten, and steamed, the pith mats into an insulating felt that never fully dries and therefore never cracks.

   • Glass-fungus: the same smoked-glass buttress you saw serving as a stair.  Thin slices, cured over steam, flex like mica sheets.  Patches of it are quilted into coats at joint-points—elbows, knees—where extra slip is useful.  When the wearer bends, the laminae click faintly, a sound like distant typebars.

6. Tools double as planters.  A hammer’s haft is drilled with seed-wells; a coil of cable is wound around a damp moss-core so it will root to the gantry you eventually fasten it to.  Return a borrowed implement too clean and it may be refused for being “sterile.”

7. Speech is low-pressure, more hiss than consonant.  In hot, wet air vibration carries poorly, so most meaning rides on exhaled vowels and the shape of the mouth.  A common greeting translates to something between “Your coil runs smooth?” and “Your vines are drinking?”—the phrase compresses both checks into one.

Castes (though they would bristle at the word) arrange along thermodynamic gradients rather than heredity.

A. Violet Keepers  
   Latticewalkers who patrol the ore columns with pocket refractometers, measuring how much daylight the rock has hoarded.  When the daily bleed of radiance grows thin they reroute mirrors topside, or in worst case damp turbines to let the chamber cool enough that the stone will consent to drink again next noon.  Their bodies run cold; Keepers fast on fatty slurries that drop their core temperature so they can lean against the columns without perturbing the charge.

B. Dripwrights  
   Equivalent of plumbers, midwives, and brokers all at once.  They wear chest harnesses hung with hundreds of glass pipettes.  (Each pipette’s bore size denotes a different clan’s claim on condensate; trade is performed by swapping tubes.)  Dripwrights settle disputes: whose fungus-rafter shadowed whose gutter, whether a cracked pane counts as “intentional diversion.”  Their signatures are thin ropes of solder run across a leak and left to cool.

C. Combustors  
   Their trade is waste.  Any heat too dirty or too violent for the communal loops—the sputter of a misfired kiln, the flash from a seized bearing—is offered to the Combustors.  They pack it into clay ampoules lined with phase-change salts and bury those in the guts of compost piles, where the leftovers keep tropical mushrooms at a steady forty-five degrees.  An old Combustor smells singed and sweet, like caramelized sugar; their fingerprints are often gone, melted smooth against hot crucibles.

D. Listeners  
   Not quite priests, not quite engineers.  They carry tuning forks keyed to the dominant frequencies of root growth, pipe expansion, and human pulse.  Moving through the hall they strike the forks, hold the stems to different surfaces, and judge health by which notes ring true.  A cracked pillar, a root gone hollow with rot, or a heart in fibrillation—all reveal themselves to a Listener before any visible failure occurs.  Apprentices start at the periphery where wrong sounds are harmless; masters may dare the sump itself, tuning against the slow heartbeat of the turbine.

Children  
They swarm the overhead catwalks because the gantries are the coolest air in reach.  You will know them by the nets strapped across their torsos: seed-pouches woven from gecko skin.  Their sole formal lesson is to pass hand over hand along cable, sprinkling spores into any trickle that looks underpopulated.  When a child slips (rarely fatal—the sump’s foam catches), the vines that later grow from the splash zone are named for them.  It is a backhanded honor to have an entire stairwell of haphazard creeper called “Lita’s Folly” or “Jun’s Drop.”

Life cycle and death  
Average span is short by surface reckoning—fifty, fifty-five years.  Kidneys choke on metal, lungs silt.  Yet death is accepted as a design spec.  The moment breathing stops, the body is fed to a cradle-fungus whose hyphae spiral the limbs into a kneeling shape; months later the fungus petrifies into a support strut.  People shake hands against the struts the way surface folk touch headstones.  The oldest load-bearing ancestor they can name died during the first decade after the blasting and now props open a tram tunnel.

Religion, if the word is not too stiff, is mutualism.  One common proverb: “Heat, water, light—have none alone.”  Another: “The ore remembers the sun and lends us the memory.”  They make no icon of gods, only of ratios.  In each residence a small abacus hangs—beads of ambercondensate on copper wire—showing daily counts of leaves pruned versus machines installed.  Let the upper row overtake the lower and the household goes “debtor to breath.”  Neighbors arrive unasked, bearing cuttings, until the balance re-rights.

Visitors  
To be granted stay you must contribute one of three things:

   • A seed that will germinate in warm dark.  
   • A coil or bearing sized to fit their pre-industrial threadings.  
   • A new story explaining the sky—the concept remains exotic entertainment.

Bring none and you will still receive drinking gel, but only from a charity gutter where water tastes of old brass.  Stay past a week without earning place and the grotto itself will enforce departure: vines find the salt in your sweat too tempting, moss climbs your bedding, your lamp’s hood is requisitioned for a fern in need.  Eventually your belongings are so colonized the only merciful act is to leave them—and, politely but firmly, you—with the Dripwrights to break down.

In sum, the dwellers of the violet grotto are not quite engineers, not quite gardeners, not quite any single thing.  They are the hinge between metabolism and mechanism, bodies revised by a generation of negotiated truce with heat and scarcity.  If they resemble anything it is a guild of organs operating at metropolitan scale: each person an alveolus, each workshop a ventricle, each vine a nerve axon creeping outward to sense, to bind, to live a little further.  Speak softly, breathe steadily, share what leaks from you, and the city will accept you as another cell.  Fail the exchange and the chamber will ceil over again, closing behind your retreat like the suture of cooling basalt—quiet, violet, complete.
