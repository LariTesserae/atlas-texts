The first lives you notice are the ones the eye can follow without help.

1. The Lens-Bearers  
   They look like slugs only until you watch them long enough to see the differences. Their bodies are seam-less cylinders of gel, so clear they almost vanish except for the refractions that comb across their length. Each carries an ovoid, perfectly formed droplet on the high arc of its back. The droplet is not ballast; it is organ. Inside the water-lens pulses a haze of motile cells that harvest the weak ochre light and crack dissolved iron the way other creatures crack sugar. The slug itself is only scaffold and locomotive muscle for the lens. When the animal pauses to graze, it lowers the droplet against the mineral film, wetting both sides of the interface; enzymes diffuse outward, lift a molecular dusting of silica, and drift back to be stored as opalescent plates within the lens. Every thirty hours or so the slug shudders, pinches a seam across its own shoulders, and leaves the spent droplet glued to the wall like a slack bubble. The abandoned lenses are what give the basin’s higher reaches their incidental glitter. When the slug glides away it is already swelling a new globe, distilled from its own liquefied tissues and the broth it ingests as it goes.

2. Seed-Crabs  
   They appear only if you go still enough that your heartbeat muffles the drip-hush. A constellation of rice-grained dots begins to rearrange itself on the submerged stones. Each dot unfolds into something improbably tall: six spindles of chitin rising from a central capsule no wider than a pin head. The legs are hollow, fluted, and more joint than shaft; they tremble constantly, sifting boundary layers for inexhaustible fines of manganese oxide. One leg at a time breaks the surface in slow, rheostatic movements, wicking air down to a pocketed lung that cannot tolerate the water’s dissolved metals. Through that same leg the crab excretes—bubbling out a thread of nearly pure oxygen that curls like smoke and pops soundlessly. In life the crabs are colorless; in death they brown as iron finds them, and their remains become the filamentous rust that later generations scrape for nutrients. Their population never grows or shrinks. When one capsule fills with precipitate the animal stops, stiffens, and waits. Hours later two larvae, soft as mucus, ooze out of hidden sutures and walk away on legs of already mineralized shell, each destined to live a fixed number of steps before repeating the exchange.

3. Film-Workers  
   You will not see them at all, yet you touch their product each time you brush a wall. Where other worlds would host moss or lichen, this basin harbors a communion of archaeal cousins whose only pigment is a faint, ultraviolet-nibbling violet invisible to human sight. They build the glassy patina molecule by patient molecule, performing a dance of deposition and dissolution that keeps the walls forever young and forever ancient. The biofilm is no more alive in one place than another; cytoplasm and mineral switch identities so fluidly that to take a sample is to interrupt a sentence mid-phoneme. Whenever sunlight dares to sharpen, the collective tilts its chemistry to spread the glare, trading brightness for heat it can hold until the night asks it back.

4. Things Wearing Silence  
   At irregular intervals—never witnessed, always inferred—the hush itself condenses into articulate form. Visitors who sleep inside the bowl report no dreams, only the sense of having been politely examined. On waking they find the heel marks of bare feet beside their own prints, yet no residue, no blemish of oil or salt. A forensic look would reveal that each “footprint” is not compression but coalescence: minute beads have crawled together to mimic the arcs of toes, holding shape for ten, maybe twelve minutes before redistributing. Local folklore, sparse as it is, calls the makers Stewards, Supplicants, or simply Others; the few repeat travelers claim the air thickens into anatomy whenever the strict internal ratios of the basin—evaporation, condensation, diffusion—tip a measurable fraction out of alignment. The apparition is the system correcting itself: the atmosphere loaning coherence to a caretaker that performs the tiny labor a slug or a seed-crab cannot. Sometimes a Steward kneels at one of the stone bowls. When it rises, the bowl’s water stands a fraction clearer, as though impurities had been plucked out grain by discrete grain.

5. The Basin Itself  
   If the definition of life is contested metabolism enclosed by membrane, then the entire depression qualifies. Walls act as integument, springs as heartbeat, heat retention as slow, peristaltic breath. Its chemistry is not merely environment but intent, a self-editing script that erases every disturbance except the platforms it long ago adopted as organs. Those eight tables are electrochemical nodes: places where trickles of slightly different mineral load meet, pool, and equilibrate. In cloudy weather the floor-water migrates toward them, raising the waterline by less than the thickness of a thumbnail, enough to keep the system’s salinity inside the ancient parameters written into the glass skins of its microbes. When the sky thins and outside wind threatens to intrude, vapor lifts from the central vents, building an umbrella of fog the way an octopus balloons ink.

Hierarchy—if that word still fits—runs sideways rather than up or down. Each resident uses what it needs, excretes what another requires, and refrains from ambition. Predation would cost more thermodynamically than collaboration returns. Even the transient Stewards never display dominance; they smooth rather than break, their passage as gentle as the resetting of a meniscus. Power here is the power to leave no footprint that lasts.

Were you to remain a year, you would witness an entire epoch in miniature. You would see a Lens-Bearer family lineage plate a wall in mother-of-pearl, watch it flake, dissolve, and seed new slugs. You would chart the marching death of a cohort of Seed-Crabs and the crystalline births that follow minutes later. You might, on one windless dawn, sense a presence rise out of the warm exhalation, walk the basin’s diameter, and fold back into vapor. You would never hear a cry, a clash, a wingbeat—only the unbroken conversation of drip answering drip.

That, finally, is who lives here:  
• Creatures of gel that shepherd their own sunlight in droplets.  
• Articulated seeds that breathe through flutes and recycle their bodies into rust.  
• A skin of microbes writing and rewriting itself in glass.  
• Occasional caretakers condensed from the air to repair what time disarrays.  
• And beneath them all, the basin—older than the memory of waves, alive in slow, mineral thought, content to host a community whose highest ambition is to keep existing exactly, exquisitely, as it already does.
