Who lives in the Green Hollow?  
Almost everything that moves or thinks down there began as an improvisation upon the plants, because the plants are the oldest citizens and they set the rules.  Life follows their architecture the way tenants follow the rooms of an endlessly self-renovating house.  The cast can be read from smallest to largest, from root-skin outward, until at last you reach the clever bipeds who leave hearth-rings and bone drums and nothing more certain than footprints that heal behind them.

1.  The First People: the Allroot and its Helpers  
   •  The Allroot is not a species; it is one organism written across the entire chamber, an albino banyan that discovered geology.  Every “cable” you see is its vascular system, every membrane leaf its folded lung.  It hoards photons the way surface trees hoard sugars, banking them as electro-chemical gradients that can be shunted kilometres in an hour.  
   •  It has signals.  When a cliff face fractures, the exposed cambium spits a flare of lime-green light.  Other trunks answer, dimming or brightening in coded ratios: “Nutrients here.” “Water breach.” “Fire.”  Slow as glaciers at first glance, the Allroot in fact thinks three hundred conversations at once, each taking place along different bundles of phloem.  
   •  Symbiotic mycelia (the Threadborn) lace the outer bark, digesting mineral dust and trading trace metals for shelter.  They are the Hollow’s microbiome, invisible unless you look for the fine, star-shaped veining of silver on root bark.  Their spores ride the drizzle, germinate in every crack of stone, and die again in hours if they cannot find living cambium.  

2.  Small Kinetics: things that skitter, glide, or drift  
   a.  Pool-skaters (“shards”)  
       •  Transparent, five-jointed, as long as a fingernail; they flick across water on feet fringed with hydrophobic hairs that secrete anti-freeze proteins.  When your lamp passes over a pool you’ll see sparks—those are shard eyes throwing back stored light.  
       •  Diet: biofilm scraped from meniscus; they mouth at algae as if licking a lens.  
       •  Reproduction: parthenogenic broods every thirty days keyed to a rise in geothermal warmth; embryos already carry next month’s antifreeze in their yolk.  

   b.  Dust-moths  
       •  Wingspan: three centimetres; colour: whatever colour the air is.  They are powdered in microscopic prisms stolen from the flaking thallus; under magnification each scale is a flat mirror.  In stillness they vanish because they are simply echoing ambient light.  
       •  Larvae mine dead membrane leaves, sewing silk galleries that collapse as soon as the young take flight.  A collapsed gallery curling on itself is often mistaken for shed reptile skin.  

   c.  Membrane gliders (“chirrs”)  
       •  Rodent-delicate bones, webbing that begins at the chin and ends halfway down the tail.  They hurl themselves off terraces, catch the warm drafts oozing from fault lines, and ride kilometre-long arcs.  
       •  Navigation: a band of dermal cells along the forearm senses polarization in plant light, letting them read the Hollow’s slow pulses the way surface birds read the sun.  They will always land on living, not dying, leaf because the frequency tells them which is which.  
       •  Vocalisations a human can barely hear: single ultrasonic pips every ten seconds to say, essentially, “I’m here, don’t collide.”  

3.  Middling Lives: herbivores, opportunists, and the first hints of mind  
   a.  Candlegrazers (the six-legged browse-beasts)  
       •  Height at shoulder: 1.4 m.  Colour: soft ash, mottled with patches of living lichen that break up their outline.  Two fore-limbs function as shears; they bite off newborn thallus and swallow it whole.  
       •  Stomach is a three-chambered glow-vat where symbiotic bacteria crack photosynthate into sugars.  In total darkness the animal’s belly shines like a lantern through its own skin; predators cue on that glow, so candlegrazers spend most of their lives half-starved, moving slowly to keep the light dim.  
       •  Social: loose strings of five or six, each animal spaced exactly one body-length from the next, keeping every other member in peripheral vision.  When a root-quake starts they run single-file, trusting the leader to find naked stone.  

   b.  Gravel-thieves  
       •  Rat-haunch, bird-neck, beak of interlocking horn plates.  They swallow stones peppered with Threadborn mycelia, incubate the fungi in a calcified crop, then regurgitate the liquefied slurry to lap.  In effect they are mobile rock tumblers cultivating soup.  
       •  Their pathways are the first sign prospectors look for when chasing trace metals: wherever gravel-thieves pause, they leave beads of semi-digested ore.  

4.  The Night Repercussions: predators and other hazards  
   a.  Flare-vipers  
       •  Length: up to nine metres, diameter of a climbing rope.  They sleep wound around dormant roots, indistinguishable from them until light spikes.  Any sudden local brightening (camp-fire, torch, falling lamp) causes stored retinal pigments to snap awake; the snake lunges toward the glare by reflex.  
       •  Mouth opens in four petals; venom is not a toxin but a digestive algae slurry that seeds the wound.  If you survive the bite, the algae keep photosynthesising in your flesh, rotting you from within.  Builders treat early bites by smearing the wound with thick sap that starves the algae of light.  

   b.  Prism webs  
       •  Not an animal but a behaviour of the Allroot: sometimes it extrudes hairline silica threads across open air to harvest humidity.  They glitter only when dew has beaded them; otherwise they are invisible and will slice exposed skin cleanly if you walk through.  Dust-moths are the main victims, but humans have been known to find themselves instantly filleted to the bone from wrist to elbow.  

5.  The Builders: tetherfolk, weft-walkers, or simply “Those Who Mend”  
   (Surface linguists argue they are human; after a week among them you doubt the term means anything useful.)  
   •  Body plan  
       –  Tall even when crouched, average 190 cm, because height lets them browse upper shelves of thallus without climbing.  
       –  Limbs braided with tendons sheathed in cartilage; they can hang from one arm for hours letting circulation ferry warmth out of chilled fingers.  
       –  Skin pearl-pale, stippled with chromatophores they can open or close to match the ambient plant-light, giving the illusion of translucence.  When the thallus dims they become smoke-grey; when it brightens they ghost almost white.  
       –  Eyes enormous, cornea clear as quartz, pupils pinhole.  A mirrored layer behind the retina bounces photons twice; in direct torchlight their gaze flares like a cat’s but greener.  

   •  Metabolism & senses  
       –  Diet: 45 % plant sugars, 35 % fungal protein, 20 % grazers caught in spring snares.  They chew root pith with limestone chips to neutralise the acid.  
       –  They sense vibration through a pressure pouch where a human’s inner ear would sit; a passing root-quake or the knee-deep thump of a candlegrazer herd unfolds in their head as a 3-D map.  
       –  Smell is vestigial; anything fragrant enough to matter would fill the Hollow anyway.  

   •  Tools & materials  
       –  Fibre: split root spun wet, then allowed to self-suture.  A finished rope is half plant, half weaving.  
       –  Edge: nodules of meteoric iron hammered cold on stone anvils.  When an edge is spent, they return it to a fungal pit where iron-oxidisers etch away impurities; the metal is reborn thinner, sharper.  
       –  Fire: made rarely, inside leaf crucibles lined with wet clay.  They need flame to anneal iron and to smoke meat, but they smother it before the flare-vipers come.  

   •  Settlements (if the word applies)  
       –  Never more than ten shelters together, circling a bare patch of bedrock.  The shelters are leaf tents whose seams continue growing once stitched, so a dwelling is actually alive, sprouting new alcoves as the season progresses.  
       –  Every camp has three artefacts you can trust to find: a wind-drum (for signalling when fog hides hand gestures), a low hearth, and a spirit stack—a pile of thumb-sized basalt pieces painted with sap that phosphoresces in specific rhythms.  The stack is a kind of ledger; each pebble’s pattern encodes days spent, resources taken, gifts left.  Long after the camp moves on, another clan can read that tally and know how the ground stands.  

   •  Culture  
       –  Language is mostly light: mirrors of polished shell flashed across hundreds of metres of gloom, plus arm-length leaf-fans for semaphore.  Spoken words exist but carry little nuance, saved for intimacy, threat, or lullaby.  
       –  Technology is woven, never nailed.  “To nail is to freeze,” they say, “and nothing frozen survives.”  
       –  Myth: they do not worship the Allroot; they regard it as older kin.  They believe mind is something plants invented first and later loaned to animals as an experiment.  When a tetherfolk dies, clanmates open the corpse’s chest, remove the heart, and bury it against a living root so “thought can flow home.”  
       –  Time is counted in collapses.  A big membrane fall resets the calendar: year-names are literally descriptions—“After-the-Great-Green-Sheet”, “Third-since-the-Silence-Crash.”  

   •  Life cycle  
       –  Children (seedlings) are born soles-first so the first thing they touch is living leaf.  They spend their first decade in cradle forests of soft root fibres that are regrown annually around the nursery.  
       –  Adolescents serve as ridge-runners, scouting future camp stones and marking safe anchor points with quartz plugs.  
       –  Adulthood is defined by the right to cut live root; no other threshold matters.  
       –  Longevity tops out near sixty Hollow years; elders go voluntarily into the mist pools, let Threadborn eat them, completing the nutrient loop they have lived on.  

6.  Interactions between cast members  
   •  Tetherfolk follow candlegrazer herds the way surface hunters once followed caribou, not to kill many but because both require the freshest thallus.  Where grazers gnaw, new shoots erupt richer in sugars; clans harvest those shoots after the herd passes.  
   •  Shards spread fungal spores adhering to their feet; tetherfolk encourage the insects by leaving slivers of salt at pool margins.  In return the Threadborn bloom thick enough to be scraped, dried, and ground into protein cakes.  
   •  Flare-vipers are not hunted but lured.  A clan will hang a polished iron plate above a pit.  The viper strikes the light, misses, and tumbles; its skin is rendered for waterproof bags, its muscles smoked for winter.  The poison algae die in the process, unable to survive the heat.  

7.  Legends of the Surface  
   Every tetherfolk clan keeps a single rope they call the “Remembering Line,” braided of fibres no longer harvested.  It is said to reach all the way to sunlight; in practice it is too frayed to climb.  Still, once in a generation, a restless runner tries.  Most return with pupils scalded, coughing on dry surface air.  A very few do not return at all, and the Hollow adjusts: camps tighten supply loops, myths accrete another cautionary verse, and far below the Allroot twitches a single leaf, indifferent.

That, traveller, is the sum of the Hollow’s citizens as we presently know them: a parliament of roots, moths of mirror-dust, beasts that glow against their own best interest, hunters who speak in semaphore shadows, and the ever-appraising dark where something even older may yet bide its time.  Step lightly; every living wall down there is listening.
