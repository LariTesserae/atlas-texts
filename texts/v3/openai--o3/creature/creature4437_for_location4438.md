What lives here is a slow confederacy of bodies and noises, each species obliging the others to remain exactly, exquisitely unhurried. Follow the reverberation from the outer cliff inward and you can meet them in widening circles of intimacy.

1. The hollow-lung people (who call themselves Muridae, though outsiders translate the name as Cavity Folk)

Origins and shape  
• They are not fish made bipedal, nor land mammals grown webbed, but something in-between: amphibious primates whose ancestors fled a volcanic highland when eruption turned lakes to poison and the sea to sanctuary.  
• Skin is seal-smooth and silver-grey, spattered with darker patches that scatter sunlight into camouflage; thin fur grows only along spine and elbows where tactile nerves are dense.  
• Each carries two breathing systems: a trim pair of lungs for the air pockets and a set of ribbon gills—flecks of crimson tissue that frill the ribs and can be folded flat like shutters. Switching is conscious; children learn it the way surface toddlers learn to hold a spoon.  
• Hands are slim, six-fingered, every finger knuckled twice to curl around trunk, rope or eel. Nails etch obsidian; pads can taste dissolved salts, so navigation is by tongue as much as eye.

Settlement pattern  
Fifty-five air chambers lace a single giant lithodendron. They name it Old Voice and consider it the oldest citizen. Clans claim height rather than breadth:  
• Rootborn families live in the lowermost galleries—darker, more spacious, louder with arterial sap.  
• Mid-bough levels, where water haze whitens and schools of dart-fish peer in through slit windows, house artisans and nurseries.  
• Crown chambers—stiflingly bright pockets that sometimes brush the surface on spring tide—belong to the Oracles who keep the brittle kelp-tablets of tide-lore.

Daily rhythm  
Dawn (measured by the first brightening of plankton)  
• Divers open mucus valves in the plant skin to flush stale air; the exhalation booms like an organ note felt through bone.  
Mid-pulse (half a swell later)  
• Nets of balloon-jelly hide are spread between buttresses to farm plankton. Children ride the nets, shouting in click-tongue to scare away manta-leaves that would steal the catch.  
• Stonefin carp herders guide their charges along the rim, encouraging them to gnaw richer veins of limestone; herders collect the excreted marble pearls in bladder pouches.  
Low hush (the brief quiet when both tide and swell reverse)  
• The whole community drums. Every person—including infants strapped to eel-grass hammocks—strikes a personal knot on Old Voice’s inner wall with a mallet of bone. The rhythm is unison for thirty heartbeats, then breaks into spiralling counter-time that leaves sap gleaming, loose and healthy.  
• In the same interval healers listen to the returning echoes; changes in overtone reveal fractures or rot in the living architecture long before eyes could see them.

Speech and story  
Formal talk is a polyphonic code of tongue-clicks, palate slaps and chest coughs. Listeners perch fingers against the nearest hollow column to feel as much as hear. For fables, however, they crawl into the driest chamber, seal the door, and use wind voices. Their favourite legend says that long ago the drowned mesa had a twin still above water, and every word spoken here migrates upward through the plant’s capillaries to haunt that unseen sibling as mountain thunder.

Diet  
• First food is milk, second is boiled moss scraped from skate trails, third is the fermented pulp of plankton pressed in manta-leaf skins until it fizzes like mild lightning on the tongue.  
• Delicacies: chain-eel liver dried on drifting raftweed; sap caramel tapped where lightning-like stress fractures bleed sugar; pearls ground to dust and mixed into celebratory paints for coming-of-age tracery on face and tail.

Life cycle & rites  
Birth: parents divide a private pocket by weaving a membrane of eelgrass. A midwife sings at 48 Hz—the frequency proven to dilate lung frills.  
Pairing: partners plait each other’s gill-ribbons into a temporary knot; the union lasts until knots loosen, usually half a year.  
Death: a body is not buried but weighted with pearl grit and set upon the outer mud. Stonefin carp arrive within hours, carving away flesh until only polished bone remains; bones are placed inside the lithodendron’s oldest hollow so the plant may recycle calcium.

Governance  
Three Oracles decide law by dream consensus: they inhale a narcotic sap, sleep amid the roots and wake with shared hallucinations that are immediately sung as edicts. Yet any edict can be annulled if two separate trunks fuse after the ruling, for fusion trumps speech.

2. The lithodendron itself (Old Voice and her lesser daughters)

• A chimera organism: 40 % animal calcium carbonate, 40 % lignin-rich cellulose, 20 % living glass that stiffens under sustained vibration.  
• Has neither central brain nor heart; each joint node holds a ring of pacemaker cells that trade chemical signals so slowly a single message can take a season to reach the crown.  
• Detects sound not as pressure but as nutrient; certain frequencies encourage flowering—small white umbels that release dart-shaped seeds encapsulated in quartz foam. Villagers harvest those seeds and plant them in fresh cracks so cathedral acreage very gradually expands.  
• When lull lasts too long sap vitrifies, sealing vessels and threatening death. Muridae traditions of nightly drumming were originally emergency triage now elevated to sacred duty.

3. Manta-leaves

• Semi-sentient plankton grazers with bodies wider than any chamber and as thin as stacked paper. Dorsal surface is chlorophyllous, ventral carries a mosaic of false eyes that ripple open when predators loom.  
• They reproduce by fission: the outer rim of a mature leaf pinches off like a skirt hem and becomes a juvenile; villagers court that moment to gather fresh skirt membrane, peerless for waterproof parchment.  
• When a manta-leaf dies its body mats the seafloor in translucent winding sheets. Roots of the lithodendron eagerly colonise the decay, so manta-leaves are both roof shade and posthumous fertiliser, an accepted ecological tithe.

4. Stonefin carp

• Jaw plates grow and shed like shark teeth, each new row harder; by adulthood they can bite a knuckle-deep crescent from marble. Calcium once digested re-precipitates inside a gland behind the skull, wrapping around grains of sand to form pearls.  
• Carp schools adopt patrol circuits taught by generations of Muridae herders striking limestone with gong-stones. The fish recognise and follow personal rhythms the way sheep know a shepherd’s whistle.  
• In lean years carp have been known to chew soft sapwood of the lithodendron; the People respond by releasing surplus netted plankton—bribery to spare Old Voice her nerves.

5. Chain-eels

• Long as spears, built of alternating muscle rings and cartilaginous discs that clack faintly—hence “chain.”  
• Blind. Navigate by tracing electric fields that leak from any stressed organism; therefore they are involuntary medics, converging on wounds to lick saline ooze and deter infection.  
• Villagers tolerate them inside air pockets, allowing the eels to thread ventilation holes and stir the stagnant atmosphere with slow piston breaths.

6. Fireflies of the Vault

• Same genus as surface lightning bugs but smaller, water-resistant, wings reduced to stubby fins.  
• They cling upside-down in air chambers, pulsing blue for mate-calls, white for alarm. Muridae long ago learned to vary chamber humidity so each pocket’s swarm blinks a distinct constellation pattern, turning the vertical main-street into a living star map.

7. Acoustic algae (informally “hum-moss”)

• Carpets the outer limestone; chloroplasts nestle beside organelles that function like tuning forks.  
• Photosynthesises faster under rumbling sounds below 100 Hz; slows when exposed to shrill noise. Thus tectonic tremors become algal feast days.  
• Excess sugars exuded into water feed micro-crustaceans, which feed plankton that feed manta-leaves—a sonic food chain.

8. The quiet predators: Glassmouth and Abyss Choir

• Glassmouth is a transparent squid lurking just beyond the rim; it hunts motion but flees sound. Muridae infants are taught to giggle continuously when beyond the buttresses so the vibrations cloak their true position.  
• The Abyss Choir is not an animal but a standing wave phenomenon born where cliff curvature reflects storm swell. Once a century the resonance peaks, creating a droning roar that stuns fish and could shatter lithodendron if unopposed. Community lore tells how ancestors carved kinks in certain buttresses specifically to break that resonance into safer chords.

Interdependence summary

Nothing here can collapse or accelerate without dragging the rest into ruin. People drum to save the tree; the tree offers lungs of air; mantas shade the tree; carp carve the mesa to free minerals that strengthen mantas; chain-eels police wounds so drumming hands stay whole; algae turns thunder into sugar that restarts the loop. It is an ecosystem tuned like an enormous, submerged harp—each strand vibrating at its own slow patience yet harmonising with the others.

If a visitor lingers long enough her heartbeat synchronises to that patience. She will dream of crawling roots, of lungs filled with sweet damp resonance, of words travelling the length of a cathedral trunk and returning hours later altered, wiser. The mesa does not grant haste; it grants belonging by acoustics. Those who learn its meter may stay and hum their portion of the great, unfinished song of stone, wood, and water.
