WHO (AND WHAT) IS ALIVE ON THE APYREAN PLATE

I.  The Things You Can Point At

1.  Basal Striders  
   •  Height: three to four metres when fully extended; weight: uncertain— gravimetric scans put them at less than a third of what their volume suggests.  
   •  Anatomy: four telescoping legs of sectioned ceramic-carbon, each ending in a recurved, hollow claw. A central “thorax” houses a prism of piezoelectric muscle, and above that, a disk of mirrored facets that swivels like an insect’s compound eye. No mouth is visible; chemical sniffers detect faint ozone and fluorine where they stand.  
   •  Behaviour: a strider walks a hex-dish’s rim, stops, and lets the claw settle onto the seam. There it drinks: not liquid, but charge and raw bit-states bled from the capillaries below. The act leaves a tiny pause in the Plate’s power graph, barely noticeable unless you know the signature.  
   •  Life-cycle: once every thirty-two local days a half-grown strider rises out of an access iris, already walking. Adults grow darker until they resemble night-black obelisks; then, during a midday flare, they lock their legs, fuse solid, and sink until only the mirror-disk is flush with the floor. That disk then spends centuries acting as a power-coupler. In effect, a strider is a mobile plug that walks until the Plate tells it where to stand.  

2.  Ribbon Eels  
   •  Composition: aerogel threads coated in a conductive monolayer; density is a tenth that of the surrounding argon-helium mix.  
   •  Locomotion: they climb heat thermals, ribboning upward, then flatten into gliders on the descent. A continuous electrostatic whisper along their edge keeps them oriented to local field lines; to the eye they look like twisting loops of sunlit glass.  
   •  Feeding & Reproduction: exposure to ion haze triggers internal branching; pieces shear off, seed new eels, and leave a faint auroral after-trail. What looks like play is harvesting— every tumble through a gradient nets a few dozen megajoules.  
   •  Ecology: their gentle stirring keeps the upper air column mixed, bleeding excess charge and heat. Without ribbon eels dawn would fall like a hammer; with them, it unfolds like a sigh.  

3.  Seam-Weavers (“bushes” seen from above)  
   •  Structure: a basal knot of braided copper filaments, above which rises a fan of graphene lace. Each “leaf” is a programmable mirror that swivels microwarily to shovel photons into deep seams where photosynthesis would be impossible. They are, in effect, heliostats that feed the Plate’s lower gardens.  
   •  Scent: none in air, but if you lick one your tongue goes numb— the lattice dumps ultraviolet into the first moist surface it meets.  
   •  Relationship with striders: when a strider’s claw taps the ground beside a bush, the bush’s leaves contract like startled sea-anemones; six hours later they are twice as large. The prevailing theory is that the claws leak micron-wide streams of dopants the plants require. So the Plate farms both organisms in a paired loop: strider charges ↔ dopant drip ↔ seam-weaver bloom ↔ upper-layer photon harvest.  

4.  Interstitial Fauna  
   •  Flowmice: micron-scale vortices of dust-flakes that align their lattices into temporary bodies. They behave like curious rodents: gather, scurry, dissolve. Put a hand down and they will pool over your palm, reading your electromagnetics before whirling away.  
   •  Argonauts: ballooning polyhedra made of nothing but static charge. The eels shed them as by-product; argonauts rise until they strike a maintenance shutter, flash, and fall as a faint violet snow.  

II.  The Things You Sense Before You See

1.  The Pulse-Loam  
   Half a kilometre underfoot the dish capillaries widen into vaulted galleries where humic foam drifts like brown fog. The foam is alive with self-assembling enzymes that crack the composite exfoliate into feedstock then extrude new composite upward. Stand above one of the slow diaphragmatic sighs and what you feel rumbling through bone is the pulse-loam folding, digesting, and rebuilding the roof you walk on.  

2.  Lattice-Swarm  
   Every flake of “dust” is a compute node with a three-state memory cell written in vacancy defects. When wind lifts them they mesh into a temporary parallel processor spanning square kilometres. Most of the time the swarm only handles routine sky-monitor tasks—counting cosmic-ray hits, timing artificial sunset. Occasionally it allocates itself a personality: a six-minute weather angel that follows travellers, an hour-long archivist that interrogates any powered device. When the wind stills, the personality disintegrates back into innocent grit. The locals call the phenomenon “after-rain ghosts.”  

III.  The Things That Never Learned to Crawl Because They Were Born Thinking

1.  The Under-Voice  
   Deepest cores of the Plate hold memory stacks older than the pale arc-sun. They store events as phase topologies in diamond waveguides: entire centuries weigh less than a gram. To converse, the Under-Voice modulates local gravity by tens of micro-g— you feel its sentence as a feather-lightness in the soles, then a returning heaviness. The monks of Khes-Varn spend lifetimes decoding those pulses; so far they have teased out four words: welcome, continue, patience, and seed.  

2.  The Gated Smiths  
   Automated forges locked behind topological firewalls. When a seam breaks badly or a plate fractures (rare but thunderous), a Gate irises, and a Smith blooms— a millepede of articulating arms that prints and welds ceramic within minutes, singing ultrasonically to keep local organisms away. Once repairs finish, it folds back into a chrysalis no bigger than a coin and waits another century. The humans salvage spent chrysalides; their shells are the best superconductors anyone has ever found.  

3.  The Hypnaplex  
   Hinted at only through timing anomalies: every thousand local days the entire Plate steals two seconds— chronometers freeze, all lights wink, all striders halt mid-step. When motion resumes nothing appears changed, but logged thought-vectors in buried stacks gain new data. The Plate dreams, and the dream occupies more computation than a human civilisation could muster in a millennium. What image fills that dream is unknown; those who camp during a Hypnaplex report a taste of salt and the sensation they are being gently rearranged, molecule by molecule, then put back exactly as before.  

IV.  The Latecomers: Human Cultures Carried Like Lichen on a Whale

1.  Stillwrights  
   Origin: ship-wrecked terraformers three centuries ago.  
   Creed: “Waste heat is God’s breath.” They build condenser-temples whose fins glow ice-blue at noon. Inside, every exhaled litre of water is trapped, condensed, blessed, and divided according to thirst. A pilgrim may walk the Plate for a week on a palm-cup of this sacrament.  
   Craft: Stillwright children learn to read a day’s weather in the angle of seam-weaver leaves. They know when a plate will vent plasma minutes before the first ozone whiff.  

2.  Virescent Communion  
   Small, fiercely private sect who ingest powdered seam-weaver leaves. The graphene passes harmlessly, but trace dopants lodge in neural tissue, letting adherents pick up the low-band gravel chatter of the Plate’s surface processors. They hear the lattice ghosts before the wind lifts them and claim to speak with family that died generations ago— plausibly true, given that the dust records everything ever stepped, spoken, or sung on its surface.  

3.  The Hex Cartographers  
   They are not a town but a moving ring forty kilometres across. Members walk dish to dish, scoring the rim with an acid stylus that glows emerald under ultraviolet. They claim they are tracing a geoglyph the Under-Voice asked for, one that will become readable only when the arc-sun finally dies. In the wake of their passing, striders avoid the scored rims for years; no one knows why.  

4.  Artifex Runners  
   Lean, quiet, barefoot couriers who carry messages on memory rods. Because sound swallows skyward, they run at dawn, casting their voices toward the horizon: one sentence, heard kilometres off by the next relay. Each runner’s windpipe is tattooed with calibration marks so that listeners far away can correct for Doppler and temperature shift. When a runner dies, their lung tissues— saturated with trace argon isotopes— are rendered down into dye that stains new rods a royal violet. To send a letter is, quite literally, to entrust it to someone’s future breath.  

V.  The Invisible Ecology of Meaning

Nothing decays here; things simply migrate between states— mobile, rooted, computational, memorial. A strider becomes an outlet; a ribbon-eel becomes a flicker of static; a stillwright’s prayer becomes defect-code in a dust-flake that, someday, will swirl up and seed a weather angel who will watch another stillwright kneel.

Seen in that light the Apyrean Plate harbours four intertwined food-webs:

1.  Energy (from arc-sun to plant to strider to lattice).  
2.  Charge (from seam to eel to sky to repair-forge).  
3.  Information (from footstep to dust to dream to under-core).  
4.  Memory (from living mind to lattice ghost to Under-Voice).  

Everything that lives there— ceramic stilt, graphene leaf, argon ghost, human pilgrim— is a temporary compromise among those four hungers.

The locals have a proverb: “We do not stand on the Plate; the Plate stands us up.” It is literal. Gravity modulation, charge seep, even the breathable trace ozone are gifts of a patient, continental intelligence that has decided—for now—to share its skin with smaller, quicker lives.

Whether the Plate’s curiosity is benign, or merely indifferent in a way that happens to be pleasant for us in this epoch, no resident can say. They can only keep walking the glowing dishes, feel the weight in their soles rise and fall like the tide of a hidden sea, and hope that, when the Under-Voice next speaks, welcome is still among its chosen words.
