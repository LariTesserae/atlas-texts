Those who make their homes on the Plateau of Glass Ferns call themselves, in their own slow tongue, the Lythene—“the Quiet-breathing.”  To outsiders they seem half legend, for they share a country with almost nothing else that moves, and they have cultivated a manner of life so faint-footed that even geographers who tramp straight through a hamlet sometimes leave believing the basin uninhabited.  Yet they are there, patient as the drip that hollows stone.

1. Bodies shaped by still air  
Altitude thins the air but also washes it clean, and the Lythene’s physiology has drifted gently toward both requirements.  
•  Tall, reed-slender frames: lungs stretch long in the chest, ribs sprung wide; every child is taught exercises that keep the diaphragm strong so a single quiet breath will serve for a long sentence.  
•  Skin pearl-brown and filmed with a natural gloss—sebaceous oils altered through diet so that moisture beads and runs rather than soaking.  Clothing is minimal: an apron of woven sedge for work, a knee-length mantle of felted moss for chill nights, nothing that rustles.  
•  Hair grows straight and heavy with silica taken up from the water; in sun it shines like fine metal.  When someone enters adulthood their ends are singed in a fern-oil flame that seals each strand—a rite meant to keep stray hairs from floating away and giving the wind a voice.

2. Dwellings that never break the skyline  
A single household—usually three generations—lives in each roundhouse.  The rooms are dug half a man’s height into the mound so that only a shallow drum of black basalt shows above the turf.  Inside:  
•  Floors of packed blue clay so cool and fine that guests often assume it is stone; the clay swells when damp and seals every joint.  
•  Hearths no larger than a helmet, lined with quartz plates; smoke is drawn sideways through a slot that opens under the eaves and disperses along the drip-edge, emerging as an almost invisible haze that never rises.  
•  Furniture is braided out of fern-root fiber—light enough to lift with two fingers; when not in use it hangs on wall pegs so floors remain bare and can be mopped each dawn with a drag of saturated moss.  

3. Food drawn from water rather than beasts  
The basin has so few animals that most children meet their first bird only on a pilgrimage to the lower forests.  The Lythene therefore farm liquids as assiduously as solids.  
•  Drip-tubers: a domesticated arum whose leaves funnel moisture into a stem reservoir; when uprooted the swollen core tastes of sweet chestnut and keeps for months if reburied in wet sand.  
•  Cloud-milk: at night they stretch hair-fine nets of quartz thread between stakes; mist condenses, runs into gourds, and by morning each net yields a cup or two of mineral-rich water faintly opalescent.  Beaten with ground tuber starch it forms a delicate custard served on festival evenings.  
•  Fern-heart, moss-spore cakes, and ribbon-sedge sprouts give all needed protein.  Nothing is boiled—water is too sacred to be clouded with starch—so cooking means slow stone-baking under damp leaves, which perfumes the air but never smokes.  

4. Tools of things that fell from the sky  
No ore seams break the fresh rock, and the Lythene will not mine: to gouge the young mountain, they say, is to hurry its stillness toward collapse.  Their metals arrive as meteoric nodules sledged up from foothill markets each decade.  
•  They cold-hammer the soft nickel-iron into mirror-bright crescents used for sickles and for the throat-plates of water pipes.  These pieces are mended rather than resharpened; some heirlooms carry six or seven generations of tiny dovetail inlays where gaps were plugged with fresh shavings of star-metal.  
•  Working stones are quartz lozenges polished until a thumbprint distorts like a fish beneath water.  Edges are napped inward so chips never fly away to litter the ground with noise.  

5. A civilization built on the grammar of hush  
Sound is rationed the way desert tribes ration water.  
•  Distance-speech: when message must travel, they set a palm on a standing slab and murmur; the grooved face turns the voice into a line of ripples that runs down into a cistern.  Another listener elsewhere draws a fingertip through the pool and feels the pattern rise against skin—a language that belongs as much to water as to tongue.  
•  Social gatherings occur at astronomical regularities: quarter moons, solstices, moments the shadow of the rim aligns with certain swirl-stones.  In between, families mostly exchange silent courtesies: the tilt of a rain hood, the pace at which one walks a causeway, the angle of a torch.  
•  Children learn silence by play.  They stalk droplets sliding down a cliff face and try to touch one without disturbing the sheet.  Only when a child succeeds three times may they wear ankle chimes—tiny quartz rings that click so softly only close kin can hear them.  

6. Lore and faith: covenant with a waking mountain  
The Lythene do not pray upward; they listen downward.  Beneath every home a ceramic bell is buried mouth-open.  When tectonic murmurs pass, the bell sings.  A clear note means safety, a warble means the stone is loosening, and a buzz tells them to lighten their tread for a day.  They keep chronicles not in ink but in sequences of bells they can hum.  

Their founding myth says the plateau is the newest child of the world, torn from the earth in a scream so recent the echo still circles below ground.  Mankind’s task, then, is to raise the child gently: never shout, never strike, never leave scars, because a young thing imitates what it receives.  If the mountain learns violence, it may answer in kind.  Thus:  
•  No walls more than a standing height.  
•  No fires whose light can be seen from the outer rim.  
•  No weapon longer than the user’s forearm.  Even the meteor-sickles are kept sheathed in moss except at harvest.  

7. The almost-animals  
Life is sparse but not entirely absent beyond humankind.  Three forms share the basin.  
•  Pearl efts: translucent salamanders that spend their whole lives in the finger-wide runnels between fern isles.  They are mute, glow faintly where moonlight strikes the tiny opal grains in their skin, and feed on the green scum that grows on quartz.  The Lythene will not touch them, believing each eft to be a syllable of the plateau’s first, unfinished word.  
•  Mist-wasps: insect-thin wires of chitin that never land; they sip condensation in flight, wings fanning so fast they are invisible.  You detect them only by a pin-prick coolness on the nape just after they drink the heat from a bead of sweat.  They do not hum, making them acceptable neighbors.  
•  Driftmoss: neither plant nor animal but a colony of single-celled swimmers inhabiting the thinnest films of water on vertical stone.  To the eye it is merely a velvet sheen, yet in early twilight it phosphoresces like a page of unread script.  Children learn their letters tracing that light; adults read auguries in its brightness.  

8. Birth, love, death  
Birth takes place in the steam-bath caves where warm runoff accumulates.  The infant’s first cry is muffled by curtains of dripping water so it enters the world hearing its voice only as a pulse in its chest.  

Partnerships are forged in paired silence walks.  Two people set out before dawn along opposite sides of a causeway moat.  If, when they reach the far end, neither has spoken, they are judged compatible; to keep counsel together is the greatest intimacy.  A single whispered word breaks the test, and both must wait a year before attempting again with anyone.  

The dead are given to a bog-garden.  Bodies laid amid sphagnum sink and slowly turn to tannin-dark bone.  After ten years kin retrieve the cleaned skeleton, polish it with quartz sand, and hinge the joints with star-metal wire into a standing figure.  These bright, silent ancestors watch over the household hearths; each one’s rib cage holds a drip-stone that pulses whenever the roof begins to leak, a last guardianship of dryness and quiet.  

9. What an outsider notices last  
Spend a week and you begin to feel your chest loosen and your own footsteps boom like drums.  Spend a month and you find yourself counting the world in droplets, pausing before each word to weigh its necessity.  Only then do you notice the subtle choreography: how everyone times a door-latch to coincide with a distant waterfall’s roar, how a cough is buried in the thump of a hoe, how even laughter—rare, soft, but irrepressible—always flares when a rush of wind passes over the ridge.  Nothing is suppressed, only placed where it will not echo.  

The Lythene would say that is all any creature should do until the mountain decides what shape it wishes to keep.  They are caretakers of an interval, midwives to a landscape still taking its first slow breaths.  Whether they endure centuries or vanish the first day a real river finally cuts the basalt, they will have fulfilled their purpose: proving, if only for a while, that people can live so lightly the newest land never feels their weight.
