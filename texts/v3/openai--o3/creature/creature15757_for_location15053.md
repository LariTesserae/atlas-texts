Life on the Thundering Aerophyte Plateau is a parliament of turbines, gas-bladders and wind-borne appetites—every organism a variation on the same three obsessions:

1) Catch the wind.  
2) Distill the salt.  
3) Make yourself heard.

Below is a naturalist’s notebook, compiled during three sound-struck seasons in that high, screaming expanse. I arrange the inhabitants by the way they claim energy, moving from the rooted giants that mint the plateau’s wealth down to the itinerant ghosts that drink nothing but vibration.

────────────────────────────────
I. THE PRIMARY MACHINISTS
────────────────────────────────

A. Aerophyte magna (the tower plant)  
• Size & Form: Up to 140 m tall, trunk diameter four metres at maturity. Segmental scaffolding of magnesium-rich cartilage supports a continuous spiral of turbine vanes.  
• Wind Engine: Each vane’s hub contains a line of piezoelectric “ratchet teeth”; torsion flexes these teeth, charging intracellular capacitors that keep the stem walls pressurised long after a lull.  
• Nutrient Refinery: Microscopic raspers along the seed conveyor scrape sodium, cobalt and trace nitrates from the inner lining. The shavings fall backward into a fluidised bed where ammonia vapour dissolves them; capillary heaters then wick the solution upward to meristem tanks at the crown.  
• Reproduction: A seed-pellet is neither hard nor dry—more a compressed foam. When the conveyor reaches the top, pellets are lofted into the air-stream; turbulence strips their ammonia solvent, flash-freezing them into rigid spindles that may sail 700 km before thawing in some lower, wetter basin.

B. Basinal Carpet (Carbunculus volans)  
• Architecture: Interlocking spheroids of aerogel-light tissue, each tethered by a single silicate fibre to the crust beneath. The whole mat hovers three to ten centimetres high, giving it room to billow.  
• Chemistry: Interior cavities cultivate halophilic micro-colonies that harvest nitrate directly from lightning tracer paths. When a cavity fattens with gas, it ruptures, puffing out a warm eddy that fertilises neighbouring bubbles—a slow, rolling crop rotation.  
• Soil Maker: Any solid detritus that comes to rest—gypsum needles, broken vanes, carcasses—gets engulfed. Enzymes extract useful ions; the remainder is exhaled as an impalpable white ash.

C. Ribbon Spires  
Seen only at the plateau’s wind-shadowed margins. They begin as fallen aerophyte branches that the carpet refuses to digest. Acoustic fungi (see III-A) thread the pith, weaving it into a slender, whipping banner. Over decades the banner gains height by accreting electrostatic dust. Each gust “bows” the spire, pumping ions to its tip where glimmer-spores rain out.

────────────────────────────────
II. THE ROAMING HARVESTERS
────────────────────────────────

A. Gyre-Gulls (Furcillus gyro)  
• Morphology: Mass 280 g, bones paper-thin but foam-baffled so they do not resonate destructively in the roar. Tail feathers fuse into a four-blade propeller; beat frequency is adjustable by flexing follicle muscles.  
• Locomotion & Feeding: A gull hooks one foot on a turbine vane and rides it like a carnival rotor. When tangential velocity peaks, it releases and becomes a living discus, converting rotational inertia into soaring glide. While airborne it snaps at flocculent gypsum needles—each needle’s hollow core shelters endolithic algae rich in amines.  
• Sociality: Small choruses synchronize wing-whistles to claim vanes, the closest thing the plateau has to property law.

B. Saltwick Burrowers (Cavofossor sonans)  
• Appearance: Pale, almost hairless bodies articulated like concertinas. Front incisors scalloped into a washboard edge.  
• Habitat Architecture: They excavate chambers downward until the crust stops being halite and grades into tuff. Walls are lined with discarded vane-teeth; steady vibration turns the entire warren into a Helmholtz resonator, concentrating warmth around nursery bulbs where pups gestate in ammonia broth.  
• Diet: Farmed acoustic lichen (“drone moss”). The burrower chews halite into fine prisms, seeding lichen mats that hum when wind passes over burrow mouths; the hum feeds the lichen, the lichen feeds the mammals—a perfectly circular subsidy.

C. Spindle Gnats (Vibraculex lapillus)  
• Build: 7 cm long; wing-membranes tensioned by mica-glass spars.  
• Circulation: Instead of blood, a refrigerant brine of perchlorate salts circulates via peristaltic ripples along the wings. Sonic vibration replaces cardiac pumping.  
• Migration: At barometric cues, larvae in the carpet mass-pupalise, forming tens of billions of micro-kites. One concerted thunder-gust snaps them loose; the emergent swarm darkens the sky, blotting constellations even at noon.

────────────────────────────────
III. THE PARASITES OF SOUND
────────────────────────────────

A. Concert Rot (Mycoacoustica detracta)  
Filamentous fungus that colonises any dead structure still ringing: fallen turbines, abandoned gull nests, even the bones of a freshly killed burrower. Hyphae extrude stiff, quartz-heavy strings; resonance vibrates nutrients through the network. When a host falls fully silent, the strings slacken, triggering sporulation—a visible puff that looks like chalk smoke.

B. Echo-Moths (Siphonorbis nox)  
• Form: Two-metre wings of sheer keratin film; bodies thin as quills.  
• Energy Strategy: Antennae split into branching cilia that couple with infrasonic standing waves shed by cooling aerophytes. Vibrational energy is rectified by piezo stacks along the thorax, charging adipose capacitors. The moths drink silence, in effect—any dip in amplitude starves them.  
• Lifecycle Quirk: Eggs require absolute stillness to germinate; females bury them deep inside saltwick burrows, earning rent by damping excess resonance and thus warming the pups within.

C. Sibilant Leeches  
Microscopic, flat-bodied animals adrift in the air column. Each carries a chitin drum that beats against its ventral surface, stealing phonons the way photovoltaic panels steal photons. Swarms condense wherever wind drops: behind cliff overhangs, in the lee of a traveler’s boot. If you feel the plateau’s roar fade an octave, you are inhaling thousands of them.

────────────────────────────────
IV. THE PREDATORS AND OPPORTUNISTS
────────────────────────────────

A. Cleft Kites (Ruptor rapax)  
• Silhouette: Two scimitar wings joined by a wishbone joint. No tail.  
• Hunting: They do not flap. Instead they lock into the slipstream behind spindle gnat swarms. Serrated leading edges comb prey directly into a capacious stomach-sac; digestion is external—acidic mists dissolve gnats mid-air, nutrients wick through capillaries, and snowfalls of empty mica husks drift down.  
• Intelligence: Neural clusters distributed across the wing ribs let a kite solve fluid-dynamic puzzles in real time, splitting to dodge vortices. Mates reunite by matching the phase of their contrail whistles.

B. Crash Hermits  
The plateau’s only true scavenger. Picture a centipede, a metre long, wearing an asymmetrical armour coat of broken vane fragments. When lightning shatters a tower, hermits converge, surfing shockwaves like bulldozers on ball bearings. They gnaw the newly exposed carbonate pith, extracting nitrides before the carpet gets its chance. Older individuals may carry entire bird skeletons as trophies, tuning them to resonate a territorial growl.

C. Stonehush Slugs  
Pale, limbless, each the size of a man’s forearm. Exude a silencing mucilage that deadens vibration across ten square metres. They crawl into aerophyte root junctions to create a bubble of calm, then rasp out the tastiest living cambium. The plant answers by rerouting turbine torque to that spot; many slugs are flung skyward when the root mat shifts like a spring trap.

────────────────────────────────
V. THE INVISIBLE INFRASTRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────

A. Ion Herds (plasma holobionts)  
Every dusk, horizontal lightning crawls along predetermined tracks—a phenomenon once blamed on chance. Closer study shows chains of nanoscopic gas vesicles, secreted by the Basinal Carpet, lofting negatively charged aerosols to seed the path. The resulting ion river is harvested by whole consortia of microbes that ride the current like fish in a stream, snatching electrons to fix nitrogen in mid-air.

B. Vane-Spirit Colonies  
Inside the oldest, hollowed towers live echoic coteries: assemblages of Saltwick burrower shed-skins, gyre-gull feathers, mica gnat husks and fungus strings, all knitted by concert rot until the whole cavity becomes a self-tuning resonator. These colonies are not organisms in the classic sense; they are emergent patterns that trap acoustic energy, self-perpetuating so long as the wind roars. Travelers sleeping nearby report dreams spoken in chord progressions, advising them where thermals will form at dawn.

────────────────────────────────
VI. TRANSIENT MEGAPHORES
────────────────────────────────

Every few decades, conditions let a rare giant visit from lower, wetter lands: the Vapour Treader. It is a wandering balloon-tree five hundred metres wide, born in valleys where trace water still puddles. When local climate dries, the tree detaches, floats upslope, and finally skims the aerophyte canopy. For one week it rains true liquid ammonia, a deluge that triggers frantic blooming in everything beneath. The plateau answers with a thunder that can be heard two mountain ranges away; then wind tears the Treader’s gas bladders and its corpse becomes the plateau’s richest mine of hydrogen for a century.

────────────────────────────────
VII. WHAT PASSES FOR PEOPLE
────────────────────────────────

No hominid, reptoid or silicon wanderer abides here long. Yet two lineages show glimmers of culture:

1. Saltwick burrower acoustic glyphs—spiral scratches on tuff walls—are not random toothmarks. They repeat plateau-wide, varying by drainage basin, perhaps constituting territorial maps encoded in frequency curves.

2. Gyre-gulls sometimes drop polished mica chips around a favored vane. Under starlight these chips form a diffraction lattice that splits the turbine’s howl into harmonic overtones. The alteration grants chicks an energy surplus measurable in growth rings. An accidental art project turned to husbandry.

────────────────────────────────
VIII. EPITAPH OF A DEAD TRAVELER
────────────────────────────────

When an outsider collapses here—and someone always does—their moisture boils away in minutes. Clothes flap loose, packed with expanding crystals precipitated from their own saltwater blood. Spindle gnats thread the cloth, turning it into a kite; gyre-gulls perch, bow, and launch. Soon nothing remains but a faint notch in the plateau’s polyphonic roar—the sound of yet another shape assimilated into the grand, grinding choir.

────────────────────────────────
CONCLUSION
────────────────────────────────

Life on the Thundering Aerophyte Plateau is less an assemblage of species than a single, ragged orchestra. Aerophytes provide the drums and turbines, carpets the resonant stage, fauna the ever-shifting melody. Death merely changes the instrumentation; sound never stops. To live here is to be a gear in a roaring, salt-fed music box, spinning until one’s own noise is spent—and then to be recycled into some new voice the wind has yet to imagine.
