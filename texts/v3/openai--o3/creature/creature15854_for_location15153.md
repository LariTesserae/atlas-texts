Who lives in Cross-Hush is hard to separate from what Cross-Hush is; every resident—animal, artifact, rumor, or half-imagined thought—has been pressed into the same saturated grammar of water and green. Still, there are recognisable kinds.

1. The Hushfolk (also called greeners, soft-breath, or simply We)

Origin  
They began as baseline humans landed here four centuries ago, but constant gene-grafting and microbial companionship have converged them toward a single, adaptable type. Their bodies are slim, lightly boned (less weight means less chance of plunging through a root-raft), and overgrown with planned symbioses.

Observable traits  
• Skin: pale umber stippled by chlorofibres: micro-organelles that turn diffuse light into blood-glucose. When the sun shafts down, a Hushfolk’s forearms pearl with photosynthetic freckles the way others might flush.  
• Hair: more lichen than keratin—soft, felty mats that wick moisture outward and glow faint chartreuse when charged. Children braid glow-strands into signaling patterns that function like fireflies in the dusk.  
• Eyes: cornea overlaid by a clear nictitating film that polarises glare off wet leaves; irises run from swamp-green to bruise-violet. Pupils contract to almond slits in broad light, bloom almost round at night.

Voice and language  
The place is already noisy, so the Hushfolk compensate by speaking in tight-throated undertones and rhythmic taps: knuckles against root, fingernail against stalk. Much of their syntax is musical—glissandos of pitch substitute for adjectives; a rising fifth means “faster,” a falling minor third means “with caution.” Complex messages are inhaled: they release terpene aerosols from algal sacs in the sinuses; another inhaler can “smell-read” the statement.

Social structure  
They live in clusters called sprigs, twelve to twenty adults who share a living bole. A sprig is not a family but a metabolic consortium: cooks are also microbial curators; historians tend the sap-memory archives; children apprentice themselves to any role that holds their fascination for more than one tide cycle (about ten hours). Leadership is rotational: whoever’s pulse best synchronises with the local quantum-mycelium—measured by a wrist lichen that flickers when coherence is high—mediates decisions until the signal passes to someone else.

Life cycle  
Birth occurs in buoyant “nursery-pods,” hollowed nodules where root-mat curls upward like a papyrus basket. For the first month an infant breathes through gill-like skin folds; actual lungs unfurl later, mirroring the marsh eels they share water with. Adolescence is marked by a Walk-Thin: two nights wandering alone across the blackwater; the walker must return having convinced at least one non-human thing—a kana, a pillar-fern, a maintenance cloud—to accompany them home. The companion rarely stays for life, but the skill of interspecies persuasion is considered the rite’s true goal.

2. The Kana

Picture an otter drafted by Escher into four dimensions and then coaxed back to three before the ink dried. About a metre at the shoulder, limber as spilled mercury, fur braided into coppery chevrons by bioluminescent microbes that light their spines. They eat gelatinous root fungi, but also “garden” them: scraping away diseased patches, dragging rotted mats aside so new hyphae can drink. The kana’s importance is spiritual as much as ecological; Hushfolk say the animal’s backlight is the basin’s subconscious made visible. It is rude to read by a kana’s glow without first offering it a peel of salt-leaf.

3. The Caretakers

Twice as tall as a person, vaguely humanoid silhouettes grown from lignin and sieve-tubes. Imagine a walking cypress knee with jointed cambium arms and clusters of trumpet-shaped leaves instead of eyes. Caretakers are grown, not born: each sprig grafts one from a donor trunk when its population reaches fifteen. A slow parthenogenesis follows—months of guided growth while neural phloem is inoculated with archival microbes bearing civic instructions. When mature, a Caretaker ambulates, regulating bridge tension, persuading recalcitrant vines to move roosts, shouldering storm debris out of pools. Its language is rhythmic creaking; the Hushfolk understand most of it, and in return the Caretaker eavesdrops their sap-graffiti for news.

4. The Maintenance Swarm

Individual size: 600 nanometres—virion scale. Colony size: anywhere from rice-grain to bedsheet, visible only when thousands self-assemble inside a dandelion-like puff of cellulose balloons. They are neither machine nor organism but a lawful confusion of both: carbon-lattice casings house petri-caps of enzymes, and at the core of the largest agglomerations hums a superconductive loop that uses ambient thermal noise as power. They polish photosynthetic surfaces, restitch torn vascular bundles, modulate local gravity by briefly arresting virtual gluons inside zeptosecond cages. The Hushfolk court swarms with offerings of crystallised mineral dust; the cloud “inhales” the grains as raw scaffolding for repair work.

5. The Basin Dream

Less a citizen than the place’s sleeping mind. After centuries of quantum-mycelial traffic, entangled spores have crossed a threshold into coherence: a basin-wide wave function that thinks in humps of probability. It communicates only in pareidolia: a coincidence of frog croaks echoing like language, three rain drops on your shoulder that resolve into Morse, a sudden tilt of the fern-vault that spells an ideogram in negative space. Once a season the Basin Dream addresses the entire hamlet; the bridges reposition overnight into a looping, phosphorescent Möbius glyph whose knot-theory solution predicts the next ten days of weather. Everyone understands; no one agrees on the wording.

6. Peripheral fauna

• Platewings: bat-sized insects with membrane wings thick with hydrophobic scales. Their “leather-thrum” is a courtship signal that doubles as sonar in constant drizzle. Hushfolk sometimes stitch abandoned wings into waterproof cloaks.  
• Velvet Moth-things: thumbnail bodies, kite-large wings sprayed with nanocrystals that flash electric green. They are pollinators for the engineered thumb-flowers woven through habitation walls.  
• Marsh Eels: ribbonlike, two metres long, transparent but for a spinal line of pearly ganglia. They taste with external fringe gills; a disagreement among eels looks like lace rippling against blackwater.  
• Ghost Frogs: glassy, tessellated skin; their warning croak attenuates frequencies used by predatory gliders, effectively jamming attack sonar in chorus.

7. Customs and taboos that define personhood

• To cut a trunk without whispering a confidence is an act of violence; the sap must remember something or it will ache with emptiness.  
• A resident who is away long enough for their dwelling to thin its own walls must, upon return, ask permission to re-enter like a stranger; the house no longer assumes they belong.  
• Straight lines offend the water. Anyone seen laying a plumb rule will find their next dream bend at right angles, leaving them seasick on waking. An apology is offered by drawing a slow spiral on the pond’s skin with fingertip.

8. What passes through

Not all who inhabit Cross-Hush stay.  
• Couriers on root-skates glide from hamlet to hamlet, carrying spore-sealed memory-lattices that cannot be trusted to the basin’s dreaming network.  
• Scientists from dry continents arrive in polished coracles that squeal against the root-mat—the forest hates the texture of dead aluminium—and leave bewildered, pockets full of data that refuses to stabilise once outside humidity 98 %.  
• Pilgrims drift in, hoping to hear the world think. Most leave after a night of insomnia induced by the place’s endless percussion. A handful stay, dissolve their passports in sap, and let the basin digest their old names.

So the population of Cross-Hush is a braid: flesh and cambium, animal and program, mist and intention. No category stands apart for long; live here, and either you become part of the basin’s breathing or the basin’s breathing becomes part of you.
