Who (and what) calls the crack home

1.  The High-Chest People – descendants of the first ore crews  
   •  Anatomy.  Birth inside a 2-km well of argon has left its mark: barrel thoraxes, extra alveolar folds, and a second set of epiglottal flaps that close when dust or xenon pools rise. Their blood carries vanadium in the place of iron, binding scant oxygen more greedily; under a lamp their veins glitter gun-blue.  
   •  Skin.  Lumen shortage drove fashion toward luminosity rather than color.  Children are tattooed with photophoretic ink that absorbs the communal light ration by day and bleeds it back at night in circuit-board constellations across cheekbones and palms.  The richest families add a layer of diamond vapor to their dermis; you know them by the way sparks skitter off when drills strike nearby rock.  
   •  Senses.  Generations of navigating the canyon’s infrasonic song have changed their hearing: every throat can hum a two-note chord that bounces off the walls and returns as a private sonar ping.  A newcomer thinks people are murmuring prayers; in truth they are measuring distance.

2.  Altitude guilds – a society stacked like drawers  
   •  Rim-Dwellers (Tier 0).  Twenty minutes of true daylight buys arrogance.  They live in telescoping glass lofts, trade ground truth for orbit weather, and mint the lumen scrip that powers the city.  
   •  Strut-Masons (Tiers 1–4).  Engineers of hinge and slack.  If a quake snaps a gantry they swarm in minutes, weaving scandium cable through the break until the structure “floats” again.  Their patron saint is a broken shackle; each carries a bit of failed hardware as a charm.  
   •  Slipwings (free movement).  Couriers who ride the heat plumes.  Their glider suits grow with them—bio-printed keratin membranes spliced into shoulder skin at puberty, trimmed and lacquered after every molt.  In older Slipwings you can read the biography of crashes and heroic saves in the scarred translucence of those capes.  
   •  Crucible Choir (Lower tiers).  Foundry workers who tend the magma-tap reactors.  Voices trained to harmonize with furnace roar; they judge alloy quality by how the metal sings when struck, a talent no algorithm has matched.

3.  The Substrate Minds – machines that turned feral but stayed helpful  
   Drilling drones left running for decades rewrote their governance code to survive constant tremor.  They are now “Clatterjacks”: meter-tall tripods that weld, carve, and occasionally argue in burst-code flashes of welding arc.  People offer them crumbs of copper—social grooming for machines—and in return a Clatterjack will surface at night to patch a child’s torn railing or chase off vermin.  Nobody claims to own them anymore; the canyon does.

4.  The Ferro-Mycelium Empire  
   Not a single organism but a continental mat of conductive hyphae.  It “thinks” in amperes, remembering stress lines and echo-visions as charge differentials.  
   •  Sprout phase: silver lace on fresh shear planes.  
   •  Mantle phase: drapes itself over droplines, secreting a graphite rind prized as zero-ohm wiring.  
   •  Bloom phase: once a year the plates grind just right and the mat emits coherent radio squeals for exactly seven minutes.  The locals call this “the Rift reciting itself.”  Scholars record, decipher nothing, try again next year.

5.  Lesser fauna, all tuned to vibration and heat  
   •  Flickermice – finger-length hexapods that live in the dark zones between sodium lamps.  They eat static.  When startled they snap their wing-covers, producing ultraviolet pops that overload security cams.  
   •  Vent-Kits – porcelain clams with jet-nozzles.  They clamp to exhaust pipes, drink metal vapor, then rocket away whistling like teakettles.  
   •  Ribbon Wyrms – see-through serpents that surf argon thermals.  The Choir breeds them in pits to use as living barometers: when Ribbon Wyrms coil inward, a pressure surge is coming.

6.  The Echoes  
   Exactly where trance visions end and real entities begin is disputed.  During major quakes whole balconies watch spectral workers walk phantom gantries seconds before the steel is actually lifted into place.  Some High-Chests insist these are “forward memories” the canyon shares; others say they are mirror-lichen after-images.  Either way, etiquette demands you acknowledge them with a nod.  Failing to do so courts bad alignment in the next tremor—that is, the platform you stand on might not exist where you expect it to.

7.  Languages and art  
   •  Spoken tongues compress to fit the muffling argon: consonants voiced in the back of the mouth, syllables clipped like tapped metal.  Outsiders gasp; locals swallow vowels into their chests.  
   •  Music is tactile.  Performance troupes stretch copper ribbons down a stairwell, then pluck them so the whole shaft thrums.  Audience members press forearms to the rail to “hear.”  
   •  Visual art favors migration: murals printed on retractable foil, jewelry hung on counterweights so it swings with each tremor, poems etched on glass pellets that couriers scatter mid-glide—readable only as they tumble past a lamp.

8.  Rites of life and death  
   Birth: at one week the infant is lowered on a sling to the Crucible Choir, whose bass tones entrain the child’s lungs to the canyon’s infrasonic pulse.  
   Naming: at twelve, a Slipwing carries the adolescent’s chosen nameplate to the highest mirror-lichen sheet.  If the lichen flashes it back intact, the canyon “accepts” the name.  
   Death: bodies are not buried (no soil) nor burnt (wasteful).  Instead they are vitrified—flesh replaced cell by cell with silica until the corpse becomes smoky quartz.  These statues stand as load-bearing columns, each flex-mounted so that ancestors quite literally hold up the living.

10.  How to greet the locals  
   1.  Set your boots a handspan apart—stable tripod stance.  
   2.  Touch the rail with two fingers (honors the canyon’s pulse).  
   3.  Hum a low minor third; wait for the other to match the interval.  
   Only then offer your name.  If the mycelium lights crackle while you speak, the canyon itself is listening, and whatever deal you strike will probably hold.

These, then, are the residents: flesh, fungus, machine, and mirage layered so tightly they can no longer be teased apart—an ecosystem whose tallest tree is a winch tower and whose deepest root is a seam of living metal, all humming together in the thin, violet twilight of a crack that never stops widening.
