Who – or what – calls this dim nave home?  

The place keeps more kinds of life than first sight admits, layered the way its ledges are layered: the obvious, the ordinary-strange, and the almost-invisible traces that nevertheless steer everything that happens below the quartz ribs.

I. The Folk of the Terraces (“the Shelf-Born”, “Stream-Kin”, or, in their own wet, consonant-heavy clicks, ɱrhk)  

1. Bodies  
• Stature: seldom taller than the crook of a visitor’s elbow, yet astonishingly broad across chest and shoulder. The rib-cage balloons outward like a bellows; each breath is slow, five heartbeats in, five out, to spare the scant oxygen.  
• Skin: color of candle stubs left too long unlit—gray-white, translucent enough that pulse and tendon show as blue threads. Where rock salt dust abrades them the dermis thickens into mica-like scales that sparkle in lamplight.  
• Eyes: vestigial, no wider than lentils, milk-filmed. They detect only the waxing and waning of flame. Sight is secondary; their primary organ is the ear. An outer ear exists—torn, ridged crescents tucked against the skull—but inside is a triple spiral of cartilage that works like a living Helmholtz resonator. They hear the length of the room as a blind man reads Braille.  
• Lungs & blood: high myoglobin; blood runs almost black. A diver among them can stay under the ankle stream—where deeper potholes drop—long enough to win wagers from naïve newcomers.  
• Hands: six fingers if you count the double thumb. Joints hyper-extend, letting them hold horn chisels flat against the stone for hours without cramping.

2. Lifespan and rhythm  
Thirty winters on the surface equal a round life here. Growth is fast: child to work-able adolescent in six years. Middle age, from ten to twenty, is the season of real strength; past twenty the cartilage in the ears stiffens, blurring echoes, so elders mentor rather than carve. At the first serious hearing lapse they volunteer for drift-burial, believing the stream will carry their dulled senses back to whatever lent them in the first place.

3. Social topology (rank is vertical)  
The nave is a map of prestige.  
• Bottom tiers (“Splash-Benches”) hold apprentices, tool-keepers, water-tenders—anyone who needs to react quickly when a sluice sticks or a corpse jams a spill-slot.  
• Middle galleries belong to the mature carvers and to the Mothers’ Bay—the only warm hollow where births are allowed, directly beside the cistern whose water never chills.  
• Upper shelves—rarified, mist-wreathed—are for the Echo-Shapers, four or five individuals whose hearing is acute enough to sculpt sound itself. They sight unseen visitors by throwing a single cluck and judging the rebound; they umpire disputes, time the work shifts, and, once each year, perform the Synchrony: a drumming duet with the quartz vault until the mineral overtone answers, proving the roof has not cracked.

4. Language  
Three interlocking strata:  
a. Chest-voice for close conversation, pitched so low an outsider feels it more than hears it.  
b. Palate clicks and tooth taps for mid-range orders among workers.  
c. Mallet code for public announcements. Five limestone claves, each tuned by length, hang where the sound is cleanest; the sequence of strikes scrolls out news—birth, death, surface trade—exactly twice daily. One error means the message must be begun anew; echoes do not forgive sloppiness.

5. Nourishment  
• Mainstay: the blind isopod “teul”, baited with rotten reed fibre, boiled in cistern water, then mashed into gray paste. Protein enough, and their shells burn smokeless, supplying charcoal for lamps.  
• Supplement: a biofilm they call “quintessence skin”—not plant, not fungus, but chemosynthetic bacteria that lace the salt stalactites. Scraped, dried, it tastes of copper and cedar smoke and delivers every mineral their marrow demands.  
• Luxury: surface barter brings the occasional pouch of fatsedge seed or knot-wheat. Grains are eaten singly, rolled on the tongue like gems.

6. Craft  
Nothing disgraces a Shelf-Born more than adding weight where the rock cannot bear it. They tap each new slab in three places; if the note rings dull they plane another millimeter. Rope is wound gut from the isopods, plied wet, shrinks tight as iron when dry. Every generation the winch beams are lifted two finger-breadths to compensate for the slow rise of silted floor—incremental, reverent adjustment rather than overhaul.

7. Belief & Ceremony  
Quartz hum is scripture. They say the stone “dreams” once it has stored enough of the day’s clamor; the sub-audible vibration is that dream leaking. When it comes, every hammer falls silent, every throat closes as though mid-sentence were sacrilege. Afterward they gift the roof a libation: one clay saucer of oil poured straight onto the water so a brief, shimmering rainbow slides down-stream.

II. Other Tenants  

1. The Glow-Gnats  
Not insects at all but neuter, winged amphipods. They spend most of life folded inside cracks where geothermal warmth breeds sulfur bacteria. Once an hour the colony exhales a scouting swarm; gut chemicals meet the cooler air and blaze green. Shelf-Born children gamble on how many heartbeats before the last wink—winner pockets a polished quartz chip for sling-ammo.

2. The Once-a-Decade Moth (“Lamp-Widow”)  
Size: a soup-bowl in span, yet light as shed skin. Blind like everything else down here, it orients by magnetic whiskers and is fatally drawn to the iron flecks in quartz chandeliers. The crash is audible: a rolling parchment crackle, a fall of snow-fine scales. Old keepers sweep the dust, filter it through linen, and stir the salvage into oil: one moth buys the city eleven years of steadier flame.

3. Rivertongue  
A worm-thing seldom seen whole. Length estimated at eight, maybe ten meters by how long water burbles after it passes. Transparent except for a russet veinwork, it grazes bacterial mats and never surfaces. For the Shelf-Born it is omen rather than prey: if a corpse launched downstream is spun in the creature’s wake, the dead was judged worthy; if the current runs flat, the soul must wander.

4. The Salt Familiar  
Strictly speaking it is not alive—but the folk insist some ledges grow a crust shaped uncannily like a kneeling figure. The growth always chooses the places where voice strikes hardest, suggesting resonance guides crystallization. Newlyweds chip a thumb-sized flake from the mantle of any Salt Familiar and wear it in braided wrist loops until first child; then the flake is returned. Call it superstition, call it micro-geology—the shelves are dotted with knee-high statues formed without a sculptor.

III. Invisibles that Rule the Visible  

1. Aerosaprotrophs  
Millions of bacteria motoring on trace methane from deep faults. They lace every breath with an oily tang. Without them the Shelf-Born would drown in their own exhalations; with them, oxygen hovers at the bare minimum for mammalian life, holding the city in a permanent margin between wakefulness and faint.

2. The Echo Field  
Not a species but a standing wave ecology. Predictable drip rhythms strike the same spots on the same ledges for centuries, carving tone-holes in stone. Birds have air-sacs; this chamber has echo-pockets. As inhabitants thin or swell in number the reverberation changes—harsh when mouths are many, tender when births run slack—making sound a living feedback loop that teaches the folk how populous they truly are without ever needing to count.

IV. What the Folk Think Lives Beyond  

Ask any adolescent perched high enough to sniff the dry breeze from cracks and you’ll hear of the “Sun Fish,” a creature so bright it burns those who reach water-side first. No adult repeats the tale; none refute it either. Faith in an unconquerable upstream keeps curiosity aligned with caution. They already own one perfect constant—the stream—and suspect a second would be superfluous, even dangerous.

V. Summary Image  

Imagine peering from the twentieth ledge at shift-change: eighty gray torsos clustered about ankle-deep current, mallets stilled, lips closed, waiting for the quartz dream-hum to fade. Glow-gnats lantern the mist. Somewhere beyond sight the Rivertongue ripples past a silent cargo of honored dead. All motion tiny, deliberate, cumulative—life here is a geology practiced in meat and breath. Everything accrues: salt on skin, memories in echo, wisdom in shelves that thicken like tree rings of stone. These, then, are the dwellers: short-lived yet sediment-patient, deaf to the sun yet fluent in water’s consonants, keeping a cathedral they forever remake yet never redesign, because to change a thing is to confess it was once unfinished.
