LIFE ABOVE THE CLOUD-CEILING  
(an annotated bestiary of the basalt plateau)

1. THE GREAT ENGINES

1.1  Fumaroles  
Size: 8–12 m tall; mass ~40 t.  
Body plan: six-legged column, each leg a telescoping stack of chitin lamellae whose edges lock like bayonet fittings. The torso is a double shell: an outer carapace of iron-rich chitin and an inner pressure vessel of tough collagen lined with catalytic silicates. Between the two circulates super-heated steam.  
Metabolism: litho-chemotrophic. Ingested frost sluices across powdered basalt held in gastric bins; liberated iron(II) oxidises violently, raising core temperature to ~280 °C. Excess heat is vented in slow pulses through cranial chimneys—those grey plumes you first mistake for weather.  
Reproduction: once each half-century two adults align side by side and fuse their base plates, forming a single, broad crucible. Mineral-rich slurry is extruded into a common brood chamber and left to “bake” for sixteen auroral cycles. Juveniles the size of cooking pots eventually crack their way out, already hot enough to steam.  
Ecological role: mobile hearths. Their vent heat keeps melt-pools open, letting lesser creatures drink. Over geological time a marching herd can anneal an entire valley floor into obsidian.

1.2  Plowwyrms  
Size: 18–30 m long, but rarely higher than your knee.  
Body plan: 140–200 vertebrae encased in braided keratin cables; each trio of vertebrae sprouts a paired fin that doubles as a heat sink. Head terminates in a tungsten-hard spoon of apatite crystal.  
Metabolism: detrital-nitravore. Skull gouges millimetres of rock, which is swallowed into two serial gizzards. The first crushes basalt with quartz nodules stuck in muscular ridges; the second ferments the powder in acidic brine, extracting trace nitrates. Residual meal is exhaled as dry, metallic dust.  
Sense organs: vibration only. A chain of pressure plates down the flank turns flexion into nerve impulses; footsteps carry farther in stone than in the thin air above.  
Behavior: perpetual drift. A wyrm never stops moving unless breeding or brumating. Migrations draw pale lines across the plateau so straight that early cartographers thought they were surveyor’s baselines.  
Reproduction: pairs twist together in sun-warmed caverns, using body heat to melt a shallow bowl in the floor. Eggs are laid directly into that glassy dish, which hardens around them as it re-cools, forming a readymade incubator.

1.3  Glider-rays  
Wingspan: 3.5–4.2 m; mass: 6–8 kg.  
Structure: cartilage lattice filled with nitrogen foam; integument is a semi-transparent collagen sheet traced by copper-bearing veins that double as Faraday nets.  
Lift: gained from the plateau’s gentle but constant thermals and from electrostatic repulsion—wing veins charge under auroral flux, letting the animal “lean” on the planet’s field lines.  
Hunting: phototropic ambush. The ray positions itself between prey and the dim sun; its translucent body steals what little light is left, throwing a living eclipse that disorients ground dwellers. At the stall point it collapses like a parachute, engulfing victim and ground together.  
Nesting: eggs are injected into fist-moss domes; the moss’s decade-long blooming cycle coincides with hatching, bathing chicks in a drip of sugar water nobody else can spare.

2. THE MIDDLE CASTE

2.1  Pocket-hares  
Burrows resemble coin slots in the basalt. They spend 90 % of life torpid, thawing only when a fumarole wanders within scent range. Fur is hollow glass fibre; blood carries antifreeze proteins potent enough to stay liquid at –18 °C.

2.2  Frost-crickets  
Exoskeleton exudes microscopic prisms that scatter auroral light into directional beacons. Males groom one another to tune the colour of reflection; disputes are literally colour arguments. Their stridulation is subsonic—you feel it in boot soles more than ears.

2.3  Bastion Turtles  
Rare. Eight-legged, shell fused from silica pearls collected at tarn edges. They farm lichen on their own carapaces, abrading patches with clawed hind-legs to stop the crop from going senescent. When a turtle dies the shell survives as a miniature mesa, immediately colonised by new lichen lines.

3. THE QUIET KINGDOM (MICRO-LIFE)

3.1  Glasswater Plankton  
Live only in the smoked-glass tarns. Cell walls contain vanadium crystals that harvest ultraviolet otherwise lost in the thin air. At night they fluoresce teal, turning the water into inverted aurorae.

3.2  Rift Archaea  
Occupy capillaries inside basalt. They ride pressure waves generated by distant mantle swell: an underground weather system. Some leak above ground in warm mist pillars that appear, write impossible curls of condensation, then collapse.

3.3  Moss Symbiotes  
The fist-moss domes house endolithic bacteria that switch to superconducting filaments during the once-in-a-decade bloom. Those filaments channel charge from nightly aurora into the bloom stalks, making them crackle blue—an accidental advertising board for glider-rays seeking nest sites.

4. EDGE CASES AND RUMOURS

4.1  The Anvil Choir  
Travellers report hearing deep, bell-like chords under certain auroral harmonics. Hypotheses: (a) sub-surface colonies of plate-sized bivalves clapping shells; (b) resonance in vast crystal caverns; (c) artefact machinery still operating on forgotten instructions. Evidence: none collected, recordings always blank.

4.2  Melt Circles  
Perfect discs of vitrified basalt appear overnight, unconnected to any known fumarole. Edges ionised, centre magnetic. Local plankton blooms turn amethyst for weeks afterward. Plateau folk mark these as “landing sites” in logbooks but do not say for what.

4.3  Shadow Shepherds  
Occasional silhouettes far larger than fumaroles drift across ridges, accompanied by sudden rises in trace radon. Plowwyrm trails point toward but never through the last reported location, suggesting a predator of predators, or perhaps a mirage that even stone-eaters fear.

5. INTERACTIONS WITH VISITORS

•  Fumaroles treat campfires as kin signals; they will quarter-turn to face a flame, tasting its exhalations through peripheral spiracles. If they decide your combustion chemistry is useful they may root beside you for days, harvesting your exhaust heat.  
•  Plowwyrms ignore metal tools but follow backpacks that jingle—rock flour in their lateral lines mistakes clinking for sub-basalt tremor, a sign that fellow wyrms are feeding. Seasoned guides carry wind-chimes to draw a herd away from tents.  
•  Glider-rays view bright colours as thermal anomalies and investigate by circling; a mirrored cloak will make you invisible to them, an orange tent will make you bait.  
•  Pocket-hares steal inflated drink bladders, believing the soft, warm shape to be dormant fumarole spawn. The bladders freeze solid overnight and are later used as windbreaks outside hare dens.

6. IF YOU BECOME ONE OF THEM

Stay longer than a single moss decade and the life here begins to recruit you. Exposed metal on your gear oxidises into the same rivet-red that striates fumarole shells. Your breath whitens, thickens, hangs in the air long after you finish exhaling—practising for the day it will rise continuously. Learned slowness settles into your joints. Eventually the plateau’s ledger will list you not under “visitor” but under “resident, ambulatory heat source,” and some future traveller will add you to their own notes, right after pocket-hares and just before rumours of the Anvil Choir.

Until then, move softly. Everything that lives here is listening, even if the listening is done through stone, frost, or the faint green shimmer of the sky itself.
