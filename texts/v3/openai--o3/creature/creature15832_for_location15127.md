Who lives in the Basin of Hammer-Shadow?  
At first glance you think the answer is simple: “the builders.”  
Stay a little longer—long enough for the torch smoke to salt your lungs, long enough for your ears to learn the drum code—and you discover that the builders are not one people but a whole, muttering ecology of hands, bones, and obsessions. They are as unfinished and as restless as the structures they raise.

I. The Folk of the Hammer

1. Shapes hammered out of flesh  
Children are born on flagstones still warm from the chisels that split them. Their skulls harden while they sleep beneath a rain of dust; by ten winters their foreheads are ridged like adzes from the habit of bracing stone with the brow while both hands are busy. Spine and shoulder roll forward—permanent crouch for ducking under half-made beams—yet their legs are sprung like drill bits, able to lunge six stair-heights in a breath when a wall begins to heave. Skin along the shins and palms turns nacre-grey; slate powder works into the pores and never comes out. A master mason’s body glitters faintly in torchlight, mica caught in old scar tissue.

2. Dress, if you can call it that  
Nothing loose—loose hangs, and hanging things kill. A typical garb is three layers:  
• innermost, a wrap of eel hide, oiled nightly so dust slides off;  
• over that, copper-link lattice, wide as thumbnail to deflect flying shards;  
• outermost, a kilt of scrap timber slabs, each piece engraved with the bearer’s tally of surfaces conquered.  
Headgear is simply more function: a split cedar bar clamped around the temples so a hammer blow can be taken in the wood, not the skull.

3. Tool as organ  
At adulthood every person forges—or barters for—a single tool whose handle is fitted to the hollow between radius and ulna. That socket is kept raw until the wood beds directly against living bone. When the bearer dies, the tool is snapped free; the notch in the arm is stuffed with slate chips and mortared shut, leaving a crescent scar called the “second thumb.” Martyrs may have three or four such marks.

4. Naming by erosion  
A newborn is “Blank.” The first tool they dull earns them the name “Edge.” Each significant alteration of the Basin—dropping a column, opening a new sluice, redirecting a lightning-scar—adds a vowel or consonant. Lifelong veterans recite titles that are whole sentences, their voices rasped thin by stone dust. When they can no longer speak the length of their own name in one breath, they volunteer for Walling.

5. Walling  
Death is not a hole in the Basin; it is another surface. The old climb into niches freshly carved behind load-bearing plates, take a resin draught that stops the lungs without bloating the flesh, and are mortared in upright. Their corpses serve as acoustic dampers and humidity buffers; torches gutter less in passages lined with the dead. Every wall is a biography, if you know how to read the curve of rib beneath plaster.

6. The Doctrine of Surface  
You have heard the summary—“nothing may remain untouched”—but the living doctrine is subtler. A surface is alive only while someone is altering it. The instant a face lies fallow, entropy has the upper hand. Therefore the people divide all existence into two tempos:  
• The Quick-worked (stone under tools, wood under wedges, water under sluice)  
• The Drift (everything else, including day, sleep, memory, even the shapes of their own bodies)  
To stay among the Quick-worked is salvation; to drift is to invite oblivion.  

II. Orders and Roles

1. Drum-callers  
Bare-footed runners whose only instrument is the great rawhide battery suspended over Central Well. They hear collapses before they happen—claim they feel the stress-tones through the soles—and answer with rhythms that jerk crews out of danger. Ears cauliflowered from the volume, hearts swollen to twice normal size from the sprinting.

2. Angle-tenders  
Scholars of slippage and warp. They carry no hammer, only chipped abaci made of clay beads on frayed cords. By morning light—thin as it is—they measure how shadows fall across a datum board; by torchlight they divine how much a span has crept. When the drift exceeds one part in sixty, they order the wall razed and rebuilt smaller, cleaner.

3. Surge-warders  
Gaunt, mildew-smelling figures who speak in flow rates and sleep beside the dams. Copper claws are riveted to their knuckles for punching soft clay stoppers free. They alone drink the first gush of a release, because they trust water only while it is moving fast.

4. Flint-midwives  
Half physician, half sculptor. They own knives sharper than any chisel, used to cut away bone spurs that would catch on scaffold gaps, used also to carve the Basin’s writing into newborn fingernails. A midwife’s smile is black—so much copper dust has plated their teeth that they appear the color of old blood.

III. Lesser Life, Half Acknowledged

1. Latch-eels  
Pale as finger bones, jointed like centipedes. The people prize them not as food but as augurs: an eel that swims against the gutter-flow means a back-flood is building somewhere upslope. Kill the omen, lose the warning—so they are spared.

2. Echo-rats  
Rare. Those that survive do so by intuiting the hammer cadence and darting only on off-beats. Children trap them for sport; adults let them be, believing each rat eats just enough stray seed of chaos to keep the angles honest.

3. Ember moths  
Wings filmed with oily soot. Their presence around a torch is said to sharpen the flame, make it cut shadows cleaner. They are never harmed. Indeed, if a moth settles on fresh-laid mortar, work halts until the creature departs of its own will. No one recalls why; the rule is older than the oldest wall.

IV. Beliefs Whispered, Not Etched

1. The Quarried Self  
A heresy claiming that beneath skin the body is unfinished stone, waiting for chisel and mallet to reveal its perfect grain. Some youths practice self-carving: abrading cheeks, beveling kneecaps. The orthodoxy tolerates it—more surfaces, after all—provided they do not bleed where water could carry corruption into the runnels.

2. The Stone That Dreams Back  
Old wallers feel it first: the sense that the Basin is not merely responding to their labor, but planning reroutes, suggesting new load paths, leaning into the blow. When lightning lances through a roof-gap and splits a tower exactly where an apprentice forgot a counter-brace, the story spreads again. No one argues out loud; hammers just swing a little faster, as though trying to stay ahead of a partner who might one day take the lead.

V. A Day in the Hum of It

Wake to the tremor of tread-wheels turning. Swallow a slurry of crushed grain and condensate. Slip the wrist into the haft socket—relief, like a joint sliding home. First task: redress the step that chipped under last night’s torrent. Second task: wedge greenwood into a fault line, hear it groan as it widens. Eat at the wall: eel jelly, salt, a lick of copper to keep the blood honest. Toward dusk (a slightly lighter violet) stand beneath the central roof gap and pray—not to the sky, which they have never seen—but to the moment’s next surface.

When sleep finally comes you dream of two things only: an edge so sharp it can split shadow from darkness, and a silence so complete you could hear dust settling on dust. Both are terrifying. You wake grateful for the drums.

VI. Why They Stay

Ask, and they will answer in proverb:  
“The world beyond is round; on roundness nothing holds.”  
Ask again, and an older one might confess: out there, rain falls where it chooses, stone breaks itself, time chews edges without permission. In the Basin of Hammer-Shadow, they alone decide how the world shall crack. Dominion enough for any lifetime.

So the Basin’s inhabitants are not merely builders; they are the quarry made incarnate—bodies mortised to tools, laws mortared to flesh, voices tuned to the pitched ringing of copper on slate. They live to keep every surface young, to make ruin impossible by accomplishing it continuously. And if, some night, the stone truly begins to dream back, it will find in them willing collaborators, chisel in hand, eager to carve whatever face the darkness proposes.
