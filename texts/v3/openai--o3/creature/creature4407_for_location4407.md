Who lives here?  
Begin by standing very still and listening. Everything that follows can be heard before it is seen.

1. The Wind-Counters (people)

• Appearance and physiology  
They are short-limbed, barrel-chested, the result of generations selected for oxygen thrift. Their skin browns almost purple from years beneath a killing sun; their lips and nail beds run permanently blue. The cartilage of every external ear is stretched into a thin paddle, a natural wind-vane; elders can name a coming weather change when the breeze merely brushes that cartilage. Children are taught to sleep on a pillow of smooth pebbles so that the back of the skull flattens slightly; a flat skull fits the stone headrests carved inside Breath-Chambers, letting a sleeper keep one ear in perfect line with the resonant fins.

• Settlements  
A “village” is six to twelve cave mouths threaded along a south-facing cliff, nothing visible from above except a guttering of smoke at dawn. Platforms of vine-rope and riverstone cantilever out over the void; goat kids grow up learning to walk on surfaces no wider than their own hooves. Between villages stretch pilgrimage lines—beaded strings of Breath-Chambers tuned by ancestors into descending pentatonic scales. Travelers recite the scale as they pass each chamber, confirming that cliffs have not yet shed the rooms into the abyss.

• Livelihood  
Ribbon-grass is combed at sunrise with bone rakes; the dew it relinquishes is funneled into gut-skin bladders worth their volume in salt. From fern-trees the people strip braidable fibers and a sweet pith that ferments into a thin, sour beer. Goat wool is spun with grass-silk to make a cloth whose copper-oxide dye reflects ultraviolet and refracts visible light; to lowlanders the Wind-Counters seem to flicker like heat-mirage when they walk.

• Culture of sound  
All speech is pitched low, in the gaps between the prevailing chords of the plateau. Personal names are whistles you could not register below 3 kHz—only dogs and children truly hear them. Possession is marked by harmony: if you can sing a tool’s overtone series perfectly you claim it as yours. Courtship happens in pairs, mouths against opposite ends of a clay tube; when the blended tone inside the tube beats slower than a resting pulse, the couple is judged compatible.

• Cosmology  
The highland creation story has no gods, only compression and rarefaction. “The world is a breath the stone forgot to exhale.” Dying elders choose a canyon, sit inside a newly chiseled hollow, and match pitch with the wind until voice and vortex become indistinguishable. After three days the body is gone—flensed by dust, dragged off by whiptails, or simply fallen—but the note remains, another timbre sewn into the endless chord.

2. Cloud-Goats (Capra nubilus)

The plateau’s most conspicuous mammal weighs less than a full backpack. Cloud-goats have pneumatic sinuses that open behind the horns; when the animals leap they expel stored air, gaining a half-second of buoyancy—just enough to clear the knife-edge arêtes. Their fleece is hollow and floss-white; when a herd beds in fog it looks as though the fog has chosen a place to rest. Wind-Counters harvest shed underwool to felt the ear-hoods worn by newborns—first protection against the roaring world.

3. Sky-Whiptails (Draco remex)

Part lizard, part dragonfly sailplane. Six membranous “ribs” hinge along an impossibly narrow spine; a whiptail can reef or billow those ribs like a ship’s canvas, riding thermals for hours without a single muscle twitch. They hunt by barometric ambush: sensing a downdraft, they collapse the wings, plummet rock-straight, and spear any moving shadow on a ledge. Flesh is translucent, so when they climb back into sunlight you can watch the fresh kill travelling down the gut like a faint, dark comet.

4. Chorus Beetles

Almost never seen in daylight, they rule the dusk. Each beetle carries a pair of tympanic sacs that pop open like book covers; friction ridges on the inside make a chord when struck by vibrating wings. Tens of thousands hidden in cracks can tune themselves into a slow arpeggio that ripples along an entire canyon wall. Local farmers time the sowing of ribbon-grass seed by the tempo of that arpeggio: fast music means a storm front is compressing the air and rain will beat the seed into soil by nightfall.

5. The Plateau Stag (Cervus amnicus)

Pale, yes—almost freckled with absence—but up close the coat is densely patterned with pores that exhale water vapor. The stag literally builds its own fog robe; predators see only mist threaded with antlers. During mating season males clash not with antlers but with echoes: they stand on opposing rims and bellow. The first to split his own call into a perfect octave compels submission—the bowls themselves serve as amplifiers, and a faulty overtone returns to embarrass its sender seconds later.

6. Ribbon-Grass Microbiome

Pull a blade and split it lengthwise: embedded along the mid-rib are iridescent nodules. These are colonial cyanobacteria that drifted up from vanished seas eons ago and struck a pact with grass ancestors. The grass traps mist; in payment the bacteria fix nitrogen at a rate unknown in any valley crop. Wind-Counters chew the nodules for altitude sickness—within minutes hemoglobin leaps, vision sharpens, and the heart’s panic subsides.

7. Aeroliths (rumor, or something stranger)

Once every few dozen storms, inhabitants speak of shapes forming inside those impossible vertical rivers: columns of water that resist the wind’s theft, faceted like glass yet flexing. They appear busy, as if assembling and disassembling themselves in search of a faultless architecture. No one has touched one; those who tried say their arms were seized by perfectly cold suction, then let go. Wind-Counters leave offerings at the bases of such rivers—shards of obsidian, goat milk, crushed beetle wings—just in case the aeroliths are the plateau’s way of accounting for debts.

8. The Silent Parliament

Not animals in any ordinary sense, yet undeniably alive. Somewhere under the conglomerate runs a lattice of mica plates created by ancient metamorphic heat. Constant flexing from wind loads charges that lattice piezo-electrically; the result is a sub-audible thrum most ears can’t detect. Ribbon-grass roots penetrate the mica, moss mats cover it, beetles drum above it, goats tread on it, people carve chambers in it—the entire biome plugs into this slow, mineral heartbeat. Shamans of the Wind-Counters lie belly-down on the warmest stones, teeth against rock, and claim to hear councils of voices debating whether the surface creatures have earned one more century of ground to stand on.

9. Absences worth noting

• No large raptors. At this altitude climbing flight is wasteful; any bird big enough to prey on a goat would be blown past the horizon before it could brake.  
• No true trees. Even the tallest fern-trunks are hollow and jointed—designed to fold rather than snap when the gales arrive.  
• Very few scavengers. Protein is too scarce; nearly everything here is obliged to make its own kills or its own sugars.

Living here is, in sum, an apprenticeship in attention. Every organism—human, mammal, insect, or rumored spirit—survives by leaning into the plateau’s orchestra, finding a resonant niche, and letting the larger music carry the cost of being alive four kilometres closer to the stars.
