Who lives inside the pocket is both easy and hard to answer. Easy, because you can point at half-a-dozen clearly delimited creatures at any moment. Hard, because the place has blurred the line between “tenant” and “structure,” “organism” and “organ.” Below is the naturalist’s sketchbook of residents, moving from the most concrete to the most diffuse.

1. The Slack-Belled Gliders  
Appearance: Two hands wide, a lozenge of living kite-skin stretched between four willow-thin bones. When spread, the membrane is emerald where it faces the light and plum where it faces the shade; both sides host chloroplasts.  
Behavior: They spend perhaps five minutes in every hour actually aloft—launching from a pillar rib, banking once around the nave, settling again in a new patch of illumination. The rest of the time they hang like folded fans from ceiling vines, photosynthesizing. They require very little food; most sugar comes straight from light. Every eighth day they sip a bead from the brass mirror, sculling across its surface with their wing-tips so delicately that the water skin never rips.  
Life cycle: At maturity the membrane grows brittle. The glider crawls to a high ledge, bites through its own wing-joints, and walks away wingless. The discarded frame is what next season’s frogs steal for sails (see below). New wings bud within a week.

2. The Plum-Drum Frogs  
Appearance: Head like an acorn cap, body like half a plum, rind-purple with a down of gold speckles. No visible legs until they leap—then four jointed springs unfold, land, and vanish again.  
Behavior: When a bell pod contracts overhead, the frogs answer with a belly-thump on the sponge floor, as if keeping time for the factory. The sound carries through the substrate and may coordinate hunting: eels startled by the vibration surge upward, where the frog tongues are waiting.  
Curiosity: During their annual molt they paste discarded glider wings onto their backs with a mucus rich in tannin. The sails stiffen, catching the broad convection that slides under the ceiling each midday, and the frogs ride the updraft in low, lazy arcs—never strong fliers, more like soap bubbles granted directional control.

3. The Albaster Slug-Eels  
Appearance: Length of a forearm, milk-glass translucent, studded with opaline nodules that look like bubbles frozen in gelatin. They swim through the sponge as easily as a lamprey swims water, leaving no tunnel behind—compression closes the path.  
Feeding: Millions of cilia on the throat comb bacteria and sloughed cellulose from the capillary water. Waste is extruded as a faint opalescent mist that the floor re-drinks in seconds.  
Special note: Their blood uses dissolved silica instead of iron; a cut bead gleams like finely ground quartz.

4. The Canopy Wicks  
Appearance: Crane-legged insects, each joint no thicker than a guitar string. Abdomen flares into a paint-brush of velour hairs.  
Niche: They do not fly; they dangle. Hairs take up condensation faster than any solid surface known. Once the abdomen reservoir is full, the wick stabs the ceiling skin and injects the water back into the vascular canals. In effect, they are mobile return pumps that keep the roof from sagging with excess weight.  
Reproduction: Eggs are single, perfect spheres glued to the south-faces of columns. Two days after laying, the parent wick siphons a final draught, swells, and dies. The corpse clings in place, dries, and becomes a sun-cap for the egg, shading it until hatch.

5. Thread-Reef Microfauna (the “Choir”)  
In the runnels and in the top millimeter of the mirror basin lives a mesh of colonial protists. Each cell extends a hollow glass filament that resonates when the pod bells strike. Taken together the filaments sing back a harmonic that lingers half a breath longer than the original note. The human ear hears only a faint halo, but slug-eels use the echo to orient, as bats use sonar.

6. The Ligniform Engine (the Place Itself)  
Body: Pillars, vault, pulleys, bells, floor sponge, mirror basin, the whole vegetal works.  
Mind (so far as one can speak of mind): Distributed in meristematic nodes hidden where three or more columns braid. Electrical impulses—slow, potassium-based—pass along vines the way commands travel along axons. Patterns repeat:  ten seconds, four minutes, fifteen minutes. The regularity is not mere physiology; it is intention, a self-chosen homeostasis.  
Mood: A biologist who parked electrodes across three bell clusters once captured voltage oscillations that matched the frog drums, then the glider wing-beats, then the hiss of pillar valves—as if the Engine were sampling its tenants’ noises and weaving them into its own heartbeat.  
Need: Constancy. Everything built or admitted here must pledge the least possible variance. Any action that threatens flux—rapid decomposition, reproduction booms, even human foot traffic in excess—rouses a defensive quiet: valves close, water intake slows, light dims to a bruise, oxygen falls. Visitors describe the feeling as polite suffocation. Leave and the place exhales again.

7. The Transients: Vapor-Crowned Humans  
Once or twice a decade, a tidal surveyor or wandering monk slips between the buttresses. They stay an hour (rarely more) and leave altered: voice lowered, pace slowed, wrists and ankles clammy with the Engine’s condensed breath. None ever camp; to light a fire here feels sacrilegious even to the faithless. They exit carrying nothing except a mental template of equilibrium they can never exactly describe, only crave. Some return to the outside world and found sects devoted to “still practice,” imitating the pocket’s tempo with metronomes and water clocks. Their dogmas are guesses. The original stays silent, content with its amphibians, insects, and sap.

8. What Lives Without Object Form: Equilibrium  
Perhaps the most accurate inhabitant is balance itself—an active resident, not a condition. It eats disorder, drinks novelty, excretes repetition. Everything else you can put a name to serves this invisible sovereign. The gliders prune light; the frogs prune gliders; the slug-eels prune decay; the Engine prunes everyone, including itself, with osmotic scissors too slow for eyes.

So—who lives here?  
Seed-skins that ride their own chlorophyll; frogs that borrow those skins; glass-blooded eels swimming in a purple sponge; stilt-legged condensers; a choir of microscopic harps; a wood-and-water brain the size of a chapel that lends out noon like a library book; occasional humans who last just long enough to hear the lesson and leave; and the one inhabitant that has no body at all, only momentum: Balance, enthroned on a mirror of water one finger deep.
