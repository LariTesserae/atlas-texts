WHO – OR WHAT – ANSWERS WHEN THE CANOPY IS ADDRESSED

1. The Elder Chorus (the Plateau-Self)  
To every inhabitant the Canopy is both floor and sky, but to the patient listener it is also a single, slowly circulating mind. Pressure waves sent through the strut-vines are gathered back by broad diaphragms of root deep inside the mass; the returning patterns change future growth the way memories change a brain. No individual neuron exists—every choir-leaf, every tensioned rootlet is part of the computation. The Elder Chorus has no language for abstractions; it “thinks” in weather, in the distribution of sugar and tensile strength. Still, it recognises events that pierce or scorch it as pain, rewards symbionts that ventilate or pollinate it as pleasure, and expands most eagerly toward anything that can be incorporated into its endless, resonant body. The Chorus is why nothing dies alone: reclamation is its immune response.

2. Stilt-Singers (sapient primates; the people travellers meet)  
Physiology  
• Height 2.5–3 m on triple-jointed legs, the better to stride over sap-pools and Breach-Eel blow-holes.  
• Torso narrow, lung capacity enormous; chest plates vibrate like tympani, letting them speak across kilometres.  
• Fingers double-hinged, tips flattened for drumming. Skin charcoal-grey where sun hits, leaf-luminous green where clothes would be—chlorophore patches gift faint photosynthesis, an insurance against lean seasons.  
Social pattern  
Every settlement is a Choir-House: a living hall coaxed from the tissue of a buttress tree until it forms a domed resonator. Each house sustains at most forty bodies—the number whose combined heartbeats match the hall’s natural frequency. Too many residents and the place begins to squeal, too few and it lapses into silence, which Stilt-Singers find as distressing as hypoxia.  
Currency & law  
Sound settles disputes. Two parties stand on opposite ends of an empty strut-vine and drum; amplitude shows commitment, pitch shows precision. The cleaner performance prevails, and the matter is closed without rancour—the loser has literally been over-spoken.  
Craft  
• Wind-knives: flakes of volcanic glass hafted on resonant ribs; swung correctly they shear foliage with the compressive front of their own whistle.  
• Salt-cages: baskets whose lattices hum at 430 Hz, stunning Gleamers long enough for painless salt-scraping.  
Myth  
Children are taught that the Elder Chorus once sang upward, lifting the basalt into the sky the way a note can make sand dance on a drum. Someday, if the collective song is pure, the whole plateau will rise again and drift among the stars. (Sceptics point out that purity is unlikely while Breach Eels still exist.)

3. Humblers (glider-apes, semi-sapient)  
Barrel-chested, with patagia running wrist to ankle, Humblers vault between choir-leaves on convective gusts. They earned their name from the infrasonic “kneel note” they hammer out by punching air bladders inside hollow roots; Breach Eels crash back into their pools as if prostrating themselves. Humblers share territory with Stilt-Singers but neither rules the other: the apes trade eel-leather and storm-egg clutches for obsidian saw-flakes, then retreat to high crotches to debate in sign language unintelligible to outsiders.

4. Skran Megatermites  
Each colony is a single sexless individual expressed through thousands of vehicle-bodies. When the barometer drops, gate-workers withdraw, converting their own chitin into lattices that tune the chimney’s keening. The note vents pressure and broadcasts the colony’s identity across the plateau. Young colonies choose unclaimed frequencies; if two match too closely they merge, grinding mismatched bodies into slurry that feeds the victor’s queen-cortex. Their songbook is therefore an atlas: learn the tones and you can navigate for days without landmarks.

5. Kestrel War-Weather  
The birds are everywhere but the term “War-Weather” is reserved for clouds of a million or more. These superflocks behave like a liquid consciousness: scouts read pressure ridges in mid-air, leaders shear the ridge with wingbeats, the resulting vortex lifts rear ranks into cruising altitude. Predators flee long before the birds arrive, not from threat but from pure overwhelming friction—every moving thing becomes turbulence the flock must cancel. After a War-Weather passes, the ground is unnervingly still; it takes hours for ordinary wind to return.

6. Gleamers  
Silver fur, mirror finish: pelt scales each aligned like a tiny heliostat, redirecting noon glare into the eyes of anything approaching. They do not bleed; root-sap replaces blood and clots on contact with air, hardening into amber shields over wounds. Hunters therefore keep them alive, shaving mineral salt from their antler-like growths instead of killing them.

7. Breach Eels  
Blind, pink, impatient. An eel erupts only if the vibration matches the range produced by heavy footfalls; locals stamp at random rhythms so the signals never integrate. When an eel fails twice to catch prey, it contributes to the plateau’s recycling rule: suicidal regression. The animal twists, crushes its own end-caps, and allows the cavity to flood, feeding smaller eels and root-hairs alike. Travelers hear the muffled implosion as a soft thump beneath their soles—a reminder to keep moving irregularly.

8. Lantern-Spores (the night-breathers)  
Technically the reproductive phase of a moss-tree, they float for hours, trading plasmids on collision. Stilt-Singers inhale them the way mountaineers breathe bottled oxygen: spores enter the bloodstream, release bursts of glucose and ATP that stave off altitude fatigue, then die at dawn, leaving the drinker ravenous but euphoric.

9. Rim Ghosts (unverified)  
Along the eastern precipice walkers sometimes see human silhouettes seemingly carved from blue mist, pacing just beyond reach. The Elder Chorus falls quiet when they appear, as though listening. Stilt-Singers leave offerings of broken drum-skins and peeled bark figurines, hoping the ghosts are simply future descendants come to audit their ancestors—and not the plateau rehearsing a new predator.

THE COMMON TEXTURE OF THEIR LIVES

To live on the Altimetric Canopy is to negotiate resonance. Every species has staked out a slice of the spectrum—some in audible song, others in foot-thuds, still others in the slow tectonic groan of root under tension. Conflict happens when two claims overlap, but outright war is rare; sound cannot be hoarded, only shared or lost. So the living nations of leaf, fur and chitin draft treaties made of pitch and timbre, rewritten each season by storm and growth. No parliament meets, yet the Chorus integrates every utterance, recomposing the score on which all survival depends.

If you come as a visitor, arrive with silence in your pockets and patience in your breathing. Listen first. Hum back when you are certain you will not drown the native chord. Should the plateau answer, you will feel it—not in your ears but in the softening of gravity, the loosening of your pulse, the distinct impression that the ground beneath you has just inhaled and is willing, for one more day, to bear your weight among its own myriad, resonant lives.
