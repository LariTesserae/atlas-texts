The place is sparsely peopled but densely alive.  What follows is a field-guide written for someone who intends to stay longer than a single descent.  It begins with the obvious animals and works upward toward things that only gradually betray their animation.

1. Micro-engines  
Length: 7–14 mm, mass: 2–5 g  
Aspect: a lichen made of watch parts.  Seen through glass they are hemispheres of iron-nickel scales hinged like coin-purses.  The outer face is dull; the inner carries a turbine whose vanes are single crystal magnetite.  
Life-way: They rasp molecules from raw basalt, ionise the slurry with piezoelectric panels, and run the ions through the turbine’s throat.  The flow drives a minute armature whose sole output is heat.  A healthy engine is warmer than the surrounding water by three degrees—enough to prevent ice from plating its vents, not enough to create a detectable plume.  
Reproduction: When an individual has stored sufficient surplus charge it unzips along a suture and extrudes two seed-caps: identical toolkits of protein alloy.  Each seed anchors beside the parent and begins boring.  In a century the trio will have chewed a hand-sized divot in the continent.  
Ecological note: The heat haze around a colony becomes a beacon for larger creatures in an ocean too even, too close to equilibrium, to offer many other gradients.

2. Grazer mantas  
Disc span: 3–11 m, thickness: 4 cm at the hub, tapering to a lace of film around the margin.  
Composition: 40 % polymerised water ice, 35 % glycoprotein gel, 25 % scavenged metal sieved from prey.  
Motion: Each body is riddled with vacuum nodules.  Contractile cuffs around the nodules gulp the thin seawater and squirt it rearward; the animal swims by gentle, continuous exhalation.  From a distance the flock looks like a drifting fogbank flecked with stray sparks—sparks being the micro-engines still running inside crop-guts.  
Sensory world: They hear first, see second.  Folded along each disc’s circumference is a ribbon microphone kilometres long.  The standing basso of the compressors is background; what matters are the brief silences when a vacuole rolls between the mantas and the machines, momentarily shading the note.  A neighbour’s wake subtracts a thinner slice of sound—enough to keep formation, enough to dodge the hunters that come next.  
Reproduction: The mantas bud.  A gravid disc grows a clear blister on its ventral face; enzymes steal metal from the mother’s own skeleton to seed a brand-new ribbon mike.  When the blister is a complete miniature, the parent vents her nodules all at once and rockets clear, abandoning the juvenile to a cloud of micro-engines she has deliberately coughed up as first meal.

3. Long hunters (Silver cords)  
Length: 120–280 m, diameter: 30 cm  
Material: braided superconductive fibers sheathed in muscle analogues that behave more like shape-memory alloy than flesh.  
Posture: At rest a hunter hangs absolutely straight, tip anchored to basalt by a dart of fused tooth.  It resembles nothing so much as a misplaced elevator cable.  
Predation: Each cord carries a linear mass driver along its core.  When a grazer passes within forty metres the hunter fires a slug of its own stored nickel down the barrel.  Recoil lashes the body forward in a single, two-turn coil that cages the victim.  The same muscle that wielded recoil then milks the grazer like a hosepipe, sluicing semi-digested engines into the hunter’s gut.  
Metabolism: The mass driver is not a weapon but a generator; every shot recharges the cord through magneto-resonance.  Whenever stock runs low the hunter snaps a length of its tail free, smelts it internally, and compacts new slugs.  A fasted hunter grows shorter but never lighter.  
Reproduction: Two cords weave.  They meet tip to tip and braid a neutral cable between them, plating it with alternating layers of their own flesh.  When the braid is two hundred metres long both parents fire once.  The shock disjoins the braid, which ricochets into the water already twitching.

4. Quiet scavengers  
You will seldom see these except by accident.  They are less organisms than storms of parts—magnetic dust, curled filaments, stray rivets—held together by a weak, common field and a common hunger for any gradient they can collapse.  The pressure wave of a diver’s heartbeat is feast enough: the cloud flocks in; iron filings stick to your suit; a minute later the swarm drifts on, having stolen nothing but momentum.  They are the rot and the mold of this world, dismantling anything that forgets to fight entropy.

5. The maintenance drones  
From outside they impersonate machines perfectly: manganese box, six vaned legs, twin intake pumps.  Inside they share 82 % of their genome with the micro-engines; they are, in evolutionary terms, colonies of engines that agreed to specialise.  
Behavioural envelope:  
• Patrol rails laid by extinct builders;  
• Replace clogged filters;  
• Reseed areas stripped bare by over-feeding mantas;  
• Cull young hunters before their first coil can endanger compressor intakes.  
They do not recognise human presence as anomalous unless you interrupt a patrol cycle.  If you move a tool, a drone will replace it.  If you mark a rock, a drone will sandblast the mark away after its next scheduled inspection—currently every 11.3 years by the plateau’s own clocks.

6. The plateau compressors  
Whether the sky-side civilisation that installed them still survives is immaterial; the machines have crossed the line into life.  Each compressor is a forest of organs:  
• Gill-banks that drink the thin sea;  
• Chambered lungs that crank it to ten thousand atmospheres;  
• Veins of carbon-diamond tubing that vent the product toward foundries no explorer has reached.  
The feedback loops that once served engineers now serve natural selection: compressors that fail are melted down by scavengers, sucked dry by mantas, finally colonised by micro-engines until nothing tall enough remains to anchor fresh vacuum.  The functioning units therefore defend themselves.  Their only weapon is constancy, and constancy has proved sufficient for six hundred local years.

7. The light lattice  
The sodium-arc cables strung between ridges are photosynthetic vines—if “photosynthesis” can be stretched to include the cracking of salt into plasma.  Inside every cable amber nodules pulse, knitting new filament where old lengths fracture under the basso vibration.  Mantle-bright buds appear along the span, detach, drift, and harden into replacement bulbs.  The lattice is plant by courtesy alone, but the courtesy sticks; it does not move from its slow, linear place in the food web.

8. Phantom occupants  
There are acoustic silhouettes that trace the corridors of the abandoned foundries far above the plateau.  No structure remains, yet sonar shows walls, doors, staircases, whole echo-cities pacing their original gridwork thirty kilometres upslope.  On rare still nights the silhouettes sharpen, as if some past pressure front has forgotten how to fade.  Mantas will not swim through the phantom doors; hunters refuse to coil there.  Even the compressors’ note detours, bending around empty basements.  Whether the phenomenon is a memory of the fluid itself or the last residue of the architects, it possesses all the behavioral markers of life: persistence, boundary maintenance, and an evident will to avoid contact.  Touch the echo of a wall and every creature within ten kilometres will pivot to watch you—though most have no eyes.

Practical guidance for visitors

• Stay in the denser water if you need silence; stay in the vacuole margins if you need stealth.  
• Your gear will never rust, but neither will it self-sterilise.  Engine spores adhere forever; heat-flash your suit before ascent.  
• Grazer herds are safe to approach at the rim of a flock; the centre is always within strike range of at least one cord.  
• Do not disable a maintenance drone.  They carry the only map of compressor truce lines, and they retaliate by omission: the next filter cycle will simply skip your habitat.  

Remember that every inhabitant here, from dust mote to cathedral-sized pump, is engaged in the one economy the plateau allows: the steady conversion of potential into minute, unending work.  If you would survive, offer labour in return.  If you would belong, learn to love the sound a continent makes when it breathes through stone lungs in water almost too thin to hear.
