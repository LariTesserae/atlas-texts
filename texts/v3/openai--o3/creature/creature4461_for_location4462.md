Who fills this water-hung citadel?  Everything that draws breath atop the basalt—human or otherwise—has been shaped, shaved, and slowly tutored by three kilometres of altitude, the tyranny of wet stone, and the unending throb of pumps.  A census, beginning with the quickest hearts and ending with the ones that beat a day between pulses:

1. The Plateau Folk (usually called Kalites)

Body  
• Stature: middling in height but long in lung; their ribcages flare barrel-wide, an adaptation to the thin, damp air.  
• Skin: basalt-brown through copper-red no matter their ancestry; iron in the water stains the stratum corneum the way tea darkens porcelain.  Older citizens polish their elbows and knees with pumice until they shine like dull agate.  
• Hair: always braided, not for fashion but to keep it out of street-runoff.  The favoured binding twine is spun from desiccated gutter-coral fans; it smells faintly of iodine when wet.  
• Voice: low and sibilant.  A lifetime of leaning close to be heard above machinery trains them to murmur; consonants ride the hiss of mist so neighbors can hear without raising a shout that would echo off the stone.

Dress  
Three layers:  
1. An inner wrap of oiled eel-skin that sheds water the way a lotus sheds rain.  
2. A middle tunic of worm-clay felt, absorbent and insulating; colours range cream to brick depending on the clay’s iron content.  
3. An outer shell—chitin plates hinged by brass rivets—worn like half-armor when one must brave the cliff roads or the cavernous pump halls.  

Domestic life  
Homes stack like books in a flooded library.  A “house” means one vertical shaft, perhaps four metres square, with platforms for floors and retractable linen screens for privacy.  The family kitchen is usually two platforms below the bedroom, the workshop one platform above.  Wastewater pours down a clay throat at the centre and joins the public greyline beneath the street.

Economy and guilds  
• The Gutterwrights: maintain every carved channel, spit silt from every sieve; the city pays them in shares of the recovered metal grit.  
• Pump-Chaplains: one part engineer, one part priest, they walk the pumping stations counting strokes that set the civic clock.  No marriage is official and no contract binding unless witnessed during a pump cycle.  
• Lift-Loomers: harvest glider-eel molt and weave it into buoyant cloth used to hoist fragile cargo on helium-sails.  
• Worm-Tenders: follow the herds of lickworms, milk them of clay the way low-landers tap rubber trees, and sing slow antiphons whose bass vibrations keep the animals calm.  
Status flows not from coin—most daily trade is barter of work hours—but from water allotments recorded on knotted eel-gut cords worn as bracelets.

Faith  
Not temples but “Listening Rooms”: perfectly spherical caverns whose walls drip at a mathematically constant rate.  Worship is the patient cataloguing of drip intervals, because believers hold that the hidden intellect of the mountain confides its plans in that rhythm.  A child hears only water; a lifelong adept hears instructions for when to raise levees, whom to wed, when to let a tower slump.

Life cycle  
Birth happens high, on rooftops, where the air is cleanest.  The umbilical blood is thought to “teach” newborn marrow the correct red-cell count.  Burial never comes—bodies are defleshed by ritual tressels and the bones cribbed in the Sky Parlor—but the old speak of “sinking,” a long sabbatical spent descending, story by story, to live among younger descendants before finally entering the dripping ossuaries.

2. Symbiotic Domesticates

Lickworms  
Two metres long, fifty kilos of slow muscle encased in hide that looks sewn from wet boot tongues.  They rasp biofilm with a quartz dental plate and leave behind coils of buttery clay that potters shape still warm—if you wait too long it sets harder than marble.  Every household keeps at least one juvenile in a wall niche; its constant exhalation of warm, filtered air dries laundry.

Tressels  
Hexapedal, low to the ground, their shell a mosaic of re-cycled cobblestones they cement to themselves.  Kalites raise special “white-jaw” lines in domestic pens; the animals’ saliva contains a saurian collagen used as mortar.  Funeral tressels are black-jawed and never bred—only wild ones are invited to strip the dead, ensuring the lineage between citizens and city remains untamed.

Glider Eels  
Domestication is one-sided: you may coax, never command.  Children learn to tie “wind letters”—ribbons dipped in snail musk—onto feeding lines strung above courtyards.  An eel that accepts the offering will revisit that courtyard for decades, devouring pests and occasionally loosing a ballast fart that sends it shooting upward like an unmoored banner.

Stonebreaker Snails  
Thumb-sized bivalves that secrete hydrofluoric acid strong enough to etch glass.  Households corral them in clay pens, harvest the acid for cleaning rust off gear-teeth, then feed the snails to eels in a neat trophic loop.

3. Slow, Possibly Thinking Things

Gutter-Corals  
Genetically animal, metabolically plant, socially enigmatic.  In the dry season (a relative term—it merely rains less) their stalks extend yellow icicle sheaths rich in potassium.  Storm electricity plays across those sheaths, and the corals retract, storing the charge chemically.  Scholars suspect the colony keeps a running tally of lightning events—what it does with that data no one knows.

Basalt Mould  
A lichen-fungus alliance that grows inside the rock, stitching microscopic glass threads that increase tensile strength.  Entire water towers rely on mould-stitched columns.  If a tower must be demolished, workers play infrasonic “kill notes” that stun the fungus; otherwise the walls prove all but indestructible.

4. The Unseen Strata

Mist Angels  
A local superstition with teeth.  Men and women descending alone into hot vent shafts sometimes return raving about tall shapes outlined in condensing steam—figures that exhale opposite the wind and step effortlessly across vertical drops.  No photograph has caught one; no body has been found.  Yet once every decade an empty, neatly folded pair of chitin plates appears at a shaft mouth, still warm, as though something shed its disguise and moved on.

Pump Spirits  
Machinist folklore insists that consciousness accretes where metal moves with unwavering rhythm.  They leave offerings—one drop of snail acid, one flake of rust, one tooth from a broken gear—in tiny shrine-boxes bolted to flywheels.  Whether spirits exist, the practice works: offerings force crews to inspect every critical joint each shift, and breakdowns stay mercifully rare.

5. Immigrants and Passers-Through

Lowland Traders  
Gaunt, sun-bronzed people allergic to the plateau’s air; they wear charcoal respirators and soft goggles.  They bring coal, tropic spices, news of wars the plateau will hear but never feel.  Most stay a season, make what profit they can in worm-clay ceramics, then descend before their joints swell and their dreams fill with pump cadence they cannot unhear.

Pilgrim Biologists  
From coastal universities, armed with waterproof notebooks and hopeless optimism.  They map gut microbiomes, test coral neuropotentials, and inevitably lose half their kit down a swallow-hole.  Vord Kal tolerates them; fresh viewpoints, like fresh wind, are rare and therefore prized—so long as the pilgrims remember to wash their soles at every doorway.

6. Things that Outlive the City Itself

The First Pumps  
Iron, oak, and gutta-percha colossi six centuries old.  Their axles have turned so long the grain of the wood re-aligned under pressure until it mineralized; they are half fossil already.  Engineers say if every citizen vanished tomorrow the pumps would continue for fifty, maybe sixty years—rocks drip, wheels turn, and the plateau keeps its own counsel.

The Plateau  
Not alive, perhaps, but not indifferent.  Its crust flexes with each lost tonne of water, joints sigh, obsidian windows open.  Sometimes the cliff calves; sometimes a new vent appears in a child’s bedroom floor.  In either case the Kalites adapt, as they always have, by layering solution upon solution until the emergency itself becomes another storey of the city.

So: who lives here?  
A people whose lungs are wide as bellows and whose gods tick in units of piston strokes.  Slugs that mint pottery, eels that ride warm breath, corals that borrow lightning, stones that remember pressure.  Together they compose a vertical ecosystem where nothing is ever dry, silence is an exotic myth, and eternity is measured not in centuries but in the numberless, patient beats of machines lifting water toward a sky that will never let it pass.
