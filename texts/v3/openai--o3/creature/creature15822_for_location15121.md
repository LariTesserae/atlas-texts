Who lives here?

Begin at the scale of breath—at the size of what drifts, crawls, or pulses through a single lungful of the enclave’s resin-rimmed air—then move outward until the living weave that coats the basalt ridge comes clear.

1. The motes  
Every shaft of sunlight floating between the basalt spires is thick with oleovores: microbes whose cell walls are wax, not fat, and whose cytoplasm is more terpene than water. They ride thermals in loose skeins, amber-tinged dust no larger than pollen. What they eat is scent. The faint metallic sweetness you noticed on the climb is nothing more than their waste, oxidized in the heat. Breathe them in and they die instantly, too specialized for the wet dark of mammalian lungs; exhale and a thousand more replace them, born by binary division in mid-air.

2. The basal layer—film feeders and mirror lichen  
The basalt’s sheen is maintained by a living lacquer. A few hours after any shard is exposed, mirror lichen colonizes it: an ultrathin sheet of plastic-clear cells that drink radiation directly and store it as liquid resin beneath their skins. The sheet thickens toward evening, then fractures during the cold of night into glassy flakes that slide down crevices, delivering their hoarded oils to roots below. The entire ground crust is really last year’s lichen peel.

3. The glassoforms—primary architects  
From that lichen’s seep rise the structures you mistook for plants. Glassoforms are neither flora nor fauna but a third thing: hollow towers grown from stacked, hexagonal bladders. Each bladder is a pressurized lens of volatile oil clad in living quartz. By day the lenses focus light onto the photosynthetic core ribbon that spirals up the inside of every tower, a slow corkscrew of emerald tissue no thicker than a finger. The energy gathered here powers growth outward rather than upward, so whenever two towers touch they fuse, exchanging oils through capillary bridges. Over centuries the fusions knit into arcades, balconies, entire annexes—a city extruded rather than built. The ovals you walk through are self-reinforcing ogives: arches chosen not by intention but by the mathematics of surface tension in hot, dry air.

4. The siphon deer (Perdatus hexapodus)  
The dominant grazers are made of ankles. Six stilt-jointed legs keep their lacquered bellies well above the shard-littered ground, while the head—little more than a jointed proboscis—dangles below the belly like an ornate plumb bob. At each slow, ceremonial step the deer press a capillary bundle through lichen skin and sip until their internal reservoirs glow topaz. They have no eyes, only paired tympanic pits that map the enclave by resonance: each basalt alley returns a different hush. Their famous patience is not gentleness; startle one and it will simply lock its joints and silently starve rather than risk spilling a drop of its treasure.

5. The gliders—membrane philosophers  
Thumb-long, cathedral-ribbed, they spend most of their lives folded flat against the underside of overhangs with the exact shade and reflectivity of the basalt. When a shaft of sunlight strengthens beyond a threshold the glider awakens, splits its back seam and unfurls two chocolate-thin sails veined with ruby resin. No wingbeats follow: they fall, banking by flicking their ankle spurs against the air, steering toward vortices of fragrant vapor that rise wherever a grazer has fed. Their tongues are little mops; each landing is a ritual cleaning of a deer’s puncture wound, for a fee paid in residual resin. By night they refold and dream—literally. Electrochemical tracings show that while immobilized they run continuous, complex neural loops; no predator here is large enough to disturb them, so evolution promoted conjecture over vigilance. The locals joke that if intelligence ever evolves on this ridge, it will arrive first inside a sleeping glider’s skull.

6. The scarlet polyps—slow masters of layout  
Imagine a cathedral organ stop, carved from meat and sealed in glass, breathing once every hour. That is a scarlet polyp. Rooted at birth, they remain where their larval spore first found a crack. Two meters tall at full maturity, each polyp grows a crown of ciliated funnels that sip the air itself, teasing out trace aromatics produced by every other creature here. They do not think quickly, but they remember scents perfectly. When resin in their personal airspace drops below a preferred bouquet, they ooze an acid filament and mill a new corridor through the basalt, redirecting traffic and therefore aromas back past their crowns. Centuries of such olfactory urban planning have shaped the enclave’s entire floor plan. Visitors walking the alleys are quite literally touring a perfumer’s sense of beauty fossilized into stone.

7. The glass-wells and their guardians  
Deep where columns knot too tightly for sunlight to reach, the fused glassoforms create vaults of perpetual dusk filled with pools of almost-liquid resin kept warm by geothermal whispers. Around these wells live the one overt carnivore of the ridge: the curtain eel. It looks like an empty silk scarf until a grazer arrives, drawn by reflected scent. Then the scarf snaps taut, a mouth appears along its hem, and the deer is reduced to husk. The eel’s body is so thin it can pass through the slits between a visitor’s fingers; the only safe course is to keep one’s palms closed and, as the locals advise, “offer no surface bigger than a paragraph.”

8. The silent quorum—rooted intelligence  
Beneath all, laced through basalt itself, lies a web of carbon nanotube filaments extruded by the oldest glassoforms. Electrical pulses shuttle along them in rhythms too slow for mammalian time. Tap a column with your knuckle and, days later, another column half a meadow away will answer with a single chiming fracture. The network is not yet sapient, but it is self-aware enough to shunt growth resources away from areas that suffer repeated loud noises. In effect, the city edits itself around manners: speak softly and architecture will welcome you; shout, and future visitors will find the corridor you stood in has vanished, rerouted into a wall of newly fused glass.

9. Ephemeral migrants  
Once every thirty years, on the week the glassoforms bloom, a cloud of silver needles rises from the lowlands, rides thermals up the ridge, circles the enclave exactly seven times, and departs. They are the only creatures here that contain liquid water. Nobody knows why the drought-born ridge tolerates them, but each needle sheds a micron-thin rind of ice that sublimates before touching ground—just enough moisture to spur the bloom yet not enough to break the enclave’s law. Then they are gone, leaving residents drunk on perfume and pollen for a single incandescent night.

10. The human niche  
You do not live here, yet the enclave has made a space for the phenomenon called “visitor.” Glassoforms near the ridge edge produce benches of fluted amber wide enough for a human haunch; siphon deer learn within hours that your sweat is deadly salt, giving you a berth of respect; and the scarlet polyps hold still their acid threads, sensing that the moisture in your veins is poison to their streets. The place recognizes you as weather in slow motion: tolerable provided you pass, gentle provided you do not rain.

Leave and the oleovores will scour your footprints, mirror lichen will grow, crack, and slough, and within a single season even a tracker from your own species could not prove you were ever here. What remains is the hush, the warmth, the resin breathing in a loop older than memory, tended by creatures who have chosen, with exquisite unanimity, to build a city out of everything water can never touch.
