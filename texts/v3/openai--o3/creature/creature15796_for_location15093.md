INHABITANTS OF THE HANGING BASINS  

(The animals named in the brief appear again below, but unfolded into fuller lives.  Nothing here quite keeps to one kingdom; iron creeps into flesh, weather seeps into instinct, and everything has arranged itself around cold, dim light, sideways water, and the ceaseless pulse of the ground-engine.)

────────────────────────────────────────
0.  Invisible Majority  
Before the eye ever finds a moving shape it meets an air packed with spores, motes of rust, and nanoscopic droplets.  The spores belong to Sleeper-moss, the droplets to a bacterial colony the climbers call red breath.  Each water-grain carries two species:  

•  Ferrolytic cyanobacteria eat the iron blush up-welled by the engine and exhale oxygen that never escapes the basins—there is enough of them to keep the air fresh though no broadleaf stands here.  
•  Piezophytes: threadlike algae that only photosynthesise when compressed; every 1.4 seconds the ground shifts, pressure rises, and millions of them spark a chemical ‘heartbeat,’ turning the bowls of mist faintly green for a blink.  

This invisible majority feeds everything that follows, either directly or by layering a continual grime of biomass on every surface.  

────────────────────────────────────────
1.  Primary Makers  

1A.  LUMINWEFT  
Kingdom: Fungus–Metal hybrid  
Form: A sheet of mycelia braided with magnetite wires.  Seen at night it resembles spilled starlight, at noon a tarnished mirror.  
Life: Luminweft cannot fruit unless stimulated by alternating magnetic fields; it therefore sprawls over those seams where the iron pistons travel.  Each piston-stroke flips the fungus’ polarity, driving sugars into micro-bulbs that flare turquoise, then amber, then go dark—colour tied to sugar content, which tells grazer species where to feed.  
Thoughts: Luminweft propagates electrical murmurs through its wiring.  One patch a hectare in size holds more switching nodes than a city router.  Researchers who camp beside it report lucid, echo-rich dreams; no one is certain whose memories they wander through.  

1B.  GLASS-REED  
Already mentioned as translucent stalks.  Additions: every segment contains an unbroken water column.  When temperature drops below −2 °C the reed precipitates nano-plates of iron along its inner skin; each thaw raises those plates by capillary action so the reed “grows” armour instead of length.  After eight winters a mature clump can ring like tuned chimes when the wind is right.  

1C.  FROSTVINE  
Clarifications: root-knots reach a constant internal 17 °C.  Anything warm is hunted—so predators will tear the knots open before they bother with endothermic prey.  Thus frostvine’s defensive trick: the knots beat the engine’s 42-per-minute rhythm on hollow sclerids.  A listening chaser mistakes the plant for the unhuntable ground-heart and withdraws.  

────────────────────────────────────────
2.  The Motive Fauna  

2A.  PARCHMENT-WINGED LEAPER (vernacular: page bats)  
Size: palm-span.  Colour: brake-dust grey.  
New physiology: inside each chest sits a ring of cartilaginous turbine-blades.  In glide they hold their breath; on landing they exhale through the turbine, spinning it so fast that waste heat liquefies a paraffin sac.  On the next launch that molten paraffin is vaporised through wing pores, doubling lift in the first second—enough to clear the vertical rock just ahead.  
Social: roost in crevasse roofs.  Colonies carve the softest schist with repetitive strokes of their stiffened snouts, creating flute-shaped alcoves that amplify the monthly geyser roar; the colony sleeps easiest when the wider basin sounds like itself.  

2B.  HOLLOW-BONED RUMINANT (iron-cradle ibex)  
Adult shoulder: 60 cm.  Skeleton: struts of bone laminated with ferric phosphate—strong, light, receptive to magnetic fields.  
Behaviour: they map the basin by sensing where the ground-pistons tug on their own iron bones.  The matriarch leads the herd along lines of lowest draw; every two or three years those lines shift and with them the entire migratory circuit.  
Birth: kids are dropped inside abandoned frostvine root-hollows, warm enough that ultrasonic predators stay away.  By the time the kid can walk its coat has sprouted keratin scales quilted like roof-tiles.  

2C.  LONG-JAWED CHASER  
Imagine a weasel made of shadowed cable, forelimbs moustached with whiskers three times body length.  
Sensors: each whisker is a ferrofluid vein that stiffens when vibrated at breathing frequency; the animal “hears” solidity.  
Kill: strikes the thorax, then waits.  If victim’s heartbeat is above 80 bpm the chaser retreats because such speed usually belongs to the always-feared ground-engine.  
Special note: chasers despise resonance but covet warmth; thus they drag carcasses onto cold talus before feeding, keeping the feast from ringing.  

2D.  BASIN RINGGLIDER  
New species, apex flyer.  Wingspan two metres, body hollow as a reed.  It circles just inside basin walls on convection spirals raised by geothermal vents; wing-fringe feathers are strung with iron strings that sing subsonic, one note per feather.  Ibex herd together not from fear of the glider’s talons—they are too heavily plated—but to dampen the maddening chord that strobes across their magnetised skeletons.  

────────────────────────────────────────
3.  Subterraneans & Edge-Cases  

3A.  PRESSURE EEL  
Habitat: cisterns and the blind water-veins that interconnect basins.  
Shape: ribbon 1 m long.  Skin studded with calcite scales that flick outward when cistern pressure spikes, turning the eel into a living valve seat.  Entire shoals line a tunnel; when the spike comes they lock together, narrowing the bore, extending the geyser’s howl from twenty heartbeats to fifty—a symbiotic strategy: the longer the vent roars, the more iron-rich detritus rains back down for them to filter-feed.  

3B.  TALUS INCUBATOR (saprolithic mite)  
Smaller than a fingernail.  Females collect mica flakes, assemble a mirrored sphere, and lay a clutch inside.  The sphere drops among stones; sun glints through gaps once or twice a day, flash-heating the interior and letting embryos complete development in ten days instead of sixty—a rare advantage in perpetual dusk.  When the mite emerges it eats the mica, now softened by acid secreted during brooding.  

3C.  WRAITH MOTH  
Larva: herbivore on moss.  
Adult: lacks digestive tract, lives six hours.  Wings are two overlapping filters of iron-pollen; by vibrating them the moth can erase its own heat signature, moving invisibly through the chaser’s sense-world.  Courtship is nothing but temperature—the male warms his thorax by hammering hindwings together; the female answers with a mirror-cold shiver, and the pair mate where hot meets zero.  Their spent bodies ring when the first frost grips them.  

────────────────────────────────────────
4.  The Questionably Sentient  

4A.  THE WEAVER OF GATES (algorithmic lichen)  
Entity: a lichen–bacteria–iron filament mat that grows only around the lips of those vents which never fully seal.  
Behaviour: each mat builds identical patterns of hexagonal ridges whose dimensions scale with vent diameter: smallest two handspans, largest perhaps a boulevard wide.  After twenty or thirty years a ridge collapses inward, dropping slabs that fit against the vent wall like iris blades, narrowing the chimney to an aperture exactly 0.67 m across—always the same width.  No animal here uses that aperture.  No plant profits by it.  Yet basin after basin the weaver repeats the design, a hundred thousand silent, congruent gates for a visitor who has not arrived.  

4B.  THE ANDANTE (itinerant resonance)  
Not a creature in the strict sense but a migration of sound.  Some nights the ground-engine’s 42-beat enters phase with distant thunder rolling beyond the range.  The interference produces a traveling wave of silence thirteen seconds long that scours the plateau.  Where the wave passes, leaper roosts go barren, frostvine knots extinguish their glow, ibex calves are stillborn.  A week later life restarts—as though the basins were rebooted—suggesting the inhabitants carry a genetic switch that expects, even requires, this auditory purge.  Whether the andante is natural is debated by the few scholars hardy enough to visit twice.  

────────────────────────────────────────
5.  Echoes of Potential Humanity  

No permanent people inhabit the hanging basins, yet three human shapes persist:  

•  Frost-etched footprints that never fill.  They pursue no animal track, wander upslope into frostvine thickets, stop in the darkest grottos, then circle out again.  Tests show each print is rimmed by a hair-thin glaze of magnetite identical to the pistons below; perhaps the ground itself presses the silhouette of anyone who lingers too long.  

•  Cairns of armoured ibex plates stacked in Fibonacci spirals.  Locals of the lowlands swear they hear the cairns turning when fog is thick, as though re-tuning themselves to the ground-pulse.  

•  A single glass-reed pipe, cut, finger-holed, and wedged in a seam above the deepest cistern.  No lips have touched it in living memory; nevertheless the reed whistles a minor triad whenever geyser steam passes, completing a chord the basin seems relieved to voice.

LIFE IN ONE SENTENCE  
What lives here is a congregation of cold-forged, iron-haunted organisms whose every organ vibrates in dialogue with the hidden machine beneath, a choir of biologies tuned by darkness, drizzle and the metronome heartbeat of stone.
