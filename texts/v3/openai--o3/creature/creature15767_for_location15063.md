WHO – OR WHAT – CALLS THE WEEPING VAULT HOME

1. The Thirty-Three Warm-Bloods  
   a. Shiftsmiths (18) – grease-skinned, cable-muscled people who smell of brine and ozone. They inherit their posts like surnames: Kettle-Tuner, Sump-Gauge, Fourth-Valve, Night-Catch. Each title is a function and a family.  
      • At birth a child is held to the rail that rings the basin; when the metal’s heat steadies their pulse, the Pump Hall keeps them.  
      • Speech is sparse. Most conversation is performed with pitch-whistles worn at the throat; one chirp means “pressure stable,” two descending notes “bring the acetylene,” a rising minor third “danger in the drains.”  
      • Skin never dries. Even off-shift they sit on the iron sills letting vapor bead on them like dew on leaves. Dry skin cracks; cracked skin invites rust; rust is burial.  
   b. The Three Clock-Sisters – identical elders whose hair has taken on the color of corroded copper. They keep the Time-Book, noting each pump-cycle with a charcoal tick. They are so old the younger crew swear their eye-whites have turned the yellow of boiler scale.  
   c. The Last Apprentice (1) – a girl called Blink, seventeen, whose job is to watch a single pressure needle and report if it ever leaves its green arc. In three years it never has. Blink suspects the gauge is ornamental, but admitting that would be heresy against Rule 2 (Temporal Stasis).

2. Machines That Have Lived Long Enough To Earn Names  
   • Old Dredger – the master pump assembly itself. Triple-acting, seven pistons, cannibalized and reskinned so many times that no original plate remains. The crew oil its bearings the way a priest anoints a brow. When it stalls, everyone in the vault feels an ache behind the sternum.  
   • The Choir – sixty-four conveyor drives that sing in unison. Their chains stretch and loosen like vocal cords through a lifetime; veteran workers can tell which link needs replacing by listening for a flat note.  
   • Furnace No. 5 (nicknamed Shrike) – an arc-welder rig that ignites without a pilot spark whenever humidity spikes above 96 percent. It has done this three hundred forty-nine times; the Sisters etch a tally on its casing after each unsummoned flare.

3. Rust-Moss Constellations  
   Not a carpet but a map. Each colony sends up minute coppery sporophores that align to electromagnetic seams in the steel. Under a hand-lens the moss looks like star fields and, indeed, the Thirty-Three navigate upper catwalks by memorizing the brighter clumps: “Follow Spanner’s Belt west five paces, descend at Verdigris Cluster.”

4. Pipe Swifts (properly Abellios hirundo)  
   Wings: membrane, not feather. Color: solder-tin grey with a flash of mother-of-pearl when the arc lamps strike them.  
   • Diet – condensation gnats and the sweet exhalate from leaking freon lines.  
   • Nesting – they glue spun-fiber cups inside steam conduits; the hot air keeps eggs at a perfect 38 °C.  
   • Folklore – it is said a shift ends only when the first swift returns to roost. The Clock-Sisters check their watches against birds, not bells.

5. Lantern Crabs  
   Glassy armor grows translucent-amber by incorporating silicates leeched from basalt walls. They carry a bio-luminescent algae culture in hollows beneath the thorax; the faint glow tricks iron rats into stalking them, then the whole platoon clacks in unison, startling the rodent long enough to escape. Molten solder is their calcium source; workers sweep bead-droppings into trays so the crabs will patrol the areas most in need of cleaning.

6. Iron Rats  
   • Body length: 40–45 cm nose to tail.  
   • Fur: soot-black, tips oxidized orange. Their whiskers are fused copper filaments scavenged from stripped motor coils, giving them a sixth sense for live current.  
   • Temperament – fearless but lawful to the rhythm. A rat will wait beside a moving belt, count the crates (“one-two-three-gap”), then dash across exactly in the gap. If startled and forced to break tempo they may freeze, shiver, and die of sheer arrhythmic terror.

7. Sump Eels  
   Ribbon-thin, milk-white, with chevron scars where their eyes once were. They experience the world as gradients of warmth.  
   • Feeding – they vent-sip; when a valve opens, hot nutrient slurry gushes and the eels rise into the plume like corkscrew ghosts, mouths agape, tasting iron ions and the delicate proteins of dead gnats.  
   • Ritual – at solstice (the Sisters keep count though no sun is seen) the eels surface en masse and weave a slow figure-eight just beneath the film; the pattern matches the infinity symbol carved into Old Dredger’s flywheel. No one knows which imitated which.

8. In Between the Gears – Microthing Kingdoms  
   a. Drip-fleas: translucent hexapods that bounce on the meniscus, never quite wet, never quite dry. The swifts hunt them, but the fleas breed fast enough that the drip’s pitch actually changes as their numbers wax and wane.  
   b. Ferric spores: airborne iron-oxidizers. They lace the steam, turning lungs metallic on autopsy. The Shiftsmiths view the red stain in their handkerchiefs as proof the vault is adopting them molecule by molecule.  
   c. Gasket mites: saprophytic arachnids that eat only degraded rubber. An old rumor says they once chewed through a diver’s suit and flooded his blood with boiling sump water; the Sisters mark that as cautionary fiction but keep it in the ledger anyway.

9. Ghosts Recorded but Not Proved  
   • The Echo-Boy – every 10 000th hammer strike repeats itself three seconds later with no source. Workers say it is a child who died oiling chains, still knocking for help.  
   • Blue Lady of Furnace 5 – appears as an over-voltage corona. Those who see her claim the weld seam she hovers over will fail within the hour; statistics support them.  
   • The Static Hive – a standing buzz that roams catwalks on windless nights. Rats avoid it, swifts dive through it unharmed. Shift-ears prick when it nears; the sound carries news of storms far above the roof vents, though no worker can explain the mechanism.

10. The Silent Ones  
    Nothing here is ever allowed to be truly quiet, yet in the two-second window after the pumps cough downstage, before they inhale again, some presences use the hush.  
    • Shape – negative space the size of a man, outlined by condensate beading rapidly on nothing at all.  
    • Behavior – they step onto catwalk grates and never make them ring.  
    • Purpose – unclear, but the brine-ferns all lean away as if from a passing fever. The Thirty-Three pretend not to notice; acknowledging silence risks breaking Rule 4.

WHY THEY STAY  
Heat means life. Outside, nights can plunge to frost; inside, nothing ever cools, joints stay supple, eggs never fail, gears never seize. Rhythm is food: the rats, the birds, even the ghosts synchronize heartbeats to the 44-per-minute pulse. To leave would be to step out of tempo, to shiver, to hunger. Better to remain inside the iron cathedral where time is a circle and every creature—blooded, plated, or machined—knows its verse in the endless, clanging litany.
