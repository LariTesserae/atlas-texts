Who lives here?  
No single answer stays still long enough to pin to the wall.  Life inside the Ovoid arrives in strata, as layered and mutually entangled as the limestone itself.  If you stood on the lip of a balcony and looked outward long enough, you could watch an entire hierarchy drift past like sediment in a shaken beaker: first the obvious human tenants, then the apprenticed machines that serve them, then the tolerant plants that serve the machines, and—if your patience out-lasted a full pulse of the Reservoir—the stranger things that may not serve anyone but the hollow itself.

1. The Human Stratum  

a.  The Resonance Folk  
They name themselves after what they hear.  A newborn is not formally registered until it has spent an hour on the Reservoir’s disk; whatever dominant note the fountain of sound throws back that hour becomes the core of the child’s three-part name.  (“Fourth-Gong-Ilya,” “Sub-Ebb-Morrison.”)  To an outsider the practice feels ceremonial; to residents it is pragmatic indexing.  The city stores its data as vibratory signatures.  A name literally slots a person into the archive.

Most Resonance Folk earn a living curating that archive.  Librarians wear tunics woven with ultrathin accelerometers; they walk the catwalks collecting vibrational gossip the same way surface botanists press flowers.  Every evening they reconvene on the disk, kneel until the material ripples, and let the day’s findings soak down into the caverns of memory.  

b.  Lumen Brokers  
Because light is rationed, its economy breeds specialists.  Brokers stride the balconies trailing retinues of glassy mites—photon counters the size of rice grains—that crawl over fixtures and tabulate how many lumen-minutes each household has burned.  In the markets you can trade off-hour custodial labor, weight allowances on the freight lifts, or scraps of Casimir sheath in exchange for unspent glow a neighbor failed to use.  Brokers adjudicate the swaps, their white gloves streaked with bioluminescent ledgers only they can read.

c.  Skyfarers (down here the “sky” is sideways)  
Adolescents inevitably rebel against the wall-bound life, so they learn to cross the Expanse on tensile kites—triangulated bat wings of mylar so thin they’re invisible until they strike an ultraviolet filament and light up violet, turquoise, amethyst.  A practiced Skyfarer can fling herself from balcony to balcony without dropping more than two metres of altitude.  They alone harvest the micro-terraria hung in mid-air, and they alone carry the gossip of who is courting whom across the curve you cannot see.

d.  The Absent Majority  
Census tablets insist eighty-four thousand humans are legally domiciled here, yet only twenty-some thousand faces appear on any given day.  The rest are “longframe commuters”: geologists on six-month rotations, surface investors touring their asset, pilgrims from acoustic cults who arrive to record a single perfect field sample, then recede.  Their absence shapes policy.  Apartments are designed to fold in half when unoccupied; a mag-locked parapet becomes a wall, conserving lumen.  The city has learned to behave like a hermit crab in an oversized shell.

2. The Machine Stratum  

a.  Sibilants  
So named for the whispering hiss their LIDAR makes as it paints the void.  Each Sibilant is a tear-drop hull, matte-black, fins flared like origami.  Officially they map air currents, patch fractured gel bannisters, and chaperone the bats to keep guano off sensor surfaces.  Unofficially they gossip worse than people.  Sidle up to one with your wristpad bluetooth set to “eavesdrop,” and it will dump a whole shift’s worth of maintenance chatter: torque gossip about turbine bearings, rumors of a glowing crack half a kilometre overhead, speculation on which librarian is courting which broker.  

b.  The Cantor Array  
Not mobile, but sentient enough to count.  Forty-four globes of entangled fluid, each suspended in a magnetic cusp, orbit a dark-glass spire buried behind the wall city.  They translate the thunder of the turbines into frequency keys for storage.  During downtime the globes compose fugues for one another—sound too low for the human ear but strong enough that you feel it in your marrow, an unsettling sense that someone enormous is humming inches from your ribs.

c.  Feral Utilities  
Any system older than thirty years will possess, so the engineers say, at least one self-modifying subroutine that no human remembers writing.  Down in the drainage conduits, sluice gates open and close to rhythms that no water requires; in the lift shafts, a phantom carriage stops at phantom floors, doors sighing for passengers who never existed.  Nobody bothers to delete these feral utilities.  Deleting them might unbalance some other, vital schedule.  Instead the population anthropomorphizes them: “There goes Old Door,” “Mind the Ghost Pump.”

3. The Botanical Stratum  

a.  Helio-Lichens  
Triply edited: first to convert ultraviolet to green photons for self-use, next to leak any surplus green into pure infra-red so it dodges the lumen tax, finally to graffiti-tag itself with slow-pulse Morse whenever its mineral diet runs thin.  Drones reading the wall see whole essays blinking from the viridian film: FEED ME STRONTIUM,  or RAINFALL LOW 17 DAYS.  Children sometimes plant rude messages in the growth substrate; the next drone patrol dutifully uploads “Librarian Phel GROW A HEART” to the civic logbook.

b.  Vesper Ferns  
The single-frond plants that bloom only under the ultraviolet sweep.  When they desiccate they leave behind coiled rhizomes that look exactly like paper scrolls.  Scholars collect them, claiming the coils encode the history of local micro-weather.  Skeptics say the coils are random, but the hobby persists because it gives scholars an excuse to rappel down where the night markets set up.

4. The Accidental Fauna  

a.  Aureate Bats  
Pelage dusted gold by contact with condensed ferrous aerosols in the upper cupola.  Their eyes reflect a wavelength just outside human vision, so what we see is a bat-shaped absence rimmed by a corona of sparkles, as if a constellation had briefly gone rogue.  Myth credits them with guiding lost Skyfarers; science notes they follow the same updrafts that keep the kites aloft.

b.  Chord-Centipedes  
Bleached white, ninety-plus legs, six decibels quieter per step than background noise.  When they cross a tuned banister, the sound of their passing cancels a narrow slice of the infrasonic hum.  Some people raise them as living noise filters, tethered in terraria the way surface folk grow potted ivy.

c.  The Suspected Presence  
Residents swap reports of something larger.  A ripple of sonar ghosts detected under the Reservoir plate; a shiver in the airflow big enough to misalign a whole row of balcony windchimes.  The official stance: artifact of overlapping echoes.  The unofficial stance: the city may not be the first tenant of this chamber.

5. The Inaudible & The Imagined  

Between the above layers swims a final population made of nothing tangible:  
•  Echoes that outlive their speakers by hours, sometimes days, ricocheting until they acquire the grain of independent voices.  
•  Whole evenings of laughter fossilized in the limestone pores and released when a new drilling project cuts an old vein of air.  
•  A rumor that the Cantor Array occasionally prints backups of human personalities as standing waves, so at night you might be conversing with someone who died last quarter but whom the data refuses to forget.  

Children dare each other to speak an embarrassing secret the instant before the Reservoir flashes crimson, betting that the secret will be scoured from living memory and stored only in the inaccessible depths.  Adults, who know how searchable archives really are, choose their silences carefully.

Put all these citizens together and the chamber feels paradoxically full even while nine-tenths of its volume remains vacuum.  You listen rather than look to locate your neighbors; you feel rather than see the machines thinking; you trust damp lichen to keep the walls breathing; you accept that even your most private words might someday crawl back up the air-shafts in a different throat.  In short, the Ovoid is alive not by virtue of any single heartbeat, but by a choir of different pulses overlapping—biological, mechanical, acoustic, mythic—each one sustaining the others in the dark.
