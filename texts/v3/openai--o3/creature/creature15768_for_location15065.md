They call themselves, without embroidery, the Dwellers, as though no one else anywhere ever earned the word.  To outsiders—rare visitors dropped off by high-altitude freighters and just as quickly lifted away—they read at first glance as merely human.  Limbs, eyes, voices, tools: all present, all recognizable.  But the longer one spends inside the city of iron lattices, the more distance opens between the Dwellers and every water-blooded people elsewhere.

1. Anatomy

   • Height and Mass  
     Generations of thin air and scant gravity have stretched them tall.  Two metres is average; three is far from unheard of among the kiln-masters who rarely leave the furnace balconies.  What mass they carry is bone and cable muscle; fat is a fable like rivers.

   • Skin  
     Epidermis the colour of turned clay, filmed with microscopic plates of fused silicate.  A fingernail drawn across a forearm raises not dust but a faint glassy squeak.  Sweat glands exist but are inert vestiges; moisture loss in this climate would be fatal, so perspiration became evolutionary extravagance and was edged out.

   • Blood  
     Copper-rich, viscous, almost black.  It carries dissolved oxygen badly, so every Dweller bears under the ribs an auxiliary organ—a bellows sac—that rhythmically pumps the low, dry air across catalytic membranes.  A heartbeat is almost silent; the bellows click is not.

   • Senses  
     Vision is second class.  Pupils spread wide as coins, yet can’t collect what the sky withholds.  Instead they favour audition and thermoception: tiny ossicles behind each ear translate pressure engines, footfalls, even body heat into an inner architecture of location and distance.  In greetings they stand motionless, letting each study the other’s contour by the hummed chord of their own blood moving.

2. Social Machinery

   • Castes are acoustic rather than hereditary.  Children apprentice where their personal resonance fits:  
     – Sub-bass voices gravitate to Forge Choirs, whose chant modulates furnace draughts.  
     – Mid-registers enter Surveying, memorising street-tilt and power-pulse timings.  
     – Trebles become Tenders, caretakers of the pressure engines’ higher harmonics.  
     Speech itself is engineered music; a wrong pitch can stifle a boiler or overspin a turbine.  Language is grammar plus note.

   • Reproduction  
     They carry young, but gestation ends early.  At four months a fetus is transferred to a glass-walled creche submerged in warmed nutrient slurry—no water, only vaporised mineral solution.  Newborns crawl out when bones set hard enough to stand; first breath is taken under the open night.

   • Governance  
     No councils, no throne.  Timing sheets posted beside the grand manifolds declare when each engine must rest or wake.  Whoever writes the sheets—no one admits authorship—effectively rules.  The Dwellers accept it as the plateau accepts nothingness: unquestioned.

3. Sustenance and Craft

   • Food  
     Paste cultured from ground basalt, volatilised zinc, trace biopolymers.  Tastes of distant thunder.  They eat sparingly, supplementing with flakes of their own shed skin, rich in silica and micron metals—a closed loop physiology regards the body itself as a rechargeable resource.

   • Drink  
     Heat.  Blankets of radiance sluice off furnace throats; they stand beneath, jaws slack, letting subdermal capillaries bloom with borrowed calories.  A cooled Dweller wilts like an unstrung kite.

   • Tools  
     Everything hand-held is lanyarded: drop a spanner and the plateau keeps it for a hundred years.  To lose an implement is less a misdemeanor than an act of archeology against one’s descendants.

4. Myth and Memory

   • Water is religion.  They recite stories of a fluid that fell from above and touched everything, so abundant one could drown.  Children are taught the cautionary rhyme: “Liquid flows / Order slows / Hold the line / keep furnace-time.”  The moral is always that motion without purpose (like water) ruins edges and erodes intent.

   • The Second City  
     During the scheduled silences many sit with ears pressed to the glass ground.  Some claim to hear aeon-deep pistons, others choir-notes so slow a single syllable spans a lifetime.  Whether delusion or geological fact, the rumor disciplines them: if they grow careless, the elder engines below will surface to repossess the plateau.

5. Other Lives

   • Heat-Drinker Insects (informal: “Coppers”)  
     Beetle-sized, hexagonal wing-scales bright as hammered coins.  Internal thermovoltaic stacks let them metabolise infra-red directly.  They appear when chimneys flare crimson, surf the updrafts, then vanish into peripheral sheds where alloy dust pools knee-deep.  The Dwellers neither hunt nor worship them; they are, at most, proof that some covenant between life and furnace can be struck without intellect.

   • Pale Fringe Lichen  
     Not plant, not fungus, but a lacework of boron-silicate filaments crusting the windward faces of derelict cranes.  By day (a meaningless term, yet used) it folds flat, storing electromagnetic charge; in darkness it unfurls into ghostly fans that hiss faint static, a power bleed so gentle the Dwellers use it to tune ear sensibility in adolescence.

6. Life Cycle of a Night

   First Pulse: Engines wake, pressure hollows the streets, and every Dweller turns ear toward the nearest column for the signature choke that means optimal torque.  
   Bright Interval: Chimney sparks strike skin; work proceeds—grinding ore, casting pylons, sounding the glass for micro-fissures.  Sung commands skate kilometres.  
   Fall-Quiet: Maintenance.  Belts stop.  The plateau expands in the mind until each person feels a universe of black glass around a single heartbeat.  Some kneel and taste the silence, a ritual older than records.  
   Restart: A bellows cough from the grand manifold; city resumes identical to yesterday.  Comfort is defined as repeatability.

7. How They Will End

   They assume they will not.  Rust lacks leverage here; water, the master of decay elsewhere, is legend.  If entropy reaches them it will arrive as failing memory: the day no one remembers the correct harmonic for boiler re-ignition, the day the posted timing sheets are blank, the day the plateau hears, for the first and final time, its own natural silence unpunctured by engines or breath.  Until then the Dwellers live as though each spark reseals the night, and each straight line, though tilted, insists the universe can still be instructed to behave.

That is who occupies the slab of night in higher night: long-boned bellows-breathers, half-sung engineers, heat-drinkers who build cathedrals of certainty atop a world that long ago mislaid every softer element—and who, in the absence of dawn or rain, have learned to manufacture permanence out of nothing but rhythm, metal, and faith in repeating darkness.
