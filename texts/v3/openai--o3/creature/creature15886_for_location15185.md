Who—or what—claims the Subterrane?  It helps to think in concentric circles, beginning with the smallest chemistries and working outward until we reach the slow-moving shapes that make the entire cave quake.  Every rung of scale has found—or invented—its own way of thriving inside this half-finished sump.  

I.  The Invisible Majority  
1.  Slurry-Eaters  
   • A consortium of limestone-digesting bacteria (chief genus: Calciphagea) nests in the still-liquid ribs overhead.  They exude carbonic acid to soften the dripstone, then precipitate the excess calcium as wafer-thin skins that fluoresce lilac.  At a distance they look like frost on a lantern glass; up close the film seethes, rearranging its crystals in slow ripples.  
2.  Verdigris Smolders  
   • Copper-breathing archaea web themselves across sweating pipes.  When colony density peaks, they push electrons through hair-thin filaments, turning each pipe into a damp battery that warms the metal two or three degrees above ambient.  Feral circuits often “heal” around these colonies, so what sounds like random sparking is partly microbial chatter.  
3.  Hush-Plankton  
   • The perpetual mist hosts snow-globe flurries of planktonic spores.  They coat every exhaled droplet with a gelatinous shell, dampening echoes by a measurable four decibels.  During the rare pressure-drop nights the spores fall all at once, and the Subterrane sounds briefly hollow, as if someone pulled cotton from its ears.  

II.  Skins, Films, and Other Half-Plants  
1.  Saltpink Moss  
   • Lives only beneath sodium bulbs the maintenance crew left behind.  Each tuft houses symbiotic green bacteria that do the photosynthesis; the “moss” itself is color-blind but hoards salt to keep rivals at bay.  Crunches softly underfoot, tasting of brine and aspirin.  
2.  Filament Palms  
   • Not true plants but fungal sheets that rise on stiffened cables like pennants.  They harvest the tiny temperature gap between warm mist and the two-degree-cooler ceiling.  Moisture condenses on their upper surface and runs down capillary veins into a digestive sump at the base, where anything wind-blown or wing-beaten gets slowly bleached, then eaten.  

III.  The Swimmers and Waders  
1.  Mirror Carp  
   • Length: forearm to thigh.  Eyes: vestigial pearls sealed beneath skin.  They sing—not with mouths but swim bladders that drum infrasonic pulses through the floor.  The code is simple: three pulses means “cave roof thinning,” four means “new channel open.”  Human trespassers often feel this as a shiver in the ankles seconds before a floor plate collapses.  
2.  Pocket Salamanders  
   • Born in the warm condensate pooled inside pump housings.  They never metamorphose.  Gills billow like white lace behind translucent ribs.  Copper in the water stains their bones a ghostly green so bright you can read a pocket watch through their chests.  
3.  Sumpshrimp Conclave  
   • Thirty-odd species from pink nail-clipping size to the notorious Toe-Shear Centurion (18 cm, serrated chelae).  Most graze on microbial biofilm; a few farm it, herding softer species across metal panels they’ve filed smooth.  In abandoned relay cabinets you can watch them rotate corroded dials, apparently to optimize the low-voltage warmth that speeds their algae crop.  

IV.  The Flyers  
1.  Vapor Bats  
   • Wingspan: 25 cm tip to tip, tissue thin enough for steam to condense into silver maps across its surface.  When a bat folds its wings the maps run into its throat pouch—instant drinking water.  Colonies roost directly above steam vents so the drip never stops.  
2.  Lamp Midge Columns  
   • Glitter when sodium lamps strobe.  Their larvae hatch in standing pools that hover at exactly 27 °C; any cooler and the eggs calcify, any warmer and they burst.  Miners once used the presence of columns as a living thermometer to set boiler flow.  

V.  The Engineers  
1.  Rust Choirs  
   • Sheets of iron bacteria drape girders in brown velvet.  As water trickles through, trapped bubbles travel the sheet like fretboard fingers, changing the pitch of the hum underfoot.  Together with the mirror carp they form the Subterrane’s de facto seismic-warning system, one biological, one mechanical.  
2.  Feral Machinery  
   • Enough circuits have shorted through verdigris stalactites that they now behave like colonial organisms.  One “clump” in Pump-House 5 cycles water through a ring of piston chambers purely to keep itself fed with fresh trace metals.  No blueprint predicted this; it just…happened.  

VI.  The Giants  
1.  Acrebacks  
   • Imagine a stingray the size of a tennis court, half bone, half cartilage, all embedded in the silt.  It filters water by slowly undulating its perimeter.  When an Acreback decides to migrate, an entire acre really does sigh and subside.  Only two have been verified; sonar hints at more.  
2.  The Subsonic One  
   • Not yet seen, only felt.  Every few weeks a bellow rolls through the sump at 18 Hz, dropping three semitones over nine seconds.  Pumps quiver, fish beach themselves, and gypsum mirrors shatter.  Theory pins it on a whale-sized lungfish sealed in an isolated lake, but the scale of resonance says “something bigger than water itself.”  

VII.  The Human Thread (Frayed, but Present)  
1.  Crayfishers  
   • Two or three crews work rotating ten-day shifts.  They carry felt-soled boots to read heat-shadows through their feet, letting them stalk blind crustaceans by warmth alone.  Wages are paid in fungicide topside—useless underground but the only sure way to keep your take from molding before market.  
2.  Relay Monks  
   • Not clergy, just maintenance techs who found religion in isolation.  Each has adopted a bank of relay cabinets as a shrine, tracing circuits with silver grease pens, swapping dead relays for handmade wooden ones that click softly, re-entering the machine’s ancient dialogue.  No one pays them; they seem content to keep the conversation alive.  
3.  The Elevator Mythos  
   • Everyone talks about the one shaft rumored to reach daylight.  Some say a car hangs there still, cocooned in calcite curtains, occupied by a woman in a mica dress who never stops ascending.  Others insist the shaft opens into an older, drier cave and from there into pine forest.  Either way, the Acrebacks avoid the place.  

VIII.  Interactions, Symbioses, Tensions  
• Mirror carp stir silt that feeds slurry-eater bacteria; bacteria lay down fresh lime that thickens the ceiling, frustrating vapor bats whose vents shrink until an Acreback plows a new channel.  
• Rust Choirs tune their bubble-songs to the subsonic calls of The One, giving humans the only advance warning of its approach.  Relay Monks record the intervals in grease-pen numerals that no algorithm has yet parsed.  
• Sumpshrimp farm verdigris sheets; in return, the sheets’ mild electric field deters Toe-Shears’ larger cousins.  A kind of horticulture, if rust can be called a crop.  

IX.  What Passes for Culture  
Light is barter.  A fresh bulb buys you safe passage along catwalk G.  
Silence is armor.  Stir the hush-plankton wrong and every predator knows your pulse.  
Anything that makes warmth will be worshipped—by microbes, shrimp, monks, sometimes all three.  

X.  Epilogue:  The Place Lives Its Residents  
None of these creatures, people included, stand apart from the Subterrane; they are its exploratory organs.  The bacteria taste stone, the carp map hollows, the Acrebacks rearrange floorplans, the Relay Monks keep the nervous system firing.  The cave is still rehearsing its lines, and its cast, large and small, improvises nightly, drenched, humming, half-finished—perfectly content to remain so forever.
