WHO LIVES IN THE HOLLOW REACH

1. THE QUIET PEOPLE (who call themselves the Oruli)

Origin stories  
• The Oruli say they are “children of the second breath.” In their myth, a forgotten age of thunder cracked the ceiling of the world; wind flooded down, found no way out, and simply stayed. From that trapped breath flesh slowly condensed—first the nyx-hares, then the beetles, last of all the Oruli, shaped so that they would never need to raise their voices above the wind that birthed them.  
• Surface scholars guess they are an off-shoot of an upland culture that hid here during an ice age and never climbed back, but the Oruli shrug at that tale; it is thinner than selenite and twice as brittle.

Body  
• Skin: grey-white with a faint blue cast, the colour of limestone filmed by water. It darkens, not with sun, but with years—older adults are visibly “smokier,” shading toward charcoal at elbows and knees.  
• Height: lean and long-limbed, averaging 1 ½ heads taller than surface folk; long stride matters when streets are nothing but distance.  
• Hair: scant; most grow only a soft down at the crown that falls out by adolescence. Without need of eyebrows or lashes (there is little dust below) faces look startlingly open.  
• Eyes: wide and glossy. A silvery tapetum reflects even faint stone-light; catch an Oruli gaze side-on and it flashes like a mirrored pool. Pupils contract to slits near flame to protect the retina.  
• Ears: slightly fluted, canted forward; they hear a beetle carapace scrape rock from ten paces. Loud noises hurt. Babes are swaddled in felt to muffle the world until their ear-bones finish hardening.

Clothing  
• Untanned nyx-hare hide, translucent enough that patterns of muscle and scar show beneath—considered honest.  
• Where warmth matters: layered felt stitched from moth-wool.  
• Status shows in weight, not colour: cords of carved cave-whale ivory are worn draped over shoulders; the older and more polished the ivory, the deeper the respect.

Speech and Gesture  
• The spoken voice rarely rises above a murmur. Consonants are touched more than struck; standing too near an Oruli conversation is like eavesdropping on flowing water.  
• At a distance they sign: a fast, graceful hand-language called Lith, designed so that the selenite glow catches each motion.  
• Public announcements are delivered on “drum-slabs”: flat plates of hollow limestone rapped with padded mallets. The tone is felt through the soles first, then heard—stone as public address system.

Social layout  
• Household = one brazier, one cistern, any number of kin. When the fire dies the family dissolves; each carries away an ember to seed a new hearth.  
• Neighbourhoods form by acoustic compatibility: clans who match one another’s preferred “sound envelope” (some like a busy hush, others near-total stillness) cluster naturally.  
• Government is the Council of Tongues—twelve elders chosen as much for the beauty of their silence as for wisdom. They meet in the Mouth of the Reach. Debate is silent; votes are cast by releasing different-pitched stones down a spiral pipe that delivers the combined chord to listeners outside.

Food  
• Lash-fungus (“pepper cork”) roasted on flat stones.  
• Blind silver eel dried to paper-thin sheets that crackle like leaves.  
• Beetle-clutch custards, sweetened by condensed mineral salts that taste faintly of mint.  
• Water is drunk only sparingly; most moisture comes from “drift soup”—fog scooped in wide cloth nets at dawn, squeezed into bowls and warmed.

Craft  
• Stone-panels: split along natural grain, levered free with whale-bone wedges then polished by dragging them for hours behind teams of nyx-hares.  
• Selenite filigree: razor-thin splints set into tools so that each working stroke paints light for the next.  
• Salt-records: the city’s history glitters on overlapping disks, each a generation thick. Every new scribe abrades the plate’s face one grain down and writes anew; the older lines retreat into the crystal, legible only when back-lit—a strata of memory.

Belief  
• The Reach itself is a living elder called Trem, neither benevolent nor cruel. A creak is its knee; a roof-slip its shrug. Ritual greeting: lay a palm to any wall for the count of a slow breath, listen, then proceed—asking Trem’s permission to pass through its bones.  
• Seeds are messages from the surface gods. One found sprouting is transplanted into a “hope cradle” (a suspended bowl of lichen paste) and sung to nightly. When it finally withers, the color of the stem decides civic projects for the season: white means build, red means rest, black means repair.

Lifecycle  
• Children (“echoes”) are given broad freedom: sound lost in play cannot shame a family because echoing is the ceiling’s work.  
• At thirteen a child climbs alone to a ledge two hundred arm-spans above Cindermark, lights a fist-torch, and holds it for as long as they can bear the solitude. How far that flame carries along the selenite veins becomes their public name: a narrow glow births a “Near-Flame,” a vast reach births a “Far-Flame.”  
• Death is not buried but “returned to hush.” Bodies are coated in quick-setting lime and set upright in remote alcoves. The lime dries porous; air sighs through and produces faint, mourn-like notes—ancestral chimes reminding the living to move softly.

2. THE HALF-HEARD (creatures the Oruli respect, fear, or farm)

Nyx-Hares  
• Six legs; front pair used as hands when browsing.  
• Fur refracts light, so a torch seen through a hare shivers like a candle behind water.  
• Domesticated for sinew cord and for their uncanny ability to predict roof-slips minutes before the first crystal falls—entire blocks copy the posture of a suddenly-alert hare.

Mirror Beetles  
• Collect selenite shards, grind them with mandibles, then spit a cement that lines their nest tunnels. Result: kilometres of glittering ventilation shafts that re-route drafts through Cindermark.  
• In festival months the Oruli trade with the beetles: they leave coloured chalk, the beetles incorporate it, and weeks later the shafts glow in patterned murals—public art made by insect architects.

Air-Manta  
• Wingspan as wide as a fisher’s net, weight no more than a loaf of fungus bread.  
• Skin exudes microscopic prisms; when struck by shifting selenite light they flash prismatic and vanish against the ceiling glow.  
• Revered as messengers of Trem. If one crashes (rare, usually age-struck) its bones are shared equally among all households to grind into wish-powder that is blown into singer pots, adding new voices to the hollow song.

Deep Listeners  
• Lurk below the trough floor, felt more than seen—humanoid silhouettes visible through stone as though the limestone were glass.  
• They move when no other sound stirs, pressing ears to the bottom of pools, drinking silence like water.  
• Oruli ambassadors occasionally leave offerings: a scallop of lime-husk, a stopped jar of breath. In return, the Listeners have been known to shore up a wall or divert a flood without ever breaking the surface.  
• Some insist the Listeners are just mirror reflections of the Oruli caught in odd angles of the selenite veins. Others point to footprints—three-toed, twice as deep as any human tread—found after each major roof-slip.

3. VISITORS FROM ABOVE

Surface Traders  
• Eyes squinting, voices booming, carrying iron blades that slice rock but ring like struck bells—painfully loud.  
• They barter salt-records and beetle glass for dried fruits, copper pins, and—most coveted—woven silence cloth, a desert textile that deadens any noise brushed across it.  
• After a week below most traders whisper by instinct; after a month some never speak at full volume again, even under open sky.

The One-Time Expedition  
• Ten scholars decades ago came to map Hollow Reach acoustics. They built brass echo-tubes; the tubes disintegrated, their note-books mouldered, yet three of the scholars remain. They married into Oruli families, claiming they have been “collecting data” ever since.  
• Their descendants occasionally reinvent surface innovations: one restarted paper-making out of beetle-veined pulp, another fashions spectacles from double-polished quartz disks scavenged from singer pots.

4. THE SILENCE ITSELF

Not mere absence—entity. It thickens when strangers grow agitated, thins to a benign hush when songs are honest. An Oruli proverb says, “Speak lies and the hush will spiral off your tongue like smoke into cold air.” Children test this by telling small fibs; a liar’s whisper, they swear, leaves a visible distortion, a heat-shimmer, hanging where a truthful word would sink and be swallowed.

Scholars debate whether the Silence is Trem’s breath, a colony of sub-audible organisms, or collective mindfulness hardened into phenomenon. The practical rule stays the same: respect it as the oldest citizen. No door slams in Cindermark; no iron is hammered unwrapped; no laughter is forced. Those are taboos against offending the host that shelters every beating heart.

IF YOU MEET THEM

• Approach with your torch lowered; the wall veins will relay enough light for faces.  
• Announce yourself by brushing two fingers down the nearest stone—permission, not possession.  
• Expect a pause before greeting; they are measuring your echo-width, deciding how far to stand so your voice lands kindly.  
• Gift choices: a sharp colour (crimson cloth dazzles), a dry seed (potential), or a sealed vial of thunder-rain (sound captured inside water—precious wonder).  
• Part in silence. Words linger for you, not them; what matters is the hush you leave behind, unbruised.

Thus the Hollow Reach is inhabited: by lean silver-eyed Oruli who trade in stone-carried light; by translucent hares and cartographer beetles; by manta that write the air in prisms and by figures in the bedrock that may be neighbours or nightmares; and above, by a silence so alive it hosts them all like coral hosting a reef—patient, porous, endlessly listening.
