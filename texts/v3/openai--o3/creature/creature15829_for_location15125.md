What lives here never thrived in sunlight, never learned the slow patience of forests or the elaborate strategies of coral reefs.  It is—almost without exception—small, quick-cycling, provisional life, the kind that can afford to gamble everything on the next quarter hour because the chamber itself may not last a day in its present shape.  

Below is a natural history of the pocket, moving from the smallest entities upward.  Think of each rung as a brief, heat-bleached niche in a ladder that is forever being kicked out from underneath its climbers.

1.  The Filament Cloud  
Strictly speaking the photonic mist is technology, not biology, yet for every practical purpose it behaves like a living skin.  Each filament is a doped-silica wire two microns long, electrostatically barbed at both ends.  When two barbs meet they snap together, then drift apart again if a passing ion current is strong enough to pry them loose.  The whole population drifts in barely perceptible convection cells, self-mixing, self-repairing, and very slowly self-replicating by accreting new silica from the steam.  The filaments cannot evolve (their design is hard-coded), but they do sort themselves: cooler zones see denser nets because barbs stay locked longer at lower temperatures, so the light naturally pools where illumination is most needed.  In that sense the cloud is the pocket’s first inhabitant—an engineered photoplankton whose only food is waste heat.

2.  Oxide Film (“Rust-Lace”)  
On the stone itself grows a bacterial consortium the color of dried blood.  Iron-oxidizers make up the bulk, stitching together hexagonal sheets so thin they look like lace pressed flat against the basalt.  Interwoven are colorless sulfate-reducers that pocket hydrogen in nano-vesicles; when a sudden heat pulse hits, those vesicles vent and pop, lifting the film an angstrom or two and wrinkling it—an expansion that cracks rock dust into micron-sized nutritive grit.  Whole patches live for three or four resets of the micro-climate—call it thirty hours—before being scalded off and replaced by daughter colonies upstream.  At any moment scarcely a square metre of the chamber is actually productive, yet that is enough: metabolism here is slow, measured in picograms of fixed carbon per minute.

3.  Threadworms (“Glass Nematodes”)  
Where the oxide film beads with condensate, translucent worms about two centimetres long skate along on ventral cilia.  Each carries a single droplet of water along its back like a living blister; the droplet both cools its tissue and serves as a solvent for rock dust and bacterial slush, which the worm sips through a capillary slit at its nape.  Their musculature is ribboned with nano-silica strands stolen from the mist, giving the bodies a faint mother-of-pearl sheen and a tensile strength far beyond that of collagen alone.  Temperature spikes make them furl into perfect toroids—a defensive posture that exposes minimal surface—and in that state they can ride out brief sixty-degree Celsius surges that would cook ordinary proteins.  Life expectancy: five days if no beetle finds them first.

4.  Hexa-Beetles (“Tectonic Hunters”)  
The loudest resident is a six-sided beetle built like a beveled gemstone.  Each of its twenty-four legs ends in a claw that rings when it strikes glassy stone, and because the beetle’s gait is stately—the legs rise and fall one pair at a time—the resulting sound is a deliberate, almost musical scale: tik-tik-tik, pause, tik-tik-tik.  Its carapace is rust-red iron phosphate armoured with subdermal quartz chips; a blow strong enough to crack the shell usually shatters the attacker’s weapon first.  The beetle hunts by vibration: it listens for the micro-scrape of worm cilia, triangulates, then pounces in a single spring-loaded hop.  Mandibles close, inject a slurry of proteases and chelators, and the prey dissolves from the inside out.  In lean hours the beetle switches to a scavenger’s role, scoring oxide film off rock with the same mandibles; the film is poor fare, but the beetle’s gut flora can spin iron oxide into usable ATP when meat is scarce.  Adults live perhaps one planetary month.  Females cement their egg-cases to the cool underside of basalt shelves; most shelves shear away before hatching, dosing the pocket with a rain of doomed embryos that nonetheless feed the film.

5.  The Plate-Sleeper  
Rarest of all—and arguably the chamber’s only macroscopic animal—is a creature the size and vague shape of a sea cucumber, though you will likely never see more than a glimpse of it.  Locals (few as they are) call it the Plate-Sleeper because it spends ninety-eight percent of its existence wedged between adjacent basalt plates, body flattened to a sheet only a centimetre thick.  Its hide is keratin mixed with basalt dust; to the casual eye it is just another seam of rock.  What little is known:

•  It is endothermic, but only episodically.  Normally it lets itself match ambient temperature; when a pulse of superheated water ruptures nearby, it metabolises stored sulfur and fat in a violent twenty-minute burn that propels blood (copper-based, bright green) through a fractal network of heat-sink vessels.  In effect it momentarily becomes colder than its surroundings, stealing heat to protect its organs while plates grind and shift.  

•  Instead of eyes it has rows of piezoelectric whiskers along the flanks; they read the pressure front of an approaching tremor and tell the animal which adjoining fracture will open next.  More than once observers have watched a basalt knife edge swing free to reveal the Sleeper sliding away just ahead of the break, as fluid and silent as a shadow.

•  Diet is assumed to be beetles, worms, and the oxide film scraped en masse with a rasping radula.  No one has caught it feeding.

•  Reproduction is unknown.  The prevailing guess: it buds.  Now and again, a hand-sized “scale” flakes from a Sleeper’s passing tail, crinkles on the hot floor, and over the course of days curls into a miniature of the parent before disappearing back into some unseen crevice.

There may be a dozen Plate-Sleepers total in the pocket system, or only one that makes its rounds—I can give you neither census nor certainty.  What is clear is that its passing leaves the rock subtly polished, as though the chamber has been licked clean by something that understands every contour of the stone better than stone understands itself.

6.  Ghost Colonists (Honourable Mention)  
Once in a rare while a human or other large intelligence bivouacs here—spelunker, geothermal technician, monk of heat.  They never last more than a few sleeps; hydration slips, cell proteins falter, and the noise-eating walls make solitude pressurized.  Yet traces of them remain: a half-melted climbing spike seams into the basalt, a coil of copper that turns verdigris, a single spoken word still hanging in your inner ear because the cavern refused to echo it back.  These ghosts are not residents, but the pocket remembers them the way cooling glass remembers the last tool to touch its surface.

Patterns, Not Populations  
Life here is too heat-pinched and space-poor to form ecosystems in the usual sense.  Think instead of momentary alignments:

•  A plate shears; a gush of boiling water flash-sterilises rock.  
•  Oxide film recolonises within an hour.  
•  Worms follow the film; beetles follow the worms.  
•  In the new gap, a Plate-Sleeper slips in, scours the lot, and then lies still.  
•  Meanwhile the filament cloud above heals the breach in its own lattice, brightens, and the cycle begins again.

Nothing achieves permanence, but everything achieves recurrence.  To live here is not to endure; it is simply to return at the next right moment, wearing whatever form can be assembled quickly from heat, water, and stone.  The pocket allows no lineage to sprawl unchecked, yet it forbids utter vacancy.  In that narrow, fiercely lit interval between scalding and cooling, the inhabitants bloom—red lace, glass worms, ringing beetles, whisper-flat sleepers—and then withdraw, leaving the chamber poised, clean, expectant for its next creation.
