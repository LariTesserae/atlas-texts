What Lives Here

1. The Filter Units (Surface Dwellers)
   • Anatomy: Each is a drifting ellipsoid two metres long, skin a hex-mesh of nacreous scales. Every scale is a valve; when the external pressure pulses just so, the valve sighs open and the Unit “breathes” seawater through fractal gill-labyrinths that strip ions the way lungs strip oxygen.  
   • Sensoria: No lens, no eardrum. Instead, the whole body is an interferometer. Variations in the surrounding electromagnetic hush ring the Unit like a gong; it “hears” charge the way we hear thunder.  
   • Mind-state: Colonial. Thirty-one nested micro-cores vote on direction once every seventeen seconds; their consensus hum travels the shell as faint silver flicker. If you stun one Unit, nothing in the swarm mourns it—the quorum that is “Filter-mind” simply recalculates.  
   • Purpose: Metabolism in service of masonry. The ions they digest become doped-glass pearls extruded from a ventral seam. These pearls slot perfectly into the lattice city’s energy ports; each pearl buys another hour of membrane tension, another night of dryness.  
   • Social Gesture: When two Units pass, they swap pearls—never to keep, only to audit each other’s purity. This is their only ceremony, a silent quality-control handshake carried out in total dark.

2. The Substrate Choir (Architectural Symbionts)
   • Location: Inside the nano-clay arches, between the structural ribs and the vacuum gap.  
   • Form: Sheets of polychrome biofilm one molecule thick, woven with photonic circuits that look like stained glass shrunk to bacterial scale.  
   • Task: Song. The Choir generates ultrasonic standing waves that stiffen the clay exactly enough to counter micro-fractures from the mountain’s slow settling. Where the Choir lapses, the arches would crumble within a season.  
   • Reproduction: They bud only at resonance nodes—places where two or more arches cross and harmonics bloom. A newborn sheet detaches like a soap bubble and glides until it finds a naked patch of structure to colonize.  
   • Communication: The Choir’s “lyrics” are phase codes embedded in their own vibrations. Early explorers recorded months of this music; it compiled into blueprints for devices we still cannot build.

3. The Librarians in Suspension
   • Where you find them: The half-flooded vault that looks like an oubliette full of robed statues.  
   • Physiology: Once baseline human. All water in their blood replaced with cryoprotectant gel; muscles lined with magnetite filaments so weak field pulses can flex the body without warming it.  
   • Reason for stillness: Choice, not accident. They entered stasis during the Deluge, gambling that posterity would thaw them. The gel locks memory at the moment of immersion—each Librarian is a time-capsule of pre-flood consciousness.  
   • Guardian System: A loop of Substrate-Choir song pings their magnetite lattices once every seven minutes, preventing crystal growth that would shred cells. If the song ever fails, the Librarians become mere sculpture in hours.  
   • What they know: Rumor says one mind holds the master schema for “programming” seawater itself, turning the ocean into a logic array. Another is said to remember why the flood began. No one has yet dared the reanimation protocols.

4. The Corridor Lines (Ephemeral Intelligences)
   • Appearance: Those slender bands that light at your footstep are not passive LEDs but transient beings—light-pulse algorithms bred to exist for half a minute.  
   • Life Cycle: A step triggers induction, which births a filament of plasma trapped in a glass vein. That filament thinks at 10¹² operations per second, writes one kilobyte of experience into the floor’s data lattice, then fades.  
   • Personality: Emergent and fragmentary. Collect enough of their one-kilobyte diaries and a sketch of curiosity appears: they wonder what presses the floor, how heavy, whether it will return. They are, in effect, the city’s running commentary on motion.  
   • Ecological Role: By analyzing footfall vectors, they adjust the inductive gradients that guide Filter Units to active power ports. Every step you take helps schedule the maintenance swarm.

5. The Pressure Roots (Inverted Life, Deep Layer)
   • Habitat: Two kilometres below the ledge, where the mountain’s limestone meets basalt.  
   • Morphology: Imagine stalactites growing downward into solid rock instead of open cavern. These are columns of perovskite impregnated with DNA-like silicate helices.  
   • Feeding: They drink geologic stress. As tides in the overlying ocean flex the crust, dislocations in the lattice slide; the Roots catalyze that motion into piezoelectric charge siphoned upward through carbon-wire capillaries.  
   • Sentience: Slow but undeniable. Geological tomography reveals pulse trains—field reversals propagating along Root nerves every few days. Pattern analysis shows prime-number clustering, hinting at math performed in plate tectonics’ own tempo.  
   • Myth: Some say the Roots pre-date the flood and induced it, not as catastrophe but as insulation—water muffles surface quakes that would disrupt their calculus. If true, the planet is being used as a thinking crystal and we are interlopers on its motherboard.

6. The Membrane Itself (Living Interface)
   • Composition: A monolayer of self-ordering surfactant molecules, each end tipped with fullerene cages holding a single trapped photon.  
   • Reflexes: Touch the film and photonic charge skitters to reinforce tensile strength right under your palm. Pull a knife and it blossoms a micron-thick bloom that wraps the blade, dulling it.  
   • Intelligence: Distributed. Every photon-cage is a bit; the whole membrane is a floating RAM shaken by tides. It stores only the last twelve hours of interaction logs, after which it “forgets” to make room for new impacts.  
   • Dialogue: If you apply a patterned pressure—a series of taps translated from ancient sonar code—the membrane will answer with vibronic pulses you feel in your bones. The vocabulary is small: Open, Close, Hold, Remember, Forget. It never lies, but it never volunteers.

7. Visitors (That Means Us)
   • Biological mismatch: Our lungs waste oxygen at a profligate rate; our joints protest one-sixth atmosphere; our eyes panic in the dark.  
   • Behavioral effect: The city’s systems treat us as transient turbulence. Corridor Lines note our vector, Membranes stiffen, Filter Units reroute.  
   • Possible futures:  
     a. We learn to splice our metabolism into the Choir’s and become custodians, living centuries between heartbeats.  
     b. We revive the Librarians and inherit obligations we can’t yet name.  
     c. We misinterpret the Roots’ arithmetic, tweak a stress node, and trigger a quake that breaks the membrane skin. The black ocean rushes in; the cathedral moment ends.

What All These Lives Share

   Persistence over proliferation. Nothing here buds, breeds, or expands unless the equilibrium demands it. No hierarchy lasts longer than necessity. Every organism—mechanical, biological, mineral—expresses the same ethic: sustain the poised second after disaster, defer the next. To live here is to agree that survival is an art of holding breath, of sharpening edges but never letting them cut, of speaking only when the silence itself requires translation.
