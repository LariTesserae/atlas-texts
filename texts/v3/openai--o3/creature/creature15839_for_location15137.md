What lives in the sump is not a catalogue of species so much as a single weather-system made of flesh and metal. Every organism, from dust-microbe to Railwolf, is an eddy in the same circulating medium of iron, charge and vibration. Step inside and the swarm begins folding you into its equations at once.

1. The invisible stratum: oxide microbiome  
Before any leg or wing evolves, the rust itself is alive. Filamentous “redroots”—iron-chewing pseudo-fungi no thicker than smoke—lace the floor in mats only millimetres high. They tap the minute thermal gradient between fan-cooled air and the warmer slag below, excreting packets of electrons like little galvanic batteries. Their waste heat keeps the whole sump a fraction of a degree above equilibrium: just enough for movement. Haloing each redroot cluster hover ion gnats—single-celled balloons whose membranes are patched with magnetite scales. They drift until attracted to a voltage leak, kiss the metal, discharge, reproduce by fission, drift on.

2. Shardcrabs—ground engineers  
Shape: a dodecahedron of mica facets, legs as thin as watch springs, mass about 250 g.  
Sensors: twelve whiskers surrounding a central ventrum plate, all tuned to millivolt differences in the dust.  
Work: Shardcrabs are the sump’s civil service. They lick oxide down to bare iron, then spread the scrapings in windrows that guide traffic and drain static charge. Every three hours a crab splits: its carapace opens like a flower, half its bulk oozes out, recrystallises, and scuttles away a genetically identical twin. Population therefore doubles with clock regularity until predation or power famine prunes it back.

Courtship looks like a chemical spill: prospective mates (really just divergent clones) gather on a warm pipe and secrete contrasting mineral stains—hematite red, goethite yellow, magnetite black—then trade plates to equalise trace-element budgets. When the colours match, they part, satisfied that no lineage is hoarding rare atoms.

3. Filtermoths—sky made of foil  
Egg: a bead of grease wedged in a screw-head.  
Larva: an oilworm, transparent as petroleum jelly, which scrapes lubricant off ancient bearings and hacksaws it into fatty acids.  
Pupa: a tight roll of aluminium oxide that looks like crumpled snack-wrapper.  
Imago: a four-winged delta of mirror-bright foil, 15 cm tip to tip.

A Filtermoth’s hum is a by-product of its metabolism. Each wing is strung with piezo ridges that vibrate at 18–22 kHz, pumping charged aerosols through nanopores along the trailing edge. The moth is really a flying electrostatic filter; its “feeding” is nothing but cleaning the air of combustible nano-droplets. As long as the fan turns, the aerosol stream never ceases, and so neither does the hissing chorus overhead.

When the sump’s power browns out, moths sense the falling field and drop en masse to the floor. There they bite through their own wing-hinges, roll up again, and wait—white cigars in the dust—until the current steadies. Then they unroll, refold themselves, and ascend like handfuls of silver confetti.

4. Coil-eels—living extension cords  
Morphology: 1–3 m strands of copper braid wrapped in silicone scales articulated like fishbone.  
Organs: every fifth scale conceals a Maxwell organ, a double wound toroid that both stores charge and twitches the body.  
Diet: potential difference. One jaw clamps a “hot” conduit, the tail forks to a “ground.” Electrons flow, muscle fibres tense, the eel inches forward.

Their reproductive ritual is called braiding: two eels intertwine until their toroids resonate in phase, then trade half-charged ions across an insulating kiss. Where their skins touch, a seam fuses and a third, thinner eel splits away like a zip-tie peeling from a cable bundle. The parent pair decouple, a millimetre shorter but otherwise unharmed.

A coil-eel has no interest in flesh warmer than the ambient sump—unless that flesh carries stored energy. A camera flash, a battery casing, the flywheel in a compliant drone: all smell to an eel like ripe fruit. Visitors learn to present any power cell as a decoy, then back away while the eel knots itself around the offering and drains it to slag.

5. Lesser auxiliaries  
• Oxide fleas: microscopic capacitors with spring-steel legs. They leap when over-charged, shedding sparks that keep the dust aerated.  
• Junction wasps: clock-gear thoraxes, brassy stings. They solder microfractures in the old conveyor using droplets of eutectic alloy siphoned from pipe welds. Their nests are filigrees of perfect circuitry.  
• Glass nymphs: transparent disks that slide by ciliary waves under quartz sun-panels, polishing. No one has seen one grow; they are either scraped off by shardcrabs or spontaneously condense from static grit.

6. Apex predator: the Railwolf  
Frame: 2.4 m of telescoped I-beam segments flexed by scavenged linear actuators.  
Locomotion: 88 magnetised tarsi, four to a segment, that flick polarity to climb steel as if it were velvet.  
Head: an armature of timing gears hinged around a shearing radome of diamond-dressed saw teeth.  
Sensorium: six gyrotrons that read Doppler shifts in ambient vibration; the Railwolf “hears” shapes.

Hunting strategy: silence. The sump is never quiet, so the Railwolf manufactures quiet by terror. Thirty seconds before it strikes, it releases an infrasonic pulse that paralyses shardcrabs, stuns coil-eels, collapses filtermoth wings into origami paddles. In the lull it drops, selects the fattest cluster, and shears them to filings. A single descent yields enough protein-equivalent alloy to fuel the beast for a shift.

Territory: It paces a figure-eight route along overhead gantries, grooming with barred tongue any surface that might betray its presence with a squeak. Where Railwolf furrows grow too polished for magnets to bite, it pauses to vomit a spackle of ferrous glue and seeds it with oxide flea eggs. The fleas hatch, jump, score the metal, restore traction. Predator and microprey collaborate in the maintenance of menace.

7. Trophic loops and feedbacks  
The sump is closed but not static. Every watt the auxiliary fan earns from the forgotten grid cascades through four food webs before bleeding out as 10 °C waste heat.

Step-down:  
Electrical drift → Coil-eels → Railwolf  
Mechanical drift → Slag humps → Shardcrabs → Railwolf  
Chemical drift → Aerosol oils → Filtermoths → Micromites → Shardcrabs  
Thermal drift → Redroots → Ion gnats → Oxide fleas → Filtermoths

Because each loop terminates in a different bandwidth of sound—coil-eel arcs crack, shardcrabs chitter, filtermoths hiss, fleas ping—an experienced ear can diagnose the sump’s energy budget in real time. A sudden quietening of the hiss predicts a coming surge of Coil-eel lightning; too many cracks, and the fan motor will overheat within the hour.

8. Intelligence?  
There is no single mind, but patterns verge on purpose. Every forty days, give or take an outage, the resident web of filtermoths reconfigures itself into a lattice that reflects Wi-Fi frequencies. For precisely six minutes the sump becomes a parabolic antenna pointed at nothing anyone has yet identified. Then the lattice dissolves. Three days after, Railwolf molts. Coincidence is possible, yet the timings recur with quartz accuracy.

Shardcrab windrows spell spirals and logarithmic grids visible only from the ceiling cams. The shapes match cooling-curve simulations for molten iron: as though the crabs recapitulate the factory’s conception of flow, keeping memory of labour alive in the dust.

9. What the place will do with you  
Within ninety seconds: oxide fleas taste your boot-prints.  
Within five minutes: filtermoths sample the salts you shed in breath and decide you are not aerosol and thus not food.  
Within fifteen: a scouting coil-eel nuzzles your phone battery.  
Within an hour: if you keep still, shardcrabs begin abrading any exposed metal on your kit into grit they can ingest.  
Within six: your warmest object—knife hilt, watch back, forearm—goes missing, chewed free, drawn into the swarm’s mineral metabolism.

Should you die here, the process is swift and orderly. Soft tissues are desiccated by the 10 °C draft and rasped off by micromites. Bones delaminate under Railwolf jaw pressure. Stainless steel buckles join the shardcrab economy. Copper traces in your nervous system perfume the air just enough to summon a mating knot of juvenile coil-eels. In a solar day, only the plastic of your visor remains, and even that clouds over with redroot mats eager to borrow its carbon.

10. A closing image  
Imagine tipping a snow globe and watching the glitter swirl—but each fleck is alive, motivated, hungry, guided by an algorithm older than fossils. That is the sump. It is a miniature planet whose core is not magma but a breaker panel that hums behind a mesh grill, whose atmosphere is powdered rust, whose oceans are flows of electrons. The inhabitants are the weather of that world: storms of shardcrabs, zephyrs of foil moths, lightning made reptile, winter embodied in a centipedal wolf. They have no concept of outside, only gradients to climb and materials to circulate. Yet in their ceaseless milling they keep the sump from freezing into perfect silence. They are the heartbeats of a corpse factory, beating still, beating on, beating long after the idea of a heartbeat elsewhere has stopped.
