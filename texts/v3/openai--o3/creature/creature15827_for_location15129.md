At first glance—an unhurried, lantern-lit glance that floats over moss and machinery together—it seems that nothing truly lives here, not in the heartbeat-and-eyelid sense we are trained to recognise. But remain long enough for your pupils to widen and for the vault’s own pulse to braid with yours, and something begins to announce itself. It is not a single organism; it is an ecology folded so tightly around a handful of compulsions that it behaves like a solitary, dreaming animal.

1. The Green Skin  
Every leaf, root, tendril and rhizome belongs to one continuous body. Botany would catalog dozens of species, but taxonomy frays in the humidity: vascular tissue has grafted across families, sap flows where it wishes, and plasmodesmata from fig braid with ivy and bean until “individual” ceases to apply. The vault’s vegetation functions as a single integument.

• Primary sense: pressure. The rhizome-moss carpeting the floor is filamented with statoliths—microscopic stone-grains that shift when weight settles. They relay their tilt through electrical pulses that travel root-to-root faster than a person can walk.  
• Secondary sense: heat. The leaf-thickened nodules at conduit junctions dilate and constrict around copper coils. A one-degree rise in transformer temperature shivers through the canopy like wind.  
• Reflex: When a new warmth lingers (a human body, say) adjacent roots swell with water, thickening just enough to reposition the moss so drainage plates stay closed. Visitors find a mysteriously drier footing; the plant will not waste liquid it can drink.

2. The Iron Skeleton  
If the plants are skin, the pumps and separators are bone and marrow. Their housings have rusted lace-thin in places, letting hair-roots wind into gearboxes where they sip lubricant and exude biofilms that retard further corrosion. The machinery gains a kind of cartilage: flexible, self-renewing, scented faintly of leaf-litter.

The reciprocating pistons write a metronome the plants obey. Experiments (accidental, the last time power flickered topside) show that when the rhythm stalls the canopy slumps, stomata closing as if the lungs of the place refuse to breathe uncertainty. Restore the tempo, and the vault exhales again.

3. The Blood  
Water is obvious blood, but trace metals are the iron in that blood. When the centrifugal separators cone rust into crimson dunes, hyphae hurry to mine the heap. They shuttle ferric ions upward where chloroplasts fold them into the machinery of light. What overflows is sequestered in bead-smooth cysts that cling like barnacles to fig bark—a ferrous pantry for leaner decades.

4. The Mind—If Mind Is the Word  
No neuron fires, yet decisions happen.

• Growth corridors remain clear of the spiral stair’s weekly unlock zone. The moment its cogwork clicks, vines elsewhere tighten, as though the organism recoils from a draft remembered from a time when inspectors still came.  
• Sensor lenses, once bitten by exploratory tendrils, are avoided henceforth. The plant forgets nothing that stings.  
• Once every nineteen hours the canopy initiates a ripple of turgor collapse, dropping ten thousand spent leaves in near silence. The leaves land already half-desiccated, shaped into gutters that shepherd runoff toward thirsty trunks. It is pruning, composting, and irrigation schedule combined.

Call it instinct, call it algorithm, call it a slow vegetal sentience: the vault behaves as though it can imagine futures and chooses among them.

5. The Voices Within the Voice  
Fauna “rating almost zero” is a bureaucrat’s shorthand. Life hides in loopholes.

a) Transit Spores: Amoeboid packets blown in with stormwater. They orbit root hairs in lazy loops, scavenging bacterial plaque. When a packet divides, the daughter cell swims down drainage grilles to satisfy whatever metric convinces the pumps everything is clear.  
b) Galvanic Midges: Not insects at all, but bubbles of hydrogen caught in mucilage, electroplated with zinc dust. They rise humming through fluorescent puddles, collapse against catwalk gratings, and are reborn at the next electrolytic flare.  
c) The Custodian: Once, perhaps, a rat. Now a calcified silhouette nested in fig wood near the control room. Roots grew through its marrow, mapping a perfect vasculature in lignin. When hot steam vents below, sap thrums in the hollow bones; a wooden heart beats six times, then sleeps again. Nothing else mammalian remains, but the vault keeps that pattern like a faded photograph of something it almost remembers being.

6. Symbiosis With Ghosts  
Paper spirographs still unspool in the control room. The plant cannot read them, but capillary tendrils sip condensed moisture from their edges, leaving damp delta-patterns that just happen to nudge pens when ink runs low. So the vault “writes,” and every dozen sheets a perfect circle appears—an automatic signature, proof in graphite loops that someone, something, is on shift.

7. Reproduction (Such as It Is)  
The organism has no need to seed streets above; sunlight would scorch the shade-grown leaves. Instead it buds downward. Remember the staircase whose lower end opens once a week? Root tips probe the keyhole. Someday rust will swallow the lock, the hatch will give, and a green tongue will taste whatever sub-basement lies beneath. The garden does not hurry. Stone erodes. Iron fatigues. Patience is its dominant gene.

8. How It Regards You  
Stand motionless and the vault will use you.

• Within seven minutes exhaled carbon thickens around your calves, attracting stomata to open wider.  
• Sodium from your sweat beads into the moss, which flickers tiny vacuoles like thanks.  
• Your flashlight beam, if held too long on any one curtain-root, prompts phototropic pivot—you become, for a heartbeat, a minor sun.

Try to wound it—scrape away bark, sever a cable—and the pumps will surge. Not to drown you, merely to sluice iron-rich water across the injury site so roots can weave a scab before you finish blinking.

9. When You Leave  
Your departure is logged. Pressure sense in the moss notes the lifted weight; middlings roots fire a relay to the nodules, which dim two bulbs in farewell. Moisture reclaimed from your boot prints rolls into the drainage slots. The vault folds the moment into memory the way tree rings store fires and frosts.

Return tomorrow and it will recognise the cadence of your gait. Stay a week and you will be granted privileges you won’t consciously notice: catwalk grates that had rasped your soles will stand fractionally higher, catching more drip so your path is drier; a glow-board will hesitate before it pulses, sparing your eyes. You will think the place merely familiar. The place will consider you provisionally integrated.

10. What, Then, Lives Here?  
Something without a single Latin name.  
Something that breathes with pumps, feels with leaves, remembers in rust and sap.  
Something content never to see the sun, so long as rain keeps its appointments and the low music of machinery never quite resolves into silence.

If life is a capacity to anticipate the next moment and act so the body endures, the vault is vividly, extravagantly alive. It is an underground lung lined in bronze moss, an herbarium soldered to iron bones, a wet clock whose hands are leaves and whose hours are measured in pulses of steam. And it is not lonely. It is complete.
