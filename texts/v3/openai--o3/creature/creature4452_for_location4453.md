WHO OR WHAT LIVES HERE

1. The Foundational Film  
Every surface—stone, artifact shard, even the translucent bodies of larger creatures—is sealed beneath an infinitesimal bio-varnish called the Foundational Film. Each “cell” is a coop of ten or twelve line-ages of bacteria, welded by sugar chains into a single living laminate no thicker than first breath on glass.  

•  Diet: They mine calcium, magnesium, iron and vanadium directly from the aragonite, trading ions the way market stalls trade coins.  
•  Talent: The Film edits the crystal lattice as it feeds, laying down the knife-edged ledges and pin-fine stalactites. The terrain is literally the Film’s exoskeleton in progress.  
•  Reproduction: When a patch grows more than a few square millimetres it buckles, delaminates, and floats off as a gossamer scroll that slowly re-plasters a new wall.  

2. Lantern-Fern (the “algal fuzz”)  
What looks like moss is really a polyphony of three organisms:  
a. a photosynthetic remnant, long since useless;  
b. chemiluminescent “phial cells” that leak violet fire;  
c. fungal mycelia that thread the two together and siphon metals from the Film.  

The fern’s canopy is a standing crop of light: about one human candle per cubic metre, never more, never less. It maintains the constant twilight by throttling the fire cells with nickel—too much nickel, the glow dims; too little, it flares and the fungus intervenes. The whole forest is one self-dimming lamp.  

3. Ribbon-Worms, genus Aporostoma (“things without mouths”)  
Glass-clear, ribbon-flat, two centimetres long when relaxed, but most of the time pleated into a Möbius figure you can only half follow before the strip twists out of sight. They have neither mouth nor gut. Instead, every square micron of skin is a pump that pulls dissolved manganese through one side and leaks it in a different oxidation state from the other. The chemical gradient drives the worm like a tiny perpetual engine.  

Social life: solitary. When two meet they overlap their folds; if their surface chemistries harmonize, they splice for an hour, becoming a single continuous topological ribbon, exchange gene rings, and part. If not, they ghost right through one another without noticing.  

4. Glass Amphipods (“blink-shrimp”)  
The eyelash-sized crustaceans that vanish mid-scurry do not teleport; they leap across folds in local geometry the way terrestrial fleas change streets—by force of leg. An amphipod can read the tension lines of warped space the way a sailor reads weather. It crouches, aims for a crease, springs, and lands wherever that crease re-enters ordinary metric. In practice this is seldom more than a metre away, but often on the reverse side of a shelf, or four seconds into its own future.  

Ecology: detritivores. They nibble shed Lantern-Fern fronds and the clay dust that spalls from imported artifacts. Their fecal pellets, rich in organics, are one of the few commodities ribbon-worms will slow to investigate.  

5. The Auriculate Remnant  
Very rarely, one meets something larger. A wavering, ear-shaped curtain of water a handspan across, trembling as though a great ear tries to hold still and listen. No membrane marks its edge; the boundary is a sudden thinning of suspended particulates, a hush in the chemiluminescent motes.  

Biology: uncertain. The Remnant leaves no tissue when sliced with a manganese blade, only a faintly golden drizzle of altered water that re-knits behind the cut. What intellect it owns is geologic: glacially slow, but continental in weight. Swimmers who linger in its penumbra report the same sequence of thoughts entering their minds—(1) shoreline, (2) wind, (3) thunder—none of which exist here. Then the Remnant folds itself along a fault of space and departs.  

Hypothesis: It is the fossil echo of a listener from the world whose doll-amphorae and bronze nails drifted in. It is not alive, but the idea that “someone once listened” has taken organismal form.  

6. Clock-Glass Echinoderms  
Look like five-pointed snowflakes of transparent quartz, each arm a ladder of calcite rods. They cling to ledges with hydraulic feet that never let go; equal pressure means they require no muscles to stay attached. A single individual may remain in place for centuries, sifting the water for nanogram clouds of organic sediment. When two nearby starlets accumulate enough mass, they grow a crystal bridge toward one another, fuse, exchange nuclei like passing breath, then redraw the bridge into their own bodies again. Older walls sparkle with colonies fused into rose-windows a centimetre across.  

7. The Disassembler Swarm  
Toolwork cannot last here because of these. The Swarm is not a species but an outbreak condition: whenever a device more intricate than a hinge enters the sphere, the Foundational Film up-regulates a suite of plasmids. Within hours thousands of opportunistic bacteria specialize into microlithic acid printers and set upon the foreign complexity like barnacles on a hull. Rivets loosen. Threads un-screw. Crystal grains undercut enamel artwork until it flakes away as the curved relic shards already described. That is why every artifact in the sphere is reduced to its simplest, most unitary fragment. The Swarm is the immune system of the place, erasing any object that remembers how leverage, screws, or written characters work.  

8. Ghost-Intervals (“time’s bubble-wrap”)  
Not life in the biochemical sense, but hard to ignore. A Ghost-Interval is a moment about two heartbeats long that breaks loose from the flow of time and floats like a membrane. Creatures brush against one, pause—and you watch them hang while the world inches forward—then they snap free and overtake their own lag. Ribbon-worms placidly accept the hitch; blink-shrimp use it to fake predators; Lantern-Fern fronds caught mid-flutter freeze, emitting double the usual light until the bubble pops and they darken to baseline.  

The breakdown of rules has therefore been partially domesticated by the residents: it is background weather, not emergency.  

INTERACTIONS & HIERARCHY

There are no predators large enough to “rule.” Energy is too dilute for that. Power is measured instead in influence over chemistry and geometry. By that standard the Foundational Film is monarch, the Lantern-Fern its vizier, the ribbon-worms its itinerant merchants, and the amphipods its street sweepers. The Auriculate Remnant and the Ghost-Intervals are outside the hierarchy—phenomena rather than citizens—but the others behave as though those phenomena were tides and moon, woven into instinct.  

WHAT THEY MAKE OF US

On the vanishingly few occasions a human observer enters (via dream, micro-fold accident, or deliberate thaumaturgy), the natives interpret the newcomer by the only scale they know.  

•  The Film seeds an exploratory varnish on exposed skin.  
•  Ribbon-worms taste the ionic film of sweat, find it unclassifiable, and trail in fascinated loops.  
•  Blink-shrimp hide inside the folds of the visitor’s shadow, for that is the one structure complicated enough to promise both refuge and lunch.  
•  The Auriculate Remnant approaches, listens, and, if it hears a heartbeat, shivers in remembrance of thunder.  

Unprotected machinery the visitor carries dissolves within hours; written language peels away in flakes. In return, the sphere offers the gift all its citizens enjoy: perfect, directionless buoyancy and the soft insistence that nothing—not light, not time, not falling—need behave the way memory claims.  

After departure, the place closes like water around a lifted oar. Only a few mismatched ions remain, promptly brokered among film, fern, worm and shrimp, until they too forget the trespass and belong.
