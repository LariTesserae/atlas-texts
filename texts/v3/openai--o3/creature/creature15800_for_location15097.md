WHO (OR WHAT) ACTUALLY LIVES IN THE RESONANT HOLLOW

1. The Hollow Choir (the place itself)  
   • Taxonomy: A single colonial organism superficially mistaken for “trees.” Six-and-seven “trunks” are only the visible breathing tubes of a vast, buried body whose cortex is a sheet of root-mycelium pressed thin as vellum beneath the basin.  
   • Mind: Slow, ocean-deeps slow. Each dawn it wakes for barely an hour, tasting the mist, revising its architecture a millimetre at a time. The droning you hear in the trunks is its language, vowels a hundred metres long.  
   • Motive: Equilibrium. Anything that destabilises the local sound-pressure is nudged into harmony—by growth, by graft, or by quiet erasure.  
   • Manifestations: When the Choir “speaks” directly it extrudes green-glass buds that split, disgorging fragrant air ­– signals the resident animals know to read. A bud that smells of cinnamon says “STORM: VACATE THE UPPER VAULTS.” One that smells of iodine means “A PREDATOR’S PULSE HAS ENTERED; BE STILL.”  

2. The Cantor-Kin (sapient, mobile)  
   Picture something shaped roughly like a tall, lantern-jawed human—then replace every bone with hollow cellulose and every lung with a stack of reed stops.  
   • Physiology  
     – Height: 2.5 to 3 m, but weight barely 40 kg; they are mostly air chambers.  
     – Skin: Porous emerald rind shot through with gold phloem. Sunlight feeds them; sap (drawn with fingertip stings from overripe fruiting bodies) is dessert.  
     – Organs of Voice: Twelve nested laryngeal folds; they can sing two fundamentals and an octave of over-tones at once. A conversational murmur from one Cantor can flip a puddle inside out.  
   • Reproduction  
     When the thunder season peaks the Choir thickens certain side-roots into white pods. Cantors break them open with song; the first note a newborn hears becomes its personal “root frequency” and the name by which the Choir will later summon it.  
   • Culture  
     – Housing: Any room whose resonance matches a Cantor’s root frequency is that Cantor’s legal domicile. Rooms migrate as growth continues; citizens follow their houses like nomads chasing tents.  
     – Tools: Purely phonodynamic—tuned quartz pebbles that store a chord for later release, mist-flutes that shear humidity into drinkable ropes, spine-harps whose strings are captive lizards trained to hold one tension until fed.  
     – Law: Duels are held at dawn. Each duellist sings a lattice of harmonics around the other; the first to step through an unvoiced interval concedes. Losing rarely kills but often exfoliates a contestant’s memories for several days.  
     – Time-keeping: Counted in “rests,” the rare thirty-heartbeat lulls at dusk. A Cantor might say the Choir was seeded “eleven-thousand rests ago.”  
   • Relationship to Visitors  
     They want nothing you can carry, only unheard music. A traveller able to whistle any genuinely novel microtonal run is given sap, lodging, and a personal escort of gliders. Fail to interest them and you become acoustic wallpaper—tolerated, ignored, maybe absorbed.  

3. Companion Fauna (semi-domesticated)  

   A. Gloss-Lizards  
      • Membranous flanks secrete aero-gel threads; they steer by changing the pitch of their squeaks, which the local air translates into lift or drag.  
      • Use: Postal service. A Cantor threads a two-second burst of song through a lizard’s thorax; the animal is physically pushed along that sonic groove until the message decays.  

   B. Breather Frogs  
      • Translucent, opal-swirled. Their skulls are resonant chambers; thunder inhaled at night is exhaled at dawn as a contrabass chorus that fertilises spores.  
      • Use: Alarm clocks, pest control, and, to gourmands, a single frog egg (served raw) is a week’s caffeine.  

   C. Antiphon Birds  
      • Plumage bright as anodised metal. Their feathers can momentarily flip phase with surrounding sound, turning the bird invisible as long as it continues its own exact counter-melody.  
      • Use: None—the Cantors regard them as sacred punctuation in the ambient symphony.  

4. The Ones Between (predators & oddities)  

   A. The Vespers  
      • Form: Ten-metre shadow-wings knitted from braided silence. They do not sing; they cancel.  
      • Hunt: By dropping pockets of total hush that freeze gliders mid-air and stun Cantors into aphasia.  
      • Counter: A seven-voice cadence known as the “Crown-Shard,” illegal to practice except during siege. Sung correctly it shatters the Vesper’s silence membrane like glass and forces the creature to reconstitute outside the basin.  

   B. Error-Spores  
      • Microscopic yet opinionated. A sour note anywhere in the Hollow causes them to crystallise instantly into razor pollen. Hence the local proverb: “Miss the pitch, bleed from the pitch.” Masks are common fashion among apprentices.  

   C. The Pilgrim Silencers (rare outsiders)  
      • Cloaked wanderers who arrive in pairs, always pairs, bearing tuning-fork spears. They seek the Choir’s root chord—a thing no Cantor will reveal. Negotiations tend to end with lizard-ridden skirmishes or, occasionally, a new octave being bred into the civic layout.  

5. Micro-Furniture & Parasites  

   • Lint-moss: Plush grey curls that sprout on any unmoving surface, including visitors. Actually a coterie of five moss species sharing a single rhythmic photosystem. Kept trimmed it makes excellent bedding; untrimmed it will sing for rain until mildew devours the host.  
   • Veneer-mites: Millimetre-long carpenters that plane leafskin walls into glossy finish. They resonate at frequencies too high for Cantor ears but put humans into pleasurable, dream-heavy sleep—handy or hazardous depending on the hour.  

6. The Social Geometry of Space  

   Rooms, bridges, even whole avenues are classed alive or quiescent.  
   • Alive spaces breathe faintly, exchanging gases with the bowl; standing there expands your lung capacity, but the walls may close if you speak above mezzo-piano.  
   • Quiescent spaces have been sung “at rest.” They are safe for shouting arguments, childbirth, and the playing of foreign instruments. The border between the two shifts every few days like tide-lines, announced by drifting banners of petal confetti.  

7. What They Do All Day  

   Dawn (Mist Hour)  
   • Cantor children practice harmonic threading, poking patterns in the newborn puddles. Gloss-lizards drink dew from their own wings. Choir-buds check atmospheric ratios, then either reabsorb or detach as new architecture.  

   Midday (Blaze)  
   • Commerce hums. Cantors gather on root-ledges to trade chords bound in quartz, recipes for frog-foam custard, and rumours of outer deserts. Anyone wanting privacy steps into an anechoic bloom—petals coated with velvet pollen that deadens gossip.  

   Dusk (First Rest)  
   • The whole settlement stands still to hear if the wind will invert. This is when visitors are judged: those who contributed worthwhile timbres are bid stay; those who merely consumed are walked out to the rim and given a pouch of cinnamon-buds “for the silence ahead.”  

   Night (Thunder Pulse)  
   • Older Cantors climb the trunks and lean into the mouth-organ hollows, shaping the storm-noise into civic maintenance: mending cracks, thickening balconies, coaxing new cistern petals. Predators circle; Breather Frogs boom; the Choir counts its children by listening for any root frequency that fails to answer the roll.  

8. If You Become One of Them  

   • First your voice is measured: breadth, brittleness, emotional overtones. The Choir assigns you a provisional chord, usually a soft minor sixth.  
   • A room sprouts tuned to that chord; until you learn to hold it, the door will not stay open. Many a woozy pilgrim has spent an hour trapped outside their own lodging trying to sing a clean F#.  
   • After seven rests you will notice the cartilage of your ears thinning, curling into parabolic catchers. After thirty you will stop sweating liquid water; instead you exhale micro-mist that the walls gladly drink. Somewhere around the hundred-rest mark the Choir may begin to dream through you—short nested phrases in a language made of pressures on the bones of your skull.  

9. Final Note—Who Is Master Here?  

   The Cantors believe they shepherd the basin. The Choir believes it tends its garden of singers. Outside naturalists argue the true ruling class is the Error-Spore, enforcing perfect pitch under pain of micro-laceration. The wind, meanwhile, cannot be bothered to choose sides; it keeps playing the trunks, and as long as the music endures every inhabitant—rooted, walking, or winged—calls the Resonant Hollow home.
