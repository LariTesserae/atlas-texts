Who lives in the sun-shafted bowl is a parliament of quiet specialists, each trimmed by the same three chisels that shaped the land: running water, thin light and almost-frozen stone. No creature here was allowed to keep anything that made unnecessary noise or wasted warmth. They have been pared down to the minimum that still lets them move, breed and sense one another.

1. The Root-Hall Fish (Glacial Charling)
   Size & Form: A handspan long, glass-pale except for a sash of hematite scales along the lateral line.  
   Niche: They occupy the vaulted hollows inside the fused spruce rafts. By day they suspend in mid-water like motes in amber; by night they graze the bacterial films that sheet the inner bark.  
   Adaptations:  
     • Pectoral fins that end in stiff, finger-like rays; the fish clings to wood when sudden surges race through the hall.  
     • A forehead pit organ that maps minute temperature gradients—a way to locate new seep-inlets where nutrients are richest.  
     • Egg filaments tipped with mild adhesive; a female lays them along the ceiling so meltwater never scours them out.

2. Moss-Harriers (Catapult Gnats)
   Size & Form: Insects as heavy as cranberries, with under-inflated wings that can only manage short, vaulting flights.  
   Life Cycle: The larva is aquatic, living inside the micro-canals of moss stems and whispering air out through spiracles so the plant never drowns. The adult emerges already gravid; it has one job—find a new moss clump, bury 40 eggs, then collapse back into the mat where fungus completes the recycling overnight.  
   Behaviour: Their wingbeats are muffled by flexible nodules along the veins, turning each stroke into a push more felt than heard: the basin’s first concession to the rule against loud movement.

3. White-Masked Water Voles
   Coat: Graphite grey on the body, but the face, paws and the tip of the tail are porcelain-white, a broken outline that disappears against glare from the water skin.  
   Tunnels: They do not dig: they part moss with splayed incisors, the way swimmers make a channel through reeds. The displaced strands fold back the moment the animal passes, erasing all sign.  
   Communication: They drum with the hind feet on soggy rootwood. The percussion carries farther through waterlogged fibre than through air, letting neighbours keep territories invisible to predators that hunt by vibration.

4. Frost-Marten
   Build: A cat’s length but bone-thin, wrapped in fur so dense it squeaks under a fingertip like compressed snow.  
   Circuit: Every four hours it loops the basin in the same order, never doubling back, so prey can predict the danger and waste no energy on constant fear. The marten, in turn, never starves; a timed predator is efficient for both hunter and hunted.  
   Tactics: Instead of pouncing it “falls” into a moss mound, punching a hole with its forelegs, jaws already open. The impact is almost silent thanks to specialized carpal pads that spread like bellows the moment they touch substrate.

5. Cliff-Swifts
   Colony: A ledge may house a thousand gourded nests, yet the birds are monastically mute. Parents feed chicks by landing beside the entrance and shivering the throat; nestlings recognize the vibration pattern of their own kin and open only then.  
   Flight: Wingsnaps are cushioned by downy alulae that bloom just during take-off and landing, shearing away airflow vortices that would otherwise clap.  
   Sex & Architecture: Mated pairs renew the mud gourd every season by secreting a protein glue in their saliva. At dusk the entire colony weaves a low, continuous hum—subvocal, felt as a tickle in the sternum of someone standing on the amphitheatre floor. Ornithologists argue it is both sonar and ceremony.

6. Spruce Rafts (The Living City Itself)
   Not merely plants but structures with behaviour.  
   Growth Logic: When a trunk falls and bridges two wetted ridges, hormones flood the cambium on the underside, coaxing roots downward until they kiss water. Whenever three trunks interlock, the inner ones abort needles, convert to pipes and ventilate the Root-Halls beneath.  
   Longevity: No single stem exceeds eighty years, but the raft as a network is immortal; as one section rots, grafts up-river engulf it. Carbon dating shows some rafts have persisted two millennia without a break in canopy cover.  
   “Speech”: Researchers lowered hydrophones into the halls and recorded a sub-audible creaking that repeats every midday: sap rushing as the diurnal pulse lifts, identical to yesterday’s pattern within 0.03 seconds—a vascular clock for the entire basin.

7. Lichen Wolves (Symbiotic Assemblies)
   Appearance: To the eye they are irregular stains of copper green on stone. Under a lens each “stain” is a continent of stacked foliose lobes that lift and drop in slow peristaltic motion, following the sun shaft.  
   Function: They digest mineral flakes unearthed by freeze-thaw, releasing trace metals essential to the spruce rafts. In return, the rafts shed microscopic resin nodules that the lichens incorporate as antifreeze. The exchange keeps both partners a fraction above lethal cold.  
   Mythic Note: Local mountaineers refuse to tread on bright patches, claiming the basin itself “growls” if you bruise its wolves—really the crack of slate adjusting under foot.

8. Borrowed Eyes (Blind Amphipods)
   Habitat: The ankle-deep rills between rock ribs.  
   Trick: Lacking vision, they harvest shed gnat corneas—tiny chitinous lenses—and embed them in dorsal pits. Each lens focuses a single pixel of light onto a photocyte. One animal may wear sixty such eyes, enough to gauge noon brightness and retreat before the sun leaves and chill returns.  
   Predator Avoidance: When the Frost-Marten’s pressure wave hits water, the amphipods flop sideways and stiffen, mimicking sloughed spruce needles until danger passes.

9. Fog Columns (Microthermal Spirits, if you’re a poet)
   Composition: Merely condensate, yes, but up close each pillar performs coherent oscillations at twenty hertz, audible as a faint throb in the jawbone.  
   Ecological Role: Those vibrations loosen moss capsules exactly when humidity peaks, ensuring spores launch into upward draughts. The basin’s plants have, in effect, trained the weather to act as pollinator.

10. The Absent Ones
   What you will not meet is as telling as what you do. No frogs (too cold), no crows (too loud), no large herbivores—the maze would starve them before they found a straight line to flee. Everything present is either small enough to vanish or collective enough to defend by numbers.

Social Geography in brief:
• Verticals—cliff-swifts, frost-martens, lichen wolves—rule the walls.  
• Horizontals—voles, amphipods, gnats—work the carpets.  
• The spruce raft is parliament floor, cathedral ceiling and city plumbing all at once.  
• Water is the sheriff; its flow schedules every birth, hunt and spore release.

If you linger and keep the civic law of quiet, the inhabitants will show themselves one attribute at a time: first the treetop sigh of sap, then the pulse of fog, then moss that twitches when a vole tunnels beneath, until finally even the frost-marten trots by, indifferent, convinced you are just another wet, temporarily warm extrusion of the ground.
