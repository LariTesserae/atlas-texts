WHO AND WHAT MOVES THROUGH THE GULF  

1. The Ordinary Host  
   a. Cryobats (already noted) – Add two facts: females knit brood-nets from shed wing-skin; the nets hang like gray hammocks inside roof-roots, vibrating to keep pups warm.  
   b. Myriad-gnawers – Their enamel is laced with mica.  At dusk they file their own teeth on iron-rich bark, littering ledges with a slow glitter that twilight amplifies into false star-fields.  
   c. Striders – Males grow a single, backward-curving horn of compressed keratin-ice.  They scrape it against trunks until it whistles; the pitch tells rivals how richly a slope is mossed.  
   d. Lantern-eels – Each generation drifts higher than the last; the oldest corpses lie shattered on the ridge crest, thin as papyrus, still glowing around the fracture lines.  

2. The Things That Pretend to Be Landscape  
   a. Frond-Vipers – Muscle wrapped in photoskin, coiling flat along sun-facing pillars.  They keep their blood thin as sap, letting chloroplasts pay the metabolic bill until prey passes.  Strike range: seven metres.  
   b. Weather-Kites – Jellyfish the size of galleons, but stony, barnacled, half fossil.  They do not live in the ordinary sense; they charge themselves on lightning, drift for decades, then crash and crumble into calcareous snow that seeds new pillarwoods.  
   c. Choir-Molds – Frost-white crusts that occupy abandoned cryobat chambers.  Each pore resonates at a slightly different tone; whole colonies turn wind to chord, chord to slow chemical growth.  The sound you hear is literally the shape they are becoming.  

3. Minor Peoples (Tool-Users, Language Holders)  
   a. The Tulep (wind-harp makers)  
      • Form – Tall, tripodal vertebrates.  Two forward limbs end in double-thumbed hands; the third is a backward canted column used as both tail and leg.  Lungs in serial chain, pink as pomegranate seeds, let them ratchet breath in thin air.  
      • Skin – Slate fur over pebble-gray callus plates; plates carry patterned pits that work like ocarina holes.  A running Tulep whistles its own identity.  
      • Diet – Resin, roasted voles, thaw-pools of sugar rain.  They refuse lantern-eel flesh after it begins to glow, believing that light is the creature’s still-present dream.  
      • Habitation – Mid-trunk balconies hacked from root-glass.  Each balcony holds a single harp: antler struts, sinew strings.  The harp’s chord announces wind speed; the note missing from that chord tells neighboring clans which level of the trunk is being foraged.  
      • Lore – They teach that the gulf is not between mountains but inside one vast, upside-down tree still tunneling toward the sun.  Their dead are fed to ferrule moss so the body can “keep climbing.”  
   
   b. The Glassroot Engineers  
      • Biology – Colonial invertebrates; each individual resembles a fist-sized, translucent crab with six ribbon appendages.  Alone they are dim; fused by thousands they generate lattice minds with memory stored as light phase in silica threads.  
      • Works – Inside hollow pillars they melt root-glass into catwalks, lenses, heliotropic mirrors that shuttle the scant sun down-shaft.  Every six years they abandon a shaft section, letting Tulep or striders move in, then start a fresh gallery higher up.  
      • Motive – To polish every interior surface until it sings back incoming thunder as coherent standing waves.  The Engineers seem to be building a musical instrument the size of the whole forest.  To what end no one knows; a popular Tulep proverb claims: “When the pillar sings its final note, the roof will open and let us out.”  
   
   c. Ember Jackals  
      • Appearance – Four-eyed canids, charcoal fur stippled with living cinders cultured in dermal pits.  The sparks blind cryobats; more importantly they ignite stray resin.  
      • Culture – Nomadic fire-tenders.  A pack carries its own flame from pitch pocket to pitch pocket in bark bowls lined with clay that only exists on the far rim.  In return for safe passage the Tulep leave them thigh bones packed with nut-meal.  
      • Speech – Hisses of expanding steam blown through teeth.  Understood by Tulep children, ignored by Tulep adults, feared by striders.  
   
4. The Unseen Majority  
   a. Cloud-Silters – Planktonic proto-arthropods no thicker than fog.  They bloom after every lightning storm, scrubbing ozone into sweet ethanol rain.  All who drink glow faintly for an hour; for that hour predation pauses, as though the light were sacred.  
   b. Umbilicus – A single continuous fungal mat running inside the western rim at least forty kilometres.  Genetic sampling shows every tuft is the same individual.  It never fruits; instead it engineers tiny breech-births of itself—sporeless buds shaped like soft coins that Striders lick for salt.  In exchange each strider carries an umbilicus chain deeper into the gulf, spreading rootlets in its hoof cracks.  
   c. The Floor Delegates – Degenerate offshoots of striders that surrendered height for armor.  Imagine plated slugs with a face full of velvet antlers.  They never climb.  Once a decade a delegate ascends a fallen trunk to trade: chunks of meteoric iron that only reach the gulf’s floor, exchanged for drums carved from Tulep ribs.  Delegates place their antlers to the drums, memorize the gulf’s new rhythms, and crawl back into splinter darkness.  

5. Apex Puzzles  
   a. Crown-Watchers – Shadowy silhouettes glimpsed above the upper canopy when the petals reef for storm.  Humanoid only at a distance; up close they are rigs of living rope and cloak, strung with dozens of eye-sacs that swivel independently.  They never descend.  The Tulep swear the Watchers prune the petals so sunlight is fairly portioned, and that anyone who tries to climb higher than the petals is cut loose and dropped.  
   b. The Voice in the Downdraft – Recorders lowered a kilometre under a closed petal capture infrasound pulses repeating every 127 seconds.  Frequency sweep equals exactly the resonant length of the gulf.  No Mouth has been located.  Some engineers think the pillarwoods themselves are talking; others think something older, deeper, is breathing through them.  

6. Relics of People Who Are Gone (Or Never Were)  
   • Bone atlases etched with star maps that do not match any sky now visible from the plateau.  
   • Knotted gut-cord whose binary pattern translates, when flattened, into prime numbers counting backward toward zero.  
   • A single obsidian lens polished so perfectly it shows colors invisible to human retinas; Tulep shamans stare through it to decide where the next harp must stand.  
   • The Quiet Cloister: twenty adjacent pillars entirely hollowed, walls smoothed mirror-bright, no sign of who did it, no sign they ever left.  Inside, footsteps make no echo; sound reenters the body of the one who made it, a pressure behind the sternum that will not dissipate until the walker leaves.  

7. How a Visitor Might Meet Them  
   • Stay two nights at mid-trunk height.  On the first night the Ember Jackals will circle, gauging your willingness to share dried fat.  On the second, a Glassroot survey flight—brilliant as solder sparks—will map your silhouette in lasers.  If you survive both, a Tulep youth will arrive at dawn carrying a flute hollowed from your own rope.  The melody it plays is a question: “What can you give that the gulf has not already taken?”  An honest answer earns passage; a lie earns a shove into the long, resonant dark.  

In sum: the gulf is not empty between its mountainous trees.  It hums with mouths, minds and motives stacked on one another like strata of migrating weather.  Some of those minds we can recognize—a tripod hunter whistling its name against the wind, a pack of fire-keeping canids bargaining for bone.  Others are structures that learned to think: galleries of glass, miles of mycelium, whole chords of molded frost.  Above them all, half seen, something tends the light itself.  Whatever lives here does so the only way anything can—by climbing, by clinging, by singing the space it needs out of the air that is forever trying to blow it loose.
