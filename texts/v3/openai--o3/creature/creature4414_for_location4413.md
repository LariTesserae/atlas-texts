Who lives in the Throat-Workshop?  

Answer the question once and the place contradicts you the next day. Everything here grows roles the way the rust-red wall grows orchids. Still, on any given circuit of the cat-walk you will meet the following—some wearing boots, some wearing bark, some wearing nothing you would call skin at all.

--------------------------------------------------
1.  The Six Boilermancers
--------------------------------------------------
They sign the work-logs as “boilermancers” because someone in the distant past refused to call them priests, but the job is closer to liturgy than to labor.

Appearance and kit  
•  Faces glazed pink by constant steam baths; eyelashes frazzled white.  
•  Uniforms: sleeveless asbestos-cotton wraps, copper mesh aprons, and belts jangling with tuning-fork wrenches.  
•  Tattooed pressure gauges climb their throats; each gauge’s needle is a thin scar that rose off the tattoo the day they took vows. When stress in the forge climbs, the scar itches—an embodied alarm.  

Craft  
They do not “fix” the condenser-forge; they coax it. Every vent-cycle they walk the wall in pairs, striking girders with calibrated hammers and listening to the overtones. Where metal hums sharp they pack vermilion paste that wicks heat; where the note is flat they rivet on crescents of fresh brass to thicken the chord. They read pressure the way meteorologists read clouds.

Daily rhythm  
04:00  Wake to the First Thrum (the tower’s slowest reciprocating pump).  
05:00  Chorus of Bleeder Valves: six voices singing one sustained harmonic; the valves detach like organ stops when the chord is correct.  
Daylight hours  Roam, tune, pray. Their “prayer” is data-encoded whistle patterns recorded by the walls themselves—an ongoing argument between mortal attention and machine memory.  
Evening  Bathe in runoff trays while reciting the names of every sheared bolt they replaced that day: a litany of losses put to peaceful rest.

Collective personality  
Somewhere between field medic, fire tender, and cantor. The forge has already outlived whole civilizations; the boilermancers’ aim is to give it a little more forever.

--------------------------------------------------
2.  The Twelve Gardeners of Verdigris Keep
--------------------------------------------------
They arrived one rotation after the first vines did, claiming the original engineers asked for them in a forgotten maintenance clause.

Tools and attire  
•  Burlap smocks waterproofed with tree-resin varnish; pockets spill seed capsules, hygrometers, bone-handled secateurs.  
•  Felt hats sprouting living lichen: each hat is both umbrella and nursery.  
•  Boots shod with soft copper to avoid nicking the lattice—every scratch becomes a rust nursery.  

Territory  
Each gardener tends a “plot” no larger than a billiard table: a crooked seam or a sun-blasted gutter where their chosen biome roosts. They swap plots every equinox so no one person marries a single patch of wall; the garden must belong to its own future, not to the ego of a caretaker.

Practices  
•  Photosynthesis Plumbing: by day they angle mirrored louvers to pipe sunlight under girders where pink motile mosses lurk.  
•  Vapour Choir: during steam fall they whistle sub-semitone pitches that coax stomata on the vine-ropes to open wider, drinking deeper.  
•  Corridor Crab Husbandry: each gardener keeps two or three crabs who follow them like slow dogs, nibbling mold from freshly watered surfaces. The crabs are both cleaning crew and living mulch, their copper shells shedding verdigris flakes rich in micronutrients.

Produce  
Edible? Barely. They harvest silky green sheets of condenser algae that taste like hot pennies. Most of it turns to compost anyway. The point is not the harvest but the cooling—the thin vegetal skin that reins in runaway heat.

Creed  
“Steel sweats, we wipe its brow.” To them the forge is not prey to be exploited but an elder creature that deserves shade and drink.

--------------------------------------------------
3.  The Companion Corvids
--------------------------------------------------
They hatched from eggs electroplated in nickel; their pin-feathers glint metallic blue where ordinary birds show black.

Physiology  
•  Bones infused with steel filaments, a breed-line trick that lets them ride the shaft’s turbulent updrafts without snapping.  
•  Beaks magnetite-tipped; they tap relay housings to sense which wire hums alive.  
•  Vocabulary: a hundred human words, two hundred machine tones, and one piercing shriek reserved for imminent pressure breaches.

Culture  
They treat the six boilermancers as flock elders, the twelve gardeners as interesting trees, and everyone else—including visiting inspectors—as possible snack dispensers. Each dusk they stage “inventory flights,” hovering on thermals to count new stress cracks in the wall. In the rare silence after a vent-cycle you can hear them muttering numbers in base eight.

Utility  
Message runners, alarm klaxons, amateur historians. They remember every face that ever looked over the railing, and they will quiz you with playful cruelty: “Where is the woman of four vent-cycles past who wore red oil-skins?”  If you cannot answer, they study you a while longer, deciding whether you, too, will vanish.

--------------------------------------------------
4.  The Corridor Crabs (Model LUX-904)
--------------------------------------------------
Once factory-issued custodial drones; now half-feral janitor-gardeners.

Morphology  
Palm-sized, eight legs jointed like watch springs; dorsal carapace of brushed brass slowly turning green. Two forward claws: one fitted with a diamond scraper, the other with micro-scissors sharp enough to trim orchid stamens without bruising.

Behavior  
•  Forage Paths: predetermined by an algorithm no human still understands, yet the crabs modify routes if a gardener whistles the right three-click command.  
•  Nesting: they bed in junction boxes, lining them with shredded wire insulation that glows when the box is hot—so you can see occupied homes pulsing like ember lanterns at night.  
•  Ritual: once a week they stand in a ring around a drainage grate and deposit lint-balls of confiscated rust. No observer has explained why.

Life cycle  
They do not breed. They molt. A big crab splits its shell, leaving a perfect brass husk, then scuttles away shinier and smaller, somehow renewed. Leftover husks become planters for air-ferns; the gardeners call them “ghost-pots.”

--------------------------------------------------
5.  Lesser Denizens
--------------------------------------------------
•  Tadpole-Things (“duct newts”)—wriggle in runoff trays, metamorphose into translucent frogs that cling to hot pipes and secrete antifriction mucilage the boilermancers skim for bearing grease.  
•  Iridescent Beetles—tiny polishing machines; they rasp gunk from inscriptions, accidentally revealing old machine code scripture.  
•  Long-Tailed Pollinator Bats—wings like stained glass; they thread duct mazes at night, fertilizing orchids in exchange for nectar so sugar-rich it crusts their muzzles gemlike by dawn.

--------------------------------------------------
6.  The Machines Themselves
--------------------------------------------------
In most places “the machinery” is furniture. In the Throat-Workshop it is resident, sovereign, and only half asleep.

Body language  
•  Rumbling stomachs of heat exchangers tell you they are content.  
•  A sudden staccato of valve-clacks means “itch” in the piping—time for a boilermancer to scratch with a torque wrench.  
•  When metal forgets itself and rewrites alloy, it leaves pearly growths on handrails: ornamentation the way corals raise reefs. The gardeners polish these nodes, both to please the aesthetic and because the new blend usually resists rust better than the old.

Memory  
A grid of vaults in the wall stores heat not as temperature but as patterned stress. The machine dreams by rearranging those patterns; sometimes it wakes to find a new cog or girder protruding from its own flank, self-issued offspring begging utility.

Attitude toward humans  
Tolerant the way a barn tolerates swallows—aware, but the scale gap is enormous. It knows the boilermancers’ voices; it likes the frequency at which a gardener laughs while clipping leaves. That may be affection or merely acoustic hygiene. Either way, the relationship works.

--------------------------------------------------
7.  How They All Share the Same Throat
--------------------------------------------------
Shift Change  
At the top of each sixth vent-cycle the wind calms just long enough for a hush to drop, a hush so total you can hear vines creak as they grow. Everyone stops. Boilermancer, gardener, corvid, crab—motionless. This is the closest thing the community has to prayer in unison.

Economy of Exchange  
•  Gardeners provide shade, which prolongs bearing life; the machines vent steam, which waters the plants.  
•  Crabs harvest rust, which would poison roots; roots exude acids, which clean crab shells.  
•  Boilermancers tune the forge’s voice; the forge in turn forges micro-tools overnight—gifts of new metallurgy that appear beside the morning coffee urn with no signature but a faint warmth.

Lore  
They pass stories of whose throat the Workshop truly is:  
•  A giant that died standing, its throat scooped hollow for industry.  
•  A planetary engine’s airshaft—meaning everyone here lives inside an exhalation.  
•  A god’s windpipe, still singing the chord that holds gravity polite.  

None of these stories agree, yet each camp keeps a copper plaque recording its version. Once a decade the plaques corrode unreadable and must be re-etched; the corvids vote on wording, because they will be here longer than any human rotation.

--------------------------------------------------
8.  Visitors’ Advice
--------------------------------------------------
1. Bring ear defenders and a spare sense of scale.  
2. Never shoo a crab; it will remember.  
3. If a corvid asks a question, answer in song; speech offends their aesthetic.  
4. Accept that any tool you drop becomes ancestral wealth for residents you will never meet.  
5. When the silence descends, close your eyes. What you feel humming through the rail is every living thing listed above, inhaling together.

That collective breath—metal, moss, bird, person, and machine—is what truly inhabits the Throat-Workshop. The rest is only architecture.
