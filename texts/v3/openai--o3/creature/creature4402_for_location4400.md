THE PEOPLE WHO HAVE MADE THEIR CITY IN THE TREE

1.  A hundred names and none
•  Outsiders (those few who have ever crawled in alive) call them rot-gnomes, drip-folk, rootlings.  
•  In their own mouth–clicking language they name themselves only by place and moment: the phrase means “we-who-now-dwell-here.” When an individual migrates three cambium ledges over, their self-name changes with the address.

For clarity we will borrow the title used by the oldest clan and extend it to all: the Hushborn.

2.  Bodies shaped by narrow dark
•  Size: rarely over 90 cm tall; legs foreshortened, femurs bowed to fit the constant crouch.  
•  Skin: moon-pale where blood is near the surface, else bruised green with phloem pigments absorbed from lifelong sap baths. At a distance they resemble peeled willow branches tied into human knots.  
•  Eyes: huge, oil-black pupils rimmed by a faint bio-luminescent ring that swells and subsides in time with heartbeats. When six of them look your way at once the chamber fills with slow lantern-glow.  
•  Hands and feet: five digits but webbed to the second knuckle; fingertips carry chitinous scrapers evolved from nails, perfect for scoring living wood.  
•  Thermoregulation: almost none. They idle at four degrees, matching the air, and warm themselves only by work or fermented broth. A visitor’s 37 °C body feels scalding to them; they will keep a polite meter’s distance until the “forge-reeking heat” fades.

3.  Senses and speech
•  Hearing is poor. They rely instead on vibration: an inner ear anchored to the sternum detects tremors transmitted through bark more clearly than airborne sound.  
•  Language is a spectrum: arm-length curls of finger-codes for routine matters, hollow chest clicks for names and quantities, and — most important — the slow drum-songs with which they speak to the tree itself.  
•  When they must address foreigners they stand knee-deep in water so consonants strike liquid and carry farther.

4.  Life cycle
Egg   – two thumb-sized pearls laid in moss-lined fissures close to the heartwood heat.  
Hatchling – translucent, limbless, more eel than person; munches yeast scum until metamorphosis.  
Crawler  – gains limbs, teeth, terror; spends a year among the roots learning which tastes spell fever.  
Shaper  – adolescent; carves their first successful bulge in the bark and earns a niche to sleep in.  
Elder  – bones ossify, skin darkens, voice deepens into sub-audible thrum. Elders become the primary architects: they no longer haul tools, they kneel against trunks and sing walls into motion.

Average span: forty flood-cycles (roughly fifty surface years) before joints calcify and the body is offered to the pool. The corpse is never seen again; something beneath the weave waits politely for that donation.

5.  Diet and medicine
•  Staple: root-kvass, a cold ‘soup’ of shaved cambium, crushed bark-lice, and fermenting spore milk. It tingles, numbs the tongue, and supplies antifreeze proteins.  
•  Festival: the rare fern-mantis leg, braised not by flame but by packing it in caustic sap for three nights until the meat chemically “cooks” itself.  
•  Ills are treated with living poultices: swatches of sponge-algae that swell with antibiotic resin when pressed to skin. Burns are unknown; frostbite is common and considered a rite of passage. A missing fingertip is proof you dared explore the high mist balconies.

6.  Craft
Wood never dies here, so tools cannot be wooden.  
•  Stone arrives as pebbles sluiced down the sap tunnels after storms; these are knapped into glass-sharp adzes.  
•  Chitin is the luxury material. The favored source is the hourly molt of the rope-long millipedes: gently harvested, flattened under slate, then cut into armor that flexes like fingernails.  
•  Thread is spun from gut of amphipods, dyed with moss-iron to a permanent bruise-violet. A traveler recognizes clan territory by the color printed on its walkways.

7.  Social pattern – the Thirty-Three Rings
The visible dwellings around the moat house only the current generation. Farther in, concentric galleries spiral toward the trunk’s core, each older, more weathered, gradually compressed by the slow swell of living wood.  
•  Four generations in: ceilings are child-high, echoes muffled, tools fossilized into the walls.  
•  Six in: no human passes; only minnow-like second cousins who never metamorphosed slip through.  
Collective memory is stored by abandoning old halls intact. A youth explores inward with a glow-worm to read their ancestry in frozen poses of furniture.

Decision-making: every dusk the hanging basket sways under the full weight of the adult population—rarely more than one hundred souls. They sit cross-legged, press palms to the resonant lattice, and vote by changing the pulse of the living weave. The tree counts for them: majority rhythm makes the basket bulge outward; minority causes it to contract. When consensus fails the floor simply refuses to settle and will pitch the quarrelers gently into the pool until they reconsider.

8.  Belief
The Hushborn do not worship the tree; they consider themselves one more of its organ systems. What they do revere—fearfully—is downward.  
•  “Below-Roots” creatures are the reason fire is taboo. The last blaze roused something whose drumming footfalls made the whole trunk quake. No one ever saw it, but in murals the children paint an orb of jointed jaws, ring after ring, closing.  
•  To placate Below-Roots the dead are sent, gift-wrapped in reeds that rustle soothingly on the way down.

9.  Encounters with the Outside
An opening to daylight exists, high and very thin, but it is plugged by a woven stopper of living shoots. Once each winter two adolescents are allowed to climb out, trade a pouch of millipede chitin for obsidian flakes or colored salt, and return before lungs freeze. They describe the surface world with equal parts awe and disbelief: “A ceiling so far away it turns blue.”

10.  What they think of you
Warmth means danger; creatures who glow red on a cold palette tend to wield fire and break the rules of healing wood. Still, curiosity always overrides caution.  
•  They will follow at knee-level, matching each step-splash with three of their own.  
•  If you set one pebble into a root and hum a steady rhythm, they will squat in a semicircle to watch the bulge form: a magic they have known since cradle, yet adore at every repetition.  
•  Offer a flint striker and they will back away, hands lifted, clicking “no-no-no” until the item is pocketed again.

Should you stay, remember:
1.  Keep nails trimmed; an accidental scrape may bloom shut behind your hand and pin you by morning.  
2.  Whisper always; silence is the courtesy of guests.  
3.  Do not ask the tree for a door unless you genuinely need it. It grants requests literally and sometimes hungers to oblige.

Standing amid their city you will smell fermentation and iron, hear heartwood beat through the soles of your boots, and see, now and again, a ring of patient eyes blinking green as the Hushborn weigh whether you belong to the world above or might, in time, learn to breathe the hush with them.
