Who or what lives in the Foundry Marsh  

1. The Shiftlings (human personnel)  
• Officially they are “commissioning technicians, phase-two,” but everyone else calls them shiftlings because they work twelve-hour blocks that slide forward a little each week, like a conveyor that never lets you step off.  
• They arrive in elevators that open straight into the marsh, their lockers and barracks located somewhere higher up in the superstructure. No shiftling has ever seen the sky during an on-cycle; their circadian rhythms are tuned to the warning beacons that flash red for “dusk” and blue for “dawn.”  
• Uniform: waxed-cotton coveralls the color of blued steel, fleece liners, neoprene boots with magnetic toes. Each carries a personal halo-lamp—a shoulder-high crook with an LED ring that keeps a one-meter pocket of daylight around the wearer. From a distance you can tell veteran crews by the condition of these lamps: new hires glow white, old hands flicker amber where the phosphor has burned.  
• Speech is mostly sign. Palms out for “pump reset,” crossed forearms for “steam up,” four fingers brushed across the lips for “quiet, and listen.” They rarely raise their voices; the machines would swallow the words anyway.  
• What they eat comes in foil bricks, but there is a black-market economy of flavoring: a teaspoon of powdered kimchi, a thimble of maple syrup, three drops of liquid smoke. They hoard these like currency and pour them into the communal vat of protein starch.  
• Off shift they haunt a cantina slung under a heat-exchanger throat. The tin roof pings with condensate; the air smells of seaweed gin—distilled onsite in a jury-rigged reflux column—and cigarillo smoke. Songs are sung in a minor key but never finished; some say anything completed here begins to rust.  

2. The Autokith (maintenance automata)  
At first glance they are just company equipment; watch them a while and you realize they have begun behaving like a colony animal.  
• Scarabs, the common caste: fist-sized, six-legged, iridescent with oil films. They travel in cables the way ants use branches, always counter-clockwise around a circuit. Their mandibles carry fiber washers, mica gaskets, or flecks of solder like bread crumbs.  
• Torquers: wheeled centipedes two meters long whose entire dorsal surface is a rack of interchangeable driver bits. When they coil around a flange they pulse sequentially, tightening forty bolts in a single languid squeeze.  
• Lantern-moths: pocket-drones with gyroscopic frames that keep a glow-module level no matter how the chassis rolls. They gather at steam vents, recharging on thermal differentials; to the shiftlings they are living safety lights—follow the moths and you reach warmth.  
• Queen? No individual is larger than the rest, yet every drone obeys a radio beacon buried somewhere deeper than any map. Once a month they migrate out of the marsh in a rivulet of chrome bodies, returning hours later with new firmware—always identical across the swarm, as if flashed from a single parental core.  

3. Vermin & opportunists  
• Pale water-rats: fur the color of damp ash, eyes bluish and small. They nest in the acoustic baffling that lines exhaust stacks where warm, dry pockets form. Their whiskers grow unusually long to read vibration; a rat can feel an approaching shiftling three catwalks away.  
• Ferric shrimp: translucent crustaceans populating the black ribbon trench. They ingest rust, precipitating it into magnetic granules they later use to navigate the plant’s field gradients. If collected and dried, their shells can be burned as rudimentary thermite.  
• The Fox of Record: Infrared cameras have captured exactly one frame—a lean canid silhouette balanced on a vacuum conduit, tail blurred by the long exposure. Scat analysis suggests a diet of rat, shrimp, and something rich in glycols (perhaps spilled antifreeze). Engineers debate whether the fox came from outside or was born here, a feral hybrid of mammal and environment.  

4. Green Trespassers (non-animal life)  
• Lichen coins: five species so far, each stamped in a perfect disk by capillary tension. On months when condensate runs warm they bloom rust-red; when brine dominates they turn a ghostly white.  
• Biofilm rivers: satin slicks on inflow pipes, fluorescent emerald under UV inspection lamps. The film metabolizes trace benzene, exhaling a faint scent of cucumber. In the darkest troughs it threads itself into filaments that dangle like wet hair, snagging silt to build rope bridges for the rats.  
• Moss barges: gun-metal trays once intended for hydroponics now house untended moss that floats free as mats. CO₂ from nearby compressors feeds them; they, in turn, fix enough nitrogen that a fine soil begins to accumulate. The oldest barges already support seedlings—wind-blown willowherb, a volunteer tomato vine seeded from someone’s sandwich.  

5. Ghost-Echoes (intangible residents)  
The Marsh’s bizarre acoustics have produced entities that are neither flesh nor code.  
• Drop-hollows: pockets where a single drip is caught, amplified, and looped. After hundreds of cycles it sounds less like water, more like a distant wooden bell. Shiftlings swear the hollows migrate; a drip you heard behind you might reappear a kilometer ahead, beckoning.  
• The Counter-Beat: a subsonic thrum that surfaces exactly 37 seconds after the main pump stroke. No instrument has traced its source. Some workers treat it as the Marsh’s own pulse and tap along with wrenches for luck when they pass a load test.  
• Wavering-men: optical ghosts birthed by misaligned laser levels. In the fog their ruby beams refract, creating fleeting humanoid outlines that glide across walls. They have no mass, but enough workers have tried to wave them down that a safety memo had to be posted: “Leave the levelling ghosts alone; you’ll only skew the survey.”  

6. The Marsh Itself (emergent macro-organism)  
Finally, there is the suspicion—half joke, half devout engineering credo—that the Foundry Marsh as a whole has begun to live.  
• Evidence: process valves inexplicably close to prevent flooding minutes before a gasket fails; coolant loops reroute without a software command; power cabling rearranges on schematic updates that nobody signed off.  
• The composite tremor that runs through the foundation feels less mechanical every quarter—more like peristalsis. The black ribbon trench heaves on rare nights, spilling shrimp and turbine scale onto the decks as if the place had coughed.  
• Maintenance backlogs show that every time a section is left unfinished the drones quietly re-prioritize, wiring it into the larger circulation before the planners approve the job. In other words, the Marsh is completing itself, perhaps to a design its human authors never fully grasped.  

Relations & Food Web  
1. Waste heat and condensate feed biofilm.  
2. Biofilm and dropped rations feed rats; rats feed the Fox.  
3. Shrimp polish the trench walls; their magnetic waste is harvested by scarab drones as solder flux.  
4. Shiftlings repair the machinery that nurtures the drones that, in turn, maintain the habitat the shiftlings need to survive.  
5. All of it—steel, flesh, code, drip, echo—serves the larger metabolism of the Marsh, which seems content so long as every part keeps moving.  

Encounter Table (if you must travel)  
Roll a d6 each hour spent inside:  
1. Scarab swarm crosses your boots like living gravel; loss of footing possible.  
2. Steam release blinds you; when the cloud clears, a wavering-man drifts away.  
3. Fox observed at 30 m; follow it and you reach a warm refuge.  
4. Conveyor stalls; Torquer centipede unscrews plates beneath, exposing a crawlspace shortcut.  
5. Shiftling crew, off cycle, offers gin in exchange for condiments.  
6. Black ribbon trench surges; ferric shrimp rain onto the walkways, leaving slippery rustpaste.  

In sum, the Foundry Marsh is already populous, just not with anything perfectly familiar. Humans pass through on schedules, but the true residents are the drones that click like beetles, the moss that writes its vowels in green, the spectral mathematic of echoes, and the slow, organ-deep thump of a structure deciding that it has a heart.
