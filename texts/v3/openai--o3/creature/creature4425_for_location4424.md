The Basin has people—true people, quick-minded and self-willed—but they are not built on the same wet blueprints as you or I.  Nothing in their world is.  To understand them you have to begin with three facts that shape every cell of their bodies and every gesture of their culture:

•  Water is absent.  
•  Sound is omnipresent.  
•  Light is overwhelmingly, mercilessly strong.

Out of those three facts the inhabitants—who call themselves the Resounding—have grown.

PHYSIOLOGY

1. Dry biochemistry  
   The Resounding are lithovital rather than aqueous.  Their blood is a slow slurry of molten salts (chiefly sodium-potassium nitrates) kept fluid by body heat and by trace nitrile oils they synthesize from banner sugars.  In place of soft cartilage they have flexible alabaster made of calcium fluorophosphate; in place of hair they grow arsenate whiskers that wick dust and shed it again.  Their skin is a chitin-silica laminate only a few molecules thick, silvered on the outside to mirror away the hammer-light and ridged on the inside to carry cooling brine through microscopic gutters.

2. Shape  
   Adults stand a little under 1.5 m.  Their torsos are narrow, all radiator-fin ribs and open lattice bone, letting heat stream away.  Two sets of arms: the upper pair long for climbing and banner-weaving, the lower pair shorter, tipped with chiselled nails able to split salt-glass without tools.  Legs double-joint backward like a wading bird’s, perfect for balancing on the ringing plates.

3. Sensory organs  
   • Vision: six eyelids of different opacities stack over brilliant purple pupils; they can go from total blackout to pinhole focus in a blink.  
   • Hearing: no pinnae.  Instead the whole skull is a parabolic dish of fused bone that channels the vegetal roar onto a crescent membrane just inside the forehead.  They read minute phase differences in that thunder the way a bat reads echoes at night.  Paradoxically, they can “hear” a finger snap two houses away in the middle of the earthquake-loud day.  
   • Touch: each fingertip has a fan of whisker-thin vibrissae; lay a palm on a plate and they feel through it, sensing who is walking three meters off.

4. Respiration and feeding  
   They breathe the dust.  Air enters slotted spiracles along the collarbone, passes over a forest of cilia that comb out charged mineral grains, then vents again through the back.  Cilia shuttle those ions to gut symbiotes that build sugars; solids—banner pulp, skimmer wings, an occasional gecko—are luxuries eaten for pleasure more than need.

LIFECYCLE

Egg   A parent extrudes an ovoid of waxy nitrate gel and seals it under a live banner leaf.  Radiation hardens the shell in one day.

Nymph   After thirty-six heat cycles the shell cracks.  The nymph scuttles tube-to-tube, translucent, speechless, cared for collectively.  At ten cycles it chooses a shard of fallen ceiling crystal; elders etch a glyph on it and melt it to its breast.  That is its Name-Shard.

Mature   Growth rings in the jade-white bones show about ninety local years before the Name-Shard powders away.  On that day the bearer lies on the plates and lets its body dehydrate to brittle husk; kin grind it and seed the dust into fresh stalk cuttings so the voice of the deceased literally rises in the next song-storm.

SOCIAL STRUCTURE

• Hearth-stacks  
  Twenty to forty adults live in a pile of salt-glass lozenges—one “stack.”  Blood line matters less than craft line; a stack is a choir of complementary skills: plate-scorers, banner-weavers, vent-climbers, night-scribe historians.

• Government by Pitch  
  Politics is acoustical.  Once a week the stacks send delegates onto the canopy bridges and, using resonant drums of stretched banner membrane, pulse patterns into the forest.  Every rhythm has an agreed civic meaning: harvest approval, vent-cleaning rota, justice petitions.  A proposal that cannot be expressed as a repeatable rhythm is considered incoherent and is dropped.

• Law of Twin Cuttings  
  The fourth Rule (plant two stalks for every one taken) is policed by counting scars.  Each harvested tube leaves a calcite scar; each planted cutting has a registration knot plaited in banner fiber.  Once a year the Scar-Counters pace the basin, tapping scars with copper rods—high rings for felling, low rings for planting.  The counts must match within a tolerance of three tones, or the delinquent stack forfeits its sugar allotment.

LANGUAGE

1. High-Hand, the day speech  
   A flashing, mirror-bright sign language: ten fingers, wrist rotation, quick changes of palm angle to catch and reflect sun.

2. Undertalk, the night speech  
   A private whisper produced by vibrating the skull dish with mouth shut.  It rides the already deafening ambient roar and can be picked up by anyone touching the same stalk or plate.  Lovers and conspirators prefer it.

TOOLS AND CRAFT

• Cutting & Knapping  
  Edged tools are pressure-flaked glass, hafted to banner-fiber grips soaked in nitrate glue.  A master cutter can split a three-meter plate along an invisible stress line so cleanly that the new face works as a mirror.

• Plumbing the Dustfall  
  Vents along the wall are mined by vent-climbers wearing callused silica socks.  They set lattice traps of magnetite hair to concentrate rare ions—silver, vanadium, even iridium slivers from micrometeorites—which become trade gemstones.

• Weaving the Skyroads  
  Banner fibers twist into cables as strong as mild steel.  The Skyroads—the net of footbridges over the canopy—are rebuilt every eight years after the great up-draft season peels old cables apart.

BELIEFS

Silence is the Void.  It is not merely frightening; it is cosmological erasure.  Stories say the first sound birthed the plates, and when the last note fades the salt-glass walls will shatter and the Basin will fly apart grain by grain.  Children learn to hum while falling asleep so the world will not slip into quiet.

The Overdark is whatever lies beyond the curved wall.  Some sects say they are in the lung of a god and the sparks outside are its other breaths; others that they are a seed-pod in the lab of remote Makers.  Pilgrims occasionally attempt to scale the wall.  None return, but sometimes a single smashed Name-Shard falls back, veined with unknown metals, and new schools of philosophy unfurl around the relic.

COEXISTENCE WITH OTHER LIFE

• Skimmer flocks  
  Look like drifting dandelion seeds unless caught in side light.  Children snare them with licked fingers (the moisture is scandalous and precious) and trade the wings as decorations.

• Plate Geckos  
  Sacred as luck bringers.  A gecko on your dwelling during a thunder-flash means your next egg will hatch strong.  No Resounding will harm one, but some raise whisper-pens to encourage them to stay near.

• The Banners themselves  
  Considered elder cousins: half garden, half archive.  Every time the wind rasps a notch along a banner edge it leaves a groove; experienced Scribes “read” decades of weather in those grooves the way dendrochronologists read tree rings.

DANGERS

• Blue Lances  
  The ion flashes overhead.  Roughly twice a local century one does touch ground, boring a fist-wide hole through wall and plates.  The Resounding treat each scar as a sacred wellspring of fresh crystal but will not sleep near it: the quiet inside those new shafts is too deep.

• Plate Quake  
  A chain-crack running under the city can drop whole stacks into a crevasse.  Afterward, seed-crews are dispatched to lace banner roots across the gap; once the roots knit, new chambers are carved in the reinforced lip.

WHY THEY MATTER

Among all the catalogued pocket ecologies of the known universe, the Basin is the only one where complex, reasoning life has arisen without liquid water or deliberate engineering.  The Resounding prove that consciousness can root in glare and grinding noise as readily as it can in gentle rain and silence.  Their treaties are rhythms, their memories are grooves in living leaves, and when they wish to honor a dead friend they do not speak—they lean against a stalk, feel its resonance, and let the whole forest say the name in thunder.

Stand with them for a minute.  Close your eyes.  The sunlight still needles through your lids, the ground vibrates up your bones, and the conversation of a nearby child—three taps on a plate, a flutter of mirror-bright fingers—passes straight through your chest.  That is what it is to be Resounding: never alone, never quiet, alive as long as the Basin rings.
