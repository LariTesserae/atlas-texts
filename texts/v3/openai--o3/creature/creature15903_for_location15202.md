Who lives here?

1. Root-kin (the green majority)

a. Raft-ferns  
They are not single plants but floating republics: one mother rhizome and dozens of daughter fronds rafted together by spongy tissue.  Each “republic” creeps a few centimetres each year, stitching itself to its neighbours until an entire marsh bowl becomes a slow, breathing carpet.  When the noon rains hammer down, the fronds tilt and interlock, making gutters that sluice fresh water to the rhizome’s mouth.  Small animals take shelter in the gutter-maze; the fern keeps them as caretakers, rewarding them with sugars that bead on the underside of older fronds.  

b. Cork-barked trees  
The basin’s architects.  Their limbs bow until the tips almost drown, then sprout roots from the drowned bark and stand up again, layering the forest on their own shoulders.  Every century or so a trunk goes hollow; water fills the cavity and anaerobic bacteria brew a faintly alcoholic sap.  Whole colonies of insects drink from these natural vats, weaving white paper walls around the opening to trap the vapour.  From below those walls look like cobweb gates trembling in permanent breeze.

c. Kapok-giants  
Only a few hundred still stand, survivors of lightning strikes and their own prodigious weight.  Where a limb finally loses its argument with gravity and crashes, the wound floods, fish arrive (carried as eggs on bird-feet) and a canopy pond flowers six stories above ground.  Lilies root in the rot, frogs rise through the xylem to breed, and dragonflies patrol a sky that never sees the real sky.  When the limb is fully consumed, the pond drains in a single green waterfall back to the marsh.

2. Soft-voiced animals

a. Bulb-frogs  
Each male hollows a reed, seals one end with resin, then drums with the hind-feet while inhaling warm mist through ballooned throats.  The pitch tells females two things: the frog’s size and the temperature of his chosen spring.  Eggs are glued to the reed’s inner wall; when they hatch the tadpoles eat the instrument, sentence finished.

b. Glass-wings  
Seen only as distortions—heat ripples with a glinting edge.  Thousands twist together in shoals that mimic flowing water, fooling the basin’s aerial predators into stooping at nothing.  At night they rest on the underside of kapok leaves where residual heat keeps their metabolism idling.

c. Ribbon gliders  
Squirrel-sized, otter-pelted, furious only in the silence of their going.  The loose skin between ankle and tail is translucent and striped, rendering them invisible against the clouded light.  They eat the honeyed threads braided by bladder-vines and repay the vine by carrying its sticky, mace-shaped seeds to distant trees.  A glider that misses its perch simply folds, splashes, swims to a root-island, and climbs to try again.

d. Ambush cats  
One sizeable predator does live here, but it is more patient than fierce.  Coal-black, body no larger than a housecat, it can lie motionless on a raft-fern for hours, nostrils just above water.  When heat dazes the basin into stillness, the cat strikes upward, seizing birds that skim the mist for insects.  A single leap a day keeps it in flesh; the rest of the time it sleeps coiled around a warm spring like a bracelet.

3. The small & necessary

a. Steam-spores  
Most ferns here release spores that ride their own evaporated moisture.  Under a hand lens each spore wears a crown of microscopic canals; as sunlight strikes, water trapped inside flashes to vapour and jets the grain aloft.  Billions of these hover all afternoon, manufacturing the pearled fog travellers swear is alive.

b. Rimstone shrimp  
Blind, red, translucent.  They live only in the thin curtains of water that sheet down the cliff bands.  Their entire world is two millimetres thick and flows past them forever; they survive by clinging with fan-shaped forelimbs that sieve silica and microbial fluff from the film.  Storms sometimes peel a whole veil away, carrying the shrimp out over the drop; when mist breaks against the talus below you can hear their shells crack like sleet.

c. Basalt worms  
Three metres below the surface, where hot groundwater meets cooler seepage, thin scarlet worms rasp iron from the basalt itself.  Their waste is ferric clay extruded into tubes that harden like ceramic.  These tubes knit into lattices that hold slopes in place.  A landslip here is less a collapse than a slow melt, because the worms never stop mortaring rock back together.

4. People (sporadic, uncertain)

No permanent culture endures on the plateau, but it is never entirely without humans.

•  Meteorologists land by winch once a year, install fresh sensor nodes, and leave with buckets of air so wet the lab scales them like syrup.  
•  Mycologists hike the cataract’s spray zone hunting extremophile yeasts for industrial bio-reactors below.  They talk in clipped whispers because the rim’s echo breaks sentences into ghost replies.  
•  Now and then a pair of iron-workers from the lowland foundries climb to taste green silence.  They bring nothing back but a private memory: standing waist-deep in warm mist while unseen frogs beat code into reeds.

The basin keeps them as politely as it keeps the cat or the shrimp—offspring of another biome, visiting, shedding a little skin, carrying a little pollen away.

5. Presences that are not individual

a. The Conversation  
Every drip, leaf-sigh, steam-pop, and echo belongs to a single composite rhythm that locals (all four of them) call the Conversation.  You do not hear it at first; after two days it edits your heartbeat; after a week you may catch yourself pausing mid-sentence to let a bulb-frog finish a phrase.  Leave, and factory clatter in the plains will sound off-tempo for months.

b. Heat  
Here heat behaves like a domesticated animal: it follows the river, curls in hollows, rears when the sun drives it, lies down when afternoon rain scolds it.  Anything living must learn its moods or be undone.

c. Machinery-ghost  
The wind ferries up a distant industrial hum, turning on and off with air-currents.  It never alters pitch.  Creatures have adapted: some frogs now tune their reeds a quarter-tone above the hum, some glass-wing shoals spiral exactly to its resonance node, and more than one researcher swears the rimstone shrimp snap their fans in sync with it.  Whether a real engine drones below or only the plateau amplifies a phantom, the ghost has joined the fauna.

6. Who rules?

No single species.  The place itself writes the terms: warmth, wetness, lull, shock of rain, soft dark.  To live here is to sign those terms in sweat.  Everyone—fern republic, ribbon glider, ambush cat, passing scientist—pays the same rent: brief motion, quiet hunger, and whatever trace of self can be left without tearing the fabric.

So the plateau’s census, if one could be taken, would read not as a list but as a chord: basalt tone, water murmur, root pulse, leaf light, breath of frog, lace of shrimp, whisper of distant turbines.  All resident, all necessary, none in charge.
