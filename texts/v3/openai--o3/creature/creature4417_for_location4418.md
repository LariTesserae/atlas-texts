WHO LIVES IN THE PLAIN OF RESONANCE  

I. The Breathwood (primary producers)  
1. Tower-Groves – The huge, hollow “trees” are not trees at all but colonial organisms, each trunk a stack of living glassy polyps fused around a rising spiral of capillaries. The green light that trembles inside them is bioluminescent algae cultured in slow-moving broth. Every dusk the tower opens slit-vents and exhales a warm breath rich in sugars and spores; a syrupy fog beads on nearby stone and feeds anyone able to drink vapour.  
2. Root-Choirs – Twenty or thirty towers share a single rhizome web. In the dark soil that web is studded with keratin plates that vibrate like speaker cones. When mist thickens, the whole choir hums to pump nutrients from one tower to the next: basslines you feel in your kneecaps. Grazers hear the song and know fresh spore-frost is forming above.  

II. Undergrowth that isn’t silent  
1. Litterglass – Short, hair-thin shards of silicate that sprout like grass after every silver rain. They last only a week before dissolving, but while they stand they rattle in eddies of air, creating a perpetual hiss that masks the footsteps of small animals.  
2. Quickroot Films – Microbial mats that glide across damp clay at a centimetre a day. Each mat carries its own ring of negative pressure, muffling nearby sound so nothing can locate it by echo. Quickroot is coveted by human architects: they seed it beneath new buildings to deepen the silence.  

III. The Micro-Choir  
1. Vibrio levitans – Engineered plankton that live in water pockets and keep themselves suspended by emitting 40-kilohertz clicks. They are the reason a spilled cup of surface water never lies still; it thrums like angry tea.  
2. Murmur Molds – Fungal threads that record sound as variations in internal tension. Colonists harvest mature hyphae, simmer them, and read the tension patterns as data—murmur broth is an edible archive.  

IV. Invertebrates & Small Vertebrates  
1. Pulsewing Moths – A metre across, black-opal wings curved into parabolic speakers. They fire triplets of metallic pings, then tilt to scan for the returning echo shimmer. During the falling hush they fold their wings and drop, becoming hand-sized, silent embers on the trunks.  
2. Basin Striders – Amphibious hexapods that paddle mirror-pans on float skins. Each foot carries a pressure gong; if a predator disturbs the water the gong booms and the whole raft of striders skips to shore on cavitation bubbles.  
3. Glass-Ridge Eels – Transparent serpents that live inside the hollow ridges. Their skulls contain quartz lenses that focus outside thunder into internal sonar bursts; they quite literally weaponise the landscape’s noise.  
4. Lavalume Frogs – In the gravity-lens ovals you find these creatures drifting like balloons, toe-pads extruding silken anchors to the ground. Their calls descend in pitch as they rise, a living barometer for people working nearby.  

V. Large Herbivores  
1. Thunderbacks – Six legs, low slung, a dome of bone over the chest. They drum that dome with an internal tendon, producing a pulse you can feel before you see them. Calves learn the family’s rhythm at birth; herds are recognised by beat, not scent.  
2. Umbra-Yak – Shaggy, half-ton browsers that tow a halo of condensation. Their fur is grooved, turning wind into a low chord that masks their chewing noises from predators. Colonists follow Umbra-yak migrations to map fresh forage zones.  

VI. Apex & Ambiguous  
1. Crown Sirens – Birdlike only in silhouette: elongated laryngeal sacs sprout along their necks like rosary beads. Instead of striking, a siren hovers above a victim and sings a polyphonic phrase that scrambles the prey’s inner-ear fluid; the animal staggers, and the siren feeds at leisure.  
2. The Chorus (questionably alive) – A distributed resonance that forms where many sound fields overlap. You notice it when distant calls suddenly resolve into deliberate harmony. Instruments left outside overnight sometimes return home playing themselves; no one has proved intention, but Horizon Span’s citizens greet the Chorus at dawn out of courtesy.  

VII. The People of Horizon Span  
Population: roughly 90,000 humans, almost all descendants of acoustic engineers and their support crews.  

1. Physiology & Gear  
• Ossicle Shields – Infants are implanted with micromesh around the tiny ear bones; the mesh stiffens under extreme pressure waves, preventing burst eardrums.  
• Throat Keys – Subdermal resonators in the sternum let a speaker lock onto specific harmonics; two people can murmur beneath a cyclone of noise and still hear each other.  
• Echo-Skin – Everyday clothing is woven from basalt fibre doped with piezo crystals; it sips ambient rumble and powers personal devices.  

2. Social Logic  
• The Quiet Debt – To occupy land you must return an equal measure of silence to the biome each year. Families keep ledgers of “hush minutes” earned by dampening machinery or planting absorption baffles.  
• Guilds  
  – Resonant Engineers tune buildings and glide-rafts.  
  – Mist Weavers maintain the laser filaments that carry data.  
  – Echo Cartographers hike with portable interferometers, updating the acoustic atlas all creatures share.  
• Ritual: The Hushwalk – During the daily hush everyone stops. Conversations cease, terraces empty, children press palms to the ground. When sound returns, the city answers with a single held note played from every rooftop: a pledge that Horizon Span remembers whose land it borrows.  

3. Economy  
• Sound-Farming – Arrays of flexible fins stand in the outskirts, vibrating like seagrass. Their motion is rectified into current and into raw waveforms sold to off-world studios craving “true exotic ambience.”  
• Spore Syrup & Murmur Broth – Combined, they are the staple diet: sweet with algal sugar, thick with data-fungus proteins. Recipes distinguish households more sharply than surnames.  

4. Art & Play  
• Whisper Glass – Thin panes that glow with coloured interference when exposed to specific chords. Street poets perform silent poems by waving tuning forks before suspended sheets that bloom with colour.  
• Levity Races – Held in dormant gravity lenses; contestants wear silk wings and vocalise to modulate lift. The finish line is audible before it is visible—race officials stretch a taut bronze wire whose hum drops in frequency as competitors approach normal gravity again.  

VIII. Symbioses & Edge Cases  
• Echo-Barnacles – Crusted along alloy walls, these bio-mechanical hybrids metabolise rust and exhale white noise. Colonists encourage them near bedrooms to guarantee privacy.  
• Float-Wasps – Nest around transmission filaments, borrowing laser heat to incubate larvae. In return they patrol the mist, stinging larger insects that might occlude the beams.  
• Phantoms of Quiet – When a tower-grove dies, its root-choir gives one last sub-basal moan that travels kilometres. In the following weeks animals avoid the site entirely, as if the soil itself is haunted by the remembered sound.  

IX. How to Meet the Locals  
1. Bring a pocket resonator tuned to 32 hertz; pulse it gently to announce harmless intent.  
2. Accept that a conversation may involve stretches of shared silence offered as payment into the Quiet Debt.  
3. Never mimic a Crown Siren phrase—doing so in city limits is both taboo and illegal, punishable by the confiscation of one’s throat key for a season.  
4. Before departure, step to the nearest mist-pool at dusk and exhale once. The pool will answer in your own timbre, assuring every listener, human or otherwise, that you were here and went in peace.  

These are the inhabitants of the humid plain: towers that breathe sugar and sing bass, insects that click like coins on stone, herds that drum their own thunder, and people who have learned to borrow silence the way others borrow water. Together they make a place that is never truly still, a living fugue scored across basalt and mist.
