WHO LIVES IN THE HOLLOW

I.  The Unseen Foundation  
1.  Condensate-film  
   • A skin of extremophile algae and chemotropic bacteria slicks every surface that receives the nightly drip.  Cells are colorless; only under angled light do they betray themselves as a faint opal sheen.  
   • They metabolize basalt, freeing iron that gives the puddles their metallic tang.  Every creature that drinks from the tarns carries that taste in its blood.  
   • When a ridge-lion pads across a film-rich ledge its paw prints luminesce a dusky blue for a heartbeat, then fade—a cold, slow phosphorescence that never quite becomes light.  

2.  Mist spores  
   • A near-invisible fungal diaspora drifts in the permanent haze.  Each spore is barbed like a seed of burdock but thinner than cobweb.  They hitchhike on wing, fur and breath, settling wherever condensation beads.  
   • Spores germinate into threads too small to see; the threads stitch themselves into the moss pads and knot-root bark, reinforcing any surface that will remain damp.  In this way fungus is everywhere, yet nowhere distinct.  
   • The spores’ only dramatic gesture is olfactory: when startled by vibration they release a spike of ozone-and-iron scent—an alarm to nothing in particular that nevertheless makes every animal pause.

II.  Insects, Arachnids and Other Small Voices  
1.  Gnat columns (locally “ropes”)  
   • The rope is a super-organism: neither species nor swarm but a rolling storm front of mixed midges, stone-flies and dust skeeters that breeds, feeds and dies within its own shifting mass.  
   • A rope’s flight path is governed by echo.  Each insect’s wings produce a harmonically tuned buzz; the combined chord bounces off cliffs, steering the column the way bats steer by sonar.  Mute a section with a cloth curtain and the whole rope will wobble and tear itself apart.  

2.  Drip-stitch spiders  
   • Abdomen thin as a needle, legs long and translucent.  They draw their silk horizontally between two droplets just before the droplets fall, snaring the bead in a gauze hammock that becomes a pearl of drinking water.  
   • Because they harvest water instead of prey, they ignore passing insects.  Their webs glitter with captured beads, so walkway ledges seem to host necklaces hung out to rinse.  

3.  Echo-beetles  
   • Wing-covers corrugated like organ pipes.  When disturbed they open their elytra and clap them shut, firing a rapid series of clicks that ricochet down corridors.  
   • Ridge-lions hunt by vibration; echo-beetles exploit this by swarming in sacrificial bunches.  While the lion investigates the false noise the beetles’ larvae chew peacefully at moss roots fifty paces away.  

III.  Amphibians and Tarn Dwellers  
1.  Pale-hand frogs (the fish-eaters)  
   • Skin waxy white on belly, granite-mottled on back.  Digits end in spatulate pads that leave perfect oval prints on mud, like coins pressed into clay.  
   • Have no lungs; they respire through skin and wide-lipped mouths, so when a frog calls it is literally exhaling its blood’s oxygen reserve.  A full-throated croak costs as much as a sprint.  
   • Tadpoles are carnivorous and will cannibalize any clutch laid more than a night earlier, hence all adults synchronize spawning to within minutes after the first thunder-roar of a distant ridge-lion.  

2.  Mirror eels  
   • Scale-less, covered instead in a mesh of mica dust electrostatically bound to mucus.  The dust makes them invisible from above: a tarn’s surface just shows more tarn.  
   • Unlike true electric eels they do not shock; they sense.  Each body holds a lattice of magnetite particles that pick up the minute fields generated by muscle contractions of prey above.  A single footstep on silt sets off a storm of invisible current and the eels converge like spirits rising through black glass.  

3.  Shriving shrimp  
   • Blind, bone-white crustaceans that cling to the undersides of ledges overhanging water.  They eat only shed skins—frog molt, eel slime, the occasional shred of ridge-lion paw-pad.  In doing so they keep the tarns immaculate.  
   • When supplies run low they swarm up the cavern walls in pale waterfalls, scouring whatever carcass or scat they find until nothing remains but scent.  First-makers say that to die unburied is to be “shrived,” absolved by shrimp.  

IV.  The Airborne Choir  
1.  Cliff swifts  
   • Iridescent black until they bank; then the hidden plumage under each wing flashes a scythe of pewter, the exact color of the Hollow’s light.  
   • Their echo-chirps are individually dull—stone on stone—but in flock formation each bird times its call to arrive half a beat after its neighbor’s rebound.  The effect is a rolling percussive wave that maps the void for them and deafens anything trying to listen for approach.  

2.  Mica bats  
   • Smaller cousins to surface bats.  They roost behind the same stone ribs that sieve the day.  The ribs catch capillary moisture; the bats lap droplets from their own wingtips before launching.  
   • Their guano is so loaded with ground mica and iron that it shimmers like black sand.  First-makers use it to fill reed hour-glasses; the sift of glittering grains against glass marks time in a world where the sun cannot.  

V.  Land Mammals and Other Walkers  
1.  Blind cavies  
   • Fur charcoal-grey, flecked with quartz dust they pick up while rooting.  The flecks reflect just enough light that a herd can look like a low, moving constellation.  
   • They honk by forcing air through enlarged sinuses; the pitch tells other cavies what the soil tastes like ahead, a map broadcast by smell translated into sound.  

2.  Shard serpents  
   • Limbless predators camouflaged as the broken basalt knives that litter the flats.  Scales terminate in true edges; a startled serpent can straighten and vibrate so rapidly it murmurs like a sawblade.  
   • Venom is non-lethal but narcotic; ridge-lions are occasionally found sprawled in mild stupor after using a serpent as a chew-toy.  The lions remember the experience and will seek it out when injuries keep them from hunting.  Thus pain management has evolved symbiotically: fang for claw.  

3.  Ridge-lion  
   • Shoulders the height of a tall man’s waist, massy as three.  Coat of overlapping matte and glossy hairs that scatter rather than reflect light—one more strategy to remain a rumor until too late.  
   • The keratin scutes along its tail act like guitar frets.  Passing air plucks them into a infrasonic growl the lion can modulate by arching or straightening the tail.  From fifty paces the sound feels like pressure in one’s chest more than noise in one’s ears; prey panics without knowing why.  
   • Breeds once every six years when condensing mist is thickest.  Mated pairs carve a private pocket of silence by roaring in tandem until all echo within their chosen alcove is in phase cancellation—a cone of hush in the loudest place imaginable.  The cubs are born into that hush and associate quiet with safety for the rest of their lives.  

VI.  The First-Makers  
1.  Morphology  
   • Height no more than a child of the plateau folk, but barrel-ribbed, all lung and calf muscle.  Shag-fur pallid at the tips like rock moss; naked face painted with the same charcoal-mica guano they use for timekeeping.  
   • Eyes hold a reflective layer of guanine crystals—catshine—optimized for twilight.  Their irises glitter silver when angered, blue when amused; the plateau traders call them “laughing mirrors.”  

2.  Subsistence  
   • Diet is 40 % frog, 30 % larval insect, 20 % bat and swift eggs, 10 % whatever a sling stone can reach.  They eat no cavy: cavies honk warnings that help the First-Makers avoid ridge-lions, so the little ungulates are considered “ bell-keepers” and left in peace.  
   • Fire is struck from pyrite nodules found in shale seams.  Because sparks are scarce they maintain “wander-brands”—slow-burning torches of knot-root pitch carried everywhere.  At camp they plant the brands upright so embers waft skyward in a column; from a distance one can tell a hunt’s progress by watching that faint ember ladder migrate along the cavern floor.  

3.  Social and Ritual  
   • Bands average nine individuals, always odd—an old taboo claims the Hollow dislikes symmetry.  When a death or birth upsets the count, members split or merge with neighboring bands within a day.  
   • Language is mostly whistle-clicks pitched to ride the gnat rope’s drone; outsiders hear only insects.  Names are chords rather than syllables.  A ridge-lion gains a new name every time its roar acquires a fresh echo, so after several years a single lion may carry a name so long it takes a full breath to pronounce.  
   • The bone cairns mark “returns,” deaths the band believes will be reborn into the Hollow’s next geological age.  Bodies are excarnated first by shriving shrimp, then the polished bones stacked knuckle-to-knuckle to encourage future limbs.  

4.  Dreams and Fears  
   • They believe the stone ribs overhead are the fossil cage of a sky-beast that once tried to swallow the world; the narrow slots are where its teeth fell out.  When the final slot seals, the beast will wake.  Until then every act—hunt, birth, burial—is rehearsal for escape.  
   • Children are taught to mimic cavy honks, frog croaks, even echo-beetle clicks; to speak only in human voice is to stand alone before the beast.  Communion with the Hollow is camouflage.  

VII.  Ghosts of Sound  
   • In a realm so hard and echoic, resonance itself behaves like a tenant.  Certain alcoves hold “dead thunder,” the lingering low-frequency imprint of ancient quakes.  Step inside and your own pulse syncs to the phantom rumble; linger too long and you leave thinking the earth still trembles.  
   • First-Makers weave pilgrimages around these pockets, believing the thunder to be their unborn ancestors practicing heartbeats.  Ridge-lions skirt them—the cats hunt by felt vibration and find the false quakes maddening.  

VIII.  How It All Hangs Together  
   • The algae feed the gnats; the gnats feed the frogs; the frogs feed the lions and the First-Makers.  Shriving shrimp clean the remains; fungus binds the edges; echo carries warnings, lures, lullabies.  
   • Nothing here is silent unless it must be, nothing bright unless it serves stealth, nothing wasted because replenishment is slow.  Life in the Hollow is a single, recursive organ whose beat is the drip, whose voice is the chorus of wings, whose vision is the shard of daylight filtering down the fractured throat of earth.
