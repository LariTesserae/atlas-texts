WHO LIVES HERE

1.  The Veil  
Before you notice animals you meet the Veil: a bloom of chemolithotroph bacteria so fine it is mistaken for water itself.  Each cell carries an iron-binding pigment that turns hydrothermal ions into food and, as an after-effect, sheds a single photon in the red end of the spectrum.  From human eyes the glow is imperceptible, but the creatures of the chamber see by it the way surface birds read ultraviolet on a flower.  The Veil drifts everywhere, settles on everything, and is everything’s first meal; if a surface biologist insisted on kingdoms and phyla the Veil would shrug and reply that down here it is simply weather.

2.  Glass Shrimp (adult name: Articulata lucerna)  
Look for tiny sparks sliding over stone and you will find a Glass Shrimp procession.  
•   Anatomy: six pairs of legs, the first two modified into combs that groom the Veil into mouth-parts.  The shell is 97 % silica; only the hinge joints retain chitin, thin as parchment.  
•   Color: whatever they last ate.  A shrimp fresh from a vent glimmers copper; one that has grazed a fallen Hollow Fish shows a faint opal cloud where fats have been stored in pockets along the abdomen.  
•   Behavior: pilgrims more than foragers.  They keep the carved staircase free of sediment, not for their own ease—shrimp happily climb anything—but as an inherited ritual none remembers beginning.  When an individual surpasses three years it walks one final circuit, stands atop the highest lintel, folds its limbs, and stops.  Over months the silica carapace fuses to obsidian and becomes another node in the balustrade.  Count the railings and you count the dynasty.

3.  Chamber Lamps (Order: Photocardia)  
A fist-sized mollusk glued upside-down to vaulted ceiling stone.  
•   Mantle: thin enough to let the heart show.  Blood pulses in a figure-eight loop; the outer loop houses symbiotic bacteria that flash ochre whenever fresh hydrothermal plume passes over the gills.  
•   Feeding: never moves; cilia churn all day, drawing the Veil toward a mouth that looks like the iris of a shuttered eye.  
•   Startle response: the mantle cinches shut, the light dies, and every animal in view goes still, as if night has been declared by decree.  Minutes later the Lamp relights, the nave brightens, and life resumes as if someone restarted time.

4.  Hollow Fish (Genus: Cathedra)  
Imagine a ribbon of vertebrae enclosed in living parchment.  
•   Skeleton: latticed towers of calcium-fluorapatite, each vertebra a miniature spoked wheel.  Internal cavities collect warm water; the fish is literally buoyed by its own heat.  
•   Mouth: an open funnel, always pointing downward.  It cannot close; digestion happens on a slow fall of micron-sized iron particles that stick to a mucus net strung across the throat.  
•   Reproduction: pairs spiral around one another for days, weaving yolk pearls into the cavities of their partner’s bones.  When the adults die the skeleton floats off like a dandelion clock, spilling fry in every direction.

5.  Listener Crabs (Species group: Aurescale)  
Seen edge-on they are as thin as shale.  Seen face-on they are a shield.  
•   Shell: grown in concentric ridges tuned to infrasonic frequencies.  A distant tectonic groan makes the ridges hum; the crab follows the vibration the way surface moths fly up a pheromone gradient.  
•   Purpose: to arrive first when new cracks open and fresh minerals bloom.  Where two Listener Crabs meet they duel by pressing shell to shell, each trying to damp the other’s resonance until one cannot hear the feast and wanders away deaf.

6.  Choir Eels  
The only obvious predator.  They hunt by song.  
•   A single lung-sac extends the length of the body and can be squeezed like a bellows against the swim bladder.  The note is sub-audible to humans, thick enough to push against flesh.  
•   Small animals freeze; larger ones feel the vibration map the cavities inside them and turn, confused, toward the sound.  The eel takes whatever drifts closest, unhurried, swallowing until the song is muffled and the victim is gone.  
•   One Choir Eel per chamber is the norm.  Any newcomer is sung at until its own resonance shatters—predation by music.

7.  Reliquary Turtle  
Rare, enormous, older than the flood.  Carapace the same obsidian as the walls, etched by the same long erosion.  
•   When it rests you might mistake it for a collapsed column.  Limbs fold inward, the exposed skin inflates with warm water to match buoyancy, and the whole beast drifts just enough to erode evenly on all sides.  
•   Diet: dead stone.  A rasped tongue scrapes microscopic shards of glass, ingests them, and relies on gut bacteria to leach silicates and trace metals.  
•   Once every century the turtle lays one egg the size of a melon, mortar-white, in a niche above a vent spout.  Fewer than a dozen eggs are thought to exist at once; each is guarded by the Choir Eel of that room, a pact no one remembers forging.

8.  Chime Coral  
Not a plant, not a true coral, but a colony of metalloid worms that extrude silica-magnesium tubes in branching tiers.  
•   Every tube terminates in a flared lip; currents gust through the forest and the coral sings, each aperture a different pitch.  The combined chord is what the Listener Crabs are born hearing inside their shells.  
•   Dead Chime Coral drops like dry leaves, creating drifts that settle on shrines or steps.  Glass Shrimp harvest these drifts; Chamber Lamps incorporate the flakes into new shell; the circle closes.

9.  Mythic Occupant: The Archivist  
No bone, no blood, perhaps not alive at all.  It is a lenticular cloud of silicate nanofibers—descendants of fungal hyphae—that inhabits the deepest ambulatory coils.  
•   Function: to print memory onto stone.  Where carving was once made by hand, the Archivist now continues by etching molecules away grain by grain, copying the pulse patterns it intercepts in water.  A Choir Eel’s song, the tick of a crab’s foot, even the distant tectonic murmur—all are filed onto the walls as layers of microscopic stipple.  
•   Longevity: its oldest records are already eroding, but the Archivist works faster each century, adding new grooves as stone thins.  When all matter finally wears flat, the chamber will be a hollow of pure narrative.

TROPHIC ORDER (HOW THEY INTERLOCK)

1. Thermal vent → mineral fog → Veil  
2. Veil → Glass Shrimp, Chamber Lamps fry, Hollow Fish larva  
3. Sediment & carcass fall → Listener Crabs, mature Chamber Lamps, juvenile Choir Eels  
4. Listener Crabs, Hollow Fish, Chamber Lamps → adult Choir Eels  
5. Obsidian & coral ruins → Reliquary Turtle  
6. All death, all waste → Chime Coral forest → again to Veil

SEASONS & RITUAL

There are no seasons of light, only seasons of tremor.  Every 41 months a lunar tide lifts the ocean just enough to squeeze fresh seawater across the chamber threshold.  The sudden chill and salinity shift trigger two events:

•   Chamber Lamps broadcast in cadence, synchronizing heartbeats until the whole ceiling flickers like distant lightning.  Egg threads are released and stick to any hard surface, where they spend three years filtering Veil before budding into new Lamps.

•   Listener Crabs hold a symposium.  They climb to the tallest lintels, press shell to shell in linked circles, and resonate until cracks appear in the obsidian.  The flakes that rain down seed new Chime Coral.  By week’s end the symposium disperses; the wall is smoother than before.

INTIMACY WITH THE PLACE

Touch the stone and microtremors announce your arrival to every Listener Crab within ten meters.  Your exhalation becomes a pearl the Chamber Lamps extinguish to study.  The Veil tests the salt of your skin, decides you are neither feast nor threat, and resumes its drifting.  Even the Archivist absorbs the frequency of your heartbeat; somewhere, in a spiral hallway you may never reach, a line of pin-prick dots begins to spell your story.

No creature rushes you, none flees.  Life here has practiced patience into instinct—an ecosystem calibrated to the tempo of erosion.  Stay an hour and you will see very little.  Stay a week and you might witness a single Hollow Fish hatch.  Stay a lifetime, and you, too, will round at the edges, trade hurry for drift, become another smooth curve folded into the drowned cathedral’s liturgy of hush and weight.
