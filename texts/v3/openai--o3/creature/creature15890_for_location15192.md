They do not live in isolation; nothing in the basin can afford that. Every creature, every stem, every tool-user is stitched into the next by need so constant that identity itself has become a sort of braid. Still, it is possible—if you watch an entire day seep past—to tease the strands apart and see who is moving through the dusk.

1. The Root-Drinkers (plants that drink heat)

a. Glass-cane  
• Trunks: hollow, six-sided, jointed like bamboo but sheathed in mica platelets that throw back enough sunlight to keep tissues cool.  
• Roots: single tap claws that probe thirty, forty meters down until they reach cracks warm enough to steam. The cane’s vascular bundles carry vapor upward; a faint hiss plays inside every stem, eerie in dawn’s silence.  
• Flowers: once a century, explosive. Whole copses vent lavender pollen at once—so much that the locals call the event “purple noon” even though it seldom lasts an hour.

b. Oil-vine  
• Looks limp, never is. Tendrils are leather hoses packed with triglyceride sap the color of garnet. The vine hoards calories the way desert succulents hoard water.  
• When a tendril is severed it seals by crystallizing its own oil into a waxy scab. The people harvest the runoff before it sets, pressing it into lamp cakes.  
• Seed pods ride the thermals like paper balloons, drifting until night chill freezes their membranes and the balloon pops. Wherever the seed lands, if it finds warmth, a new anchor begins.

c. Maw-frond  
• A single broad lamina, auburn at sunrise, burgundy by noon. Its under-surface is studded with tiny doors: stomata that hinge shut when interior humidity drops.  
• Each dusk, when vapor flash-condenses in the air, the frond reopens, drinking the dew in a frenzy that sounds like cloth tearing.  
• Dead fronds are as flexible as leather; villagers tan them in smoke pits and sew water skins that never actually carry water—only oil or resin.

2. The Air-Engine (insects, so many you measure time by them)

a. Humshells  
• Coin-sized beetles whose elytra are tuned like organ reeds. Each beat drags air through spiracles that whistle, creating a background chord in B-flat that never stops.  
• Larvae live in communal “nursery knots,” balls of chewed cane glued to the underside of stems. The knots are prized kindling; a single spark makes them bloom into steady flame.

b. Glider mantis  
• Wings: membranous delta kites, 80 cm tip to tip. Camber is adjustable; veins stiffen or slacken as the insect warms, letting it surf the afternoon updrafts without a single stroke.  
• Eyes: eight, four major, four pinhead. They see the ultraviolet signature of sap—hence their raids on oil-vine bladders and village cistern tops.  
• Strike: silent until the last meter. When it hits, forelimbs scissor with a crack like dry wood under a boot. Villagers use mantis leg-blades as shears.

c. Ember-moths  
• No larval stage. They bud from spore clusters in the cane litter, fully winged, and live only twenty hours.  
• Scales contain exothermic salts; when dawn chill fades, the salts ignite gently, keeping the moth at operating temperature. To predators that heat shimmer looks like desert mirage, buying the moth its fleeting hours of courtship.  
• When they die they collapse into curls of fragrant smoke, the basin’s closest thing to incense.

3. The Ground-Silks (night soil of velvet legs)

a. Blind velvet centipedes  
• One meter long but light as balsa; the exoskeleton is suede-soft, lacking hard cuticle so it can squeeze between the narrowest thermal vents.  
• Silk glands ring the first thoracic segment. During molt the centipede weaves a hammock of tensile fiber, suspends itself, and walks out of its old skin.  
• The silk is waterproof, acid-proof, and coveted. Two cocoons’ worth trades for a copper knife.  

Behavior: Little aggression unless cornered. They hunt by listening; the soft body lets them press entirely against the ground and read footfalls through dust. A walking human is a peal of thunder; the centipede retreats. A sleeping human, heartbeat slow, is sometimes mistaken for carrion. Hence the stilts.

4. The Sky-Scavengers

Cliff-hens (feral descendants of import chickens)  
• Breastbone has grown keel-heavy to anchor the new flight muscles. Actual flight remains a burst affair: sixty meters, then a controlled tumble onto whatever looks breakable.  
• Feathers: ash gray with resin droplet shine—natural weatherproofing against midday sap rain.  
• Diet: anything. They peck dead insects off fronds, yank seedlings from hot dust, steal fistfuls of boiled grain if a villager looks away.  
• Social: Harems guard vertical territory. At sunrise roosters circle the rim in spirals, screaming like rusty hinges, mapping the wind for the day’s thermals.

5. The Stone-Born (people who forgot how to say “we came from elsewhere”)

They call themselves the Amas. The word’s root is the same one they use for “centerpiece of a loom,” suggesting they conceive of the basin as fabric and themselves as its shuttle.

Physiology  
• Stature: condensed. Knees flex outward like a sprinter’s, because every path climbs. Lungs broad, nostrils narrow slits—a convergent adaptation with the cliff-hens, though neither species thanks the other for the idea.  
• Skin: muted umber that flakes if washed with liquid water; they bathe in dust, polishing the outer keratin into a sheen that throws off insects seeking salt.  
• Vision: dusk-tuned. Their pupils oval vertically, admitting maximum skyglow while shading direct glare from above.

Clothing and Ornament  
• Everyday wear: feather cloth, a felted matte weave of cliff-hen down bonded with oil-vine latex. Holds warmth until the bearer sweats, then channels vapor outward before it can be lost to the air.  
• Armored sleeves: interleaved mantis chitin over bark linen—mandatory for anyone entering raw cane stands.  
• Decoration: not color (that fades) but geometry. Bracelets of nested hexagons echo cane joints, believed to bring structural luck.

Dwellings  
• Built sideways into the mountain, not upward. The outer wall is always living cane; as stalks grow, occupants plane them from inside, renewing floors yearly.  
• Roof terraces double as drying racks. Strips of frond leather, slices of mantis thorax, pages of bark paper (inked with resin-soot) bake in the sun.  

Food  
• Morning: dust-baked flatbread mashed from spore meal, eaten with vinegar fermented from leaf oil.  
• Midday: little; metabolism slows in the heat tide. They chew cooling pellets—gel plugs made from dewfall, swallowed before the moisture can evaporate from tongue to throat.  
• Night: stew thick as tar—centipede meat, roasted humshell larvae, and cane pith boiled down until it stains bowls a permanent umber.

Tools  
• Cutting: obsidian shards hafted to mantis foreleg handles. The edge dulls fast against silica skins, but replacement stone is plentiful in rim scree.  
• Binding: tendon rope from cliff-hen legs, then sheathed in silk—stays supple even after months of dessication.  
• Fire: quartz strike plates married to pyrite anvils. Sparks sink into resin bowls where powdered ember-moth scale sits waiting; it flashes like flash paper, igniting cane-coal.

Social weave  
• Population is capped by charter—fifty adult voices. When births tip the number higher, someone volunteers the Long Walk: tethered to a glide wing made of stitched cliff-hen primaries, they leap the eastern wall at dawn. Most perish in the cloud maelstrom; a myth persists that a few found another pocket basin and started a mirror hamlet.  
• Conflict: bark-shield duels fought on the Terrace of Equal Sound. Only head blows count; the ringing crack must be audible over the insect drone or the point is void. A contest rarely lasts more than three strikes—ears cannot stand the din.  
• Courtship: two partners build a scale model of their hoped-for household entirely from humshell casings. The fragility is the test: if the model survives one full midday whirl of wind, they wed.

Belief  
• The Rim is the World’s Eyelid. Day is the eye open, night the eye shut; dawn and dusk are blinks. Anything that breaks the rhythm (gas vents, purple noon) is interpreted as the Eyelid twitching in thought, and must be answered with chant.  
• Ancestors are vertical, not temporal. Your parent becomes your “Below One,” their parent “Below Two,” and so on. Memory is imagined as depth, not distance, fitting for people who drink heat from the crust.

Language  
• Consonants carry meaning; vowels carry mood. Every noun exists as a string of clicks and throat pops; once you choose the accompanying vowels you declare how you feel about it. Outsiders, the few who visit, are forever offending by giving cheerful vowels to solemn words.

6. Invisible Lives

The things too small for tools or names:

• Grease lichen that floats like gray lace in afternoon haze, photosynthesizing on sunlight bounce and exhaling faint terpene that keeps glass-cane from fungal disease.  
• Micro-mites that swim through oil-vine sap, shaping its viscosity; without them the vine would gum itself solid and die.  
• A bacterium that colonizes cliff-hen feathers, one of the only organisms here that actually needs liquid water. It survives by metabolizing the droplet of dew that forms nightly at the base of each feather’s barb.

7. Ghosts of Stone

None living, precisely, yet presence lingers.

Tremor vents: narrow slits exhaling breaths only a degree warmer than the ambient dust. The Amas swear sometimes those breaths form half-heard speech. They leave offerings—splinters of obsidian, a pinch of crushed moth—on vent lips. If by morning the gifts are gone (often merely scattered by wind) they say the mountain has eaten and will sleep another season.

8. How it All Stays Balanced

Energy rises from depths, captured by root-drinkers, turned to oil, stolen by insects, who feed cliff-hens, who fertilize cane with droppings, which feed more root-drinkers. The Amas tap every tier: they drink the hens, wear the insects, burn the oil, yet return their own dead to crevasses so the centipedes and bacteria can start the alchemy anew. Nothing exits the basin except wind-borne seeds and the rare Long Walker—thread snipped from the braid to keep the weave tight.

Stand long enough in the dim under-canopy and you will feel it: not a collection of discrete species but one engine whose parts happen to have feathers, leaves, mandibles, words. Life here has no edges. It is a single, intricately jointed creature humming to itself atop a mountain spine, holding fast against a sky that would strip it bare if ever the hinge of cooperation failed.
