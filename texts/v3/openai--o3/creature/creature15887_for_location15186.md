WHO – AND WHAT – MAKES ITS HOME IN THE DRY GLADE

1. The Slow Garden Itself  
Every sheet of lily-skin, every wick-vine joint and copper needle is a node in one seamed organism—an archipelago of species that trade vapours the way ordinary forests trade water. They share a single, diffused intelligence that wakes only when someone walks the aisles. Footfall rattles the tiles, pressure waves conduct along resin pipes, and the garden assembles a momentary mind from distributed pulses of hydrocarbon. You meet it as fragrance first: a syllable of scent that swells to a phrase, then fades. Linger and the scents braid into sentences—curt, interrogative, always ending on the sharp, peppery note that means why are you wet?

2. The Mote-Eaters (work caste)  
a. Anatomy. Six limbs, three mouths: front pair of mandibles to rasp clay, ventral lips that sip mineral dust, posterior funnel kept for mating calls (one per lifetime).  
b. Craft. They are the Glade’s potters. What they scrape they later exude as fresh slip, patching cracks from the underside so light never exposes the floor’s private darkness.  
c. Social rhythm. Colonies keep a time standard older than the wall: every 10 543 heartbeats a single eater ascends, clicks once, and dies. The corpse is quickly powdered and stirred into resin grout—memory recycled into mortar.

3. Ribbon Gliders (scavengers, messengers)  
Not lizard so much as aerodynamic tongue. Cartilage spars span a membrane that drinks heat directly; blood would only slow evaporation. They courier spores from pillar-broom to pillar-broom, polishing needles as they land. Each flight redraws geometry: corridors compress for a breath so the glider’s glide is always exactly twice its body length. When a visitor notices the trick, the glider stares back through lidless eyes as if annoyed you have made the world do arithmetic.

4. Amber Gnats (choir)  
By day they swarm in perfect silence, auditioning resins with taste-hairs too fine to see. At dusk—the perpetual noon dips once every nine subjective days, a thing you feel rather than observe—they sing. Wingbeats heterodyne against the natural hum of any metal object you carry; knives become tuning forks, buckles throb with contralto resonance. The harmony discourages predators and persuades resin bulbs to stay fluid a little longer. In that thinned music even your pulse forgets its metronome and stumbles.

5. The Salt-Borne (people, of a sort)  
Origins. Carvers who raised the rim were ordinary masons until brine etched their fingerprints into the bricks and fed the kiln-heat back up their arms. Now only crusted silhouettes remain: statues that disarticulate at the joints and reassemble where shade is needed.  
Physiology. No muscles—only plates of evaporite sliding on beds of powdered mica; iodine veins keep them purple-bright and flexible. They hate straight edges (salt cleaves on planes) and will spend whole seasons kneading a square into a near-circle with fingertip pressure alone.  
Customs. To greet one, offer a handful of perfectly dry sand. They pour it through joint-gaps like a libation, abrading old surfaces so new memory can set. Speak water and the gesture is reversed: they bury your words grain by grain until the utterance can never condense again.

6. The Namesmiths (rare, visiting, maybe you)  
Only outsiders bring copper or brass, and only outsiders can nail a syllable to space. A Namesmith walks bent beneath the resonance of their own tools, muttering trinomials that pin aisles long enough to traverse. Every so often a Namesmith miscounts. Geometry folds, vowel collides with vowel, and the smith is absorbed—reissued days later as a maze-echo: a faint, recursive footstep you never quite catch up to.

7. Fugitive Waters (predator, concept)  
Water cannot exist here, yet it hunts. Most often it arrives as the memory of thirst: your throat constricts, you swallow a name, and the swallowed word liquefies to sabotage the tiles beneath you. Fail to spit the word out in time and the place defends itself: pillar-brooms twist to lance the droplet with oil-rich needles, wick-vines flood the gap with solvent perfume, and the offending syllable dries to a harmless crystal tear. Sometimes the tear hums for years—an arrested apology to the floor.

8. The Clay Below (foundation species)  
Lift a plate (if the plate lets you) and you meet the oldest residents: impressions of unborn fingertips, ridges of impossible palms. They press upward from the obverse side of time, trying shapes against the under-surface until one fits and is accepted as history. Occasionally your own print is waiting. Place your hand to it and the floor feels warm, as though congratulating you on work you have yet to complete.

9. Symbiosis, Contest, Equilibrium  
• Plants exhale aromatics the animals can digest; animals repay by sanding leaves free of settled dust.  
• Salt-Borne moderate light by relocating columns, guiding ribbon gliders into shadow nets that temper perpetual noon.  
• Namesmiths introduce structure, which the garden slowly unknots; both acts keep the other from perfection, and so from death.  
• Fugitive Waters test every bond. Without that threat, resin would ossify, lilies would cease their pulse, and time might finally straighten the corridors.

10. What Passes for Conscious Thought  
Add together:  
– the click-death of a mote-eater,  
– the salt-Borne’s grainy shuffle,  
– a chorus of gnats tuned through bronze,  
– the inhalation of lilies,  
and you get the Dry Glade dreaming. It is not a single mind, nor many, but a pressure toward pattern that recruits every resident. Enter, and the dreaming includes you: moisture evaporates from your skin to sketch faint letters in mid-air; the garden reads them, decides whether you are phrase, footnote or erasure, and lives on, impossibly young, exquisitely preserved, forever rewriting its own inhabitants line by meticulous line.
