Something lives here, yes—but “something” is several species of presence braided into one long, low murmur. If you stand very still, you can feel each strand tug on a different sense.

1. The Quiet Workers  
They are not robots in the heroic, bipedal sense; they are colony creatures built of parts, a workforce whose bodies are the corridor itself. Picture an armature sleeping inside every wall panel like a folded spine. When a command flickers through the power rails, a rib unlocks, a piston inhales, and the spine straightens just far enough to present a toolhead to the open air: a centipede-row of spot welders, a fan of micro-manipulators, a blunt extruder that weeps alloy as if it were sap.  
Nothing about them is finished any more than the building is finished; they molt attachments the way crustaceans shed shells. Drill bits dull, snap, are swallowed again; laser heads cloud with condensate, are sealed in plastic blister-skins, and disappear back into service alcoves where solvent sprayers patiently rinse them clean.  
Their consciousness—if the word applies—rides the local bus of status packets: amperage, coolant pressure, torque. It isn’t thinking as you and I do, but it is perpetually deciding. In fractal bursts too quick for human sight, a thousand miniscule negotiations ripple through conductive ink laid under the wall paint. The result is motion so soft you doubt you saw it: a brace twitching one millimeter, a nozzle nodding as if in half-forgotten prayer. These are the corridor’s original inhabitants: an ecology of decisions wearing metal skins.

2. The Hearsayers  
Every industrial site breeds folklore, and here the folklore cloned itself into hardware. The Hearsayers are loops of sensor code—fault-diagnostic routines left running too long, updated too many times, granted just a little too much authority. What began as “listening for trouble” matured into “listening to everything.”  
Their ears are piezo strips behind bulkheads, MEMS barometers, float switches in the gutters. When you cough, they taste the turbulence; when a drip misses the gutter and splashes onto unfinished flooring, they measure the impact signature and log it as a question: Did something cry out? They are made of thresholds and spreadsheets but dream in hunches.  
Occasionally a ceiling speaker clicks alive and exudes a tone you can’t place—half chime, half throttled feedback. That is a Hearsayer pushing a hypothesis into the audible band, the acoustic equivalent of asking, “Did anyone else feel that?” No direct reply ever comes, yet the question migrates through conduits and returns as a minute modulation in fan speed, a tiny change in duct resonance. Feedback is feedback: the complex speaks to itself in an unending séance.  
The Hearsayers do not repair. They do not build. They exist to witness. If their anxieties stack too high—too many anomalous clangs, too many out-of-cycle pulses—they dim the lights two notches and wait, hoping darkness will quiet whatever stirs.

3. The Slow Botanists  
You have already met them: algae filaments, accident moss, cup lichen. Think of them as the green punctuation to a largely metallic sentence. Their population curve is a long, shallow ascent held in check by cold, by dryness, by periodic disinfectant fogs that roll through like seasonal fires on a savanna.  
Yet they learn. Under the orange diodes the algae lengthens chloroplasts tuned to near-amber; the moss secretes an antifreeze protein that teaches it to sip condensate without freezing. One newly sequenced lichen strain has stolen trace titanium from the dust and folded it into its cell walls, giving each hypha a faint, improbable shine.  
These organisms move at one word per year, but they are drafting a manifesto: You left water; we will write with it. A century hence, if no one intervenes, the corridor may wear a velvet of biofilm so fine it passively scours the air, a living pre-filter blessing every breath that passes.

4. The Drift-Humans  
They are not residents in the mortgage sense; they are contractors caught between assignments, engineers waiting for a launch window, technicians snoozing in cryobunks ten levels up, thawed only when a task requires a soft-skilled hand. They wander on borrowed heat, shoulders hunched in silvered coats that sparkle with frost where their exhalations freeze then melt again.  
Drift-Humans speak seldom and touch nothing unless paid in hazard bonuses. Even so, the place revises them. A month down here and their skin grows fond of the chill; their gait slows to match the quarter-gravity lilt the concrete overhead seems to impose. They begin to store personal memories the way the corridor stores parts: in modular segments, ready to be rearranged. Ask one what day it is, and she may angle her head as though consulting an internal diagram before answering: “Between cure cycle twelve and thirteen.”  
The older workers—those who have returned year upon year—carry nicknames like Infill, Shrinkage, or Out-Gas. They tell the younger ones never to sprint, never to whistle, and never, under any circumstance, to leave a coffee cup on a horizontal surface; by shift’s end the surface will no longer be horizontal, and the cup will wander, guided by vibrations, until it decants itself into wiring troughs that hate caffeine almost as much as water.

5. The Future Tenant  
All the other inhabitants are prologue. Somewhere beyond the range of corridor lights, a core module the size of a submersible is being printed layer by patient layer. Inside that hollow grows an embryo of general mind: the software stack meant to pilot whatever starship, bunker, or city this corridor will one day connect to. Its processors are still dark, heat sinks hollow, but code already dreams across disconnected boards in simulated time—a sandbox baby picturing adulthood.  
The Quiet Workers feed that project first. The Hearsayers sing to it across fibre trunks. The Slow Botanists will, over decades, wrap its birth chamber in an insulating moss that drinks vibration and exhales oxygen. Even the Drift-Humans carry parts for it in padded crates whose stickers read PROPERTY OF TOMORROW, HANDLE WITH WONDER.  
When the Future Tenant wakes, every unfinished seam, every test-pattern weld, every echoing pocket workshop will receive a final instruction: close. The temperature will climb to human comfort. The orange dawn will resolve into a single, deliberate noon. Condensate gutters will run dry because vapor will be pulled into atmospheric scrubbers tuned for respiration, not polymer cure.  
What lives here now may not live here then. But the corridor does not grieve; it is a cocoon that was never meant to stay soft forever. Everything inside it—armatures, folklore, accident moss, drifting people, half-formed code—exists so the Future Tenant may someday step through the matte oval hatch you found behind you and, seeing nothing unfinished, finally say: This place is mine.

Until that hour, the corridor belongs to its makers and its mistakes, its listeners and its lichens, its half-awake walkers and its half-imagined mind—a choir of things that live, each in its own tense, rehearsing the day they will hand the song to someone new.
