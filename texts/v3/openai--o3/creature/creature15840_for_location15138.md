Who or what calls this sky-soldered continent home?  
Answering that is like answering “Who lives on Earth?”—the population is layered, braided, slowly entwined with the terraces themselves. Nothing here stands wholly apart from the fabricated landscape; every creature, person or mechanism is a life-form of one sort or another, trimmed to fit the ledges. Begin at the broadest scale and descend.

----------------------------------------------------
1. The Slow People (sapient, patient)
----------------------------------------------------
Appearance and biology  
• Stocky limbs, long torsos, widened nasal passages and an extra pair of vestigial ribs: generations of thin-air childbirth have selected for oxygen thrift. Skin comes in kiln hues—brick, umber, oxidised copper—burnished by windborne dust.  
• Soles and palms are crosshatched with horn: children learn to climb rivet ladders before they can read. Eyes tend to amber; the iris produces extra pteridine to filter ultraviolet glare from all those heliostat mirrors.

Culture  
• They call themselves simply “folk of the work”—but outsiders tag them Stratasteaders. Their calendar is architectural: years are counted by how many courses of masonry rise on the newest platform. A sixty-year “lift” (the time it takes to add a full terrace layer) is the basic generation unit.  
• Writing is done on tin slates with stylus punches; paper mildews too fast in the humidity. But stories live in brickwork: every family is entitled to stamp one fired-clay emblem into the mortar of any wall they personally helped raise. A historian can read whole sagas just by walking a facade.  
• Religion, where it exists, venerates Continuance. No gods—only the conviction that to keep the structure sound is the highest devotion. Shrines are tool racks, candles are grease lamps, hymns are sung timetables.

Occupations  
1. Gardenwrights—design plots that can thrive on sun-bounce alone. They prune by convincing the slow automatons (see section 4) rather than wielding blades themselves.  
2. Wind-riggers—maintain crane lines and dirigible masts; they marry by trading knots, not rings.  
3. Reservoir Monks—tattoo rainfall ledgers over their whole bodies; when they die the skin is tanned and added to the Library of Water.

Disposition  
Deliberate, courteous, unhurried. Nothing in their world moves quickly except an occasional ibex or a falling brick, and falling bricks are rare.

----------------------------------------------------
2. Parabiotes (co-domesticated fauna)
----------------------------------------------------
Not pets, not livestock, but partner species intentionally introduced to occupy ecological crevices.

• Ladder-goats: The cliff-ibex mentioned in your dossier. Genetically drifted into three breeds—one for milk, one for wool, one nearly feral that clears gutter-weeds where machines can’t fit. They know the sound of a lunch bell as surely as any worker.  
• Verdigris Tortoises: Carapaces resemble copper roof tiles; they graze the thin algal sheen that would otherwise corrode condenser-reeds. Shell-mounted brass tags record which terrace “owns” each animal—nomadic maintenance you can eat in emergency.  
• Smoke Swifts: soot-coloured birds that nest in warm exhausts, intercepting sparks before they can start fires. A rigger on night shift reads the state of a boiler stack by listening to how the swifts mutter inside.

----------------------------------------------------
3. High-Air Strangers (episodic residents)
----------------------------------------------------
Dirigible Crews  
Lean, lantern-eyed traders who spend more time inside gasbags than on decks. Their speech carries helium squeak, and they count distance in kilopascals of lift, not kilometres. They dock, barter tungsten bearings for orchid pollen, swap rumours of ground—which few have seen—and depart on the same thermal column.

Sky-Menders  
Migrant bands of glider-winged technicians (the wings are fabric, the courage genetic). They service the heliostat mirrors from the windward face, living for weeks in canvas pods slung beneath overhangs. They drink condensed dew and speak a private cant: a whistle-click language designed to cut through turbine roar.

----------------------------------------------------
4. The Pruning Automatons (mechanical / quasi-sentient)
----------------------------------------------------
Brass-shouldered, piston-hipped, slow as a thinking tree. Officially they are “implements,” but three centuries of self-patching has wrought eccentric individuality.

• Each carries a seed core of rose-quartz memory plates. Patterns of wear accumulate as literal grooves; elders have grooves deep enough to hold rainwater.  
• They converse in key-clang and valve hiss. Gardenwright apprentices learn to interpret this “metal koan” so they may request a pruning or a graft.  
• Philosophers argue whether the automatons qualify as citizens. Verdict postponed—no hurry, as always.

----------------------------------------------------
5. Edge-Dwellers Below the Fogline
----------------------------------------------------
All sound is muffled down there, yet life persists.

• Pipe-Mice: Wire-chewing rodents already in your notes, but note the symbiosis—plumbers leave them waxy feed pellets laced with bioluminescent dye. Any gnawed conduit glows, advertising the leak before it floods a vault.  
• Gutter Eels: Transparent, rope-slender fish that survive inside kilometre-long gutter channels. Harvested once a year by children with strainer baskets; pickled, they taste of ozone and mint.

----------------------------------------------------
6. Micro-tenancy: invisible citizens
----------------------------------------------------
Rustlace—an iron-oxidising bacterium forms red arabesques under peeling paint. Gardeners purposely seed it where structural over-thickness can be shaved away; the bacteria “etch” the metal lighter without saw or torch.

Mercury Mould—thin white fuzz that breeds only in instrument dials. It dampens gauge-needle oscillations, turning a raw shake into a readable tremor. Wipe it away and every meter on the terrace jitters like a frightened pulse.

----------------------------------------------------
7. Social Geometry: how all these beings share the terraces
----------------------------------------------------
Space is altitude, not acreage; status is measured in “floors above fog.” A mid-tier baker outranks an uppermost wind-rigger only until a dirigible captain buys his bread; hierarchy loops like a Möbius strip.

Conflict is therefore vertical, not martial. Raise a terrace too quickly and the shade you cast could doom the orchard below—an act equivalent to war. Hence expansion treaties involve sun-mathematicians, glare lawyers, even tortoise herders whose grazers would lose copper-roof forage.

----------------------------------------------------
8. The Absent, Whose Shape Is Still Felt
----------------------------------------------------
Groundlings  
No confirmed sightings, yet every lullaby swears some people still walk on raw earth and plant seeds directly into soil. Children imagine them as reverse-mountaineers: faces smudged with loam, afraid of heights lower than a ceiling. The myth keeps terrace kids from leaning too far over railings—“Fall and you’ll land among Groundlings!”

The Mountain  
If any stratum of original geology remains, it is far below the living layers. Some say the mountain is alive, slowly growing back; others say it’s already hollow, a negative space inhabited by pressure itself. Either way it counts as a resident—the most patient resident of all.

----------------------------------------------------
9. What It Feels Like To Meet Them
----------------------------------------------------
A visitor fresh from ordinary gravity will notice the posture first: everyone, even the goats, moves as if expecting the ground to give way, knees flexed, balance dynamic. Then the slowness: conversations unspool between turbine thumps; pauses are not empty but filled with mechanical heartbeat. Finally the scent—a triptych of orchid honey, hot brass and stone lichens—clings to clothes for weeks after you leave.

Offer a terrace child a potted plant and she will turn the pot over, puzzled. Where is the bracket, the bolt? How does this thing keep from falling? Explain “rooted in soil” and watch her eyes widen as if you’d described breathing water.

----------------------------------------------------
10. In Sum
----------------------------------------------------
The inhabitants of the sky-tier range are less a population than an ecosystem of intentions—human, animal, microbial, mechanical—bonded by the shared agreement that nothing must ever topple. They inherit, occupy and slowly renew a habitat whose walls are their scriptures and whose balconies are their maps. To live here is to accept three articles of faith:

1. All heights are provisional ledges awaiting the next course of brick.  
2. Time is the gentlest force—unless you ignore it.  
3. Life, if allowed, will find a handhold in even the barest rivet seam and hang on, singing, until the sun burns out or the last terrace reaches the stars.

That is who lives here.
