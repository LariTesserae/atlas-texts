Inhabitants of the Unfinished Chamber  
(a field report that never quite stops being a lullaby)

0.  First Principle  
Nothing down here lives the way surface creatures understand living.  To persist inside a stone that is still dreaming of its final shape, the organisms learned to put away goals—no hunting, no monopolies, no sudden leaps of growth.  Instead they tuned themselves to the room’s metronome of drips and echoes and became functions of steadiness.  Everything that follows is better imagined as components of one instrument than as separate beasts.

1.  Root-lace Millipedes (Chordomille tenuior)  
•  Appearance:  Individual bodies are no thicker than embroidery thread, glass-clear except for a seam of copper-green gut that glows where the chamber’s light refracts through them.  
•  Habit:  They graze directly on the “youthful” ribs, rasping away the microscopic film of bacteria that would otherwise blur the bands of calcite.  Each footfall deposits an alkaline smear; over centuries that smear hardens, becoming the next millipede’s food.  
•  Locomotion & Gravity:  A millipede can suspend itself horizontally between two ribs by humming.  The note—always B-flat, 493 Hz—creates a standing vibration in the damp air that partly cancels its weight.  To sleep, it simply stops humming and lets gravity fold it back against stone.  
•  Social arithmetic:  Population never wavers from the precise number 16 384.  Larvae appear as needed if an adult goes missing; if the count rises, excess individuals quietly unspool into threads of gelatin that the snails later lick away.  Nobody has recorded the moment of self-dissolution—it happens in the split second between drips.

2.  Prism-shell Snails (Helix vitravia)  
•  Shell:  Five whorls, each wall no thicker than a soap bubble.  Manganese impurities lay a faint prism inside the glass; as the snail grazes, refraction projects roaming rainbows that map its path on the domed ceiling.  
•  Diet:  Not vegetation but music.  The snail’s radula bears silica teeth tuned to a pentatonic scale; it presses them to the limestone and “plays” a short phrase.  The stroke harvests trace elements loosened by sound alone—nothing is visibly scraped.  When two melodies clash, the weaker snail shifts key rather than fight.  
•  Architecture:  The mica tower they keep rebuilding is less monument than metronome.  Incoming drips plink off its edges in a specific rhythm, and that beat regulates every snail’s feeding cycle.  Should a visitor knock the tower over, the colony will reconstruct it within one lunar hour, but always to the same height: 129 flakes tall, never 128 or 130.

3.  Ice-fern Crickets (Acheta lucerna)  
•  Form:  Skin like brushed quartz, legs delicately branched so they resemble frost leaves.  Their transparency is almost total until they chirp; then ions in the cuticle polarise and the whole body flashes opal white for the duration of the note—each call is a strobe.  
•  Sonic niche:  The chamber wants equilibrium, so the chorus is permanently three voices thick.  Extra adults are not killed; they simply mute themselves, singing in ultrasonic frequencies that cancel each other out—a living noise-gate.  
•  Courtship:  A pair takes the circle chewed into the moss raft, leaps in mirror arcs that never quite meet, and lands exactly opposite again.  If both hear the other land at the same microsecond, they mate.  If not, they repeat.  Some pairs have been repeating for years, looped like tape left to spin.

4.  Breath-loop Caecilian (Caecilia numen)  
•  Dimensions:  Forty centimetres long, torso like braided silk.  No eyes; instead, a ring of electrosensitive pores whirls continuously beneath translucent skin.  
•  Life cycle in one hour:  
  Minute 00 Surface.  Break the pool’s mirror, take one airy gasp.  
  Minute 01-30 Spiral below, digesting the single gulp into pure oxygen, exhaling through skin.  
  Minute 31-59 Hibernate in a torpor so deep its heart flickers once every seven minutes.  
  Minute 60 Surface again to reset the loop.  
  Nothing more, nothing less.  Researchers once blocked its ascent with a mesh; the animal dissolved rather than miss the appointment, leaving behind a perfect ghost-tube of collagen that curled into the marl.  
•  Function:  Its metabolism strips nitrates from the water, preventing algal bloom that would darken the pool.  In return the room endows it with what can only be called immunity to conventional decay.

5.  Peripheral Micro-choir (Uncatalogued)  
If you put your ear to any pocket of marl you will hear a note one semitone above the cricket song.  The sound comes from neither insect nor stone but from bacteria vibrating flagella in quorum.  They are the bloom that the millipedes and snails prevent from thickening; they are also the tuning fork for every other creature.  Remove them and the entire acoustic economy would drift, unbalancing light, drip rate, even gravity’s “politeness.” No lens has yet shown these microbes; their existence is inferred the way one infers a planet from the wobble of a distant star.

6.  The Collective Custodian  (folk names:  The Choir-Stone,  The Never-Teacher)  
All the species above behave as organs of a super-organism the surface world has never quite decided to call alive.  Evidence:  
•  Disturb the moss raft and crickets hush, millipedes retreat, the light brightens fractionally—three systems reacting as one body.  
•  Tap a rib with metal and a tuning shadow races around the dome, calms only when the disturbance stops.  
•  No individual here exceeds the complexity of a garden insect, yet the room’s total behaviour evaluates and responds to inputs with a coherence best labelled intelligence.  

The Custodian’s “mind,” if that is the word, resides in relationships—echo intervals, chemical gradients, cross-species chores.  Nothing central ever issues commands; instead, intention circulates like a current in salt water, invisible but appreciable by its push against the skin.

7.  Interaction with Outsiders  
A visitor is interpreted as a question.  
•  Enter quietly, and the system echoes your footfall once, no louder than your own step: acknowledgement.  
•  Shout, and the snails retreat, cutting refracted light by half: request for lower volume.  
•  Attempt to pocket any organism, and your muscles cramp as though you have clenched stone itself—some ion imbalance induced by the air.  Release the captive, cramp ends.  The Custodian corrects without violence, exactly as it corrects an extra drip of water.  
No emotion attaches to these responses; they are mechanical acts of balance, tender only because precision feels like care.

8.  Origin Hypotheses (three equally unsatisfying)  
a)  Subterranean Phylogenesis:  Life began here in situ, selected not by competition but by the room’s geometric desire for stability.  
b)  Surface Derivation:  Spores and eggs washed in, found growth throttled by Rule #2, adapted until survival equalled self-restraint.  
c)  Reciprocal Crystallisation:  The limestone itself is alive; animals are merely expressions of its metabolism, like fingernails on a body made of carbonate.

9.  Impossibilities Catalogued  
•  No corpse has ever been observed.  When Caesarian skin sloughs, it turns to mist before falling.  Cricket husks puff like ash the moment a light ray divides them.  
•  Genetic samples taken twelve years apart from different individuals of the same species show sequences too identical to allow mutation.  The chamber freezes heredity the way it freezes time.  
•  A tuning fork struck in the room sustains its vibration three minutes longer than physics predicts, as if unwilling to let sound go to waste.

10.  Closing Image  
Picture the entire pocket of stone as an egg lit from inside, each creature a swirl in the yolk keeping the contents from settling.  Nothing hatches, nothing spoils; the albumen of light, sound, and mineral turns by infinitesimal degrees, chorusing to itself, practicing being perfect one more time, forever.

Who lives here?  Everyone and no one.  A parliament of soft-bodied caretakers, a single distributed mind, a song shaped like a room.  You may list its players—millipede, snail, cricket, caecilian, choir of unseen microbes—but the moment you finish, the light will have rearranged them into a new chord and the list will be wrong again, gloriously, with mathematical precision.
