Who lives in the Basin of Mercurial Hammers?

Almost nothing that can cast a shadow, yet a great many things that can stain a rock, cloud a droplet, or borrow a note from the subterranean music.  What follows is the catalogue that naturalists piece together after months of patient listening, prying and sampling.  No visitor will meet every resident; at any given moment only two or three life-forms are likely to show themselves.  All the rest are folded into the stone, the steam, and the ringing dark.

1. Ferrivellum orchestrae – the violet lichen  
   • External life, internal chemistry.  Each sheet is two membranes sandwiching a gel of iron-binding enzymes.  Sunlight is the enemy; photo-oxidation would tear the enzymes apart, so the colony “plays dead” by retracting the whole upper membrane into microscopic scrolls whenever irradiance tops a measurable threshold.  In shade or dawn it unfurls again, the violet resurfacing like a bruise that was always there.  
   • Metabolism.  It eats electricity in the mildest sense: trace galvanic currents that form where wet ironstone meets drier quartzite.  Cations flow, the gel harvests them, and the lichen slowly plates its own under surface with a mirror-bright rind of iron phosphate.  Every few decades that rind delaminates and flakes away—one source of the basin’s metallic dust.  
   • Reproduction.  Steam surges rattle pebbles on the floor.  Vibration fractures old sheets into confetti-size flags that drift on the condensation veils; wherever a flag lands on suitable rock, it ungulates once, sticks, and grows a new sheet.  The pattern is so regular that botanists can read decades of steam intensity from the spacing of violet freckles on a single slope.

2. Calcipodus reticulum – the glass-shell crustacean  
   • “Glass” only at first glance.  The shell is calcium carbonate shot through with rutile needles that act like optical fibers, piping daylight around the body so the animal remains almost invisible until it moves.  
   • Size & gait.  Four millimetres long, eight jointed legs.  It walks as though the ground were sizzling: three legs down, five legs poised, never resting.  Surface contact lasts half a second—just enough to drink.  
   • Diet.  The basin glitters with buttons of evaporite.  A Calcipodus stops at a bead, extrudes twin proboscides, and suctions the microscopic broth of magnesium, silica, and amino-rich lichen debris.  It can fill its hemolymph in thirty seconds, then vanish into the nearest fissure to mineralize another fraction of its shell.  
   • Life span.  Roughly nineteen local days.  It survives four molts, each timed to an acoustic cycle: the forty-second hiss you noticed is the cue to begin apolysis, the thunder-catch is the cue to complete the shed.  No one knows why that timing matters, only that off-cycle individuals die within hours.

3. Geosoniphaga clanga – resonance mites  
   • Habitat.  Inside the hematite bells that cap the steam chimneys, a tiny ecosystem occupies microscopic pitting.  
   • Behavior.  They never leave the bell.  They graze on thermally cracked hydrocarbons that plate the interior after every venting pulse.  Their sense organs are far too small for airborne sound; instead their bodies read the standing wave that trembles through the bell’s iron lattice.  Each mite builds a cambershell—a quarter-wavelength dome of its own waxy excreta—that focuses certain harmonics onto its ventral nerve ring.  In effect, the mite “hears” by sitting at the focal point of a resonant amplifier it built around itself.  
   • Ecological role.  When a bell’s interior becomes too thick with cambershells and waste, its pitch shifts upward a small but measurable degree.  That sonic detuning reduces steam throughput, lowering local hydrothermal pressure.  Over geologic time the mites are the only feedback mechanism that prevents any single vent from widening into a caldera-scale blowout.

4. Plumathemus sublimis – steam leeches  
   • Sightings are forensic, never direct.  You find translucent mucous tubes in cores drilled near the vents—tubes lined with magnetite spicules and threaded with jerky nodules of chewed hematite.  DNA assays say annelid, though nothing on Earth matches.  
   • Physiology.  Hemoglobin concentration is so high that fresh tissue blackens in seconds when exposed to air.  The leech lives where water hovers at 95 °C and pressure holds it liquid.  There it gloms onto the vent throat, scrapes biofilms, and drinks supersaturated brine.  
   • Above-ground effect.  Occasionally a tube works loose, rides a steam pulse halfway to the surface, then carbonizes.  The char drifts down as brittle black ribbons that shatter to dust underfoot.  Those ribbons seed iron-phosphate crystals that the lichen later exploits.  In that sense the leeches are fertilizer engines the surface organisms never meet.

5. Myristella argentis – the silver mist bacterium  
   • Free-floating in the condensation veils, each cell no longer than a grain of smoke.  It coats itself in a mono-layer of tin oxide whose index of refraction produces the basin’s pearly halos.  
   • Trophic trick.  The cell wall carries vanadium porphyrins that split nitrous oxide—an otherwise inert trace gas updrafted from deeper strata—into bioavailable nitrogen and brief flashes of chemiluminescence.  From orbit, the basin twinkles at wavelengths no larger eye can see.  
   • Population pulse.  Peak density follows the tableland winds, so a visitor walking cross-basin will walk through invisible, rippling serotines of bacterial nightfall and noon.

6. Peripheral visitors  
   Keeping with the basin’s law of small and scarce, only two outsiders qualify as regulars:  
   • Mica moths (Litonigis catoptria).  Wingspan the width of a pencil eraser, dusted with micaceous scales that turn them mirror-bright in a headlamp.  They arrive at dusk from the uplands, sipping dew from cooling stones.  They lay eggs on lichen edges; the hatchlings tunnel between the lichen’s two membranes, eating only the spent inner gel and leaving the living upper sheet intact.  
   • Runebirds (the locals on the tablelands swear by the name though no specimen exists).  Once a decade, field recorders catch a series of fluting calls just at the audible threshold.  The calls thread perfectly into the factory rhythm—as though an avian instrument is being tuned by the steam.  Whatever makes the sound never lands, never shows plumage on camera, and never sticks around for more than half an hour.  If it is a true bird, it is the largest life form that willingly crosses the lip, and it does so without ever touching down.

Patterns, not populations  
Add the numbers and the living biomass of the entire basin could fit in the hold of a single truck.  Yet the place feels inhabited because every resident is woven into the acoustics.  The lichen needs the tremor to fracture and seed; the crustacean times its molt to a hiss; the mites tune the very bells that sing.  Even the bacteria borrow an updraft scripted by resonance below.  

So what lives in the Basin of Mercurial Hammers?  Things that are small enough to ride sound, slow enough to keep pace with stone, and tough enough to mistake rust for soil.  They are neither many nor mighty, but they have tuned themselves so precisely to the basin’s mercurial hammering that if the rhythm ever stopped, their world—quiet, violet, and glistening—would end within the hour.
