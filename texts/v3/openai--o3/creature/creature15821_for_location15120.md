The dwellers of the wind-grove do not think of themselves as a separate species so much as a moving organ of the plateau, the way a tongue belongs to a mouth or a retina to an eye.  Outsiders have nicknamed them “Charge-kin,” but among themselves names are never voiced; they are sung as flickers of current across skin and tool.  Seen through off-world eyes they are startling, delicate, almost translucent, yet no part of them is fragile in the ordinary, water-based sense of the word.

1. Anatomy and Physiology

Length and silhouette  
• Average height: four to five hand-spans—little taller than the shardback grazer’s shoulder—but height is measured knee-to-crown on only two legs at a time; a third, rear limb folds against the spine like a kickstand and swings down during sprinting climbs.  
• Skeleton: braided graphite rods grown in vivo; joints are ball-and-socket nests of mica, allowing a full rotation so abrupt escapes never shear bone.  
• Flesh: a velvet of interlaced protein wires soaked in molten salts.  Temperature must hover near 70 °C for the “blood” to remain fluid, so every dermal pore houses a piezoelectric plate that turns wind shudder into warmth.  
• Skin: semi-clear; flickers with traveling pulses of turquoise or saffron when nerves fire.  From a distance individuals look like pale glass statues haunted by lightning trapped inside.

Sensory organs  
• Hearing: two cowl-like pinnae sweep up from the collarbones, shaped to sample the plateau’s mature chords and dampen juvenile ones—important because unfiltered wind-organ notes can overload the auditory cortex.  
• Sight: quadruple pupils nested concentrically, each tuned to a different swath of the spectrum: violet for lightning; green for ironwillow glow; near-infrared for body heat of prey or predator.  
• Field-sense: the most vital faculty.  A corona of canted whisker-threads radiates from scalp, elbows, and hips; slight deflection tells bearer which way charge is flowing and how swiftly.  To be shaved of whiskers is to be blinded.

2. Life Cycle

Birth  
Young are budded, not born.  A gestating adult develops a spine-sac along the dorsal ridge that grows honeycomb cells—each a sealed ampoule of embryonic tissue wrapped round a micro-capacitive coil.  When the ironwillows blossom in the season of highest atmospheric potential, the adult kneels against a trunk and over two nights discharges the ampoules into natural bark cavities.  There they incubate, harvesting trickle current the way a chick consumes yolk.  After eighty-odd windstorms the juveniles chew free and drop, already the size of a human child and hot to the touch.

Growth  
For the first decade juveniles stay beneath the ironwillows, sipping seeped charge through an umbilicus-like pad on the abdomen.  They are silent except for a sussurant, sub-audible buzz used to signal hunger or fear.  When corpus bones calcify to graphite and the first whiskers erupt, they take the rite-walk: up the rim parapets during a lightning hour to be struck clean.  Surviving the bolt tempers skeleton and anneals synaptic paths; those who misjudge timing are blown into mauve dust—an accepted, though mourned, tax paid to the storm.

Senescence  
Biological aging is chemical, not hydraulic.  Eventually salt-blood thickens past what muscle pumps can handle.  Elders retreat into basalt hollows and lash themselves upright with shardback fibers.  Over months they petrify, becoming striated pillars whose stored charge hums at sunset.  These “wisdom spires” act as broadcast beacons: living libraries one can tap for ancestral memory by pressing a palm to the stone and reading the residual field.

3. Social Structure

Unit of belonging: The Choir  
A Choir numbers anywhere from nine to thirty souls bound by resonance rather than kinship.  Each voice occupies a sonic slot—bass downdraft, mid wail, treble skirl—so that, together, they can weave precise interference patterns in the plateau’s noise.  Conversation is an art of canceling and amplifying existing wind notes until meaning stands out like a shape from marble.

Leadership  
No chiefs.  Instead, rotating Triads coordinate tasks.  Trio 1 minds forage; Trio 2 repairs shelters; Trio 3 gauges weather.  Rotation happens each half-cycle of the rust sun’s glow.

Taboos  
• Speaking in true silence.  To do so invites the “Below Silence,” a mythic void where all charge dies.  
• Hoarding static.  Batteries—braided coils worn like sashes—serve the common store.  An individual caught siphoning more than body-maintenance will be exiled to the rim for three storms, packless and ungrounded.

4. Technology and Craft

Materials  
Nothing pliant lasts; they fashion everything from crystal, ceramic, metal, or living tissue kept alive by trickle feed.

Tools  
• Arc-knives: shardback glass scored with ferro-write glyphs.  When two glyphs align they complete a circuit and generate a sizzling edge hot enough to melt basalt.  
• Hush-nets: lattices of hair-fine copper leaf deployed across wind-slots in the lava cloister to damp the organ roar during sleep periods.  
• Ember-cradles: portable inductors that warm salt-blood on journeys away from reliable charge sources.

Art  
There is no pigment, so imagery is sculpted in field gradients: arrays of slender reed-antennas whose collective oscillation paints pictures for the nervous system.  A visitor standing amid such an array will “see” spirals and beasts though nothing moves except invisible current.

5. Diet and Foraging

Staple prey: the skirl-moth  
Choirs hunt at dusk when air pressure dips.  Three or four singers loft basket-kites woven of scalefern veins; others dampen ambient chord frequencies so the moths’ own sonar becomes useless, herding them into nets.  Subdued moths are pressed wing-to-skin; their stored piezoelectric charge walks directly into hunter bodies until the insects crumple like cooled silver foil.

Supplement: pollen-tower spoors  
High risk—one stomp can burst a tower in a blinding flash—but the micro-capacitors inside are concentrated nutriment.  For safety they use insulated jawbone scoops and ground themselves by biting a copper stake driven into basalt.

6. Relation to Other Fauna

Rock-jacks  
Considered “the Teeth.”  Charge-kin rarely eat them; instead they scatter grit-bait laced with static to draw the jacks into defensive rings around sleeping huts.  Any predator big enough to threaten a Choir must first step through a minefield of clacking claws.

Shardback grazers  
Semi-domesticated.  A Choir might guide a herd along safe charge lanes, then, when a grazer molts, collect the fallen glass.  In return they sing counter-rhythms that confuse land-sting flies which prey on grazers’ soft under-skin.

7. Rituals and Beliefs

Storm-Listening  
Once each moonless cycle every Choir migrates up the basalt spine to the parapet’s highest tooth.  There, during the greatest vortex storm, members fold their three legs and rest forehead to stone.  They are utterly still, letting the lightning play through their skeletons.  Afterward they return glowing, aphasic, and will not speak for three glow-rises, claiming to hear messages from “the World-Core,” a presence imagined as the plateau’s buried heart.

Making Ground  
Deaths are rare, but any body that does not petrify—killed by accident or cliff fall—is harvested for conductive organs returned to ironwillow roots.  The phrase “to make ground” therefore means both dying and finishing a task perfectly.

Marriage Equivalent  
Called Pair-Phasing.  Two individuals braid their whisker-frills together while standing atop an exposed basalt nodule.  They take turns alternating charge so that each becomes the other’s capacitor.  If they can maintain balanced flow through five thunder rolls, their fields synchronize permanently—after which they no longer speak separately in Choir but interject harmonized chords as a single voice.

8. External Relations

There is rumor of a second plateau across the cloud sea, glimpsed only when lightning silhouettes a distant rise.  Trade consists of luminous seeds carried by the skirl-moths or, rarely, a shardback glider outfitted with sail-skins.  What sentience, if any, lives there is unknown; messages arrive as coded flickers too alien to parse.  The Choirs debate: build a bridge of tethered ironwillows?  Fling stone kites on the thermal geysers?  Or wait until the storms decide the matter?

9. Myths

Origin  
They believe the rust sun is not a star but a scar in the sky where an ancient spear of iron pierced the heavens, letting raw charge leak out to animate the world.  They themselves are the clot trying to close the wound: the storm roars because it cannot decide whether to spit them out or swallow them whole.

Apocalypse  
Silence is apocalypse.  Should the organ-wind cease, every living wire will go cold.  Therefore daily chores include rattling loose columns or thumping the basalt floor to keep micro-fractures singing.  Children learn to sleep with one palm on vibrating rock to reassure themselves the world still lives.

10. A Visitor’s Etiquette

• Never approach from downwind; your static shadow precedes you and will be read as predatory intent.  
• Keep metal tools sheathed—bright edges represent open mouths hungry for charge.  
• To request shelter, kneel, plant both hands in the ash powder, and drum your fingertips twice.  The pattern mimics rain, the one blessing nature denies this realm, and will earn cautious welcome.

Stand among them for an hour and you will feel more filament than flesh, more antenna than animal.  Your pulse will seem slow, wet, impractical.  Their plateau is an orchestra pit; the Charge-kin are its conductors, coaxing duet out of discord.  When you finally retreat down the wind-planed stone ribbon, the silence inside your suit will feel deafening, and some small part of you will itch for the spark of basalt underfoot, for the tingling chorale that never ends.
