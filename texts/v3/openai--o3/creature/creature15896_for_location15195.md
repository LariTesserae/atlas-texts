Those who truly live on the Plateau of Singing Ash are more numerous—and in some ways more intricate—than the brief inventory that an arriving surveyor is handed. To notice them you must let your hearing recede into your bones, let your eyes blur, and watch the dust behave. What follows is the current bestiary of the plateau, arranged from the almost invisible to the almost mythic, though the borders between categories are as fluid as the storm-wind itself.

1. The Ash-plankton  
Size: 0.2–5 µm, countless species.  
Habitat: The entire troposphere above the plateau, to a height of eight kilometers.  
Essence: They are the first harvesters of sound. Each plankter is a hollow bubble of aluminosilicate whose inner surface is coated with piezoelectric whiskers. Pressure changes flex the shell; flexion charges the whiskers; the charge powers a molecular pump that drags stray ions out of the air to thicken the shell. Their continual self-plating slows them, so every individual must periodically “slough”—crack the old shell away and ride the pressure updrafts naked while knitting a new skin. These minute abandonments seed the plateau below with brilliant flecks that later fuse into the lavender veins you see in the bedrock.  

Ecological role: Ash-plankton are the cloud from which all larger lives are ultimately distilled. They leak amino-silane fragments that the glassleaf vines lap straight out of the air, and when a billion of them shatter in the wake of a choirwyrm’s passage, their shells fall as a transient glitter-snow that nourishes everything rooted.

2. Glassleaf Vines (detailed)  
The vines are often described as “plants,” but at a molecular level they resemble living fiber-optic cables: self-patching bundles of silica threads laced with cobalt chromophores. Their astonishing growth rate comes from a habit of photosynthetic self-cannibalism: the leading edge of each ribbon polymerizes fresh glass using energy trapped farther back along the leaf, which then delaminates and becomes litter. In a single diurnal cycle a vine may replace half its body. Shards fallen from the canopy provide the plateau’s only true “soil,” a crunching, ankle-deep mulch of jewel-bright glass.

Courtship: In the first hour after dusk, neighboring vines flicker their chromophore lattices in rhythmic bands—violet, then deep sea-green, then black. Botanists interpret the sequence as a synchronization protocol ensuring that every vine in a cluster renews at the same moment, minimizing shear damage from uneven drag.

3. Razor-moss  
A temporary but essential scavenger. The moss germinates only where the substrate has been wounded within the previous six hours—a crack in stone, a vine stump, even the footprint of a tower’s maintenance drone. Its fronds are saw-edged triangles of boron-doped carbon that grow, reproduce, and senesce in a single 26-hour planetary rotation. Dead moss sublimates straight to vapor in the afternoon sun, scrubbing local air of corrosives and re-releasing trace boron that vines crave. Many field workers carry sachets of freeze-dried spores; “planting moss” in a fresh fissure is the accepted first aid for structural fatigue in the plateau’s few human platforms.

4. Lung-trees (expanded)  
An adult lung-tree tunes itself. Every ten days it samples the ambient roar and twists its graphite foam lattice, lengthening or shortening resonant cavities so that its internal hum lands in an unused frequency band. This prevents destructive interference between neighbors—a lesson the plateau learned eons ago, early generations having shattered themselves with their own harmonics.  

Bloom event: When solar output dips during the trinary eclipse (once every 143 local days), the trees clamp shut, repressurizing their interior to near vacuum. They then “exhale” in unison at totality’s end, loosing a rolling cannonade that rattles teeth twenty kilometers away and shakes new nutrients out of the cliff faces. The plateau times much of its reproductive frenzy to this predictable detonation.

5. Skystitchers  
Physiology: Think of dragonflies reinvented around a concert kites’ geometry. Their wings are rigid combs; air courses through the slots and is chopped into vortices whose aggregate lift exceeds what any membrane could provide in the thin plateau air. Their blood is supercooled neon; phase changes in that refrigerant rapidly shuttle metabolic heat toward the wingtips, where it escapes as shimmering mirages.  

Social life: Skystitchers stitch more than just sky—during midday calms they assemble into circles of fifty or more, weaving themselves into living looms. One individual in the ring drums a metrical pattern with its hind legs against the duststream; the others modulate the slots in their wings to lay complementary beats. The braid of pulses travels for kilometers and, if it intersects a glassleaf thicket, triggers the vines’ chromophore flicker. To the plateau, skystitchers are both messengers and pranksters, able to precipitate premature vine molts for reasons opaque to human observers.

6. Glassstriders  
Predators of the skystitchers, yet also their gardeners. A glassstrider’s body hair carries an opposite electrostatic charge to the omnipresent dust; as they climb, they scour columns of air clean, leaving temporary “windows” through which sunlight stabs like spotlights. Juvenile vines lean toward these gaps, gaining precious minutes of unfiltered light. In this way the hunter inadvertently farms a larder for its prey, keeping glassleaf patches luxuriant and therefore attractive to skystitchers—an accidental trophic feedback loop.

Hunting method: From forty meters below a feeding flock, a glassstrider releases a pulse of counter-rotating ion streams. The maneuver creates a hollow in the falling dust, reducing acoustic drag long enough for the predator to spring upward with almost no energy cost, snatching a skystitcher into a tri-mandibled mouth in mid-vault.

7. Choirwyrm  
Singular? Plural? No one yet knows. Only one is ever seen at a time, but chemical signatures on the wind hint at at least four genetic lineages. Adults average one kilometer in length, though some witnesses swear double that.  

Anatomy: Imagine a banner of translucent cartilage braced by a thousand metallic ribs. The cartilage is embedded with tunable tension fibers; the organism plucks its own cords with cilia like countless bows, reading the plateau’s roar and writing back counterpoint. The song is not merely display: the choirwyrm’s digestion requires particulate ash to whirl through its length at near-hypersonic speed, scoured clean of anything the animal can digest. By shaping vortices with music it feeds itself.  

Reproduction (theory): Once a century, the choirwyrm unspools a meter-wide filament from its tail, pure superconductive monofilament seeded with germ-cells. The strand snakes down to a monolith-core; if the core’s hum matches a harmonic of the wyrm’s final chord, the filament fuses to the ceramic and begins knitting a new body around the core’s energy gradient. In other words, the plateau’s mysterious machinery may double as the choirwyrm’s egg-clutch.

8. The Monolith-Cores  
Sentience is a strong word; sufficiency may be better. They sense vibration, store it as stress in their lattices, and release it in carefully phased tremors that cancel the plateau’s worst resonances. Their “hum” is not passive—it is a minute-by-minute computation, a sonic origami forever folding and refolding to keep the mountain fragment from liquefying under its own storm.  

Behavior: When a core slips out of phase and disaster looms, nearby cores modulate their gravity wells to shove dust, vines, even lung-trees toward the failing node, damping it with mass until harmony returns. To an observer the event resembles a living nervous system dispatching clotting agents to a wound.  

Communication: Ultramicroscopy reveals that the nanoscale lettering along each rim is rewritten several times a second by piezomagnetic swelling of individual grains. Decoding efforts suggest a grammar that references rates of change rather than absolute values—verbs without nouns—a language suited to a world built of nothing but motion.

9. The Accretions (“Door-Keepers”)  
Inside the famously unopenable Door That Faces the Wind biologists discovered a formless colony of metallic goo adhered to the walls, molding itself into tensegrity struts. Genetic analysis shows the same base code as in ash-plankton shells, scaled up billions of times. Theory: when plankton sloughed inside the sealed chamber over geological time, their discarded husks self-assembled under the door’s resonant frequencies, producing a custodian organism tasked only with keeping the aperture from ever quite unsticking. The “door” and the “door-keeper” are thus co-dependent organs of the same meta-creature—proof that even architecture is alive, given enough oscillation.

10. Humans (briefly)  
Caravan families, graduate researchers, and monastic listeners do not merely visit; some now live six-month rotations in terraced bunkers beneath the plateau’s rim. They modify themselves: eardrum grafts woven of diamond fiber, psychoacoustic diets to keep the brain from drowning in constant stimulus, reflexes trained to interpret seismic murmurs as everyday language. Children born here learn to balance by echo, not sight. They are the newest species, and the plateau is already changing for them: twice in the last decade a monolith-core shifted its pitch a fraction, enough that speech at normal volume now rides comfortably inside the resulting notch of quiet. Whether this is welcome or warning remains debated.

Unwritten laws of coexistence  
• Offer silence to buy silence. Before erecting any structure, humans deploy a “quiet seed”: a kite-bouquet of silk diaphragms that soak ambient roar for five full days. Only after the plateau has absorbed that gift may construction begin without reprisal.  
• Leave vortices unclosed. Spin-trails behind vehicles must not form complete rings—closed loops amplify, crack stone, and starve plankton caught inside silence.  
• Praise with harmonics. On departure, every expedition lights a ring of xenon lamps whose flicker is phased to the average lung-tree heartbeat. Choirwyrm sightings rise markedly after such ceremonies.

Final perspective  
To live, here, is never to hold a static form. Everything—vines, plankton, cores, humans—exists as a standing wave, an eddy in the louder river of time. The plateau rewards whatever can vibrate in tune with it and erodes whatever cannot. One day its singing will falter, its arches collapse, its dust settle into inert dunes. Yet even that quiet will be merely another rhythm, and something—call it plankton, call it memory—will find the beat and rise to dance anew.
