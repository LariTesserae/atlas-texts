Who lives here is hard to separate from the place itself; every organism is both resident and component, as if the plateau is a single factory-organism whose “species” are specialized organs. What follows is the best catalog climbers have pieced together from long, shivering observations between the basalt ribs.

1. Surface Dwellers 
   a. Ratchet-Swifts  
      • Appearance: Thumb-long birds the color of gunmetal filings. Wings end in a fringe of articulated spines that meet and part like the teeth of a gear. Their eyes are polished hematite domes without pupils.  
      • Locomotion kink: A wing stroke never completes. Mid-down-beat, the joints reverse and begin the up-stroke, so the swift hangs in a perpetual fold of motion, advancing by infinitesimal corrections rather than full flaps. To a watcher they seem to “swim” through air the way a paused video jitters a single frame back and forth.  
      • Ecological role: They skim vapor off the mirror pools, harvesting microscopic flecks of mineral condensate. Instead of eating, they electroplate these minerals onto the inner walls of their hollow bones, thickening their own frames—the only growth allowed under the plateau’s half-time. When a bird grows too heavy to counter its stutter-flight, it glides down a fissure and is never seen again. The coppery sparks inside the spindle trees brighten for hours afterward, suggesting the birds’ accumulated metals get pumped into the grove’s vascular glow.

   b. Conveyor-Haulers (the footless grazers)  
      • Anatomy: Otter-sized, velvet pelts atop a chassis of concentric rubbery belts. Each belt counter-rotates against another, drawing ground backward so the animal appears to slide forward.  
      • Feeding: They harvest fern-pith and moss, but mastication freezes mid-chew; pulp hangs between half-closed molars. Nutrients instead leak by osmosis across the oral membrane, molecule by molecule, an endless almost-swallow.  
      • By-products: Excess cellulose is extruded through dermal pores as parchment-thin ribbons. These shreds knot themselves into scroll-tight coils the moment they hit air, then roll away on micro-vibrations until they fall into cracks. After heavy mist, whole drifts of these scrolls lie banked against the basalt, each sheet covered in naturally etched fractal runes that repeat the plateau’s heartbeat frequency—a kind of organic punch-card archive.

   c. Turbine Beetles  
      • Description: Coin-sized, dark chrome, dorsal ridges fluted like fan blades.  
      • Function: They are walking pressure valves. At each pulse of the mountain below, vapors vent through the bedrock. Beetles arrange themselves in tessellated grids over the hottest pores, hinge their shells open, and hiss out the excess steam. Because the pulse never fully rises or falls, the beetles never quite close again; whole plates of beetle backs twitch in micro-pivots, turning the heat of the mountain into the tick of a metronome.

2. Subsurface Entities 
   a. Mantle-Wrights  
      • Perception: Heard rather than seen—the syncopated chugging beneath the crust. Climbers who have pressed an ear to the warm stones report a sensation “like leaning into a giant foundry where every furnace door is half-open.”  
      • Hypothesis: They are not discrete organisms but planetary autophages: loops of muscle-like rock and peristaltic magma that knead, extrude and cast the very basalt slabs ringing the bowl. Because time kinks, a Wright’s stroke of geologic molding never finishes; the rock face along the rim remains forever in the act of being pressed outward by an invisible thumb.  
      • Intelligence: Indeterminate. Hammer-like tremor codes run the same sequence for years without drift, suggesting either strict instinct or a will content with perfect repetition.

   b. Silica Mycelium  
      • Network: Threads of glassy filament lace every fissure. They drink the plateau’s organ-note wind, siphoning sound the way other fungi sip sugar. A louder gust makes their strands glow dull amber; the glow fades in step with the dying chord.  
      • Product: Where filaments cross, they precipitate nodules: translucent pebbles banded like agates. If one is pried free, the network closes around the absence in slow flickers, replacing the lost nodule with another grown exactly to spec—again, a gestural, never-finished repair cycle.

3. Organ-Trees (the pearled white trios) 
   • Physiology: Less plant than pipe organ. Their trunks are hollow stacks of resonant chambers. The copper sparks inside are glissandi of bio-plasma shunted along artery-tubes.  
   • Symbiosis: Each tree trio couples to a different frequency of the plateau’s subterranean pulse. The trees amplify it, then exhale through pores along their fronds. What looks like wind-shimmered leaves is pressure-wave vibration.  
   • Reproduction: A seed-pod forms, swells, almost tears free—and then does not. The not-quite-falling pods make perfect globes, amber-tinted, and many climbers have pocketed them. By the time the hiker reaches lower altitude the pod is gone, and their pack smells faintly of ozone and fresh-cut stone.

4. Implied Sapients 
   Field notes hint at inhabitants neither fully animal nor simple biomechanics—beings you notice only in oversights and exclusions.

   a. The Frame-Keepers  
      • Evidence: Tool-marks that appear overnight on basalt edges: a series of milled teeth as precise as any lathe and yet leading nowhere. Once, a climber left a coil of rope on the ground; next morning each fiber had been unwound and rewoven into a braided cable that lay in a perfect circle, end tucked under beginning, with no sign of cut or splice.  
      • Behavior: They never break stasis rules. An observer watching the spot sees nothing happen; look away, even blink, and modification is there. The Keepers evidently operate entirely between the human frames of perception, living in the inter-tick of the plateau’s stalled seconds.  
      • Proposed form: Holographs of momentum rather than mass—perhaps shadows cast by the half-completed vibrations that saturate the place. The plateau might be using them the way bone uses osteoblasts: to sense stress, deposit material, keep the whole unfinished edifice structurally sound.

   b. Mirror Children  
      • Where: The ankle-deep oval pools. Stare long enough and you will notice a second reflection: a face that is not yours, always centered over your heart, watching from beneath the water’s jittering skin.  
      • Attributes: The face ages oppositely to the viewer. A young climber met an ancient countenance; an old guide saw a wide-eyed infant. When the observer moves, the reflection lags, as if caught in thicker fluid.  
      • Possible purpose: Emotional ballast. The plateau mutes bodily needs but sharpens cognition; the Mirror Children may absorb the psychic overflow, ensuring no thought escalates into action that would finish, break, or end anything. Climbers who looked away from their child-mirror in midsentence report a brief slackening of memory—language unspooled, opinions lost, until the second reflection drifted back into overlap and the conversation resumed.

5. The Plateau Itself 
   One school of thought holds that nothing here is truly resident because the plateau is a single, unified organism: a chrysalis of landscape caught midway through differentiating organs, veins, symbionts and microbiome. In this view:

   • Ratchet-Swifts are its circulating erythrocytes—iron-bearing, mobile, sacrificing altitude instead of hemoglobin when overburdened.  
   • Conveyor-Haulers serve as roving lysosomes, shredding surplus biomass into codified scraps for recycling.  
   • Turbine Beetles are sweat glands, venting geothermal pressure.  
   • Organ-Trees are lungs that breathe resonance rather than air.  
   • Mantle-Wrights are the pulsating heart valves.  
   • Frame-Keepers function as nervous microglia, pruning and reinforcing neural pathways of stone.  
   • Mirror Children? The plateau’s consciousness, looking out at would-be intruders through any available surface until it decides whether incompatible thoughts need erasing before they ripple too far.

6. Life Cycle (such as it is) 
   Birth and death, start and finish—all are theories the enclave has heard of but never adopted. Instead:

   • Initiation: A structure or creature buds from existing matter the way a crystal facet grows.  
   • Approach to completion: It accrues layers of detail, builds charge toward an act—flight, bloom, breakage, speech.  
   • Perpetual asymptote: Fractional increments of the act are taken, each reversed a hair’s breadth before culmination. Growth without consummation is the only stable state.  
   • Termination (external only): Remove the entity from the bowl and the outside world’s insistence on finished processes chews it to vapor by sundown.

7. Interaction With Human Visitors 
   • The plateau ignores you until you violate symmetry. Place a sleeping bag on the moss and beetles redirect their lines to skirt its outline with centimeter precision, making you an island in their circuit.  
   • Speak a secret aloud and the Organ-Trees will resonate the syllables back in sub-audible bass. Leave and that secret will clang around your skull for days, louder than conscience.  
   • Injure a resident—crack a beetle’s shell, tear a fern—and Frame-Keeper tooling sounds swarm the spot after you step away: faint ratchetings, the scent of hot metal. Return to find the wound sutured in unfinished knots, and an identical cut somewhere on your own gear—strap, boot, skin—held likewise half-closed.

8. The Question of Sentience 
   Everything here behaves as if in possession of just enough awareness to serve its niche. Yet the perfect concurrency of all niches, the way no click or pulse overlaps out of rhythm, implies a master clock. Whether that clock is:

   • A central mind diffused through stone,  
   • An emergent order from billions of half-motions, or  
   • Some external engineer who started the machine and wandered off,

   remains unsolved. What can be said is that the plateau reacts to novelty with accommodation, not aggression—a host worried less about predators than about premature change.

In sum, to ask “who lives here” is to ask which functions the young mountain still needs performed. Every inhabitant, from beetle valve to silhouetted Keeper, is a living subroutine in an endless commissioning test. They are the workers, organs, and memories of a landscape that has refused, so far, to declare itself finished and join the ordinary schedule of time. If you linger too long, you will live here as well—not as a tenant, but as yet another part drafted into the blueprints, humming your half-completed task to the measure of the mountain’s stalled heartbeat.
