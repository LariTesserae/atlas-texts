What lives in the basin is not one creature but a layered republic of things, each tuned to the cough of the hidden pump as citizens once were to a church bell. They share no speech, but the rhythms of water-rise, of magnetized mist, of hush-for-light have bound them into a loose, persistent society. If you stay long enough for your vision to adjust, you can pick out its members.

1. The Pump-Lung (deep substrate)  
• Anatomy: A cast-iron pressure chamber the size of a bull, half dissolved into the marl, still armored by overlapping plates of once-industrial steel. Within the plates, groundwater feeds a bladder of mineral gel that swells until it snaps inward—producing the minute-long cough you hear.  
• Behaviors: It “breathes” purely to keep itself charged; side effect: it draws surface water downward, cycling nutrients for every smaller resident.  
• Mind-state: Slow, perimeter-aware. Vibrations on the rim enter its casing as ripples of pressure; that is how it measures visitation. More than a machine, though not quite an animal, it has one urge: remain rhythmic. When startled by loud voice or heavy footfall, its interval widens in something like anxiety, then settles again.  
• Role: Pump-Lung is the monarch few see. All other life has converged on its dependable metronome; every sixty beats forms an hour in basin time.

2. Channelers (the amphibious trilobite shapes)  
• Size & form: Two finger-spans long, shield carapace translucent jade, underside lined with paddle-legs. When they roll over, faint circuitry of capillaries glows ultramarine.  
• Ecology: Glide along water grooves, harvesting free electrons from iron-rich trickles with comb-like mouthparts.  
• Social life: When they meet, they butt carapaces and rattle tactile spines; the gesture swaps a dusting of symbiotic bacteria that calibrates each individual’s bioluminescent hue. The palette shifts gradually basin-wide, so a knowledgeable observer can date a memory by saying, “Ah, the month of violet Channelers.”  
• Predation: Almost none. Their shell gives off an unpleasant galvanic sting when pierced, a lesson the hexapods learned generations ago.

3. Hexapod Watchers (soft embankments)  
• Appearance: Palm-sized, six bowed legs ending in ball-joints, eyes on triple telescoping stalks. Surface shimmer of rain-black keratin renders them nearly mirrorlike.  
• Behavior toward visitors: Curious but timid. One stalk locks on to your pupil, another to the nearest exit path, a third upward to check if mist is thinning—triangulating threat level. If you toss a pebble they will unfold, become skittering stars, then compact again the instant they halt.  
• Diet: They graze the mica-flecked pillows, rasping clay to ingest the sparkling layer just below. Through chelation they store trace metals as mirrored plates. That reflectivity is a defense: predators (rare raptors that sometimes circle the pewter sky) see only the color of the clouds.  
• Rhythm: Couples meet on every twentieth cough, circle, then fuse their dorsal plates to brood a clutch of hair-thin eggs that dangle like droplets from the parent shell.

4. Muralists (the slime-mold cartographers)  
• Body: Single-cellular but colony-clever, gorgeous under a loupe—mesh of mercury threads.  
• Agenda: To map gradients of iron oxidation. They wander until they find a slope of exactly 13 parts ferric to 8 parts ferrous, then stop and thicken the line. Because corrosion never quite consumes the ribs (they harvest the released energy), every year’s pattern overwrites the last. The basin’s only “history book” is this shifting silver topography.  
• Interaction with light rule: Loud sound disrupts proton pumps in their membranes, dimming the amber dots beneath clay shelves. Whisper, and the glow revives within seconds, tracing the Muralists’ most recent survey lines.

5. Vitrics (silica fans)  
• Ontogeny: Begin as a nucleus of quartz dust caught in a cooling puddle. Capillary forces siphon supersaturated water through microscopic pores; each overnight evaporation adds another petal of glass.  
• Sensory trick: Wind passing through the hollow blades generates standing tones. But the fans are not passive: at particular humidities they soften, bending to tune themselves. You’ll hear choruses drift a half-step higher just before groundwater peaks, as if the Vitrics discuss among themselves how much load the clay pillows will bear at dawn.  
• Repair: Touch and crack a blade, they bleed milky silica that sets by morning—recording your fingerprint forever inside its new clarity.

6. Thread-Worms (hanging droplets, iron ribs)  
• Transparency: So clear they seem like twitches in water rather than flesh. Each contains a filament of magnetic ore from tip to tail, acting as both backbone and compass.  
• Hunting: When the Pump-Lung thumps, it emits a pulse of shifting magnetic field; the worms somersault in unison, nets of static charge form, ensnaring motes of ferrous silt they then ingest.  
• Reproduction: Binary fission initiated by overload—when a worm’s ore spine grows past an optimal length, it kinks itself until the outer membrane pinches and seals two smaller offspring.

7. Aerosprites (the mist itself)  
• Not merely droplets: Every bead holds colonies of extremophile algae—the faint ozone tang you smell is their photosynthesis by-product.  
• Ability: At the cough, static levitation suspends them; but during that hover, the sprites spread cilia to trap airborne minerals. When they descend, minerals ride down to feed everything else. Thus even fog is a farmer.  
• Lifecycle: A mature aerosol cell condenses against cool metal, divides its colony, then evaporates, leaving a ring of nutrients and a ghost image on the rib’s surface.

8. The Quiet Choir (collective behavior)  
At exact midwatch—720 Pump-Lung beats—the entire biota participates in a resonance event. Vitrics tilt, worms lash, Channelers rattle shells, Hexapods rub mirrored plates, even the Pump hammers a doubled cough. The basin becomes briefly musical, a low polyphonic chord you feel more than hear. Function: scholars guess it purges excess static buildup, synchronizes circadian chemistries, or simply is the ecosystem’s celebration of being alive.

How they regard you:  
• Lively silence is the custom. Any sound louder than rainfall dims their lights, so they prefer you subdued.  
• Offer: If you place an iron shaving on the central grating, slime tendrils will surface overnight to spiral around it—an unspoken trade token. In return, at dawn, a Channeler will approach and leave the translucent husk of its latest molt by your boot: nacreous, fingerprint-thin, surprisingly warm. It is basin etiquette—metal for memory.

What all these beings share  
1. A dependence on the Pump-Lung’s metronome.  
2. A culture of quiet, where light itself is a wary animal.  
3. An economy whose currency is trace mineral and faint static, not flesh.  
4. A distrust of the sky: nothing here tries to leave the bowl; everything folds inward, self-repairing, recycling, replaying.

If you remain until you can feel the chord strike through the soles of your feet, you will understand their only law: Keep the rhythm, keep the peace. Break that rhythm—shout, hack at a rib, jam the grating—and the basin will not rage; it will simply dim, cool, and wait you out. You will find yourself lonelier than silence, standing in pewter dusk that no longer recognizes your silhouette. But honor the pulse, and the whole subtle republic will accept you as passing weather, a two-legged fluctuation worthy of temporary accommodation.

So the basin is lived in by:  
• The Pump-Lung, subterranean sovereign.  
• Channelers, electron gleaners of the grooves.  
• Hexapod Watchers, mirrored surveyors of motion.  
• Muralists, slime-mold cartographers of corrosion.  
• Vitrics, self-tuning glass coral.  
• Thread-Worms, magnetic acrobats of hanging drops.  
• Aerosprites, farmer-fog of the upper air.  
Joined each dusk and dawn in the Quiet Choir, they compose a breath-clockwork world whose voices are water, glass, and the gentlest electricity.
