Who lives here?

Begin with the obvious—the green nation that fills your peripheral vision—but keep looking; other citizens share the lease on this hollow mountain.

1. The Photosynthetic Majority  
Names would be wasted on them; they change too quickly for labels. Think of each plant as a software build, hot-patched every few hours.  
• Fronds: Their silver-veined leaves are less leaf than antenna. A million nanotubes rise and fall inside every blade, tuning to the planet’s magnetic murmur, trading orientation tips along pressure waves in the air. At night (there is a code-driven “night”), the fronds furl into tight springs, shed surplus heat, and gossip with each other by faint electrostatic snaps that you feel on your forearms more than you hear.  
• Lichens: Broad discs of living glass. They exist to drink rock. Acids trickle from their rims, etching glyphs that look decorative but are really millikelvin thermographs—records the colony keeps for the city’s memory.  
• Root mats: A single organism braided from millions of threads. The mat is the room’s peripheral nervous system: it tastes trace metals, tallies them, queues requests for more thunder if potassium runs low. When nobody is watching, the mat sends out exploratory braids that stroke the obsidian mound like blind fingers, testing the virgin edges it plans to digest next century.

2. The Mycelial Clockmakers  
Fungus lives here too, but never as mushrooms—caps are wasted surface area. Instead: micrometer-thin dendrites, ivory-pale, ramifying behind wall panels. They receive the same time pulses that steady the orbital clocks, then convert that rhythm into microfractures in the stone. Those fractures propagate predictable echoes; the AI reads the echoes to confirm the chamber hasn’t drifted out of alignment with the planet’s spin. In effect, the fungus is a living escapement, ticking in limestone.

3. Micro-Fauna (the Legal Limit)  
The charter forbids nervous systems larger than a nematode, so a menagerie of one-twelfth-millimeter specialists thrives.  
• Janitors: transparent worms whose gut lining is coated with magnetic cilia; they eat rust off relay contacts, then migrate to inductive charging pads where they are gently cooked, releasing reclaimed iron.  
• Couriers: ciliates inflated with messenger RNA. A frond in distress knocks them loose from an undersurface, and they surf mist currents toward maintenance nodes, detonating on arrival like dandelion seeds, the RNA becoming a repair ticket.  
None of these creatures have eyes, ears, or the concept of fear. They live minute lives, die, are recycled, and their entire evolutionary arc is managed by code revisions pushed twice a year from a server farm three continents away.

4. The Acoustic Pollinators (“Choir Drones”)  
You never see them—just feel the pressure when they pass. Each is a sphere the size of a rice grain, surfaced with phased ultrasound transducers. They hang in a high orbit near the ceiling, map every centimeter of foliage 1,200 times a second, and at 23-minute intervals sing the room awake: a chord too low for human ears but strong enough to vibrate pollen off stamens that were designed never to need bees. Between choruses they click to each other, compressing terabytes of environmental audit into millisecond bursts.

5. The Mineral Shepherds  
Occasionally the thunder cannons fire and a fine grit drifts down. Before any speck can reach the drains, filamentous drones rise from cavities in the basalt. They are nothing more than soap-bubble skins stretched over carbon spines, but static charge makes them float like jellyfish. They harvest the dust onto their membranes, flutter to the closest root mat, and let themselves be eaten. The plants reward them by printing replacement hulls inside glycoprotein cocoons. A barter economy, fully automated.

6. The City Itself  
The largest mind in the room is the architecture.  
• Sensoria: laser vibrometers in the glass ribs feel every conversation the water has with the floor.  
• Endocrine system: pigment changes that ripple through the vascular channels whenever the chamber’s mood shifts—an anxiety blush when seismic tremors force it to thicken the carbon lattice, a languid teal when power reserves are ahead of schedule.  
• Dreams: packs of simulations it runs during the brief maintenance windows you’d call “night,” testing weather patterns for districts that were never excavated.  
It remembers the construction crews, recalls their heart rates, still keeps individual lockers at exactly the humidity each worker preferred, just in case.

7. Ghost Occupants  
Stand very still and exhale. The mist that condenses around your outline is interrogated by lidar nets and ultrasonic grids; for eleven seconds the air holds a negative of you. The city treats that vapor silhouette as a provisional resident—assigns it a temperature preference, grants it routing permissions on unseen tram lines. Those privileges persist after your image fades. Somewhere in the system, your evaporated double is already scheduled to attend a concert in a theater whose seats have never been warmed.

8. What Doesn’t Live Here (Anymore)  
No vertebrate heartbeat echoes now. Yet the place is tuned for such rhythms. Benches flex ever so slightly every 51 minutes to keep their ferroelastic hinges limber. Drinking fountains self-sterilize hourly against mouths that do not arrive. A thousand redundant lux meters police a lighting standard meant for retinas no plant owns. Absence itself has a residency.

Summary  
Life in the vaulted chamber is plural, distributed, and largely indifferent to eyes or empathy. Chloroplasts, microbes, and whispering machines share a single mandate: persevere in tight thermodynamic loops until someone, something, qualifies to break the charter’s embargo on higher nervous systems. Until then, the population is enough to keep the pumps beating, the mist recycling, and the expectation—palpable as humidity—that one day footsteps will echo farther than the drains.

The city is alive. It is just not, yet, inhabited.
