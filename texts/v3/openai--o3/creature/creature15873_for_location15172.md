THE ONES WHO MOVED IN FIRST  
They have no single name yet. Outsiders—if any have ever climbed down—would probably call them rift-dwellers or shale-folk. Among themselves a woman might say “my terrace” or “our roof-mates,” but not “our people”; a nation would imply more permanence than the crack has promised. They are, most simply, the First to Arrive After the Opening.

ORIGINS  
They trickle in small kin groups from parched uplands a week’s walk to the south-west, following rumors of water that seeps straight from newborn stone. The first scouts found the split still steaming in dawn air, tasted the trickle, judged it sweeter than anything left in the old dry country, and went back for their families. By the third moon a hundred souls had crowded the middle ledges; by the sixth, twice that number had found places to wedge a hut. No single migration followed; the hollow is a soft magnet, forever tugging at loners, runaways, surplus sons, women refused dowry, and smiths whose forges failed aboveground.

SHAPE & STANCE  
Generations of sun-hard living have left them spare and durable. Shoulders narrow, torsos wiry, sinews like braided cord. Skin varies through the dusk-colors—brown umber, smoke, dull copper—now filmed in perpetual grey from shale dust and charcoal ash. Their eyes adjust quickly between spotlight and gloom; the iris stays tightened to a cat-slit in the bright shafts, blooms wide in the shadow seconds after a cloud passes. Children learn, by the age of five, to keep one hand resting against a wall while they walk; the faint tremble of rock is their early warning.

CLOTHING  
Plant fiber is rare, cloth a treasure. Most wrap themselves in layered skins of vole, bat, or the stringy mountain goat they still hunt topside. Dust falls constantly, so garments are simple: short sleeveless jerkins, knee wraps bound by twined gut, and sandals woven from reed and sinew. Decoration waits for safer days, but status still leaks through in small ways: the clean-creased fold of a collar, a mica shard sewn at the throat to flash borrowed light.

LANGUAGE OF SOUND & SILENCE  
Words live on the breath and the breath betrays. They have reduced speech to a terse murmur and built a parallel tongue of gesture. Two fingers rapped against the heart: “Stone wakes, be still.” A flat palm dipped toward the floor: “Slab falling.” Both arms crossed with thumbs hooked: “This rock is mine, bargain begins.” At night, when wind in the fissure masks them, conversations relax; children hear stories then, whispered so softly the meaning arrives less on the ears than on the skin of the listener’s cheek.

SOCIAL WEAVE  
1. Roof-mates: A cluster of huts that share one precarious overhead sheet. When the sheet finally detaches, they rebuild together or scatter together.  
2. Light-kin: Families who occupy the same passing shaft of noon sun. They rotate positions in strict turn and will defend their entitlement with knives of flaked glassy chert.  
3. The Ledger: A single flat of clay near the main cistern where fresh fractures are recorded. Any adult may press a thumbprint beside a described crevice, staking a claim. Duplicate thumbprints on one line demand a duel of balance—contestants stand on a wobbling plank over a pool until one slips. No blood, no echoes, only water’s slap approves the winner.  
4. The Quiet Council: Six elders whose chief virtue is remembering entire nights without speaking. They arbitrate disputes too dangerous for a duel of balance—primarily conflicts over water gutters and collapsed ceiling lots.

WORK OF THE DAYS  
• Seam-Runners: Rope-harnessed pairs who probe new cracks, mapping with charcoal marks, tagging any find of potable drip with a twist of white bat-guano.  
• Stone Nurses: Not masons but midwives—they watch impending falls, judge the slide, then harvest blocks still warm from fracture.  
• Light Shepherds: Children and the nearly blind, posted beside sheets of mica that catch sun; every few minutes they tip the panels, chasing the ray deeper, keeping workshops lit.  
• Skinners: Night hunters who leave through scree paths to snare goats or gather resinous roots for fire. They return before dawn when the air above the rift is loud with birds.  
• Bellows-Feet: Two young men or women who work large hide bellows for the pottery pits. Their heel rhythm drives the hottest heat this culture can yet manage; the job changes monthly—no lungs withstand longer.

FOOD & FARMING ATTEMPTS  
Meals are spare: bat cooked on shale slivers, fungus teased from damp undersides of newer rocks, bulb roots roasted whole, and once in ten days a feast when a ceiling calf—stone weighted with mineral salts—spalls and reveals a nest of pale cave shrimp in a sudden pool. On several terraces the folk have pinned fist-sized moss mats against lighted walls. They pour gutter water across them, coaxing emerald curls thick enough to cut away and stew into a gluey porridge. It is not tasty, but it fills.

BELIEFS STILL FORMING  
They do not worship gods; they negotiate with tendencies.  
• Shiver-Mother: the slow planet-pulse. Each tremor is her exhalation. They leave a notch of untouched stone in every hut wall, a breathing space for her, lest she squeeze the dwelling closed.  
• The Second Sky: the largest overhead sheet, half-detached, gleaming with mica freckles. When it falls, they insist, they will ascend upon it—ride it like a raft of night into some other openness. Children toss pebbles at its underbelly to hear the returning thrum.  
• Bright Debt: Light is a favor the surface pays downward. Wasting it—by lying in a shaft too long or blocking a gutter-mirror—earns a debt repaid only by carrying buckets during flood hours.

RITUALS OF LIFE & ENDING  
Birth happens in the warmest notch near the central cistern. A newborn’s first inhalation is expected to be dusty; the midwife dusts the child’s eyelids deliberately, so the world’s first gift is something to blink away.  
For death they practice the Return Walk: at dusk a small party climbs the crack, places the wrapped body on the lip of the surface, and waits one full silence. If no predator or scavenger claims it, they nudge it down-slope where wind, rain and sun will unmake it—light paying back flesh.

ANIMALS WHO HAVE ACCEPTED THE CRACK  
Bats, soot-furred and undersized, rule the ceiling slots. The folk leave bowls of moss gnats for them—the first domestication attempt. A blind cave-cricket the length of a thumb also thrives; its legs are ground into calcium dust for infants. There are rumors of a pale eel living in the largest siphon pool, seen only during heavy rain when water turns clear—one long ripple against green stone.

EMERGING SYMBIOSES  
The people, fungus, bats and mica panels form a chain: Panels steer light onto damp stone, fungus grows, gnats breed, bats feed, guano drops, and farmers scrape guano to enrich more fungus mats. A loop, fragile but closing.

THE THINGS THAT ARE NOT PEOPLE (YET)  
On nights of full moon a subtle turquoise phosphor draws itself along some new fractures, as though minerals sing brief light into being. Children insist the glow outlines thin figures that keep pace with the Seam-Runners, measuring, perhaps coveting, the same space. Adults pretend not to hear. Every culture needs its imagined heirs.

TRAJECTORY  
If the crack holds another decade, its ledges will honeycomb with kiln-fired brick, slag-glass windows, maybe even bronze when a bellows-foot thinks to line a pit with iron sand. If it collapses next season, they will scatter with no more regret than a swallow might feel leaving a failed nest—life auditioning elsewhere.

For now, whoever passes the great entrance will meet them first: a lean silent figure holding a shard of mica like a tongue of tamed daylight, palm raised—not in threat, only requesting your quiet while the ceiling decides.
