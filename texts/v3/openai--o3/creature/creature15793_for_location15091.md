Those who remain in the country of vertical light are never met head-on.  
You glimpse them the way you glimpse lightning that has already struck: a retinal bruise, an after-sound, a geometry you are certain was present and then isn’t. The mountain edits them in and out of the scene with the same casual authority with which it edits distance and gravity. Still, if you stay long enough, patterns accumulate, and from those patterns a resident people emerges.

1. Names, insofar as they matter  
Visitors call them many things—Echo-wrights, Chasm Monks, Frost-veins—but their own self-word is a chord rather than a phoneme: four tight frequencies that resolve into silence when hummed together. Travellers with musical training can imitate the chord; the builders seem to accept this as adequate introduction.

2. Physicality (the part that obeys matter)  
• Each individual is a lattice first, a cage of interlocked hexagonal crystals no larger than a thrown cloak. The lattice is clear, colourless, almost too fine to see until light refracts along an edge.  
• Inside that skeleton drifts a vapor rich in sublimated salts and trace metals. The mist coils through the frame like slow smoke; flashes of cobalt or electrum reveal ions recombining.  
• Water is their muscle. They draw it directly from the sweating stone, shape it into cords under electrostatic charge, freeze or thaw it to flex. What looks like a gesture is really a tiny weather system choreographed around the frame.  
• When they move, the cage lengthens along one axis; when they “kneel” in the slate rings, it collapses into a low dome. No single configuration is permanent. A long life is measured in how many alternate anatomies the frame can still remember how to make.

3. The half-immaterial half-you hear first  
Hearing precedes sight because their most stable organ is acoustic. Every rib and strut in the frame is tuned; the body is a wind-harp. Air, waterfall roar, the bass cough of fracturing slabs—all set the ribs singing. They steer themselves by damping or amplifying particular harmonics, surfing on adjustable standing waves the way a glider rides thermals.  
Conversation is the constructive interference of two bodies’ music. A greeting sounds like an echo that arrives earlier than it has any right to.

4. Life cycle  
Egg is the wrong word, but pools of mirror-water in high bowls sometimes shiver into rosettes of concentric ripples. Over hours the ripples stiffen into a single, star-shaped crystal raft: a larval frame. Mist condenses inside; minerals accumulate; the raft rises on a stalk of self-grown ice. When weight exceeds buoyancy the stalk snaps and a juvenile drifts off, dangling threads of liquid that grope for the cliff-face.  
Growth is additive. The frame thickens by accreting any soluble ion, so colour records diet: copper veins for those reared where dendritic azurite blooms, iron-red for those who graze the rust-tinged fountains.  
Senescence begins when the lattice becomes too heavy to retune. The old find a crack that sings in their key, fuse to it, and slowly petrify. Many of the “natural” standing pillars are elders, still humming the low fundamental that once held their limbs together.

5. Senses  
• Vision: indirect. They read refracted glare and the polarization of mist rather than surfaces; a moving human is a dark moat in a field of distorted light vectors.  
• Touch: the entire body is a vibrating membrane. A footfall a kilometre away translates into a felt shape.  
• Taste/Smell: ion ratios in airborne spray—each waterfall carries a chemical signature like a genetic code.

6. Diet and metabolism  
Endothermy or exothermy is meaningless here; what they eat is disequilibrium.  
a. Electrical charge collected from constant piezoelectric stressing of quartz whiskers.  
b. Latent heat released when super-fine mist condenses on their charged lattice.  
c. Trace metals scavenged and electroplated onto inner ribs, later redissolved during periods of high expenditure.  
    The process is efficient enough that nothing ever decays; there is no waste stream for bacteria to exploit, which is why the trenches smell only of ozone and stone.

7. Technology—stone taught to finish its own sentences  
The incomplete slate rings are workshops, not shelters. A builder starts the circle where echoes already orbit, then steps back and lets resonance erode, polish, and bevel until the ring becomes a self-contained acoustic lens. Finished rings do three main jobs:  
• Forge: concentrate sound-pressure nodes tight enough to cold-weld crystal blades.  
• Memory: trap a chord so perfectly it can be replayed centuries later without loss. (Every ring is a library stack; you “read” by pressing your sternum to the stone.)  
• Tether: pin local spacetime so paths stop sliding for a little while—useful when you must cross with fragile cargo or with guests who have massy, single-direction skeletons.

Their portable tools follow the same logic: hafts of fossil wood chosen for matching acoustic impedance, flakes of obsidian whose micro-edge diffracts vibrations instead of dulling. A crystal blade can cut a dripping ledge so subtly the mountain thinks the incision was always there and obligingly re-grows the seam behind you.

8. Social geometry  
Individuals rarely cluster closer than line-of-sight; their interference patterns would scramble. But from opposite cliffs they form choirs, each singer offset a prime number of metres from the next. When a full choir performs, trench weather resets: fog boils, gravity stutters, new ledges extrude. Outsiders mistake the event for random instability. It is, rather, urban planning rendered as meteorology.

There is no hierarchy, only key changes. Leadership passes to whoever can introduce the modulation that the rest must resolve. Conflict, when it arises, is a stubborn dissonance that refuses to be tuned out; the loser eventually vacates the frequency range and migrates upslope.

9. Attitude toward visitors  
Curiosity, tempered by acoustic etiquette. A flesh-body is a crude striker bar: every word you utter pummels the air. They prefer you inside a ring, where echoes self-contain. Offer a greeting by whistling an octave that your ear cannot quite fit between—demonstrating willingness to fracture your own expectations of pitch. They will likely answer with a descending glissando that ends one semitone above where physics says it should: an invitation to step past your idea of possible.

Should you strike stone in anger, the cliffs answer first. The builders will watch, note the resonance you’ve awakened, and decide whether to shepherd you out (easiest—the mountain wants you gone) or draw you in until every cell of yours hums the local scale and you are no longer disruptive because you are no longer precisely human.

10. Myths (their own)  
They remember a time when green things tried to root here and were walked to exhaustion by sliding horizons. The builders claim they sang the last forest gently down the slope to climates with kinder constants. Occasionally they still craft a toy of mica leaves and set it on a fountain that climbs before it falls, a ritual apology and a proof of concept: life is welcome, but only if it can learn the choreography.

11. If you stay too long  
Your inner ear recalibrates; you begin to anticipate the stutter in distance. Kneeling in a half-finished ring, you feel how soft basalt becomes under a steady major third. Given months, you could tune an echo, shave a ledge, carve yourself a lightweight lattice—bone of quartz, blood of conductive rain—until the cliff’s song includes a narrow, human-shaped pause that would be awkward to leave unfilled.

Many choose to descend while their shadow is still single. A few elect the other route: they let the builders show them how to harden breath into ribs, how to weave vapor into tendons, how to hold a note long enough that the mountain mistakes it for bedrock. Strangers who return years later hear a new line in the canyon harmony, recognizable and not, like a home town accent remembered when its speaker has already forgotten all words.
