What follows is not an inventory so much as a gradual listening-in.  Everything that lives in the Basin of Thirteen Throats begins as a response to pressure, vibration, glare, or thin air.  Keep that in mind and the creatures arrange themselves like voices along a score.

1. The First Lives—Films, Threads, Sleeves  
a. Sun-skin algae  
   • Colourless when the sun is strongest, copper when evening light slants low.  
   • They grow as a slick membrane on bare stone, then roll themselves inward every dusk into translucent tubes that trap night moisture.  At dawn each tube uncoils with a faint crackle, sounding like distant static.  That static attracts basin skimmer larvae, whose waste fertilises the very film they graze.  

b. Strata bacteria (“glass-knitters”)  
   • Work inside seams of mica, extruding silica in lacework partitions only a few molecules thick.  
   • Those partitions resonate in sympathy with whatever note dominates the basin that hour; the vibrations pump dissolved minerals through the mesh and keep the colony fed.  

c. Parchment-fungi  
   • Grey fans that hang on shaded faces.  In fog they soften and distend, forming funnels that guide condensed droplets down to their holdfasts.  When dry again they stiffen and hum—an inadvertent warning drone that keeps whisper-hares from nibbling them.  

2. Quiet Green Things  
Pillow moss is the nearest approach to a shrub.  Each thumb-sized mound is a mosaic of thousands of star-shaped rosettes.  Microscopically the rosettes buttress one another like gothic arches; the whole cushion wobbles but never breaks under the continuous percussion of air.  The wobble turns mechanical stress into a slow, constant tension that pumps trace water inward, letting the moss survive where humidity almost never tops five per cent.

Joint-grass rims the mirror pools.  Every hollow stem is a tuned whistle; the roar pushes through them day and night, scattering seeds in metronomic puffs.  No two stems share exactly the same pitch, so a cluster sounds like wind chimes heard through a wall of surf.

3. The Small Warm Bodies  
a. Whisper-hares  
   • Mass: 350 g; fur: mica-grey tipped with matte black that cancels shine.  
   • Ears are sealed slits; auditory bullae balloon huge inside the skull.  
   • Navigation is bat-like but upside-down: they stamp a hind foot, then read the returning tremor through the pads of their fore-paws.  Courtship happens the same way—pairs drumming overlapping polyrhythms that phase together, proving compatibility.  
   • Predators rarely hear them; the basin already drowns every sound, yet the hares thrive because they never have to raise their heads to watch.  

b. Basin skimmers  
   • Wings span 9 cm, fretted with hundreds of lenticular pits.  Sun burning down through the pits is split into halos that bleed infrared away, keeping muscle temperatures low.  
   • Larvae lurk in half a centimetre of water, their gill tufts vibrating like harp strings struck by the ever-present rumble.  They grow faster on days when the note is deep—low frequency improves oxygen diffusion through the tuft.  

c. Glass mites  
   • Minute arachnids that live only on the silica honeycombs.  Each mite cuts hexagonal windows in the bacterial lace and lines them with birefringent waxes; tilt a flake of their city and it flashes rainbows.  
   • A mite’s life is one long diapason: it eats, excretes, and mates on a beat tied to the strongest harmonic of its host alcove.  Move a shard across the basin and the mites’ cycle slips out of phase; within days they die of metabolic confusion.  

4. The Middle Weight—Lamellorn  
Picture a goat downslope from a fossil coral reef and you are close.  Shoulder-high, lean, but the skull blooms into seven to eleven stacked crests: thin bone plates, each pierced by dozens of slots.  When a lamellorn faces the gale the slots sing.  The song is ultrasonic, violent enough to dislodge ticks and rust-coloured feather-lice.  

Social life turns on those crests.  Two rival males square off, swing their heads until the plates beat together, then slowly tilt.  As soon as interference fringes cancel the shared frequency the loser’s parasites crawl outward—visibly deserting a sinking ship—and he concedes.  Fatal fights are unknown; parasites keep the score.

Lamellorn milk tastes faintly metallic from wind-scoured lichen dust.  Nomads prize it for clotting at room temperature into an astringent cheese that travels well.

5. The High Choir—Swiftkind  
Choir swifts appear black against the sky but their plumage is actually prismatic charcoal.  Bones are 1.9 times thicker than a land swift’s; marrow cavities are crossed by struts like the inside of a bird-skull drum.  Those struts resonate and warm the blood—a free metabolic subsidy extracted from noise.  

Nests are cups of laminated mica crumb cemented with fungus glue, hung deep in the thirteen slits.  At first light the colony spills out, wheeling in helices whose geometry someone once mapped as a rotating dodecahedron.  Where two helices knot, the roar falls silent for half a breath.  Those silence-beads are mating calls; only a partner who can find the same bead in the storm is worth trusting with an egg.

6. People Who Come and Go  
They call themselves, in their own tongue, the Listeners-in-Passing.  No clan keeps the basin year-round; oxygen debt drives them lower each winter.  But every thaw they return along the knife-edge ridges for four tasks.

Task One: Skinning the wind.  They quarry “singing flakes” by hammering natural seams while the note in that alcove is a perfect fourth below the human pain threshold.  A flake freed at the wrong pitch shatters, lifeless.  

Task Two: Culling lamellorn.  They fell the beasts with atlatl darts fletched in choir-swift secondaries.  A swift feather’s micro-barbs are serrated; wind catches them and the dart flies arrow-straight even inside the howl.  

Task Three: Dyeing cloth.  They grind glass mites, mix the powder with vinegar scraped from partially fermented lamellorn milk.  The brew yields a pearly ash-blue pigment that refuses to fade in high-altitude sun.  

Task Four: Remembering.  Each evening they seat themselves against separate boulders so that no two bodies share the same harmonic pocket.  One by one they strike their flake-knives.  The pitches overlap into chords that carry private narratives: a child’s arrival two summers ago, an elder’s last descent, the pattern of this year’s skimmer hatch.  Spoken words would scatter, but these chords remain lodged in stone.  Next season another listener will sit in the same hollow, strike the same notes, and hear the memory shake itself free.  

Children learn to walk by feel; adults pack their gut with dried joint-grass pith because the constant rumble nauseates the unprepared.  All wear braided hair long, not for fashion but because loose strands serve as windvanes—they hum first when a note shifts, warning the wearer that the basin’s mood is about to swing.

7. Things Without Bodies (or with bodies too large to notice)  
After midnight, when the temperature skimps, harmonics drift downward.  Every fifth or sixth night a standing wave establishes itself between opposing alcoves.  The Listeners say that is the hour the basin dreams.  Pale motes—dust, pollen, insect wings—rise into stable vortices and assemble three quick images on the air: 

• A pillar, always fractal, never the same twice.  
• A ring that turns inside out like a Möbius throat.  
• A slumped figure impossible to focus on, as though your retina were a drum-skin and someone had pressed a fingertip against it.

The motes subside by the time predawn sleet begins.  No animal reacts; only human hearts stutter.  Whether this apparition is merely fluid dynamics or an intelligence inherent in so much ordered resonance remains undecided.  The Listeners say decisions do not belong to them; they belong to the wind.

8. Paradoxical Seeds  
Very rarely joint-grass produces a seed with a silicate keel.  Those keel-seeds remain inert until wedged into a mica crack right at the focal point of a minor Throat.  When the note matches, the keel vibrates, boring inward; after three winters the husk splits, releasing a first green blade deep inside rock where sun has never reached.  That blade will never see daylight.  Instead it lives its entire term listening—root, leaf, and final withered stem—gathering the basin’s voice into a single, perfect memory.  Local belief holds that the grass dies the instant someone finally names the chord it stored.  No one has dared test the legend.

9. Closing Ear  
You can leave the Basin of Thirteen Throats, but for several days after descent your inner ear insists the world is still trembling.  Cups rattle, bedsprings tick, breath rasps louder than it should.  Some travellers never quite readjust; they wake at night reaching for a stone flute, suddenly bereft of the one companion capable of drowning thought.  The lucky ones find a quiet hill, lie belly to earth, and discover that even placid country carries a memory of pressure.  Earth hums everywhere, only softer.  They sleep, and in dreams the thirteen slits answer them, each by name.

Those are the lives of the Basin—not separate species so much as tessellations of one long sound held in stone, flesh, feather, and time.
