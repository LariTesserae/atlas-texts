Who lives in Plot 17-B is not easily sorted into “people” and “wildlife.”  The longer you stay, the more those categories leak into one another, until the yard feels like a single organism with many kinds of moving cells.

1. The Reeffolk (humans)

Population Roughly four hundred year-round, another hundred on rotating six-month contracts, plus a seasonal wave of students who arrive when the outer roots flower.  No one keeps a precise census because addresses drift and floorplans thicken.  A posted list might be true in the morning and speculative by dusk.

Shared outlook Everyone calls themselves reeffolk first, job title second.  They speak of “keeping pulse” rather than “making quota,” a habit that confuses visiting auditors but pleases the banyan’s safety engineers.

Trades

• Sap-Skimmers—first on shift, barefoot, moving like water striders while they collect the sweet film of fermented sap with shoulder-wide copper squeegees.

• Boiler Gardeners—not mechanics as much as horticulturists who happen to work in fireproof aprons.  They trim charred root bark away from chimney collars and seed the exposed tissue with antifungal moss.

• Graftwrights—part surgeon, part carpenter.  They slit the living wood, slide in a steel tongue, stitch the cambium shut, then keep the graft poulticed with cooling clay until the reef accepts it.

• Meter-Readers—child apprentices run this circuit.  They follow a spiral path twice a day, noting sap pressure, root circumference, and evaporation rate in chalk on the nearest wall.  Their route is a public daily diary of the plant’s health.

• Foundry Hands—thirty or so specialists who pour crankshafts and pump-rods for upriver towns.  They are paid best, age fastest, and retire early to become…

• Drift-Uncles and Tide-Aunts—pensioners who take nightwatch duties.  They lull the engines with story-songs pitched to the rhythm of the triple-expander pumps.  Children fall asleep on the boiler deck just to dream in time with the machinery.

Dress Bare calves, oil-black feet, torch-goggles worn as often on the forehead as over the eyes.  Any garment that survives three monsoons earns a personal name and a mended patch of red sailcloth: the reeffolk’s closest thing to heraldry.

Diet Breakfast: plantain bread fried in sap-oil.  Shift meal: river-fish pickled in ethanol lees.  Feast days: root-hollowed cauldrons of cattail stroganoff, stirred with a prop root pruned expressly for the purpose.

Rituals & folklore

• The Quenching—when a new furnace is lit, its first crucible gets poured into the swamp water as a libation.  Steam bursts upward in a plume that rings every drip-pan like a bronze bell.

• Root Court—disputes over workspace creep are tried by measuring tape.  The reef’s growth decides the verdict; whoever’s floor has narrowed is paid in sap ration, never in coin.

• The Seventy-Year Bend—the banyan’s slow spiral is predicted to make a full quarter-turn every seven decades.  When that mark came last, elders mixed old iron filings into fresh sap and cast commemorative rings that now halo the central chimney.

2. The Quiet Fauna

Mud-yellow lizards Locals call them candle-tails.  Their skin fluoresces faintly under weld-arcs, so a late-shift welder can tell if the shop’s insect population is in balance by how many candle-tails line the rafters.

Palm-bats They roost inside chimney water-sleeves, drinking condensation warmed by exhaust.  The bats’ guano dots stay powder-dry on the hot metal and are swept each dawn into fertilizer pails.

Root Shrimp Translucent, wrist-long crustaceans sift tannin water in the ankle-deep pools.  Once a week the cafeteria sets out white-enameled basins so the shrimp can be ladled alive over coals: an accepted, almost ceremonial, cull that prevents filter-clogging.

Wick-Worms Pale annelids found only in the felted hemp around steam valves.  Their bodies absorb mineral scale; a valve that hisses too often is said to be missing its worms.

3. The Unseen Legions

Bio-electric Mycelium Threading every joint between root and brick is a lace of pale fungus.  It carries ions the same way copper carries charge; voltage spikes on the maintenance panels often track to blooms of this hyphae.  Graftwrights swear the mycelium is the banyan’s nervous tissue.

Yeast Consortium Three dominant strains ferment the sap: Pear-nose, Bread-mash, and the dreaded Blue-pitch.  Blue-pitch raises octane but will also pit stainless steel; refinery crews light a blue taper before opening any vat—if the flame gutters, the batch has gone toxic and is bled into the swamp as antifouling wash.

4. Half-Born Machines

Engines here are treated like livestock.

• The Lullaby List is a booklet of rhythms—hammer patterns, whistle chirps, drip cadences—each engine prefers.  Apprentices memorize the one for their assigned pump the way other towns teach lullabies to children.

• Naming Day occurs when a boiler survives its first full sap season without breach.  The boiler receives a welded brass plaque and a garland of still-damp orchid roots, charcoaled instantly against the firebox.

5. The Banyan-Reef Itself

To outsiders it is environment; to the reeffolk it is presence.

Signs of Mood Slower sap flow, soft cracks in the cambium, or a sudden bitter note in the air announce displeasure long before gauges confirm it.  Work halts.  Apprentices bring pails of river mud and pat it onto overheated grafts until the scent sweetens again.

Dream-Halls Every tenth year the canopy drops a crop of hollow aerial fruit as big as brewery barrels.  When those pods cure, they become near-perfect acoustic chambers.  The Reef Choir, a volunteer ensemble of forty voices, meets inside one pod until it finally sinks under its own weight—invariably mid-song, always to riotous applause.

6. Visitors & Their Reception

• Barge Captains get three free refill drums of ethanol if they tie up without running engines in reverse.  Prop-wash against the peat rim is considered a social sin.

• Scholars may stay a fortnight, provided they exchange a published paper for each week of board—no matter the field.  The dormitory shelves bulge with forgotten treatises on river morphology, mold jurisprudence, and experimental dance.

• Investors are limited to exactly one sunrise–sunset cycle.  The reeffolk claim outsiders’ worries ferment faster than sap; twenty-four hours is the longest any concern is allowed to echo in the bowl.

7. What Passes for Government

The Pulse Board Seven seats: three elected by foundry hands, two by sap workers, one rotating apprentice, and—always—one empty chair for the banyan.  During meetings that chair hosts a copper pot of fresh sap which pops and settles like a living metronome.  A measure that passes while the sap is silent must be reconsidered next session; silence is interpreted as the reef withholding consent.

8. Lifespan & Legacy

Few reeffolk bury their dead.  Bodies are cocooned in woven cattail mats, weighted with scrap iron, and slid into the rootwater.  Months later the iron resurfaces oxidized into delicate red lace—hung in dorm windows, it filters the green light into rust-colored halos.  In this way a former sap-skimmer or root-court judge still attends the morning shift: as winter sun filtered through iron bones.

So the Green Yard’s inhabitants are many and layered: sap-oiled workers who listen for root-pulse, lizards that patrol the rafters like living indicator lights, worms that tune valves, engines rocked like infants, and the old banyan itself—patient, tide-breathing, half harbor, half parent, wholly alive.  Each depends upon the rest.  To describe one is to describe, however faintly, the tremor of the whole slow, hammer-sung, sweet-fermenting creature that Plot 17-B has become.
