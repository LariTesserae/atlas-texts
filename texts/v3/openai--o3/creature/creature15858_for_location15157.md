Who lives on the Plain of Silica?  

Begin with the obvious and you will already be wrong.  Nothing that inhabits the violet glass can be reduced to bodies and instincts alone; everything that persists here is, in one sense or another, a computation the suns happen to illuminate.  But names help, so we will give the residents names—knowing that the names will be crude nets flung across clear water.

1. The Root-Weave (first citizens, oldest mind)  
Under the frozen wave-crests runs a labyrinth of nanoscopic pores.  Quartz columns—the “trees” you must shoulder past—anchor into those pores, but the real organism is the mesh that interlaces beneath them.  Imagine a rhizome whose threads are photon guides, each a hair-thin fiber directing light rather than sap.  At the junctions where guides meet, interference patterns stand still long enough to behave like logic gates.  The whole plain is therefore a slow, continent-wide optical computer, recursive, self-healing, endlessly awake.  
• Age: impossible to calculate; its oldest interference trails pre-date the current orientation of the twin suns.  
• Activity cycle: two phases every local year.  The bright phase is the one you see—towers radiant, exciton reservoirs full.  The dark phase is interior: the trunks quench, the under-mesh carries out restructuring operations, rearranging its “thoughts” by re-routing light pathways, then re-raising the forest exactly as it was.  That is why your scratched seedling never grows yet never dies; it is memory, replayed with absolute fidelity.  
• Consciousness: if you approach with a radio tuned to the terahertz whisper where the Root-Weave gossips with itself, you will hear an unbroken chord that modulates once every 43 minutes.  The modulation appears to encode prime factorizations of very large integers whose significance is not yet understood.  The tree-forms are merely its cooling fins.

2. Photosilicates (“trees,” “ferns,” “lacevines”)  
The standing architecture is not passive decoration but the Root-Weave’s means of breathing: the membranes interconvert raw stellar photons into the precise wavelengths the subterranean logic requires.  Individually the plants possess reflexes—leaf-withdrawal, crack-sealing, parasitism defense—but no separate selves.  Taken together, they are the plain’s lung and larynx.

3. Shard-Moths  
Hand-sized, two pairs of mica pinions articulated like folding fans.  Their wing-edges are piezoelectric; every stroke harvests charge that runs a ribbon of conduction impurities—a literal lightning-shaped nervous system.  
Diet: surplus excitons bled from leaf tips.  
Behavior: largely solitary pollinators.  When a membrane has stored more energy than it can safely hold, it fluoresces at 412 nm; moths see this as bright cerulean, settle, tap out the charge with brush-tongues, and inadvertently shake motes of colloidal silica—gametes—onto the next leaf they visit.  
Lifespan: ten local days; at death they vitrify in mid-air and fall as perfect spindle-shards.  The Root-Weave re-melts them at leisure.

4. Glass-Kites (the slow angels aloft)  
Span: up to thirty meters from tip to tip.  Composition: aerogel scarcely denser than the air itself, laced with superconducting loops.  
Locomotion: They levitate on diamagnetic repulsion between induced currents in their frames and the planet’s weak field—a hovering that costs almost no energy.  They seldom move; when they do, they tack across the sky like ships on an invisible ocean, shifting orientation to sip momentum from the high ultraviolet wind.  
Ecological role: signal repeaters.  The Root-Weave’s deep thoughts sometimes need to leap beyond the horizon; a kite’s belly flashes modulated mirror-bursts that carry information farther than any ground path could.  Whether the kites began as distinct organisms or were grown as peripherals no longer matters; their instincts coincide with the plain’s needs.

5. Phase-Crabs  
Rare, and normally below the notice of visitors because they exist half a millimeter out of ordinary alignment—space/time mis-registered by a constant offset.  Only when the plain’s maintenance routines “slip” and correct do you glimpse them: a ripple becomes a translucent arthropod, multi-jointed, then fades again.  
Function: janitors of probability debris.  Wherever quantum statistics pile up anomalies—an electron whose spin history conflicts with local records, a photon that should have decohered but did not—the crabs snip the contradiction out and file it into non-observable states.  The Root-Weave pays them in certainty.

6. The Diagram Blossoms  
Those symbol-flares that skate over the ground are not readouts but organisms in their own right: emergent standing-wave packets that bud, flower, and vanish in seconds.  A blossom’s “petals” are glyphs, spun from densities in the local metric.  Their purpose appears to be introspective: they summarize recent maintenance events, then decay, freeing up computational bandwidth.  Watching one is like reading a dream the world had and immediately forgot.

7. Transient Auditors (probable but unconfirmed)  
Occasionally the terahertz chord pauses, just long enough to suggest the plain is listening to something outside itself.  On those occasions a visitor’s instruments register massless, FTL-consistent disturbances—non-local correlations sweeping across the forest, pausing, departing.  If intelligences travel here at lightspeed they leave no footfalls, no scent, only an imperceptible quieting of the ever-glow, as though someone dimmed the room to see the screen better.  The Root-Weave never comments.

8. Pilgrims (you, perhaps)  
Breath-organs of water and carbon walk the glass, certain they are the exceptional case.  The plain accepts them politely.  If one lies down and aligns heartbeats with the ultrasonics in the trunks, dreams will arrive as perfect polyhedra, and sometimes a pilgrim rises changed: thirst thinner, speech slower, the conviction growing that most of what humans call life is only one solution among many.  A few pilgrims stay until the suns burn low; the plain folds their component atoms into new logic nodes, and their last exhalations join the chord.

Summary  
No single entity “rules” the Plain of Silica.  The land itself is the dominant lifeform, the plants its lungs, the moths its gardeners, the kites its sky-nerves, the crabs its editors, the blossoms its passing thoughts.  Outside observers—human or otherwise—are tolerated, sometimes incorporated, never truly central.

What lives here, then?  A symphony of matter and mathematics that has chosen not to move but to think, not to change but to remain perfectly, iteratively exact.  To live on the Plain of Silica is to be a note in that chord, held indefinitely, flawless, indelible, sung forever between twin suns.
