The place is thick with life, but almost none of that life would make immediate sense to an ecologist from the surface. Every species you notice—and several you will not—has been selected, altered, or self-evolved to treat discarded machinery the way a rainforest treats old wood. Below is an accounting of the residents, traced from the smallest chemotroph to the structure-wide superorganism they have become.

────────────────────
1. The Substrate Makers
────────────────────

1a. Iron-Moss Lichen  
• Appearance: A velvety grey rind, only a millimetre thick, that grows wherever bare metal feels air.  
• Physiology: Two symbionts. The fungal layer excretes ferric-chelate acids that etch iron, freeing ions. A photosynthetic bacteroid layer uses those ions in place of magnesium to build its version of chlorophyll, giving the lichen a gunmetal sheen.  
• Role: Seals leaks, armours exposed edges, and—by locking down free oxygen—keeps the pocket’s rusting rate just slow enough for everything else to survive.  
• Reproduction: Spores ride the steam; each droplet that condenses on metal carries a potential colony.

1b. Calcium Bark (the “coins” in the mud)  
• Actually the moulted plates of Limus calcis, a unicellular alga that precipitates calcium carbonate scales around itself.  
• When a scale layers too thick, the alga simply steps sideways and leaves its coin behind. Over months the mud inherits a mosaic that later grazers lick for trace minerals.

1c. Velvet Pipe Fungus  
• Grows downward from warm pipes in plush, finger-wide curtains.  
• Instead of mycelium, its interior is packed with capillary filaments that drink condensate before it can fall.  
• When over-saturated, the curtain “rains” from its own lower edge; those drips carry sugars and micronutrients the basin’s animals have learned to anticipate.

────────────────────
2. The Threefold Vine Syndicate
────────────────────
No single vine can monopolise a support in the cramped light. Over several wet seasons three species formed an obligate braid; they germinate only where all three seeds germinate within 5 cm of one another.

A-Strip (Adhirata folliculus) – the adhesive
• Microscopic suction cups, not tendrils. Feeds almost exclusively on plasticisers leaching from the hoses.

B-Leaf (Magnifolia crepus) – the sail
• Paper-thin, broad leaves only two atoms thick at the rim. They tilt to intercept the sun-slash and funnel photons to…

C-Bead (Photocrystis alba) – the lamp
• Strings of white, bead-like chloroplasts. Daylight stores as chemical charge. When the slash darkens, the beads phosphoresce for exactly 213 seconds—long enough for the vine mass to keep photosynthesising while predators think it is still day.

When one species dies, the remaining two senesce within hours. Their seeds, however, remain viable for decades, awaiting the next close-packed trio of sprouting points.

────────────────────
3. The Motile Guilds
────────────────────

3a. Iron-Tine Micro-Crabs (Ferronixa tessulata)  
• Size & Shape: Thumbnail disks with six radial legs; carapace looks like rusted parquet.  
• Diet: Pure ferric oxide scraped from shavings, plus trace polysaccharides leaked by pipe fungus.  
• Society:  
  – Swarm intelligence built on ion trails; each crab extrudes a filament of magnetite behind it, leaving a polarised path other crabs can sense.  
  – Three castes: Prospectors (first to follow new magnetite), Processors (pair up face-to-face and grind filings between overlapping mandibles), and Nurses (carry a paste made of ground iron plus fungal sugars into brood pits inside rubber hose hollows).  
• Defence: Clamp together into hemispherical shields when a paddle-mouse pack strikes.

3b. Paddle-Mice (Myopotamus litoralis)  
• Morphology: 15 cm long; hind feet broaden into webbed oars; fur coated in a glycoprotein slime that shunts heat to the environment, letting them sprint across near-boiling puddles.  
• Behaviour:  
  – Work in squads of six to ten. One squad member squeaks ultrasonically at 22 kHz; the moth paper can’t hear it but the worms can and flare, illuminating fallen larvae for easy harvest.  
  – Nest: Abandoned junction boxes stuffed with vine leaves glued by mouse saliva; the saliva crystallises into a ceramic crust, making the box a heat trap where pink pups mature twice as fast as surface mice.  
• Remark: They do not metabolise iron but lick micro-crab paste for vitamin analogues their bodies no longer synthesize.

3c. Neon Ribbon Worms (Luxgaster thermorima)  
• Length: Up to 80 cm, segmented, translucent.  
• Light: Each segment holds luciferin bound to a heat-sensitive protein; a 3 °C rise triggers a pulse of aqua light.  
• Movement: Muscular peristalsis down super-heated pipe runs; when startled they contract suddenly, launching drops of near-sterile water that disinfect local wounds—an accidental but valuable service in the basin’s constant abrasion.  
• Communication: Chains of worms will synchronise pulses to signal danger two metres ahead of an approaching intruder, giving slower fauna time to flatten or burrow.

3d. Moth-Paper (Manifesta folium)  
• Life States:  
  – Latent: Each “sheet” is thousands of flattened adults pressed together underside-to-underside, adhering by microscopic wax hooks. In this state they act as a living reflector, bouncing the sun-slash toward shaded vine clusters.  
  – Active: A pump-triggered pressure drop cues them to unhook in a single ripple, fill the air, and cross the basin.  
• Feeding: They do not eat; they photosynthesise in flight. The crossing is a mating swarm; when the sheet re-adheres, every second moth has swapped partners.  
• Death & Afterlife: Dead moths decay into a glitter that seeds infrared-absorbing crystals—later consumed by newborn ribbon worms, completing a looping nutrient trade.

3e. The Unknotters (Amoebic Predator Colonies)  
• Texture: Looks like oil spreading on water but in three dimensions—membraneous sheets that advance along air-film interfaces.  
• Feeding Strategy: Detects CO₂ gradients left by struggling prey, envelops, dissolves soft tissue, leaves chitin or metal intact for re-use by others.  
• Limitation: Repelled by ultraviolet; thus they retreat into cable gaps whenever the overhead slash aligns with them.

────────────────────
4. The Quiet Lord: The Greywater Pump
────────────────────
Nobody built the pump to rule, but rule it does. Its only sensor is a cheap piezo disk meant to detect water hammer. Over decades the constant tension-release of life clicking, chirping, and pulsing taught the basin an accidental language: every creature that wants the rain, the heat surge, the temporary confusion of predators, has learned to still itself at the same moment.  

• Signal Ritual:  
  – Ribbon worms halt peristalsis; their glow gutters out.  
  – Cane mice bite their own tails to keep from squeaking.  
  – Moth sheets clamp shut like books.  
  – Even the unknotters shrink to a static film.  
After 4.7 seconds of silence the pump, “hearing” nothing, interprets this as safe pressure and cycles on. The basin drinks, breeds, feeds—and the timetable holds.

────────────────────
5. Composite Intelligences
────────────────────
5a. The Conductor Mycelium  
• A minority of the pipe fungus grows neurone-like cords parallel to the hottest conduits. Each cord contains alternating packets of sodium-rich sap and magnetotactic bacteria. Together they propagate an electrochemical pulse at roughly 0.6 m s-¹—too slow for thought, fast enough for coordination. When a vine leaf is torn, the Conductor directs velvet fungus to swell around the wound while signalling ribbon worms to flash, attracting paddle-mice which in turn harass the intruder. In effect, the mycelium is the basin’s immune nerve.

5b. Surface Liaison: The Amber Beetle  
• Only one confirmed. Carapace lacquered, unscuffed, clearly immigrant.  
• Behaviour: Climbs out of the basin twice a day, returns with fresh seeds, wire scrap, or dead insects from the outside world.  
• Hypothesis: Carrying out unconscious gene-import duties on behalf of the deeper collective; its brain cavity is packed not with fat but with Conductor hyphae, turning a once-free beetle into a remote antenna.

────────────────────
6. Life Cycles & Successions
────────────────────
Year 0–2 after a fresh pipe breach  
• Iron-moss floods the tear within six hours.  
• Two days: Ribbon worms colonise the warmth trail.  
• One week: Velvet fungus hangs a curtain, anchoring vines.  
• One month: Paddle-mice reroute traffic to exploit new drips; micro-crabs establish a refining station below, enriching mud with iron spangles.  

Year 3–5  
• The moss layer thickens, insulating the pipe enough that steam condenses early. With less heat, ribbon worms migrate; moth-paper adopts the curtain as its new reflector wall.  
• The pump’s duty cycle shortens as pressure efficiency improves, squeezing the feast-famine rhythm into a sharper, more reliable beat.

Generation Shift  
Every 28 months a catastrophic silence misfires—typically because a surface thunderclap interrupts the basin’s deliberate hush. The pump skips a cycle, pressure spikes, a minor pipe explodes, and half the established niches reset. This enforced disturbance prevents any single species from monopolising the system.

────────────────────
7. How to Meet the Residents and Survive
────────────────────
• Speak by remaining silent. The quickest way to be perceived as threat is to mutter or clank during the stillness.  
• Metal jewellery is pheromone here; iron rings invite crab attachment, copper bracelets coax vines to sample you, aluminium makes you invisible to ribbon worms.  
• If the air neon-flashes, drop flat—the worms are broadcasting heat hazard, usually moments before a micro-geyser of scalding condensate erupts.  
• Do not pocket a moth; their lamellar wings exude a protease that dissolves cotton, skin, nylon alike once removed from the collective sheet.  

────────────────────
8. The Place Itself, Living
────────────────────
Taken together, pipe, pump, clay, and residents act as a single breathing organ, tuned to a fifteen-minute inhalation, 40-minute digestion, and silence-keyed heartbeat. Each creature is an adapted cell:  
• Vines and fungus—capillaries and veins.  
• Micro-crabs—erythrocytes bearing mineral oxygen.  
• Paddle-mice—mobile immune leucocytes.  
• Ribbon worms—nerve axons, firing photic impulses.  
• The pump—cardiac muscle.  
Standing inside, you are not in a habitat but in a body that long ago replaced its original flesh with scrap and opportunism. It will not harm you on purpose, but your noise, your loose change, your exhaled alloy of carbon and fear—all of it will be woven into the next pulse the moment you leave.
