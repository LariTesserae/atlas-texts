Who lives here is difficult to separate from the place itself; imagine an animal made of stance and habit rather than flesh, a population whose bodies are only slightly more individual than the bricks they move. The thing that passes for “people” in the copper-washed plane appears in three overlapping registers, as if life had tried several drafts and then, pleased with all of them, decided not to erase.

1. The Kilnborn (what a human tongue would call them)  
Shape: They look at first like simple tapering pillars of clay—child-height, round-shouldered, featureless. Come closer and the pillar opens along seams into four slow, paddle limbs, a neckless head, and a belly lit from within by low ember-glow. Their skin is the same porous ceramic that carpets the ground, but shot through with hair-fine capillaries of brine; at a touch they are warm, vibratory, faintly humming.  
Senses: The Kilnborn have no eyes. They “see” by feeling tension in the tessellation. Every brick is a nerve‐ending to them; a footfall half a kilometer away flexes the floor just enough to announce itself. Chemical gradients in the thin water film give them smell and taste at once; to breathe, they simply open pores and let vapor cycle through.  
Tool use: The wedges of black glass belong to them. They grow them. Inside each torso is a crucible where silica and salt are melted together, then jetted down an arm in a short, violent burst that leaves a leaf-thin flake of obsidian already sharper than any steel. A wedge is good for three, maybe four strokes before the edge micro-chips; the Kilnborn drop it, grow another, never sentimental.  
Work: They are the brick-setters, the tilters of monoliths, but the labor is not a job—it is basic metabolism. A Kilnborn needs heat differential to stay alive: when its core cools, it pries up tiles, exposes steaming under-water, corrals it, re-lays the surface. Each rearrangement buys a little more furnace-time in the gut. To stand idle would be to chill, to vitrify, to die.

Life cycle: A Kilnborn ends where it began. When internal embers falter beyond relighting, the body slumps, smears, and seeps between bricks. In days the smear bubbles into dozens of clay bladders no bigger than a fist: spores. These roll with the tide of mist until they find a warm seam, root, and inflate into the next pillar-husk. Nothing is ever truly new here; it is only reheated.

2. The Thread-Voices  
While the Kilnborn handle heft and angle, something subtler handles decision. Between bricks, dangling in the water sheen, live kilometer-long filaments of living salt: translucent threads one molecule thick at their narrowest, braided to a hair’s width at their fattest. They are almost impossible to see until they ring with reflected light, the way a spider strand flashes at sunrise.  
Being salt, they drink the plane’s constant weep, cracking water into ions, storing charge. A million such threads lace the clay strata like circuitry. Every few minutes they discharge in patterns—a ripple of tiny pops, more felt than heard. Those pops are language: a slow Morse that tells the Kilnborn where stress is building, where temperature slumps, where drift has broken a long line of brick into a ragged one. The pillars, hearing, obey.  
No one has glimpsed an origin for the Thread-Voices. Perhaps they were secretions of the first Kilnborn, perhaps they are mineral organisms from deeper, hotter reservoirs. They are content to remain rumor: intelligence without body, order without ego.

3. The Weather-Children  
Third and rarest are the transient giants that arrive as calamity: the dust cyclones, the mirror-rains, the silver scars that open in the copper sky. Seen properly, each is a single organism, a weather-based lifeform whose whole metabolism completes in minutes. They are born from runaway turbulence in heat and ionized vapor, pattern themselves briefly into coherence—vortex, sheet, spear of light—feed on the very distortions they create, then fade.  
When a Weather-Child crosses a district, color inverts, temperature dives, geometry slips a gear. The Kilnborn pause, leaning on their levers the way desert nomads might pause for a sandstorm. They do not fear the Children; the event is too short to kill them and often leaves behind cooler pockets the brick-setters can mine for comfort. The Thread-Voices record each passage as a change in their ionic score, a kind of footnote in lightning.

Relations, culture, time  
• There is no hierarchy any more than hand is higher than heartbeat. The Kilnborn think with muscle, the Threads with spark, the Weather-Children with brief, exuberant whirl.  
• “Conversation” happens by alteration of the field: lift a slab here, reroute a rivulet there, and somewhere beyond vision another worker feels the nudge and replies in kind. Walk a straight line and end behind yourself because, while you walked, the dialogue rewrote the floor plan.  
• Art, if the term applies, consists of symmetry attempted and perpetually spoiled. A perfect radial plaza will last an hour before a gust, a cooling pool, some whim of the Threads tips one quadrant out of true. The Kilnborn never mourn the loss; perfection would mean no more work, which would mean no more life.  
• Memory is geologic. A century’s achievement is a slightly prolonged convexity in the tiles, a hint of trapped steam underfoot. To a human visitor this feels stalled; to the inhabitants it is velocity enough.

What the place is to them  
Not cradle, not kingdom, but body: a continent-sized anatomy forever adjusting its posture. They are cells, nerves, flashes of fever. When you walk there you are an infection or a curiosity—something outside the ancestral feedback loop. The Kilnborn will track your weight for a while, test whether your passage relieves or increases local stress. If you help, even accidentally, tiles smooth before you like obedient house-cats. If you hinder—say you insist on building a fire, stealing heat—walls quietly close behind, corridors swell, and you find yourself encouraged toward a mist so thick it erases thought.

Eventually you will leave or perish. Either way, your calcium, your iron, the salts of your sweat will leach down to join the Thread-Voices; your cooler corpse will be scavenged for thermal contrast by the Kilnborn; a Weather-Child, months later, may sweep through and write a passing gleam across the spot. Then the surface stills, the dim copper light resumes, and the great patient rearrangement continues at the tempo of those deep, deliberate heartbeats—life working the only craft it has ever known: the endless, nearly aimless act of making, unmaking, and making again.
