Who lives in the Yard? A simpler question would be who does not, for anything that can tolerate cold damp and constant revision seems to drift here and stay until it is replaced. What follows is less a census than a moving cross-section—one draft taken at midnight on an arbitrary Thursday.

1. The Shiftfolk (human, mostly)

• Pedigree. Many are descendants of the first ice-quarry crews who terraced the valley a century ago; others arrive by barge or winter dirigible when work thins elsewhere. Backgrounds blur after two or three pay cycles. Everyone is provisionally local.

• Dress. Triple-layer coats: oiled sailcloth outside, moss-felt middle, shearling or scrap fur within. The moss layer is kept alive; at week’s end it is soaked in a broth of soot and sugar so it will keep fermenting and remain exothermic. No coat is ever washed—washing would kill the heat culture.

• Speech. They talk in strata. One word for the immediate task, a second for the week’s project, a third for the state of the ground. “Blue-up drain” might mean
  – blue = coolant line
  – up = scheduled to be dismantled
  – drain = bedrock under it has started to sag
The grammar is collapsible; an outsider hears staccato weather reports.

• Customs.  
  – They rent their own children to the Mapwrights at age four: the smaller you are, the farther into crawl-ducts you can carry survey mirrors.  
  – At each new moon everyone stops what they are doing, faces north, and counts slowly from one to whatever number feels right; the ritual commemorates a night thirty-seven years ago when counting was the only way to stay calm as half the valley slumped thirty centimetres in five minutes.  
  – Coffins are illegal because nothing here is guaranteed to stay buried; corpses are compressed into fuel briquettes, wrapped in copper foil etched with the deceased’s last design revision, and fed to a ceremonial boiler that also heats the orphanage dorms.

2. The Scrimshankers (human, arguably)

Night-foragers, part scavenger, part guild. They wear mirror-eyed masks scavenged from old furnace sight-ports; the masks amplify every flicker of ground-light, giving them near-spectral vision. Scrimshankers harvest the foam that bleeds from newborn basalt hummocks: a silica gel prized as an anti-seize grease. They claim to hear the future moving underfoot and can predict, almost to the hour, when a building is about to become junk. Most Shiftfolk despise them but secretly pay for tips.

3. Mapwrights (human/augmented)

Not quite engineers, not quite cartographers. They carry collapsible frames that project temporary catwalks of hard-light, allowing them to stand above ground that may disappear in the time it takes to adjust a transit. Each Mapwright maintains a personal atlas that updates itself by capillary osmosis—pages soak up metallic mist in the air, darkening or lightening the ink depending on mineral content, so a map is never more than half a day out of date. The ink is derived from the same soot-eating lichen that coats the walls.

4. Furnace-Monks (human sect)

They live inside decommissioned blast furnaces, relying on the residual heat to brew a tar-black tea that suppurates from coal slag and wild chicory. Through meditative vibration they claim to slow the cooling of iron, keeping the old shells warm enough to house the valley’s wintering bats. Their chants double as seismic sensors; a change in resonance warns when underlying basalt has begun to shift.

5. Children of the Cedarducts (human feral offshoot)

Born in the upper layers of interlaced viaduct and dwarf cedar, they may spend their first ten years without touching the ground. Limbs grow long; skin is banded where steam vines have brushed them since infancy, leaving tan lines of tannin and copper verdigris. They steal steam to heat their nests and are tolerated because they prune vine overgrowth better than any paid crew.

6. The Known Fauna

• Iron-black otters. Immune to most industrial toxins; their fur traps magnetic filings which they later lick off to ingest trace minerals. Den entrances migrate with the sluice gates.

• Root-thrush. Feathers scaled like beech bark, so conveyor guards overlook them. Eggs glow faintly from accumulated slag phosphors; the light distracts predators while parent birds mine grubs out of cooling clinker.

• Hooded corvids. Adults trade shiny waste for fresh shellfish at docks—a behaviour taught, not instinctive. The shells they drop on roofs dissolve in acid drizzle, seeding lime that lowers corrosion on those sheets; thus the birds inadvertently maintain the city’s canopy.

• Wane-eels. Blind, translucent creatures that inhabit warm outflow pipes. When the pumps stop they migrate en masse downstream, a moving rope of gelatin you can hear sloshing in the dark. Superstitiously linked to layoffs; sighting them above water means someone’s furnace will fall cold.

7. Botanical Collaborators

• Soot-eater lichen. Grey-black crust that metabolises carbon dust into alcohol heat. Walls ring with a soft fizz where colonies are thick. Shiftfolk angle vents so new spores settle on fresh masonry.

• Turquoise marsh-reed. Hollows amplify wind into infrasonic pulses; ground inspectors listen to the reeds the way meteorologists study cloud shapes.

• One-cycle fern-trees. Germinate, grow three metres, spore and collapse in nine months. Dead fronds mat into insulation sheets used to wrap steam trunks.

8. Semi-autonomous Machinery

After decades of piecemeal upgrades, many line robots now contain more grafted tissue than original gear. Examples:

• Clinker crabs—four-legged casting grippers that lost central control years ago; now patrol slag fields pinching off still-molten clumps to feed their worn heat cores.

• Trackworms—repair engines that crawl inside rail lines laying new ties from pellets they extrude like termite mounds. The pellets harden on contact with drizzle, so a worm never pauses.

Shiftfolk treat these units as wildlife; no one claims liability, yet everyone schedules around their habits.

9. The Basalt Mothers (presumed litho-biotic)

No direct observation exists, only correlations: where the ground swells, steam pipes rime faster and night fog smells of ozone. Scrimshankers insist the Mothers are vast sponge-columns of living rock that rise, taste the air, and retract if conditions displease them. Furnace-Monks leave offerings of slag tea at fresh hummocks: a superstition or an experiment—no one agrees.

10. Quiet Presences

• Condensation sprites. When mercury lamplight intersects just-forming fog, small reverse silhouettes flit in the beam. Engineers swear these are tricks of eye fatigue; nevertheless, tools left in sprite paths come back bead-polished, as if sandblasted by extremely fine ice.

• Palimpsest echoes. Sounds stamped into the valley by decades of hammer work re-emit when humidity peaks: you hear yesterday’s whistles under today’s silence, perfectly timed but tonally hollow.

Life in the Unfinished City therefore occupies a gradient from flesh through fungus, iron, and conjecture. Every niche is provisional, every inhabitant complicit in the perpetual rebuild. To survive here is to accept three commandments:

1. Nothing finished is safe.  
2. Heat is a commons—hoard it and you freeze alone.  
3. Leave a handle on whatever you make; someone else will need to carry it after you are gone.

By those rules the Yard remains populated, even crowded, though no ledger could fix the tally. At dawn’s pause, when fog glows and engines hesitate, you may feel the place inhale. In that breath live otter and monk, reed and robot, child and basalt mother alike—an ecosystem welded together by condensation and the certainty that, come night, it will all have shuffled a few millimetres and begun again.
