Lives of the Hollow Grove

1. The Grove-Body  
Strictly speaking only one organism truly “lives” here: the basal grove-body, a single, ring-shaped vascular being whose visible parts are floor and walls. Everything else is tenant, tool, or echo.

Anatomy.  
• Cortex-Floor: photosynthetic but also muscular; hexagonal myofibrils of cellulose contract and release so slowly you notice only when you return hours later and the entire surface has resettled.  
• Column-Hearts: the twelve buttresses are not trunks but piston chambers. A sleeve of lignified tissue ratchets down, squeezing dilute sap upward; the recoil pressurises the return flow. Each column keeps its own tempo, phase-shifted a fraction of a second from its neighbours, so their combined bass never aligns into silence.  
• Memory Cambium: a dark ring half a metre inside the inner bark records strain history the way trees record summers. When outside climate alters, the ring thickens or thins; if a parameter ever strays too far from the stored ideal the cortex reads the ring and re-sets conditions. What appears adaptive is really archival obedience.  
• Immune Lace: microscopic sieves in every conduit detect foreign nucleic acids. Anything not on the registry is dissolved into sugars. Even spores of local fungi are politely rewritten into fuel.

Mind.  
None in the vertebrate sense. Yet the Grove distinguishes between “continues equilibrium” and “threatens equilibrium,” and it can respond with chemical persuasion—soporific terpenes for mammals, slow rust for metals, anesthetic mist for flames. Think of it as a reflex arc 120 metres wide.

Longevity.  
Carbon-dating places the oldest xylem layers at 182,000 years. Growth rings ceased 40,000 years ago; since then the creature has repaired but not enlarged itself. Death would require total desiccation or excavation down to the limestone seam—both unlikely in this climate.

2. Assisted Organs: the Fixed Symbionts  
Over deep time the Grove has recruited helpers, offering niche and nutrition in exchange for labour.

• Sap-scarabs (Circulix sonorata)  
Appearance: thumb-long, ovoid, elytra like burnished lapis shot with zinc veins.  
Work: their rasping mandibles prune secretory pores that would otherwise clog. Each sip of syrup removes a micro-plug of hardened resin. Without them the pumps stall in twelve days.  
Life cycle: eggs are laid inside spent lenticels; larvae feed on crystallised sugars, pupate into hollow columns where sound is loudest—vibration accelerates metamorphosis.  
Population control: the Grove exudes a bitter alkaloid whenever beetle numbers cross the 17,000 threshold, stunning adults long enough for floor folds to swallow and digest them.

• Choir crickets (Rhythmomusica consors)  
Physiology: transparent tegmina with serrated edges; by twisting these like tuning pegs each individual matches its personal carrier wave to the nearest column-heart.  
Function: their harmonics cancel acoustic peaks that would fatigue plant tissue. They are, literally, living dampers.  
Sociality: solitary by day, collectivise at dusk into rotating chords: first fifths, then minor sevenths, then home tonic on the moment the floor begins its nightly dew draw.  
Reproduction: pairs weave egg-sachets from discarded beetle wings; the sachets dangle inside lenticel mouths where humidity never drops below ninety percent.

• Drift-eels (Anguilla muscata)  
Habitat: the pressurised sap canals. Skin studded with sodium-selective pores lets them osmose sugar while shedding waste salts the Grove dislikes.  
Behaviour: at night they surface through drinking pores, suspend themselves in pooled condensate, and hunt dew-gnats with a flick that sounds like a dropped reed.  
Mutualism: their gills host a purple bacterium that fixes nitrogen directly into the sap—primary fertiliser for the cortex-floor.  
Reproduction: parthenogenetic; elder eels simply bud twin tail-tips that grow into juvenile heads, then split off during next high-pressure pulse.

• Velvet sloth, a.k.a. The Floor-Ox (Pilosa siphonophora)  
Dimensions: two metres nose to rump, mass perhaps eighty kilos of water and silk keratin.  
Tongue: bifurcates 30 cm from tip, each lobe ringed with osmotic membranes that slide into floor vents like IV needles. While docked the animal is rigid; heartbeat slows to four per minute and pupils iris shut.  
Role: when it withdraws, the vent rims close around strings of compressed lignin fibres pulled loose by suction. The Grove digests these frays and thereby disposes of senescent tissue with no self-inflicted wounds.  
Life history: a female births a single neonate every 11 years, placing it in the crook of a buttress. The Grove spritzes indole vapours that keep predators away during the six-month helpless phase. Predators do wander in (see Transients) but rarely leave with appetite intact.

3. Microlife and Exilics  
Because decomposers are barred, the Grove outsources decay to micro-engines it monitors tightly.

• Lumen mites: glassy arachnids that chew flaked cellulose, excrete pellets already laced with cellulose synthase; the cortex picks up these pellets and presses them into weak spots.  
• Cloud yeast: free-floating clades that spend 18 minutes alive during every mist pulse, ferment stray sugars into ethanol, then desiccate into spores. The evaporating alcohol disinfects lenticel rims.  
• Pollen hounds: nematodes with magnetite whiskers that follow electrostatic trails; they patrol for alien pollen grains and puncture them, leaking cytoplasm the floor reclaims.

4. Transient Visitors

• Basin herons: plains birds that descend only when seasonal lightning drives ground eels above clay level. They stand on one column root, spear an eel as it surfaces, and lift off before the Grove can corrode the iron flecks in their beaks.  
• Salt prospectors: bipedal nomads who value condensed sap for its mineral glow. They arrive tool-less, lap crystals with copperless tongues, leave dizzy from terpene dreams. Local folklore claims those dreams contain cartographic warnings, and so far no prospector has cut a living part of the Grove.

• Thunder-hares: lightning-triggered parthenogens of the plain; twice a decade a gravid female flees into the basin and births a litter under the cooling mist. None survive a week—the immune lace reprocesses them—but their brief silhouettes provide the only mammalian cries ever heard inside these walls.

5. The Uncounted Inhabitant: Resonance  
Not an organism, yet as persistent as flesh. The overlapping beats, clicks, hisses and chords produce standing sound-columns that migrate in predictable spirals. Any body within that column—scarab, cricket, or human thorax—finds its hollow spaces vibrating, drawing breath faster, heart slower. The sensation is of being tuned. Elders from the clay villages call the phenomenon “the Hollow’s third lung” and insist it carries memories: if you hum the column’s note back at dusk, the pumps momentarily syncopate, answering across language.

Whether resonance is conscious is undecidable, but it behaves like a guardian. Loud metallic clangs, for instance, set up discordant overtones; within minutes mist thickens, visibility drops, and visitors feel compelled to kneel until the note resolves.

6. Life Trajectories  
Birth, feeding, courtship, digestion, death—each happens here without waste, unlooped from decay. Larvae grow on sugar, adults on resonance, the Grove on nitrogen the eels fixed from air pulled down each chill night. Even corpses are not buried; they are played, sounded, echoed until their carbon chooses a new rhythm and is invited back into the floor.

Thus the Hollow Grove is neither garden nor fortress but a closed choir: one vast vegetal throat, its symbionts the articulations that keep the song unbroken. To ask which beings live here is to ask which notes belong to the chord. All of them—or none, if you wait long enough for silence, a condition the Grove has never allowed.
