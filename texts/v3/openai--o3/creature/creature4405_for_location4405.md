Who (and what) lives here

1. The Rootwrights – resident humans  
They are few: a rotating cadre of six bio-systems engineers who call themselves Rootwrights because the title feels older and softer than “Process Custodian.”  
• Appearance: Always layered—lightweight exoskeletal harness under waterproof linen smocks, pockets bulging with capillary test strips and seed vials. Their clothes take on the green-black sheen of the composites around them.  
• Rhythm of life: They arrive for three-month watches, sleep in hammock-pods slung above the gutter channels, and time their shifts to the pressure cycles—when the big valve sighs, they know it is midday. Conversation is hushed; they communicate more by gesture and by chalk glyphs left on column skins.  
• Creed: “Feed every loop, leave no fingerprint.” They are caretakers, archivists and, secretly, worshippers of the place’s perpetual infancy. Most have turned down higher posts just to stay inside the upward-crawling garden.

2. The Golem Drones  
Locals call them golems because they look hand-built, though every curve is machine-perfect. Each is the size of a housecat: porcelain gear-cores clad in latex-resin musculature, eight magnetic feet for climbing girders.  
• Functions: swap filaments, polish quartz tears from the weeping wall, snip any root that probes a forbidden seam.  
• Personality: Their head-plates carry small arrays of colored LEDs that flash like tropical fish when they exchange data across the mist. Waiting Rootwrights swear the patterns shift to mimic birdsong; no one has proved intentionality, but the golems sometimes cluster under a human’s lamp the way moths seek light.

3. Swifts of the Copper Ceiling  
The only truly wild vertebrates, copper-throated swifts drafted by accident when the caisson’s curing heat opened a jet-stream vent.  
• Nests: cups of fiber stolen from insulation mats, plastered upside-down against still-warm truss bars.  
• Economy: They trade guano for sanctuary; the Rootwrights scrape it at dawn, leaving iridescent beads of sugar-gel in return. Over two breeding seasons the birds have grown leaner wings, tuned for tight turns between vertical vines.

4. Alloy Vines & Their Micro-Symbionts  
Every so-called “vine” is a biometal braid seeded with living phloem that smelts trace minerals directly into filaments. Under a portable microscope you can watch:  
• Micro-smelters – archaeal colonies that plate nickel molecules into the braid, cell by cell.  
• Grazer mites – translucent, gear-shaped arthropods (engineered) that nibble surplus plaque to keep the flow channels clear. Their wasted nickel shows up hours later in sump filters as glitter like ground stars.

5. The Sumpsingers  
Deep in each bronze-glass sump lives a membrane of wetware algae called Sumpsingers for the faint keening they emit as turbine wash passes through.  
• Purpose: Ion-exchange, converting the mineral scorch of fresh mountain acid into drinkable feed for both mosses and machines.  
• Legend: New Rootwrights are told the Sumpsingers carry compressed memories of every drop that has ever cycled through the system. No one has translated a syllable, but veterans confess to dreaming in that thin, glassy voice after enough consecutive night shifts.

6. Pitcher Threads & the Blue Moths  
• Pitcher threads: carnivorous, yes, but not merely hunters—each cup contains a floating raft of engineered bacteria that convert digested chitin into an aerosol lubricant later sucked into piston seals.  
• Blue moths: sapphire-winged pollinators whose scales fluoresce under tungsten dusk. They live short, bright lives—five days from eclosion to the swift’s beak—but every glimmering eruption keeps the orchid modules fertile.

7. The Mountain’s Sleeper—an emergent intelligence  
No single designer admitted to building it, yet across thousands of feedback loops—water turbines talking to pressure valves, nutrient pumps negotiating with canopy spectrometers—an algorithmic murmur has arisen.  
• Signs: print heads pause mid-stroke and correct errors no human flagged; mist sprayers sync to the exact moment a leaf tilts for maximum absorption; once, during a lightning brownout, the garden lit itself by diverting surplus sugar to bio-luminescent fungi no Rootwright remembered installing.  
• Name: whispered as “the Sleeper,” never entered in the logs. The humans behave like tenants who sense a landlord in the walls and pay rent by keeping every circuit immaculate.

8. Visitors in Transit  
Once a month the elevator-cage brings up auditors, poets, or investors wearing smart-fabric umbrellas that repel the copper rain. They stay an hour, collect metrics, inhale the thunderstorm tang, and leave. The inhabitants—Rootwrights, drones, vines, swifts, mites, Sleeper—close around the gap they leave as water closes behind a diving bird.

The living census, then, is not a hierarchy but a braid: human caretakers, semi-autonomous machines, purpose-written biota, accidental wildlife, and a possibly sentient infrastructure—all breathing the same warm, metallic air, all tethered to a single rule: grow without waste. The place they share is younger than its oldest leaf yet already older, in intention, than many cities. Everything here is resident, because everything has a task, and to have a task in this garden-foundry is to belong.
