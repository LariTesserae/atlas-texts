Who lives here

1. The visible residents – the Gardens themselves  
Strictly speaking, the only life you can point to without arguing philosophy is vegetal. Every shape that moves or glimmers is a plant or something plant-adjacent.

• Frond-cities  
The broad kelp sheets are not single organisms but braided collectives. Each ribbon is an avenue of interlocking leaf-segments that merge, separate and merge again, exchanging strands of tissue the way terrestrial lichens exchange chemicals. An individual “frond” can be three kilometres long and spend a century creeping from one terrace to the next. Seen from nearby it is a slow green glacier whose leading edge searches with root-tongues for the next finger-high riser.

• The step-mosses  
Where the risers are too steep for kelp, a quilt of hexagonal cushions grows, each cell puffing and sinking in counter-rhythm with the weak tidal flex of the stone. The cushions are pneumatic: they drink dissolved gas through honey-combed interiors, then contract to send the gas to rhizomes deeper in the basalt seams. From a distance the stairs look as though they are breathing, a collective tide half a heartbeat per hour.

• Root-rivers  
Below the visible carpet the true body of the Gardens begins: roots knitted as thick as cables, insinuating themselves into joints in the masonry. The wooden pegs that once locked block to block are long since swallowed; the roots now anchor stone to stone with greater patience than the builders ever had.

These plants are not passive. They prune themselves where too much shade collects, open fissures to let buried lightless leaves be carried away by the soft current, and time their spore blizzards so that no species ever crowds out another. They behave, in short, as though guided—but there is no animal hand to guide them.

2. The ambiguous residents – The Quiet Architects  
If you stay long enough you will discover the question local explorers spend lifetimes failing to settle: are the builders gone, or did they stay and become something else? Three kinds of evidence argue that they are still here, albeit in a form no surface taxonomy recognises.

Evidence A: Living tools  
In deeper bowls you can still find mauls, awls and wedge-stones, exactly where the catalogue of earlier dives said they would be—but none of the objects are quite inert. Fibres, genetically identical to the step-mosses, have infiltrated the handles. Mineral analysis shows that the edges of the chisels regrow by microns every decade, as if the basalt itself were being recrystallised from within. A tool that repairs itself over millennia is a tool that remains in somebody’s service.

Evidence B: Pattern maintenance  
However thick the vegetal mat becomes, the finger-high risers never disappear. Roots that cross a riser stop thickening at precisely fourteen millimetres and divert elsewhere. It is grooming on a continental scale, too consistent to be chance and too distributed to belong to any single plant. Something is enforcing the masonry’s readability.

Evidence C: The Silence Wells  
At the nadirs of the oldest bowls there are pits the width of market squares and only a palm deep. Nothing luminous grows in them. Nothing grows at all. Yet sonar shows the substratum is still peat, still full of nutrients. Something removes every stray spore, every drifting filament, maintaining a blank slate of blackness. When submersibles linger over the pits, instruments register a faint magnetic flutter, like circuitry waking for one heartbeat then sleeping again.

The prevailing hypothesis: the Architects never left; they dissolved themselves into the gardens. The roots are nerves, the kelp-ribbons are limbs, the pattern-maintenance is will. They are not so much inhabitants as the place made sentient.

3. The incontrovertible residents – The Sleeper Spores  
Whether or not the stonework thinks, another kind of life unquestionably does: the sleeper spores that drift like opal-snow every few decades.

Life-cycle  
• Cotyledon phase – a spore lands, unfurls two paper-thin sails that catch the omnipresent current and tack the infant plant to a location whose chemistry pleases it.  
• Mirror phase – the sails curl downward and calcify, becoming a lens that concentrates the faint cyan pulses of neighbouring leaves. By stealing light-bandwidth the seedling pays back the favour a decade later by emitting its own amplified pulses, a kind of communal relay station.  
• Fracture phase – after roughly forty years the plant breaks itself into three identical shards, each of which resumes the cycle. No generation is older or younger than any other; age propagates outward like ripples on a pond.

Ecological role  
Sleeper spores are the only agents of anything approaching speed. They sweep nutrients from the upper terraces to the lower in less than a century—alarmingly fast for this country-sized sundial of a biome. In doing so they prevent the deepest bowls from entering perfect stasis. Their hurry is, paradoxically, what keeps the place slow but living.

4. Hypothetical residents – Things a visitor might swear to have seen  
In the middle distance of a weak cyan flare you may imagine silhouettes:

• The Librarian Fish  
Shaped like a double wedge of paper slipped into the water, each ‘fish’ glides without moving a fin, following the grooves where kelp has pressed the stone concave. No instrument has confirmed mass or temperature. Photomultipliers record them only when no other light source is active, as though they exist purely as a subtraction from surrounding brightness. Two teams swear they saw the same individual twice, forty-seven kilometres apart and six years offset. If it is real, it is either very fast or broadly omnipresent, neither of which fits the physics of this sea.

• The Echoless Choir  
A chord of five to seven hums, felt more than heard—subaudible tremors that rattle calcium in the inner ear. Divers report the vibrations when alone and far from equipment, always just before a spore blizzard. Geological sensors show no matching quake. Psychologists blame anticipatory nerves; biologists note that moss cushions swell exactly three percent larger an hour after the “choir” is felt, as though coaxed by a tuning fork only they can sense.

No solid evidence supports either sighting. Yet the community of investigators treats the stories tenderly, the way astronomers treat the odd unexplained flicker in a familiar constellation.

5. Social organisation (if the Architects are indeed alive)  
• Tempo of thought – Estimates suggest one ideation every two to three surface days. A decision on the scale of redirecting a root-river might take a decade.  
• Medium of memory – Laminar rings inside the peat record variations in trace metals laid down during spore blizzards. The entire continent is a spinning-disc hard drive three kilometres thick, played back by chelation currents through the roots.  
• Communication – Changes in bioluminescent pulse cadence function as shout, whisper or signature. Where pulses overlap they produce interference fringes that encode higher-order meaning. Think semaphore that only becomes a language after cycling for forty uninterrupted hours.  
• Politics – None observed. Either consensus is automatic or disagreement is pruned out of the genome. The place gives the impression of total internal accord, which surface anthropologists find either utopian or sinister, depending on caffeine intake.

6. Relationship to visitors  
The Gardens do not obstruct a diver, nor aid one. If you exhale bubbles (some suits still do) kelp-fronds will arch away until the last sphere of alien gas has collapsed, then return to original posture. Leave scrap metal and within a season a sugaring of carbonate film will lock it in place, turning rubbish into furniture. The only behaviour that elicits measurable response is fast motion: a sudden fin-kick, a motor burst. Then every bioluminescent margin in line of sight extinguishes at once, plunging you into blank water until you remember how to be slow.

A final courtesy: once you leave, roots close over your footprints and the cyan pulses resume with no visible seam. The Gardens accept even intrusion the way they accept erosion—as another small fact in an ocean of time.

7. If the Builders wake  
Suppose the magnetic flutter in a Silence Well grows until compass needles anywhere on the continent tremble. Suppose the chisels grow edges sharp enough to shave basalt filaments again. The best guess is they would continue exactly where they left off: levering new blocks into place to extend the pattern outward one more ring, giving their gardens another million years of promenade before the next inevitable siltfall. Their motives would be no easier to read then than now. They would still refuse light, still measure hands-width risers with stone twine, still invite kelp to claim the finished course the moment the last peg was tapped home.



So: who lives here?  
• Certainly the plants, which behave like an orchestra that has misplaced its audience.  
• Almost certainly the Architects, diffused into root and masonry, thinking one cold, perfect geometric thought every time a surface civilisation rises and falls.  
• Possibly littoral myths in fish-shapes or choir-tones, jokes the continent tells itself while it waits for its next ring of steps.  

Everything else—predator, prey, haste, accident—stayed behind in a brighter, warmer, noisier world whose sky the ocean long ago forgot.
