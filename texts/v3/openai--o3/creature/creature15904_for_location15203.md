Who or what claims the Pocket Yards depends on how fine a net you cast.  If you stay at the scale of people you will meet only one kind of resident; widen your mesh and entire nations begin to appear, none larger than a fist yet all convinced the Yard was built for them alone.  What follows starts with the smallest, accumulates upward, and ends with the single breathing folk who dare to call the place home.

─────────────────
1.  The Oxide Skin
─────────────────
Form:  A living varnish two molecules thick, woven from iron-eating archaea bound in cobalt-blue fungal threads.  
Spread:  Everywhere light can reach and much that it cannot; 96 % of hard surfaces carry the Skin.  
Habit:  Ingests rust, excretes magnetite scales that lie flat until a pressure wave passes, then bristle like fields of wheat.  The mass rise and fall of these scales is the Yard’s first response to any disturbance, a shiver felt through gloves before it is seen.

Why it matters:
•  The Skin stores charge.  A minute of nearby lightning endows an entire wall with enough potential to stun a swimmer.  
•  Patterns in its grain are readable.  Rust paleographers can date collapses to the hour by the concentric halos the Skin leaves around an impact.  
•  It is the Yard’s immune system.  Bare metal is an exposed wound, and the Skin will close it.  Strike off a flake and you can watch replacement sheets knit themselves while you count to sixty.

─────────────────
2.  The Threadborn
─────────────────
Form:  Spool-eels grown past the egg-ribbon stage into metre-long cords of cartilage and muscle no thicker than cable ties.  Each eel keeps a line of coppery myomere running its length, acting as both ear and voice.  
Population:  Tens of thousands, but seldom more than twenty visible at once—most live braided in the conduit warrens.  
Senses & Speech:  They do not see, taste, or smell.  Instead they listen for magnetic reverberation.  A Threadborn “sentence” is a rising cascade of microwatts along its body, easily mistaken for a grounding fault.

Role in the Yard:
•  The eels pinch open steam bladders inside the Boilervanes to steal heat, keeping their nests at exact incubation temperature.  
•  Their constant rasping with mineralised jaws frees new rust for the Skin to eat: host and parasite completing a loop.  
•  If startled, a shoal will twist into a single thicker rope and lash off as a whip, capable of shredding wetsuit fabric.  Divers call this behaviour “snapping the hawser.”

─────────────────
3.  The Clock Crabs
─────────────────
Morphology:  Carapace a perfect annulus, centre hollow, legs doubled and articulated like the hands of a watch.  Size ranges from ring-finger circlets to bracelets an adult could wear.  
Diet:  Equal parts flake-rust and the fizzy biofilm that breeds in warm eddies.  
Behavioural tic:  They abhor silence.  In the rare hush between turbine strokes they drum their hollow bodies on substrate until noise returns.  The longer the quiet, the faster the drumming—a living metronome that keeps the Yard’s heartbeat nominal.

Symbiosis with machinery:
•  Clock crabs shelter inside dormant gearboxes; their scuttling clears silt from cog-teeth, so if the gearbox ever does re-engage it meets no resistance.  
•  The animals remember rotations.  Fisher-engineers trap a dozen, strap them to a stripped motor shaft, then read the pattern of scratches in the shell after release: an analog tachograph pulled from living paper.

─────────────────
4.  The Machine Remainders
─────────────────
Not true organisms, yet they behave closely enough to earn territory.

4a.  Lanterners  
Broken inspection drones whose light housings survive while motors, cameras, and control boards have rotted away.  Threadborn families have moved inside the casings, tugging at the swivel joints to aim the reflector dish wherever mineral gnats are thickest.  Seen at distance, a Lanternet looks self-guided, pausing, angling its glow, resuming patrol—until you approach and find only thready fish minds at work.

4b.  Reciprocants  
Chains of three to five conveyor motors lashed together by kelp-black cables, sharing the trickle produced when any rotor somewhere in the chain is spun by current.  What one motor earns, all share.  Over years they have drifted into alcoves, wedged themselves, and now throb in chorus.  Reciprocants are felt before seen: a pulse that enters the soles, passes up the tibia, and settles in the molars.

4c.  The Mouth  
Once a waste-shredder feed hopper.  Rust collapse and sediment have left only its circular maw protruding from floor-wreckage.  Venturi siphons give it breath: draw too near during a surge and it inhales anything under fifteen kilos.  During slack water it exhales quietly, dusting the plaza with tan clouds.  No creature lives in the Mouth, yet it is fed daily by Clock crabs who roll offerings of loose metal to its edge and let the suction finish the gift.

─────────────────
5.  Human Presence:  The Rivet-Menders
─────────────────
Number:  Between twenty and thirty breathing souls, census to census.  They are the Yard’s biggest animals, though few exceed 160 cm: generations born to constant compression grow stocky, air-conserving frames.  
Origin myth (their own telling):  “We were the last shift that never clocked out.”  These are descendants of early salvage crews who found the place too valuable—or too troublesome—to abandon.

Physiology & Gear:
•  Lungs trained for hyper-capnia.  They take one mouthful from a safety bottle and can work ten minutes before the next.  
•  Eyes covered by tallow lenses that refract the Yard’s drizzle of arc-light into usable contrast.  Outside water, the lenses fog worthless; none of them see properly in air.  
•  Speech impossible in the roar, they sign with both hands and tongue.  A flicker of fingertip on lower lip can convey grammars as subtle as sarcasm.

Territory & Architecture:
•  They occupy the Switchroom and six adjacent service corridors kept in conditions approaching dryness.  Every door is sealed with mossy rubber and chalked with sigils rating how many atmospheres of ocean currently lean against it.  
•  Personal space is vertical: hammocks strung three tiers high, the uppermost reserved for pregnant sleepers so any breach floods childless beds first.  

Economy:
•  Rust reading is law and livelihood.  The caste of Paleographers interprets corrosion tides to choose which pillars to scarf-plate, which to abandon.  
•  They mine silence.  Moments when the Yard’s drums misfire are harvested onto wax cylinders using hydrophones strapped to ribcages.  That bottled calm sells for fortunes to surface dwellers who need a room with no echo to calibrate delicate instruments.  
•  Trade upward through a single freight shaft whose lift they power by swimming: six Rivet-menders harnessed to a capstan in dolphin kick, hauling goods to the waiting barge above the roof.

Religion, such as it is:
•  They keep three Rules (identical to those scrawled on battered placards outsiders may read on approach).  Anything louder than a heartbeat during the hush is sacrilege.  Anything weighed down before it tells its history is theft.  Anything that blooms without moving water to feed it is a forgery and must be scraped clean.  
•  Their dead are rust-pickled.  A body is laid on untreated iron; the Skin rushes over it, fixing flesh in a ferric carapace.  Once sealed, the corpse is wedged upright in the Boilervanes where rising vapour spins the red statues slowly, keeping watch.

Relationship with other life:
•  Threadborn are tolerated, even herded, to gnaw conduits that need opening.  
•  Clock crabs they eat—shell and all—in a single crunch, for iodine and the coppery after-taste they say keeps vertigo away.  
•  The Machine Remainders they bless or curse depending on how cooperative the apparatus is that day.  Any motor that powers up unasked receives a braided loop of eel hide draped over its axle: bride-gift or muzzle, reader’s choice.

─────────────────
6.  Visitors & Unwelcome Futures
─────────────────
The Yard is not a closed story.  Salvage syndicates send glossy-helmeted raiders; acoustic biologists come to record the infrasonic chorus; storm-tossed refugees sometimes fall through a rent in the roof and decide one pocket of drowned factory is safer than the war above.

Each arrival rewrites the fragile equilibrium.  The Oxide Skin thickens where boot thrash scrapes it raw.  Threadborn steal copper braid from new electronics and learn strange dialects of hum.  Rivet-menders fashion surface plastics into watertight nursery cribs.  The Yard accepts, digests, redistributes—never the same, never quite collapsed.

Give it another century and even the human shapes may wear down to iron ghosts, drumming in the hush so the turbines remember to wake.  Or perhaps the Menders’ children will still swing in their hammocks, reading rust rings, taking the quiet’s pulse, convinced in their marrow that no world exists higher than that thunder ceiling thirty metres above.

For now, if you slip through a fist-sized tear in the hull and listen, here is who answers:

The Yard itself with a sheet-metal groan,  
a million magnetite scales quivering hello,  
cable-tongued eels asking your voltage,  
crabs keeping time,  
machines coughing toward dreams,  
and finally human hands signing in the dark:

Welcome.  Mind the hush.  Tell the iron something true before you pass.
