Who lives here is not a figure you can point to, but a presence that lies everywhere the water lies.  The plain-folk who still cross the coast in winter call it, when they bother to mention it at all, Lung-Moss—though the name is wildly partial, the way “skin” is partial for a whole body.

1. Anatomy  
   • Seen with hurried eyes it is only moss, the thick emerald quilt that covers half the cave floor.  
   • Kneel and lift a pad and you find a second layer: threads of translucent flesh braided through the limestone’s surface, as if someone had carded spun glass into the rock.  
   • Farther down, a third stratum hardens into chalky plates the width of a fingernail.  They lock together like fish scales and merge, at last, with the bedrock itself.  
   • Moss, flesh, plate, stone: four layers, one organism.  The green quilt is its lung, the glass threads its nerve, the plates its skeleton, the cave its shell.  Nothing here is merely root or merely host; cave and creature are joined reef-tight.

2. Ways of Feeding  
   • Sunlight is almost absent, so Lung-Moss eats by chemosynthesis.  Acidic droplets drip from its own capillary pores, bite a breath’s worth of calcium from the ceiling, and carry that mineral down the flutes of rock.  Where the droplets land on the floor the moss drinks them, harvesting the dissolved calcite and the faint electrical charge released by the reaction.  
   • Once a season, when a fox or a reindeer calf dies below the slot, the green quilt blooms darker.  It oozes a clear jelly that slow-wraps the carcass.  Months later both bones and memory have been pulled flat into the living carpet, white lacework inside the green.

3. Sensory Life  
   • The organism “hears” through pressure.  Each glassy nerve-thread is fluid-filled; vibration travels this inner water faster than it can travel stone.  
   • The constant in-and-out gale gives the moss an echograph of its own room.  The drip-booms paint it thousands of contour lines every minute.  Sound replaces eyesight so perfectly that the creature thickens most where the echoes are gentlest—the same nook where the vanished hunters once sat.  
   • Torches disturb it.  Not the light—Lung-Moss is blind—but the tiny explosions of sap in wet wood.  Newcomers who build a fire notice the moss retracting from the ring like frost receding from a pot of coals.

4. Motion and Growth  
   • Speed, in its vocabulary, is measured in seasons.  A single pad advances perhaps two widths of a thumb from winter solstice to winter solstice.  The whole organism tides upslope very, very slowly, chasing the line of richest drip.  
   • What looks like a cushion can, in a bad drought year, loosen its grip on the limestone plate beneath, fold like a tongue, and slide a metre overnight toward wetter ground.  Few see this; the cave is almost never without water, and travellers are rarer still.

5. Reproduction  
   • Lung-Moss does not cast spores.  Instead it sets pearls.  When the glass nerve-threads knot densely around a kernel of calcite they form a bead the size of a raindrop, lighter than dried thistle.  
   • On the loudest days—wind shrieking, pressure plumbing—the cave exhales hard enough to fling a handful of beads up through the slit.  Most burst in the desiccating air above the plain.  One in a thousand finds another micro-cave, another seam, and begins again.

6. Mind (such as it is)  
   • Intelligence here is depth rather than quickness.  Patterns that repeat for a century carve permanent grooves in the creature’s behavior.  Change happens, but patience edits it beyond recognition.  
   • The moss can tell heavy footfalls from falling ice, bone rattle from pebble tumble, and living heartbeats from dying ones.  It does not decide in any mammalian sense; yet after eight or ten generations of pearls, the lineage that shied from iron tools now lies lower to the floor than the lineage that ignored them.  Selection becomes habit, habit becomes instinct, instinct becomes what looks uncomfortably like preference.

7. Relationship to the Cave Wren  
   • The albino wrens require warm nests; Lung-Moss generates a constant exothermic whisper—barely a half degree—during its mineral digestion.  The birds tuck their mud-lined hollows directly above the thickest pads.  
   • In return, each time a wren preens it shakes loose mites glutted with blood.  The mites drop into the moss and rupture.  Micro-kills though they are, they add nitrogen to the green quilt’s diet.  The arrangement is not mutualism in the bright-daylight sense; it is simply the only exchange here that has settled into equilibrium.

8. On the Memory of People  
   • The stone blade, the charcoal, the peg holes—Lung-Moss is already digesting them.  A century from now, the scraper will read as just another shard of flint locked inside the plates.  Two centuries, and the charcoal’s carbon will be spread so thin across the quilt that no fleck is black.  
   • Whatever language was once shouted nose-to-ear now exists for the moss as a faint preferential weighting: places where vibration matched a certain cadence encouraged the pads to thicken, much the way a violin back takes on the shape of its music.  Stranger still, those tessellated thickenings change the cave’s acoustics, feeding the same rhythms forward.  Culture curving into biology, biology curving into geology—everything trending toward roundness.

9. Hostility and Hospitality  
   • Touch it gently and the surface feels cool, plush, almost luxurious after the brutal plain above.  
   • Lie on it too long and your weight makes a hollow that never quite springs back; the organism molds around you, samples salt through your damp clothes, records the slow drums of your heart.  You stand, and an outline remains, a darker green that will linger for months, learning the shape of mammal.  
   • Slit a pad with steel and you get no protest, only a silent fount of thin, mineral-rich water that stings the cuticles.  But return a year later and you will not find your mark.  The moss will have carpeted the blade, the handle, the memory of the cut, converting all of it to inaudible hum.

10. Why No One Notices  
    • Nothing about Lung-Moss rises, shouts, glows, or flees.  It hugs stone, obeys water, measures time in melt-freeze pulses.  To travelers focused on the roar, it is furnishing, not resident.  
    • Only when someone camps long enough to feel the floor subtly pulsing—breathing opposite the wind—does the suspicion take hold that the cave is not an empty lung but a living one, and that every drip-boom in the darkness is both heartbeat and footstep of the thing beneath.

So the cave is occupied after all: not by hunters’ ghosts, not by albino beasts, but by a single distributed creature older than any artifact within reach—an organism that has married limestone and weather and its own silence into a permanence nothing upright can match.  The plain above comes and goes with the ice ages; Lung-Moss will still be here, listening, sanding all sharpness smooth, exhaling once for every eight heartbeats of the wide, indifferent world.
