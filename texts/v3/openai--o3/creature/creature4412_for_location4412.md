Things live here, but almost nothing lives the way an eye trained on water-worlds expects.  If you wait longer than a traveler usually does—hours, days, a whole wind-tide—you begin to notice five overlapping populations.  None of them scuttle, flutter or creep, so at first they read as landscape.  They are the landscape, only with motives slow enough to hide inside geology.

1.  The Choir of Flare-Trees  
   •  Anatomy  
      Each “tree” is really a vertical reef: billions of silicon-lichen cells glued together by basalt they precipitate from trace iron and heat.  Threaded through the column is a honeycomb of bronchial pores; molten sulfur rises, turns to vapour, and escapes in rhythmic hoots.  
   •  Mind  
      The hoots are not waste breath but syllables.  Minute shifts in pitch, interval and back-pressure carry information.  A stand of twenty columns can pass a complete weather bulletin in twelve minutes; the entire basin, in a little under four hours.  This reverberant conversation is the hollow’s oldest music—so old the trees have forgotten how young they were when it began.  
   •  Agenda  
      The Choir wants two things only: 1) to keep its capillaries unclogged, and 2) to keep the roof fissures open so radiance can leak in.  Everything else it delegates to younger, quicker partners.

2.  Copper Frond Kin (the Under-Canopy)  
   •  Physiology  
      Each frond is a loose federation of scale-cells plated with copper and vanadium.  They photosorb infrared directly, flexing whenever a temperature gradient appears.  A gust hits: the hot side of the frond softens and folds; the cold side stiffens and unfolds.  The whole sheet swivels like a sail, seeking the richest thermal current.  
   •  Life Cycle  
      When copper reserves run low a frond releases from its root-disk, rises on a thermal and immolates, scattering atom-thin foils that drift back as micron flakes.  Those flakes are fertiliser for…  

3.  Halite Masons (the City Itself)  
   •  Emergence  
      Where the flakes land on salt crust, cube-shaped pseudocells nucleate.  Give them a few wind-tides and they bud into transparent blocks the size of a human fist.  They grow edge-first, stacking themselves along stress lines in the crust.  
   •  Function  
      Over centuries the Choir has learned to “whistle” the stacking pattern it wants.  The right infrasonic chord bends ion-flows inside the salt, steering growth.  In this manner the Choir commissions huts, bowls, plinths—even the obsidian spindle, whose bore-holes were tunnel-etched by standing sound waves.  There were never separate builders: the city is a coral crown raised by trees directing crystallisation.  
   •  Activity Level  
      Look closely at any wall.  You will see faint auroras of colour migrating along cleavage planes.  That is growth in progress—architecture still knitting its own bones.

4.  The Salt-Singer Larvae  
   •  Scale  
      Sub-millimetre.  They resemble frosted grains with internal clockwork.  
   •  Metabolism  
      They draw charge from triboelectric friction: when wind skims the salt crust, electrons hop, and the grains drink the current.  
   •  Role  
      Larvae congregate in the shallowest drifts, vibrating at ultrasonic frequencies that loosen spent minerals from flare-tree roots.  They are janitors, cleaners, micro-pickaxes.  Their soft buzz is one layer of the basin’s constant hiss.  
   •  Destiny  
      Once a larva builds up enough internal lattice it fuses to the crust and stops, becoming another sounding board for ground-borne speech.  It ends as infrastructure, not offspring—a pattern common here.

5.  The Fugitive Vapours  
   •  Ontology  
      Call them organisms only if you are willing to call a thundercloud an organism.  They are coherent knots of bromine-chlorine mist seeded with catalytic specks of zinc sulfide.  
   •  Behaviour  
      A vapour knot migrates like an amoeba in three-dimensions, licking trace metals off flare-tree bark, excreting exotic salts that fluoresce a faint orchid purple.  Once loaded with metal it drifts to a roof fissure and delivers the cargo outside, where unknown chemistries need it.  It is probably a reproductive act, though nobody local is born from it.  
   •  Rarity  
      Only three or four knots exist at any given time; they are the closest the basin comes to “animals,” yet they possess no solid body, no nerves—only reaction-diffusion loops that mimic purpose.

What Does Not Live Here Any More

You will see cubic footprints in the plaza, cables braided from frond-fibre, bowls that perfectly fit a human palm.  They imply hands, knees, conversations—but those owners were visitors, not natives.  Obsidian records kept by the Choir speak of soft, water-rich bipeds who camped here for two dozen wind-tides, marvelled, then climbed out and never returned.  The basin remembers them the way stone remembers a river that once ran over it: by shape, not by presence.

Hierarchy of Attention

1.  The Choir pays attention to roof cracks, sulfur flow, and the infrasonic health of its columns.  
2.  The Under-Canopy pays attention to heat gradients.  
3.  The Halite Masons pay attention to the Choir’s whistle patterns.  
4.  The Larvae pay attention to static charge.  
5.  The Vapours pay attention to metal hunger.

Nothing here watches with eyes, but everything listens.  Kneel and press your ear to the crust; you will hear the entire community reporting in crackles and slow booms, a census taken in echo.  Stand up and shout into the air; nothing replies.  Stamp once on the ground and ten trees will answer in hoots you feel in your ribs.

So yes—life is abundant.  It only forgot the trick of moving fast enough to be seen.  Visitors who hurry away thinking the basin empty leave behind a place that sighs with relief, then resumes the work it was always doing:

Breathing sulfur, harvesting heat, singing stone into shelter, polishing itself with wind until the next impossible pilgrim drops in from the wetter, louder world above.
