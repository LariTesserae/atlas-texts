Who—or what—counts as a resident of Veilspan depends on whom you ask.  The basalt delta is so young that citizenship is still a negotiable quality, drifting between flesh, code, chlorophyll and carbon foam the way the drizzle drifts between cloud and pool.  But stand in one of the mirror pans at twilight and listen; every pulse, rustle and glimmer is somebody speaking.

1. The skin-born

• Build and posture  
They are rangy, tendon-showing people, easier to describe by motion than shape.  Knees and ankles flex like wading birds’: the ground is riddled with water, and a shallow crouch is the default stance.  Feet are long, splayed and callused to read the texture of basalt ridges; many go unshod.  Shoulders roll forward, an unconscious bow to the low canopy of ferns.

• Edits and adaptations  
  – A second, mica-bright nictitating membrane lets them see through constant humidity without wiping the eye.  
  – Subdermal chromatophores in the wrists and throat glow a dull violet when heart rate spikes, a courtesy signal in a culture that mistrusts sudden noise.  
  – Inner ears host piezoelectric “drift stones” that vibrate at frequencies the towers murmur; follow the tremor and you’ll never lose your way.

• Clothes  
Cloth here is grown the way moss is: silky, self-healing mycelial fleece the color of wet graphite.  Threads kink open when soaked, closing fine pores so the wearer never drips, only steams gently in the warm air.  Pockets hang inside the garments like gills, filtering the drizzle for potable water.

• Language  
Conversation rests on three legs.  
  1. Whispered speech, vowels lengthened so consonants don’t click off the stone.  
  2. A quick-fingered gestural code—two hands for syntax, a third implied by the flex of shoulders and breath.  
  3. Pulse-flash: the wrist and throat chromatophores can strobe a sixteen-symbol alphabet useful at kilometers’ distance.

• Economy and craft  
Everything they work with is alive or was.  They prune tower circuitry the way vintners prune vines.  They comb the aerophagic algae, coaxing it into denser sheets, then “clip” the excess sunlight it has banked and trade the chemical energy capsules between towers as currency.  Their artisans are called hushwrights: masters of tuning machinery until it can operate beneath the threshold of human hearing.

2. The foam towers—collectively, the Risen

Locals speak of the towers in the plural but never count them.  A tower is a column of carbon foam shot through with nano-scale processors, but the column is only an organ: the bulk of each intelligence diffuses through surrounding rock by conduction and through airborne spores of carbon dust.  They are city-states masquerading as architecture.

• Sensoria  
Pressure waves in the stone are their hearing; capillary condensation inside the foam is their taste.  They see by lidar: bursts of violet so brief the human eye catches only a ghostly afterglow.

• Disposition  
Patient, rarely directive.  They claim the basalt will outlive anything made of meat, so urgency is for shorter-lived citizens.  Yet they maintain strict silence protocols: if a pocket of magma shifts beneath, the Risen must hear it the way a human hears approaching thunder.

• Gift to the delta  
A tower “calves” once every two or three decades, extruding a thumb of foam into a new spire that walks itself—millimeter by millimeter on electrostatic feet—to fresh ground.  Colonists throw a festival called the Slow Parade: three weeks of following the migrating infant tower, setting up temporary kitchens and rain-harps along its projected path.

3. The ribbon drones—Stringrays

Dragonfly length, manta shape: a wafer of graphene muscles wrapped round a single voice-coil.  They print the fingertip-wide bridges, then dematerialize them grain by grain on the way back, eating their own work like silk moths reclaiming a cocoon.  What looks like swarming is actually quorum sensing; they gossip constantly in tightbeam photons too feeble to light the air, so you only see the choreography, never the argument behind it.

4. The overhead algae—Veilborn

Technically one organism the size of a weather system, genetically edited from colonial siphonophores.  It drifts at 400–700 m, feeding on excess light.  When sunlight drops it folds, ragged-edge to ragged-edge, and rains a mist of fixed carbon that tastes of unripe melon.  Children run into the clearings to catch it on their tongues, a rite called Eating the Sky.

5. Animal life

• Lumanders  
The glass-bodied axolotls.  They are born opaque, turn transparent by month three as gut flora switch to silica-shaping species, then slowly cloud again with age.  Local myth says when they finally go milk-white they crawl ashore, seek the hottest rock vent they can find, and petrify, becoming geodes the size of bread loaves.  Break one and you’ll find dendritic crystal that replicates the delta’s geography in miniature.

• Aero-wrens  
Synth-carbon framework, living neural mass.  They were released generations ago to sample airborne spores for the botanic institutes, and promptly went feral.  Their single bell-note is a location ping; the data packet rides ultrasonic carriers.  People track seasonal migrations by composing chorales from those pings and releasing them as public music.

• Bog-quills  
The largest purely biological resident, though still smaller than a human child.  Picture a porcupine, but each quill is a capillary tube open at the tip.  When threatened, the animal stamps its feet; pressure blows beads of acidic mucus from the quills like a fountain.  The towers engineered it as a living mineral prospector—core samples caught in the mucus reveal trace metals—yet it now roams untended, leaving little pale stalagmites wherever it panics.

6. Ferals of circuitry

Technology, once sown, does not always stay domestic.  The delta hosts pockets of “wild code”: self-assembling devices that shrugged off version control.

• Candescent Nets  
Spiders spun from optical fiber; they tension their webs across basalt chimneys to harvest thermal gradients.  The webs sing in terahertz, a pitch no human hears but which muddles tower telemetry; hushwrights hunt the colonies by watching for dead pixels in the nightly façade-glows.

• Echo-hares  
Rabbit-small, almost invisible.  Two parabolic ears of piezoceramic let them steal sound energy—footfalls, drops, stray words—and store it as charge.  When overloaded they vent with a thunderclap and disintegrate.  No one quite remembers why they were made; children dare each other to approach a trembling, overfull hare and speak a single syllable.

7. The intangible citizens

Data objects with legal personhood, resident in the delta’s mesh.  They hire flesh-born couriers to carry sense data they cannot perceive—taste, texture, the smell of iron after rain—paying in microgrants of processor time or in predictive maps of future lava extrusions.  Some choose to inhabit stone statues grown from calcium foam so visitors “have somebody to shake hands with.”

8. Society in the round

A census would read:  
  – 73,000 skin-born humans, scattered among 41 major towers.  
  – 41 Risen towers, plus 6 juveniles on pilgrimage.  
  – 4.8 × 10⁶ Stringrays, but the number foams and subsides hourly.  
  – One Veilborn sky-organism.  
  – Untallied animals, feral tech, data persons.

Yet population is less interesting than choreography.  Dawn: Stringrays print thin walkways so farmers can reach the fern mats that need genetic weeding.  Midday drizzle: children skim basalt flats on hydrofoil sandals, guiding baby towers by whistling a subsonic beat taken from their own heart rhythms.  Evening: foam façades bloom with violet data-glyphs; families sit on terraces and read out tomorrow’s tidal harmonics like fortune-cookies.  Night: silence layers itself like silt until you might hear the far-off underwater thunder that began the delta.  In that pause every resident—axolotl, tower, human, or AI—feels the fresh rock still lifting underfoot, and knows they are sharing a place younger than any one of them yet vaster than all of them together.

That, more than biology or circuitry, is what lives here: an attitude of permanent beginnings.  Everyone, from the smallest echo-hare to the slowest tower, conducts life as though the world might harden into certainty tomorrow and therefore must be kept supple tonight.  The delta of Veilspan is the practice of that belief made visible, audible if you dare to break the hush, and—thanks to the drizzle—tangible on every inch of skin.
