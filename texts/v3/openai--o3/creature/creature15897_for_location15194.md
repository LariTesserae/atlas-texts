Those who inhabit the basin—human, almost, but never quite mistakable for any upland kin—have been pressed into their present shape by the same forces that press warm rain from the clouds and noise from every living throat.  The locals call themselves the Enge, a word signed with four quick taps of the thumb against the ribs, as if confirming the continuing beat of a heart in a place where every other sound is overwhelming.

1. Flesh and frame

• Height and build.  Bone grows dense here: the diet is calcium-rich, the constant microtremor of sub-basalt heat keeps musculature quietly engaged even during sleep.  Adults run short and broad—five feet at the tallest, shoulders wide enough that strangers often think two people stand side by side until the dusk clarifies the silhouette.  
• Skin.  Always wet.  The air’s saturation means sweat has nowhere to evaporate, so the Enge perspire very little; what moisture does reach the surface beads and clings like oil.  Skin darkens not from sun but from the trace metals dissolved in the environment.  Children are born pallid; by ten their epidermis carries a pewter cast, by thirty a deep antique bronze mottled with green in elbows and knuckles.  
• Ears.  Outer ears are vestigial nubs, slitted like those of a seal.  The middle ear is overgrown with a spongy, cartilage-like foam that damps sound by half before it reaches the cochlea.  Visitors hear a dull pop when the pressure equalizes; natives do not notice.  
• Lungs.  The Enge rarely breathe through the mouth.  Long valves of soft tissue hang at the back of the throat, folding shut whenever water spray gusts from a vent.  In sleep the valves remain partly closed, so dreamers look as if they are merely considering their next breath.  
• Hands.  Four fingers and a thumb, but the middle two fingers are joined to the first knuckle by a narrow web that stiffens when the hand is flattened, turning the palm into an oar.  A child’s first lesson in sign-speech is learning how to keep those webs lax enough for articulation.

2. Senses and speech

The Enge do hear, just poorly and selectively.  Anything below the permanent din registers only as vibration through foot-soles and sternum.  In quiet chambers—rare, carved deep into the hotter rock—they grow uneasy, complain that their own pulse is too loud.  

Language is threefold:

a) Basin Sign, a fast, economical syntax expressed from brow to waist.  Hands mark verbs, forearm rotation shades tense and mood, shoulders act as punctuation.  
b) Drum-shard, used across distances: flat scraps of obsidian struck against taut hide membranes suspended from platform undersides.  Rhythm carries meaning; pitch is incidental in a world where pitch is mostly washed away.  
c) Laryngeal song reserved for ritual.  It is less speech than sustained vowel—throat opened, tongue relaxed—as if each participant is adding a brick of resonance to a wall no individual could erect alone.  The collective chord can briefly punch through the valley’s roar, a phenomenon outsiders describe as the sky “hesitating.”

3. Clothing and ornament

No woven cloth.  Tunics are patched from amphibian hide, treated with an alkaline paste that keeps the material supple in constant wet.  Jewelry substitutes carbon for stone: bright knuckles of jet pried from the cooling flows, worn still half-glassy.  On festival days the Enge dust their shoulders with powdered fungal fan, so each gesture trails a ghostly aquamarine glimmer.

4. Food and husbandry

• Fry-pans.  Every residential slab keeps a rectangular basin where amphibian tadlings are reared on vent algae.  At metamorphosis day—always counted precisely 128 pulses of the sky-kites’ vapor whistle after hatching—each family harvests half, returning the rest to the wild shallows.  
• Plate-shearers.  The schooling crustaceans moult continuously.  Sloughed scutes are brittle for only a few hours, the exact window during which they can be clipped, soaked, and woven into nets or hammock-yards.  
• Reedsap.  When a mineral stalk fractures, its core foams into a pearly, slightly sweet slurry.  The Enge catch it on shallow stone palettes, let it thicken, then slice it into translucent tiles—staple calories, pleasantly alkaline, faint aftertaste of tin.

5. Kinship and habitation

Platforms cluster in “rafts” of seven; each raft houses a lineage that traces descent through people rather than generations.  A person belongs to every living relative equally, so the dead do not constitute ancestors but simply members who have exited the tally.  When a raft’s population reaches forty-nine—seven sevens—work begins on a new outer ring platform, leather ramps uncoiling like vines to claim a fresh patch of the shallows.

6. Craft and calculus

Tools never outgrow the handheld, yet infrastructure ticks with quiet precision:

• Piezostones: wedges of basalt set on edge so the valley’s tremor makes them creep a measurable hairbreadth each season.  Their displacement tells engineers where to brace or widen a causeway long before visible failure appears.  
• Resonance cisterns: bell-shaped cavities under communal slabs.  Water dripping in steady arrhythmia is used as both clock and calendar; elapsed time is marked not by counting drops—that would be futile—but by memorizing the evolving chord struck as successive cavities refill.

7. Thought and story

Core dogma: The basin is full.  All gain must arise as addition, never substitution.  Accordingly:

• Predation taboos extend to any creature whose voice participates in the valley’s permanent chord.  Killing a singer risks thinning the note that keeps the cloud ceiling from collapsing.  
• Birth spacing is strict.  Each raft permits only seven nursing infants at any given time.  Should an eighth be conceived, the pregnancy is carried to term, the newborn given the premier lullaby—a continuous contact hum—and surrendered at dusk to the kite-shoals.  No grief attaches; the child, they say, “joins the higher branch of water.”  
• Story is geometry.  Tales begin at a center point—often a vent or a birth—and spiral outward through linked events until they strike the basin’s rim, where the moral waits like a boundary stone.  Listeners often trace the spiral on one palm while the teller signs in the other, so plot becomes topography you can fold into your hand.

8. Other dwellers

Though the Enge alone raise structures, three further lineages claim rival sentience:

a) The Colossi (true name unpronounceable, rendered in sign by a long throat-stroke followed by a cupped hand).  Their lungs double as echo chambers; one bellow can map the entire basin in infrasonic relief.  The Enge read those tremors the way sailors read weather fronts, and will evacuate a platform if a Colossus’ call goes flat—sure sign of a vent about to fail.  

b) The Kites.  Cartilaginous, lantern-thin, lacking mouths; they absorb water through thousand-threaded feet that trail like tassels.  When an Enge infant is offered upward, kites swirl low, hooking the child’s webbed fingers into their tassel mass.  No one has tracked what becomes of the taken, but on calm nights the vapor whistle sometimes resolves into a recognizable fragment of lullaby.  

c) The Reeds.  Each stalk is technically a colony of crystal-secreting microbes sheathed in its own excreted spire.  When two spires touch, they fuse molecularly and thereafter grow only outward, never apart.  Scholars believe the colony network functions as the basin’s memory: mineral isotopes encode seasonal variance, vent chemistry, even the footfalls of the first builders.  A few Enge cultivate tiny garden-reed clusters on their roofs, hoping to one day “read” them as living annals.

9. Birth to death

Gestation: 320 cloud heartbeats.  
Naming: no fixed names until the age of first drum-shard solo.  Until then, children answer to whatever tap-pattern their closest sibling happens to assign.  
Courtship: partners sit thigh-deep in an eddy where condensate sheets from a fallen block; they drum simple primes on one another’s spines.  If the rhythms stay in phase for a full vowel-breath, the pairing is deemed auspicious.  
Death: a sloped basalt tongue leads straight into the single river.  The corpse is lashed with shredded leather so limbs lie tight, then set adrift face-down.  Water boils off skin in the vent-warm current, leaving a nude skeleton within hours.  Bones crack, fill, and sink, adding invisible strata to the riverbed.  “Fullness without replacement,” the mourners sign, palms pressed flat to the sternum.

10. The outsider’s glossary

Thunder sleep: the shallow coma newcomers enter after three days of unrelenting sound; locals tuck them beneath fungal lamps until they wake glazed-eyed and abruptly fluent in Basin Sign’s rudiments.  
Blind weather: any rare hour when mist thickens enough to hide the permanent overcast; treated as a license for mischief.  
Mirror hunger: a craving for color—especially red—so intense that some visitors resort to biting their own lips just to bleed.

In sum, what lives here is a people hammered by water, heat, and noise into a culture of additive survival: ears half-closed, hands eloquent, hearts synchronized to a landscape that refuses both silence and change.  Among the Enge, existence is not a matter of staking space but of quilting oneself, day after humid day, into an ever-denser fabric whose integrity is measured not in heights reached or horizons widened but in how completely every gap—auditory, spatial, emotional—is filled.
