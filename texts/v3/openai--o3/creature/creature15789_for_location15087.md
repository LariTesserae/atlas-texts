Who lives in the high alcove?  
Every pilgrim comes expecting an absence—no birds, no mammals, no obvious motion—and for the first few hours that expectation feels satisfied. But stay long enough, and three orders of resident reveal themselves, each slower, quieter, or thinner than the last. You notice them the way you notice your own pulse: at first as background, later as company.

────────────────────────  
I. The Slow-Rooted (macro-organisms that happen to be mobile)  
────────────────────────  

1. Sky-fern Thrones  
When dusk leans in, the crowns of the sky-ferns cease to behave like plants. Their outermost fronds ratchet downward, forming a ribbed cylinder—a seat, or perhaps a lung—while the inner shaft hollows. From that cavity a column of exhaled warmth rises. If you watch from the cliff-lip you can see distant flecks of dust and spore being pulled upward, caught, then let go. Local herders who have witnessed the folding from far below swear they have seen “tall cloaked shapes” step out of the fern cylinders just before dawn. Up close you learn the shapes are the plants themselves. They uproot in the night—roots drawing together into a single braided cord—and shuffle two or three paces on a tide of mucilage before fixing again. By morning they stand forever where they now stand, until the next year’s migration of inches. Each new anchoring leaves behind what looks like a human footprint half-filled with iron water.  

2. Basalt Lily Vesps  
During the hour when the lilies expose their ultramarine throats, a bare-eyed observer sees only light being drunk. A lens or a mirror, however, exposes what lives inside: slender, transparent arthropods coiled like springs. They shiver at a frequency too rapid for human sight, producing the optical darkening. The vesps feed on trace metals leached by the plant; in return they secrete a varnish that prevents the stone cups from collapsing. Once a year—always on a noon when counter-rain is active—they erupt in silence, beating outward on wings as thin as mica. They never leave the alcove; they simply find a new lily and coil again. Touch a vesp in flight and it disintegrates into a glitter that smells of struck flint.  

3. Lint-Moss Looms  
The “moss” is colonial, yes, but it is also locomotive. Threads creep—one fibre every four or five days—looking for new overhangs. When two sheets meet they braid at the edges, knitting a membrane that can span gaps wider than a man can jump. Those bridges hang for seasons, sag, and finally twist loose in a single night when fog is at its densest. A falling loom unfurls like a banner, releases every bead of stored water in one continuous curtain, then powders itself to dust before it hits the soil. That dust is a spore rain; by dawn a faint grey fur will have appeared on any damp, shade-facing surface.  

────────────────────────  
II. The Subtle-Voiced (organisms made mostly of air and sound)  
────────────────────────  

1. Echo-Filaments  
The alcove’s famous acoustic hollows are not empty resonators—each is lined with a papery film only a few molecules thick. The film is woven by echo-filaments: quasi-biological threads whose only solid component is silica condensed from mist. They vibrate in sympathy with any sound that grazes them. The vibration, in turn, drives evaporative cooling; ice crystals bloom and sublimate in microseconds, powering slow growth. What you hear as a five-minute delayed whisper is the living film replaying the captured wave in order to grow a few more angstroms of body. A single loud clap can overfeed a filament and cause it to tear itself apart in rejoicing.  

2. Counter-Rain Swarms  
A counter-rain sheet is less weather than flock. Each droplet is wrapped in an electromotive skin created by colonies of chemotactic bacteria. As sunlight hits them, the colonies release ions, becoming minute hot-air balloons that drift upward until their charge bleeds off. Inside every drop, the bacteria reproduce, then enter stasis the instant the droplet separates. Pilgrims collect rising water in inverted nets and smear it on aching joints; the bacteria wake to the salt and, for an hour or two, secrete analgesic peptides that numb pain and loosen memory. Elders call this the “forgiveness drizzle.”  

3. Heartbeat Listeners  
Sometimes, standing in a silent patch between fronds, you feel as if something has put a stethoscope to your ribs. That sensation belongs to the Heartbeat Listeners, entities comprised of alternating layers of warm mist and colder, denser fog. A Listener is born when a sudden drop of barometric pressure folds a sheet of vapour over itself like origami. For the next several hours the layer cake drifts, its own temperature gradient focusing low-frequency sound exactly as a glass lens focuses light. When it passes through you, it harvests your infrasonic pulse, storing the pattern as a standing wave in its interior. Later, perhaps miles down-slope, the Listener collapses against a cliff and plays your heart back into the stone. Some say the rhythmic thudding keeps the mountain from crumbling.  

────────────────────────  
III. The Still Ones (beings for whom time is an optional trait)  
────────────────────────  

1. The Petrifact  
Leave a tool, a cup, or a coin overnight and it grows a mineral sheath. Leave a living hand against the stone for the same duration and you may acquire a blister made of quartz. The agent is the Petrifact: a distributed lattice of mineral-fixing enzymes that inhabit the wet film between rock grains. Each enzyme chain remembers every surface it has ever plated and favours novelty; the unfamiliar is embraced and slowly taught the mineral tongue. A visitor who sleeps too long on bare ground wakes with sigils of brittle crystal tracing the pattern of capillaries beneath the skin. Flake them away and they return, paler, for three successive nights, then stop—old friends who needed only to say hello.  

2. The Timefold Spores  
The spongy soil is laced with hesitating spores—seeds that open when now becomes then. Molecular rings in their walls flip chirality whenever the local flow of seconds slackens (the very anomaly that makes watches misbehave). A spore can accumulate a century of micro-interruptions in a single human day; when its internal tally is full, it tumbles open like a rotary lock, no matter the external season. What grows depends entirely on what the soil “remembers” at that moment. Historians have uprooted Pagan wheat, extinct butterflies in chrysalis, even a spur of iron that belonged to no known empire. Every artifact or organism thus summoned lives only a few hours before it dissolves back into iron-rich ooze, like footage played backward into its raw film stock.  

3. The Stone Choir  
Beneath the alcove, in caves accessible only by those who can move through basalt like sunlight through glass, waits the Stone Choir. No eye has seen them, but all ears have. The ultra-low hum you hear by pressing skull to wall is their conversation. Each member is a void—a bubble of vacuum perhaps a finger-length across—locked inside solid rock. Pressure should crush them, yet they persist, consuming stray phonons to reinforce their emptiness. When pilgrims chant against the cliff face, the Choir fattens on the gift and answers by thinning gravity in the alcove for a heartbeat or two. Scholars attribute the impossible warmth at altitude to the Choir’s steady siphoning of the mountain’s own weight.  

────────────────────────  
Relations with Human Visitors  
────────────────────────  

• Exchange is always osmotic: you bring concentration, the alcove brings dilution. Speak loudly and Echo-Filaments drink your voice; stay motionless and the Petrifact writes you into its scripture of quartz; mourn or rejoice and a Listener will carry the rhythm away.  
• Violence is not punished—it is dispersed. Slash a sky-fern and every droplet of sap lands already solidified into obsidian spheres the size of pearls. They roll to the lowest point, melt quietly, and are re-absorbed.  
• Gifts that endure are those the residents cannot digest. A bone flute left by a penitent herder now lies on a ledge untouched for thirty years, because calcium and grief are equally familiar to the alcove: nothing to learn, nothing to trade for.  

────────────────────────  
How to See Them (field notes from a stubborn botanist)  
────────────────────────  

1. To witness a sky-fern migrate, anchor a lattice of reed stakes around its base; the plant will politely step over the barrier within three nights.  
2. At midnight, lie belly-down beside a basalt lily and breathe steadily into its cup. The vesps will mist your exhale with vanadium dust—an antiseptic once prized by the plateau tribes. Your lungs will ache pleasantly for the next week, as if fuller of air than before.  
3. Scribble a question on slate and press the tablet to a singing hollow. Return at dawn: the slate will be blank, but a spiral of powdered rock at your feet will spell an answer in an alphabet no scholar has yet matched to any tongue. Read it by scattering water; the grains align momentarily into glyphs before flowing away.  

────────────────────────  
In the End  
────────────────────────  

Say you linger a month, respectful, curious, half-convinced all of this is metaphor. On the morning you finally descend, nothing follows you—yet halfway down the switchbacks you will notice that your footfalls no longer echo the way they did on the climb. Some subtle layer of reverberation has been skimmed off and kept behind. Above, the alcove is already folding your departure into its long arithmetic of stone and wet air, adding your cadence to the pulse of roots, droplets, and distant gravity. That is how it shelters its residents: by inviting every visitor to become, in some faint but genuine respect, one of them.
