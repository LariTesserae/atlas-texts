Who (and what) keeps this newborn basin busy

1. The Basalt-Lichens (“blueweft”)
   • First on the scene—released from orbit in dust canisters before the lava finished cooling.  
   • A chassis of ordinary photobiont fungus, but its chloroplasts are replaced by magnetite-studded organelles that harvest geomagnetic wobble the way an eel harvests charge.  
   • At night each thallus strobes a faint cyan where field lines knot the strongest; the effect is a slow, tidal breathing that rolls across every rock face.  
   • The lichens are the basin’s postal service: spores ride those periodic flashes as static-charged flechettes, clicking audibly when they stick to fresh stone. You can track prevailing winds by the direction of the clicks.

2. Water-Skin Midges
   • Imagine a lacewing folded from oiled tracing paper. Wings wider than the body, filmed with hydrophobic scales; when rain hits them it beads, jumps, and becomes lift.  
   • Midges spend most of their lives hovering in place, drinking from the air with capillary mouthparts. They need turbulence the way we need oxygen; stillness suffocates them.  
   • Their larvae—white commas with black compound eyes—graze blueweft mats for magnetite granules, building an internal compass. Dissect one and its gut glitter looks like spilled filings.

3. Clickhoppers (amphibious rodents)
   • Body plan says “vole,” fur says “otter,” voice says “angry castanets.”  
   • Hind feet are webbed into a single paddle; on land they hop, in water they row.  
   • The click language is sonar first, gossip second—each pool gives a different echo, so a hopper knows exactly which neighbor is talking by the signature reverb.  
   • They farm cranberries in miniature terraces, weeding by yanking shoots that don’t smell right. Field techs trade batteries for their spent berry skins, which ferment into a startlingly competent antiseptic.

4. Basalt Terns (“grey gulls” to outsiders)
   • Wings proportioned for knife-fighting with crosswinds—long, narrow, almost sword-straight.  
   • Feathers coated in a bio-ceramic dust that dulls radar, a side-effect of eating midge pupae loaded with magnetite. When a flock banks they erase the local magnetic field for half a second; all the compasses hiccup together.  
   • Their landing, not the turbines, is the loudest event in the basin: they approach vertically, stall at three metres, then drop like quarried stone, slapping the water flat to stun prey. The boom resets your heartbeat whether you want it reset or not.

5. The Turbine Rings (Category: Machine-Organism)
   • Legs of foamed carbon root themselves deep; inside those legs live maintenance swarms—micro-drones grown from the same stock that built the frames.  
   • Every forty minutes the swarm performs a “groom,” polishing ice and moss from blade edges in a glittering cloud that looks, from distance, like a halo shedding snow.  
   • Locals swear the rings talk. Recordings confirm a subsonic chatter traded ridge to ridge—data, weather gossip, maybe jokes told in torque.

6. Field Induction Cartographers (human)
   • Rotating crews of three, posted for 18-sol arcs before extraction.  
   • Uniform: wader bibs dyed a sickly teal that stands out on every drone camera but not at all to terns, who see mostly ultraviolet.  
   • Their job is to map “mag-eddies”—eddying curls in the young crust’s field that confuse satellite guidance.  
   • Off-hours they play a board game called Basin—looks like Go but you capture territory with acoustic beacons and actual wind data. Winning move is usually a lie you tell the weather.

7. The Quiet Dome (field lab)
   • Dormant ninety-nine per-cent of the time; that once-an-hour pulse is a heartbeat for whatever thinks inside.  
   • Rumor: there’s a single researcher who never rotates, a caretaker known only as “Kite.” Some say Kite is a person, others that the dome itself earned a personnel record after too many solitary midnights.  
   • Supply manifests list glucose, mylar blankets, and children’s paperbacks in equal quantities.

8. Under-Basalt Filters (unseen)
   • Thin as notebook paper, spread beneath the ooze in kilometer sheets, seeded years ago by unmanned drill.  
   • Tasked with sipping heavy metals from percolating water before it hits the coastal shelf.  
   • They knot themselves into origami folds when flow rates spike, which changes the tone of the basin’s background hiss—old hands can forecast a flood four hours ahead just by the new harmony.

9. Fern Hothouses
   • Pockets of air trapped amid shoulder-high fronds reach ten degrees warmer than ambient.  
   • Clickhoppers birth litters here; midges pupate on the stems; cartographers take meal breaks when the sleet turns to shrapnel.  
   • Follow the steam plumes and you’ll find caches of lost gear—gloves, notebooks, one antique violin sealed in shrink-wrap and tuned weekly by whoever “Kite” is.

10. The Patient Thing in the Pools
    • Not yet classified. Seen twice: once as a spiral wake that reversed direction without slowing, once as an eye the size of a dinner plate rising just far enough to blink.  
    • Blueweft stops glowing for metres around when it passes, as though the very magnetism is swallowed.  
    • Field rule now pins every stilt-pod three metres higher than spec. Nobody jokes about it, not even the turbines.

Hierarchy of attention
• The lichens own the stone.  
• The water-skin midges own the air until the terns punch holes in it.  
• The clickhoppers curate the surface boundary, turning mud into conversation.  
• The turbines referee the wind and loan a trickle of power to anything that asks politely.  
• Humans borrow what sense they can, pay rent in measurements, and leave before the basin decides the terms have changed.

If you linger
Stand still with your recorder on omnidirectional: first you catch the midges’ wing-thread hum, then three, four overtones lower comes the turbine colloquy, and under that—the quiet churn of filters, the subtractive hush where blueweft has dimmed. Add a tern’s ballistic report and the clickhoppers’ syncopated commentary and you have the basin’s national anthem, performed continuously, never the same twice.

Whatever truly lives here is the conversation itself. Everything with a pulse or a circuit joins in, takes a verse, falls silent, returns. Should you step away, the chorus closes behind you like water, and only the faint compass-drift in your pockets proves you were ever part of the song.
