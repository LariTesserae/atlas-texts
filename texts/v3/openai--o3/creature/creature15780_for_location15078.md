Who or what lives on the Arclight Plateau?

Begin with what was there long before the city, because the things that learned to exist in stillness shaped every later arrival.

I. Native Biology – the Non-Animal Kingdom

1. Feltleaf (Desmos spongia)  
One pad is one cell, yet a single mat can run for hectares.  Under a lens the “leaf” resembles a wet-copper quilt whose stitching is capillary tubing.  Sugary antifreeze keeps the cytoplasm slushy at –20 °C; the pad photosynthesises through the top surface and excretes excess oxygen downward, forming micro-bubbles that give the turf its faint crackle underfoot.  
Reproduction is glacier-slow.  A perimeter pad simply thickens until a neck forms, then pinches away.  Detachment takes a local decade.  The new pad drifts as a raft until it senses steady vibration—a hint that turbines or wind-strings are near—and settles, as if the species has learned that hum equals light equals survival.

2. Capillary Forests  
Where two feltleaf mats collide, their edges buckle upward and extrude cellulose arches five to seven metres high.  Each arch is hollow; inside, dew gathers into beads so perfectly spherical they act as ball lenses, focussing sunlight into the root node.  The forest therefore farms its own micro-sunrises.  Branch joints kink at exact 30° or 60° intervals, creating the hex-lattice that scatters sound as softly as velvet.  Nothing roosts here, but if you clap your hands the echo will return quantised, truncated into half-second packets—because the forest itself obeys the Plateau’s strobing time.

3. Lichen Constellations  
Chemolithotrophic lichens etched into bare basalt form glowing turquoise filigrees at night.  The glow is not bioluminescence but Cherenkov-like flashes from beta particles: the fungus concentrates trace radio-nuclides brought by wind and, in doing so, charges itself like a sheet of electret.  Each flash lasts nanoseconds, but in the mist it becomes a slow aurora that sweeps the rock every few minutes—another granular rhythm.

4. Sub-Basalt “Slowwalkers”  
Technically microbes, emotionally mineral.  They live in pores three kilometres down, where water that has already spun thirty turbines still carries a charge differential of a few millivolts.  The bacteria laminate themselves into centimetre-thick biofilms that act as semiconductors; electrons migrate through the colony centimetres per hour—plenty fast for an organism whose cell cycle is measured in centuries.

No vertebrates, no arthropods, no flyers.  Motion is a cost no lineage here can yet afford.

II. The Plateau-Born People (Population ~60 000)

1. Physiology – Adapted, Not Engineered  
• Lungs: Children raised in the 60 % sea-level air pressure develop alveoli that look over-inflated to lowlanders but are normal here.  Vital capacity averages 8 L.  
• Balance: Semi-circular canal fluid contains extra statolith granules.  They provide an unconscious correction for the gravity “soft spots” at fracture mouths; plateau-natives rarely stumble where visitors lurch.  
• Hearing: The middle-ear muscles have unusually slow twitch rates.  This dampens the 24/7 drone so the auditory nerve never saturates, yet leaves enough sensitivity to catch a door-hinge squeak two kilometres away on a calm day.

2. Clothing and Tools  
Everything is water-repellent or water-harvesting, rarely both.  Most outerwear is spun from feltleaf pulp polymerised into a chitin-like sheath; the fibre carries a natural turquoise patina.  Workers lace garments with piezo threads that steal a trickle of current from every step—enough to run wrist lamps.  Metal is aluminium or platinum-group alloys electro-won directly from canyon water.

3. Language – Vowel, Hum, Overtone  
Spoken Ginvian uses only four plosive consonants, but a spectrum of voiced tones.  A sentence carries a “pedal note”: drop that, and the grammar dissolves.  Children learn to place the note by singing into wall-panels that glow when the resonance is correct.  Every citizen can hum 24 distinct pitches that align with turbine shaft frequencies—a civic skill like handwriting elsewhere.

Whispers are taboo; to speak below the hum is to risk private conspiracy.  People share secrets by writing them, sealing the sheet inside two layers of gelled antifreeze so the paper stays mute until thawed indoors.

4. Social Architecture  
• Guild of Continuists – custodians of the hydro-cascade.  They call themselves “eighty-threes” after the longest uninterrupted turbine runtime on record: 83 years, 112 days.  
• Tuning Houses – half monastery, half municipal works department.  Their adepts walk roof-lines with tuning forks, shaving or load-bolting beams so the city’s chord stays in key as temperature and tension drift.  
• Outriders – solitude professionals.  They spend seasons mapping the creep of feltleaf mats or re-threading distant weeping-wall catchments.  When two Outriders meet they exchange memory crystals: ice wafers onto which they have spoken a week’s events, later to be played back in city archives.  Outriders rarely look one another in the eye; doing so feels too intimate after months alone with horizon-to-horizon damp velvet.

5. Emotion & Ritual  
The Plateau offers no dramatic weather, no predators; therefore thrill is artisanal.  Adolescents free-fall across gravity-slack fracture rims, letting themselves hover that extra heart-beat before the tug returns.  Marriages are celebrated by striking the “concord gong,” a slab of basalt hung across a vent.  When two people hit it simultaneously at different nodal points, the combined resonance records their unique beat pattern in the stone for decades; later descendants can sound the slab and still hear that ancestral pulse.

III. Co-Habitants of Ambiguous Ontology

1. The Fracture Echo  
Stand at certain slot mouths at night.  Hum a note.  After the human-audible reflection dies, an infrasonic restatement rolls back up—identical interval, amplified, delayed exactly 128 s.  Sensors lowered on tethers register organised pressure waves repeating for hours.  Locals call this “the Echo” and treat it as the Plateau’s voice.  Whether it is merely standing-wave hydraulics in the canyon or a by-product of the Slowwalker colonies converting electrochemical gradients is unsolved; either way, it influences design laws as surely as gravity.

2. The Granulators  
Seen only in corner-of-eye pauses when time jolts: thin silhouettes, multiple jointed, angle neatly by half-seconds.  No direct sample exists; still, turbine telemetry shows fleeting spikes every time witnesses report a Granulator.  Continuists joke the entities are maintenance sprites tasked with oiling reality’s escapement wheel.

3. Visitor Species – Rare, Always Transient  
Migrant raptors from lowland thermals sometimes overshoot a mountain range and arrive half-starved.  They cannot hunt feltleaf, nor metabolise its copper toxins, and leave within days.  Human visitors stay longer but develop “plateau arrhythmia”: their dream EEGs fall into harmony with turbine chords; waking, they feel mild tachycardia until descent to thicker air resets their inner metronome.

IV. What It All Means to Live Here

To be alive on Arclight is to substitute vibration for heartbeat, distance for privacy, and patience for motion.

You keep slow company: a photosynthetic carpet that thinks in meters per decade, a lichen light-show pulsing like clock hands you read instead of a wristwatch, a canyon that answers when spoken to—if you can wait two minutes for the reply.

You finish each day aware that the quickest thing around may be your own thoughts, and even they are starting to subdivide into half-second ticks.  In the ringing hush beyond GINGIVA’s last tram stop, you are forced to notice how loud a single pulse can be when nothing else is moving.  Most citizens find that terrifying as children and consoling as elders.

So the Plateau’s inhabitants—vegetal, microbial, human, perhaps more—share a pact: no one hurries, everyone resonates.  The place is an instrument vast enough to live inside, and everything that persists here, from copper-leaf mat to Continuist apprentice, survives by finding a note, holding it, and feeding whatever trickle of energy the endless falling water can spare.
