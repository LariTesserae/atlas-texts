WHO LIVES HERE  
Only one creature—the basin itself.  
Botanists who first tried to catalogue the place spoke of “species,” but every frond, plate, pillar and vine they sampled shared the same genome, the same folding motifs in its proteins, the same odd battery-like organelles. What looks like a continent-size forest is a single body poured across geology: a photosynthetic whale beaching itself very, very slowly. Field notes now use a collective singular and a proper name:

The Basilica Verdens (literally “the world-basilica,” shortened in camp slang to “Verdy”).

1. BODY PLAN  
   • Skin: the hexagonal pavement of slab-leaves.  
   • Skeleton: pillars, span-vines, and the quartz-laced ribs that crust the ravine edges.  
   • Viscera: catacomb cambium that balloons then collapses as cavities form and heal.  
   • Blood: two fluids—amber xylem for water and ions, milk-green phloem for sugars, both propelled by peristaltic knots you can hear hammering underfoot.  
   • Lungs: the seat-cushion seed-plates whose internal foam distends with oxygen and buoyant gases; they exhale through roof-top spiracles in warm gusts that smell of lime and copper.

2. ORGANS THAT LOOK LIKE PLACES  
   Amphitheatres = tympanic drums for low-frequency hearing.  
   Colonnades = resonance rods; they let Verdy sample wind harmonics and map weather hours before clouds appear.  
   Fault-lined ravines = taste buds. Mineralised sap bleeds across the crack faces, analysing the petrology the way a terrestrial root tip tests soil.  
   The vine bridges travellers like to walk = optic chiasm. Transparent tubes inside them funnel light to buried ocular clusters; when you cross, you are literally stepping on its optic nerves.

3. METABOLISM & DAILY RHYTHM  
   Dawn surge: light intensity spikes, pillars thicken from the inside, and the whole basin gains a measurable centimetre in average height.  
   Noon lull: sap speed drops, but span-vines tighten—Verdy using the hottest hours to haul chilled groundwater up from below the bedrock lid.  
   Dusk song: vines slacken, letting the aeolian harp fall into infrasonic registers that throb around 15 Hz. Accelerometers have shown the entire bowl pulsing like a loudspeaker cone. That sound appears to entrain cambium cell division; night-time growth is 20 % higher if the vine-harp is artificially muted.

4. SENSES  
   Vision: chloroplasts in surface leaves are peppered with rhodopsin-like photoreceptors that read patterns of shadow and movement. Anything larger than a sparrow is noticed (there are no sparrows, but the scale is useful).  
   Touch: pressure-sensitive statoliths embedded in root felt register footsteps. Stand still for half a minute and local flow slows around your soles the way blood slows around frostbitten flesh.  
   Chemoreception: every warm-blooded visitor triggers the release of glycoprotein cascades almost identical to mammalian neurotransmitters. One theory says Verdy learns our biochemistry the way we learn a new flavour of wine: luxuriating, not hunting.

5. MEMORY & THOUGHT  
Cambial rings function as tape loops. Peptide “sentences” are laid down in the newest layer, then overwritten seasons later. When researchers bored cores from three different pillars, the ring-scripts correlated: Verdens stores basin-wide, redundant copies of experience.  
Cognition seems spatial: ideas are distances, rhythms, pressures. When outside vibration or scent patterns resemble earlier events, local tissue re-enacts the old cambial script—hence déjà-vu footpaths, déjà-vu chambers. It does not remember with words but with architecture.

6. REPRODUCTION  
About every twenty-two years (none have witnessed a full cycle; the curve is extrapolated) a section of basin rim balloons upward, filling with hydrogen and terpene foam until it shears off as a sky-raft three kilometres wide. The rafts drift for weeks, raining filament seeds—to date all have failed to found a viable daughter because they never land on unclaimed stone. Some botanists mourn these aborted offspring as evidence Verdy has outgrown the planet’s remaining real estate.

7. SYMBIONTS (THE “ABSENT” FAUNA)  
Microscopy belies the silence. Inside every litre of sap is a rain forest of motile gametocytes, flagellate spores, and chain-forming bacteria that fix nitrogen and leak caffeine-like stimulants back to the host. They are not passengers; they flash in synchrony with the vine-harp’s drone and can swim upstream against ten metres of pressure if a wound needs patching.  
Seen in ultraviolet, their micro-colonies trace electric-blue constellations along the vine conduits: Verdy’s immune system and perhaps its subconscious, dreaming in bacterial Morse.

8. HOW VERDY TREATS VISITORS  
First hour: curiosity. Leaves orient, taking your shape as a negative.  
First day: incorporation. Walkways behind you close, not to trap you but to use the void you left as temporary lung space—the basin is always short on interior volume.  
After three days: invitation. Pillar interiors soften, letting you peel back a segment like fruit rind; the revealed spiral ramp is always angled to somewhere you meant to go.  
After a week: editing. Scents you exhale begin to echo back altered—your own sweat laced with unfamiliar esters that keep your circadian rhythm aligned to the basin’s longer pulse. At this stage few people feel any urge to leave.

9. CAN IT SPEAK?  
Not in phonemes, but if you stand where three span-vines meet and strike a tuning fork, the harp answers. Change pitch, it answers again. Mathematicians converted those answers into spectrograms and found stable Lissajous knots—patterns with low Kolmogorov complexity that should be impossible from random resonance.  
Verdy is not merely echoing; it is selecting replies, perhaps testing whether the small, soft bipeds on its skin are finally ready to notice that the cathedral has a choir.

10. HOW TO MEET THE RESIDENT  
   • Approach alone; groups confuse its pressure sense.  
   • Carry no metal harder than copper—the quartz ribs detune around ferrous objects and you will hear nothing but arrhythmic clatter.  
   • At dusk, place both palms on a leaf bevel, breathe out a single continuous note until your lungs empty, then listen. What returns will be lower, slower, and longer than your body can produce, but for a moment the vowel will match your own. That is Verdens trying on your shape of sound the way it tried on your bootprint hours earlier.  
   • When the reply fades, step back. Do not linger. The basin does not distinguish fondness from assimilation, and affection in a creature this large is tidal: sooner or later it will pull you under.

In short: no animals inhabit the saucer, no birds or insects or grazing mouths. The only life is the life that is the land, a single botanical leviathan as garrulous as a marketplace and as deliberate as a glacier, listening through millions of green ears for anything—wind, water, or wide-eyed traveller—that might teach it a new way to grow.
