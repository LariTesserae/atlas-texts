Who lives in the Steam Grotto?  Almost everything.

The chamber is a stacked, interlocking city whose citizens are as thin as fog or as armored as copper.  No one species dominates for long; the space is too small, the resources too finely balanced.  Instead life has divided the grotto into vertical districts, each ruled by a different guild of organisms, all of them borrowing from the others and paying back in heat, vapor, or dust.  Walk shelf by shelf and you can meet them in order.

I.  Ceiling—The Cool Breath
1. Plate-Fungi (Caerulomyces latipelta)  
   Thin as fingernails, these turquoise lamellae overlap like roof tiles along every rib of stone.  Each plate contains two sets of cells: one that fluoresces, one that photosynthesizes when light from visiting fireflies bounces back.  They pulse in brightness when geothermal pressure tugs microscopic stress fibers in their flesh, squeezing stored luciferin into fresh air.  For the dozen heartbeats of every pulse, the whole community glows as one—an inadvertent festival that lets everything below recalibrate its body clock.

2. Condensate Bats (Rhynolestes distillans)  
   Finger-long and wing-narrow, they cling head-up instead of head-down so that water condensing on their own bellies drips away, preventing heatstroke.  Their tongues end in fibrous brushes; when a plate fungus pulses light, the bats wake and comb spores from the air, an airborne grazing that prunes the fungal population.  They excrete half-digested spores into cooler ceiling pockets, planting the next generation of luminescence.

3. Roof Mites  
   Too small for eyes, transparent as spit, they live inside droplets themselves, paddling with cilia until each bead falls.  When the droplet bursts on a lower ledge the mites hurl themselves onto any passing surface—rock, leaf, or animal—using the moment of impact like a seed catapult uses wind.

II. Upper Shelves—Veil Country
4. Rockmilk Vines (Lactisaxum pendulare)  
   The soft, white tubules that drape every shelf are vines whose sap is a chalky emulsion of calcium, silica, and sugars.  Blind lizards gnaw rune-shaped feeding notches in them; the sap congeals on exposure to air, healing the wound within hours and leaving pearled scars that look like writing.  Every few seasons the vine retracts its roots, slithers a handspan sideways, and re-anchors, leaving behind hollow husks used by insects and spiders as tenement blocks.

5. Root-Threader Lizard (Caecivarus subtilis)  
   Neither fully reptile nor fully caecilian, the Threader swims through clay the way a fish works silt.  It senses the magnetic shimmer of iron grains in the roofs of its skull tunnels and follows them like roads.  Clay shivers when it passes, a tremor often mistaken for a distant footstep.  Threaders keep the vine population trimmed; without them the grotto would seal itself in an impermeable quilt of foliage.

6. Lurk-Wasps  
   Leggy, copper-green, and wingless, they skate along the undersides of leaves like inverted water striders.  Their ovipositors drill into living vine to lay heat-hardened eggs.  In return, larvae secrete a mild antibiotic that protects the vine from fungal infection; the plant tolerates the theft of sap as the lesser evil.
