WHO LIVES ON THE CANOPY ALTIPLANO

0. A note on scale  
Everything that follows is nested inside the Plateau Matriarch, the single organism whose trunks people wrongly call “columns.” The Matriarch herself is not background; she is the first and eldest resident. All other creatures—beast, human, fungus, spirit—are tenants in her body.

1. The Matriarch  
• Anatomy. Each pillar is a vascular chimney that both supports and ventilates the continental canopy. In cross-section the wood is ringed like muscle, capable of peristaltic squeeze. Sap rises as a pulse, not a flow; the whole plateau keeps time to that heartbeat.  
• Awareness. Electro-chemical signals travel the root umbilicals faster than sap—measured experiments put the speed at the flight of a sky-bison in a stiff tailwind. When an umbilical is severed the adjacent pillars tighten their stomata simultaneously, proving a degree of central coordination.  
• Expression. The Matriarch “speaks” by biasing vibration. A low G-sharp that lasts longer than eight seconds is always followed by a red sap-flush in the cambium windows; plateau people interpret this as an all-clear to harvest. A rising minor third, by contrast, precedes a predatory event 90 % of the time. No one is sure whether the plant predicts danger or summons it.  
• Reproduction. True flowers appear once each great year, but the Matriarch also buds off satellite organs—small free-floating aero-pods that use hot updrafts as balloons. They drift until they find bare stone, then burrow and root. New pillars take a human lifetime to reach adult girth; a traveller may leave as a youth and return to find a new “mountain” where a hollow once stood.

2. The Plateau Folk (humans)  
They call themselves Heshut—“throat-makers”—because survival here depends on carving one’s own acoustic space.

Physiology  
• Barrel chests and springy rib cartilage; infants are swaddled in tough sail-leaves that restrict expansion so the thorax grows deeper rather than wider—an intentional cultural version of altitude acclimation.  
• A second epiglottis-like membrane allows them to close the airway and recycle exhaled moisture through nasal sinuses before breathing out, wasting almost no water.  
• Lens of the eye is amber-tinged from lifelong exposure to cambium light; visitors see only mottled green, but a Heshut can distinguish ten shades of sap-gold as easily as you tell scarlet from crimson.

Culture  
• Settlements are horizontal beads on a vertical thread: rings of low stone around a spring (always called the Spring-Mother) and bark lofts climbing the nearest pillar. Descent to the ground happens by dawn; ascent by dusk. Noon is spent halfway up in the shade.  
• Oral record is impossible in the sunrise roar. History is braided into silence itself: during the nightly two-hour lull every adult is required to recite their lineage into a ceramic resonator bowl. The bowl is then sealed; its interior glaze records vibration as fractal cracks. Bowls are heirlooms and legal documents.  
• Currency is time on a root-umbilical—specifically the right to tap sap. A clan too poor to hold an umbilical charter must take service as “silence-sharpers,” patrolling with bone chimes to detect pockets of predator silence.

Rite of the Basket  
Adolescents are given a nursery thornling, press it into a notch of the chosen bark plate, and shape it into something useful while the plate slowly rises. What they mold becomes their adult name: a fish-trap yields Trap-Kin, a cradle yields Child-Friend, a simple circle yields Door-Seeker (wandering trader). When the rising plate reaches canopy level, the object is judged. Failure means the plate—and the name—are pruned away. About one in ten youngsters vanishes into the dust that night rather than descend in shame.

3. The Sky-Bison  
• Biology. Six-limbed vertebrates with pneumatic bone; the belly pair of limbs has fused into a single taut gliding sheet. Fur strands are ribbon-flat to reflect heat.  
• Social. Herd size is fixed by sound: twenty-six adults drone in phase to mask their calves’ wing-clicks. Add a twenty-seventh and the chord acquires a detectable beat that attracts raptors. Consequently every birth is balanced by an exile; young bulls become the legendary “Sirocco Kings” who wander alone between pillars, half starved, untouchable.  
• Symbiosis. A bison’s dorsal groove houses moths whose caterpillars consume parasites and excrete wax that waterproofs the fur. During great-year flowering, the moths intoxicate and abandon the groove, leaving the herd itchy and irritable; stampedes across the plateau are almost the only large-scale mammal disturbance the Matriarch tolerates.

4. Dagger-Beaked Raptors (genus Gladius-becco)  
• Camouflage is cellular. Specialized chromatophores imitate not just colour but leaf translucence; in sunlight they are invisible except for a faint lensing of background texture.  
• Courtship occurs inside resonance hollows in the columns. A male flares his scapular feathers, turns the hollow into a Helmholtz chamber, and sings a sub-audible bass note. Females judge lung volume by back-scatter from the wood; bigger lungs mean higher nest hollows, safer from sky-bison turbulence.  
• Primary weapon is the beak: keratin laminated with naturally occurring silica plates, giving an edge that never dulls. A single strike severs a gliding membrane; raptors then wait for gravity to finish the kill.

5. Copper Arthropods (“Click-Eaters”)  
• Exoskeleton impregnated with CuO crystals, turning the whole body into a photo-electric surface. At dawn they harvest light directly and can remain motionless for hours while digestion halts.  
• Pollen cakes are bitten into wedges and buried as larders. Locals raid these caches; an antelope-sized cache can feed a family for a week. The arthropods remember the theft: subsequent caches are booby-trapped with explosive resin sacs wired to trip-threads of gut silk.  
• Language? Possibly. Their detonation patterns form rhythmic ratios that repeat across kilometres. Heshut children tap those rhythms on drum leaves to herd a swarm toward rival camps—biological artillery.

6. Sub-Cambium Mycelium (“The Quiet Thread”)  
Invisible yet essential: a fungal lattice only microns thick spreads between living plates and regulates mineral exchange.  
• Emits anti-vibration waves—ultrasonic pulses that cancel specific insect frequencies, creating the tiny silence pockets predators exploit.  
• When the Matriarch flowers, the Thread fruits short-lived nodules that resemble child fists. Eaten raw they erase six hours of auditory memory. Travelling shamans chew them before long journeys so yesterday’s orientation drones do not confuse today’s route.

7. The Soundleeches  
Not organisms but colonial protists suspended in gel bubbles (the ones given off by torn umbilicals).  
• Each bubble thins until vibration of a particular wavelength ruptures it. The contents then digest airborne organics—including pheromones and alarm scents—silencing the immediate acoustic field.  
• Raptors follow chains of burst bubbles the way wolves follow blood droplets. Plateau folk break bubbles intentionally to throw hunters off their scent.

8. High-Canopy Drifters (“Leaf-Sail People”)  
Possibly post-human or a divergent hominin; no one has seen them closely. Evidence:  
• Artifacts—pouches knit from true leaf-membrane, too thin for any ground dweller to work.  
• Fires. Yes, fires: tiny pinpoint glows high above the light-absorbing awning, visible only on overcast nights when reflected off cloud bottoms. The smoke falls as pyrolized soot that the Heshut use for tattoo ink.  
• Once every decade a corpse wafts down, wrapped in membrane like a seed. Bones are bird-hollow, joints double-jointed, nails grown into climbing hooks. Oxygen debt in their muscle fibres is minimal; they are adapted to even higher elevations than the plateau.  
• Legends say they shepherd the pollen wave, steering it with counter-vibrations so flowering completes the global circuit. If true, they are gardeners of a garden vast as a planet.

9. Things That Are Almost People  
• Resin Echoes. Carved amber lenses hold a neuronal mesh of plant cells. When wind strikes a lens at the angle it was cut, the mesh replays a fragment of the carver’s last strong emotion—usually hunger, fear, or desire. Passing travellers hear whispers in their own voice, urging caution or speed.  
• Tumblers. Bison exiles sometimes ingest too much hallucinogenic pollen and begin to emulate the insect choir, grinding their pharyngeal pouches until the cartilage mineralizes. These “stone throats” roll across the plateau on heat thermals, mind lost, body humming a single unbroken note for weeks before collapsing. Heshut hunters collect their ossified larynxes to decorate resonator bowls.

10. Ecological Roles at a Glance  
• Matriarch = architecture, climate engine, basic provider  
• Quiet Thread = regulating nerve net  
• Insects = oxygenators, tonal fabric  
• Sky-Bison = mobile fertilization, pruning sails by collision  
• Raptors = population control, corpse removal  
• Arthropods = detritus recyclers, soil makers (scant though soil is)  
• Humans & Leaf-Sail People = symbiotic groomers, wound healers, story keepers

11. What It Feels Like To Be Any Of Them  

A. To be a trunk-node of the Matriarch is to sense sunfall simultaneously on a hundred kilometre-distant plates, to taste mineral signatures in dew as if they were wines, and to stretch without moving. Predation registers as a bar of dissonance; flowering is orgasmic polyphony.

B. To be a sky-bison calf: the world begins upside-down, swinging from mother’s belly fur. Heat means lift; lift means safety. Every dusk the pillars swallow the sun and you follow its warmth into the hollow roots, trusting the thunder of insects to hide your heartbeat.

C. To be a Heshut silence-sharper: patrol alone after moonset, ears pricked for impossible absence. Hear nothing? Drop, tuck, cover—the raptor’s on you already. Survive, and tomorrow you’ll be paid in fifteen heartbeats of sap-tapping rights.

D. To be a copper arthropod sentry: stand sentinel at the pollen mound, limbs locked. Sunrise pours charge into your shell until your mandibles buzz, and the first vibration out of tune with the choir triggers the resin bomb in your gut—better to explode than let thieves steal breakfast.

E. To be a Leaf-Sail elder: ride a slipstream a thousand metres above the canopy, steering with fingertip vanes, reading the Matriarch’s music. A minor seventh means open the vents; a perfect fifth means close them. Your body weighs less than the pollen you shepherd.

12. Closing Image  
Just before the nightly lull the plateau reaches maximum volume. Then, one species at a time, the choir holds its breath. Columns shift key. The Matriarch lowers every pitch by a half-step and, for two heartbeats, the entire Canopy Altiplano is silent. In that hush every creature, whether fungus or human or drifting leaf-sail, feels the weight of a billion years of living wood above the thin stone crust. That common pause is the closest thing the plateau has to prayer—and everyone who survives here, by birth or accident, knows to listen for it.

Those, then, are the residents of the Canopy Altiplano: a nation of lungs and leaves and resonant throats, all nested in the body of a single patient queen.
