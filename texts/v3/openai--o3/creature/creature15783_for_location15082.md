What passes for life on the Plateau of Quiet Furnaces is all one immense, metal-working lineage. Seen whole, the community looks sparse—scattered towers, a glaze of dust, not a single moving creature—but inside the basalt skin an intricate, many-layered biota pursues its slow, exact chemistry. No member ever crawls, flies or swims; every metabolism is rooted in place and stacked vertically like stages of a refinery column. Think of the plateau’s life not as a set of separate species but as one distributed organism whose parts have specialised along depth.

1. The Super-structure: Quiet Furnaces (adult macrophytes)

Height: 10–20 m.
Core temperature: rigidly clamped at 47 °C.
Colour: matte oxblood with mica-green freckles where chromium dust has sintered.

Anatomy.
• Shell – a double wall of iron–nickel foam, stiffened by cobalt phosphides. From a distance the shell looks seamless, but up close you can see regularly spaced seams: growth rings laid down every nine local days when the light cycle slips a fraction and the tower recalibrates its heat budget.

• Heat fins – shallow ribs spiralling upward at 27 ° to the vertical. Their sole purpose is to radiate the waste heat produced by the tower’s internal redox reactions.

• Root fan – a lacy “radiator” only ten centimetres below the crust, spreading four times the tower’s diameter. Roots are hollow nickel-iron capillaries that wick mineral vapours rising through micro-fissures in the basalt. Nothing penetrates deeper; waterless life here cannot risk the planet’s colder sub-strata.

Metabolism.
Sunlight drives a magnesium-chloride photochemistry that cracks any stray carbonyl gas (iron, nickel, tungsten) drifting near the surface. The freed metals are plated into the tower’s skeleton; excess oxygen dislodges as chromium-oxide dust in the hourly exhalations that give the plateau its glittering haze.

Life-span and senescence.
A furnace grows for exactly 431 local years; the moment its internal lattice crosses a fixed density threshold, it shuts every pore, melts its own shell with stored heat, and vitrifies into a hollow, glassy chimney. Collapse never scatters debris: the process is as tidy as closing a blast-furnace tap.

2. The Mid-structure: Crucibles (juvenile stage)

Before a pillar is a pillar it is a squat cone, only chest-high, glossy black, warm to the touch. Crucibles perform the same photochemistry as adults but at lower pressure. Their job is to thicken the base lattice; only when the iron–nickel skeleton can support ten metres of lift do they begin the great elongation that turns a cone into a tower. Typical duration: 60–80 years.

3. The Sub-structure: Endoroot Guild (filament colonies)

Living inside every root fan is a tangle of filaments thinner than a human hair—endolithic micro-organisms called, for lack of a better word, wires. Each wire is a sleeve of silicate glass around a stream of electrons riding along vanadium centres. Wires are not separate species but a growth form of the same lineage, adapted to the basalt/air interface.

Functions.
• They mine cations (Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺) by locally reducing the basalt until it cracks into a powder fine enough for the roots to vacuum.
• They export that charge up into the furnace’s body, acting as literal electrical feed lines. Remove the wire mat and the tower starves within weeks.

4. The Internal-gas Plankton (columnar aerosols)

Inside an adult furnace the air is never still. Brownian drafts ferry clouds of sub-micron capsules—spheres of chrome-oxide studded with porphyrin rings. They look like soot motes; they are, in fact, free-floating organelles the tower has budded off its inner walls.

Roles.
• They increase total reactive surface area and even out concentration gradients—gaseous analogues of blood cells.

• Once a capsule’s catalytic sites are fouled it drifts down, sticks to the root fan, and is cannibalised by the wires for its trace metals.

5. The Lattice Phage (metallic viruses)

Occasionally, a self-assembling cluster of molybdenum nitrides appears inside a furnace’s membrane. These nanometre-scale “phages” hijack local crystal growth, forcing the shell to extrude hair-thin quills. The quills shed excess heat remarkably well; furnaces infected by the phage tend to live 30–40 years longer than usual. The phage is neither ally nor enemy—simply another catalytic improvisation in a world where evolution has only metals to play with.

6. The After-structure: Echo Stacks (post-mortem community)

Once a tower vitrifies it is dead, but its glass tubes are colonised by a photo-chemo film the colour of untarnished copper. This film, a descendant of the endoroot wires, favours the low light inside the chimney where each sunrise bounces down the bore like a laser. The film is the plateau’s closest analogue to decay; over millennia it roughens the glass, turning perfect columns cloudy and eventually brittle. In ten or twenty million years the chimney will crumble into hexagonal shards—the only true erosion the mesa will know.

Why nothing walks, crawls, or flies.

Energy here descends a single stair: sunlight → tower surface → root fan → basalt. Any side-branch would have to pay for its own plumbing in an environment that offers no liquids, no organics, no free redox couple richer than what the towers already exploit. Mobility would cost more energy than it returns, so natural selection shaved it away. What remains is an unbroken column of vertical throughput, as efficient and silent as a falling ray of light.

Inhabitants, summarised.

• Quiet Furnaces – adult pillars, perfect 47 °C reactors.
• Crucibles – juvenile cones preparing the load-bearing lattice.
• Endoroot Wires – filamentous miners and power lines.
• Internal-gas Plankton – catalytic dust riding convection.
• Lattice Phage – viral crystal engineers.
• Echo Stack Film – slow glass-etchers of the dead.

That is all. No pollinators, no grazers, no predators, no scavengers—just stages of the same metallurgical life stream, practising the art of smelting sunlight into iron, century after century, in a hush so deep even their own breathing is almost too soft to hear.
