Begin with what you are already touching.

1. The Under-Skin  
What passes for ground is a single animal-vegetable republic: a contiguous mat of mosses, liverworts, filamentous algae, iron-tasting bacteria and transparent nematodes, all stitched into one spongy sheet.  Locally they call it the under-skin.  It breathes.  Very slowly, but it does: at night carbon dioxide seeps from a million invisible mouths; by day the sheet inhales so much light and vapor that its surface temperature drops a full degree below the air.  A step anywhere dimples kilometres of it, and the dent rebounds over hours like the settling of a down mattress.

Inside the mat flow capillary rivers—threads of water only a hair thick that migrate toward any pressure gradient.  They keep the sheet alive during the rare dry spells.  They also function as nerves.  Calcium waves travel through them at moss-speed (roughly nine metres per hour), relaying information about shade, trampling feet, or the landing of a bird.  To travellers the mat looks passive; to itself it is a continent-sized, damp thought.

2. Pillar-Trees: The Slow Poles  
Next in seniority stand the pillars, each a bundle of semi-woody tubes wound into a flexing column.  They are not quite plants, not quite fungi.  Growth rings spiral instead of stacking; if you follow one ring with your finger you will end up six metres higher and a half-turn around.  The interior is hollow, resonant, and full of warm mist.  Glass frogs crawl inside to metamorphose; technicians slip microphones in for fun, later playing back wet cathedral chords that turn out to be the tree’s own pulse.

A pillar’s mind—if that is the word—sits in its dish-shaped crown where pigments write chemical photographs of the sky.  The crowns trade those photographs through pollen sized packets of starch.  Once a decade, during a stretch of uninterrupted sun, every pillar on the plateau will tilt a degree or two toward some mutually agreed azimuth, hold the pose for weeks, then ease back.  Nobody knows what the gesture means; the outpost personnel have a betting pool on whether it is worship, semaphore, or stretching.

3. The Hangers and the Skimmers  
Bronze-green vines descend from the crowns like rewound lightning bolts.  Epiphytic fish—scaled things the length of sewing needles—swim up and down inside the vines’ liquid cores, filtering glucose seepage the way trout take insects from a stream.  When a vine finally sags low enough to kiss the under-skin, those fish exit into the mat’s water veins and vanish downslope.  Their bones, when found in marsh sediments, are translucent and jointed like the frames of eyeglasses.

On misty days you may spot a different commuter: the skimmers.  Think of a dragonfly reconstructed from panes of smoky quartz.  The skimmer’s wing beats strike water droplets out of the air; the insect then drinks the beads as they whiplash across its mouth parts.  Skimmers hatch only during overcast—sunlight sears their wings into useless, curling film—so an entire generation can bloom and perish without a single clear morning.

4. The Rumoured Giants  
Every plateau has its campfire monster.  Here it is the echo-whale, a creature no one has filmed.  You do not see it; you hear it when the wind dies for half a breath and the waterfalls hesitate.  A thrumming, as if someone plucked a cable the width of a canyon.  Geophones have traced these notes travelling underground at ninety kilometres per hour.  A few biologists argue for a migratory, cartilaginous slug as long as a train, sluicing through networks of solution caverns.  The technicians mostly hope the thing never collides with a turbine intake.

5. Human Tenants: The Rotators  
Two or three at a time, wearing oil-skins furred with seedlings, the power-station technicians arrive by gasbag and folding rotor.  They stay thirty days.  During the first week they shout.  After that they converse in clipped semaphore—torch flashes, arm waves—because the plateau’s roar eats language and the roar never sleeps.  They do not bother naming each outpost.  They identify them by smell: Outpost where the moss smells of lemon; Outpost with the sulphur trickle; Outpost where the handrails sweat cinnamon rust.

Living quarters are polished steel cubes on stilts.  Inside: condensation, bunk straps, the endless tick of drying boots.  The rotators bring libraries of shell-printed novels that can survive humidity.  They bring aluminum combs to rake algae off grating.  They bring seeds—always fail to cultivate them—and carry the same seeds back out, now mouldy, to avoid littering a world that seems aggressively patient about absorbing litter.

6. Co-workers: The Auxiliary Swarm  
Because technicians are scarce, most tasks belong to servo-moss: miniature bots the size of peppercorns whose casings are part chitin, part chrome.  They harvest themselves from the mat on their first day of life and plug into it again when their battery crystals saturate.  From above, maintenance looks like brown dust swirling over the grates; in close-up you see organised rafts of thousands, legs linked, rolling forward as a single sheet while the hindmost recharge and leapfrog to the front.

7. Symbionts of the Machine  
Hydro-turbines are not inert infrastructure.  A decade of biofilm has turned them into ecosystems.  Nickel-green fronds sprout along impeller vanes.  Jelly moulds rim inspection hatches, quivering whenever pressure shifts.  These hitchhikers cost a few percentage points of efficiency, but stripping them would mean shutting down power for weeks, so the technicians tolerate and log their growth the way foresters census saplings.

8. The Silent Predators  
Predation here is less drama than bookkeeping.  Moss ticks—slabs of protein no larger than rice grains—survive by detaching from the under-skin, rafting across puddles, and digesting whatever soft-bodied thing they happen to bump.  A single slate wader might pick twenty thousand of them out of the moss in a morning, bowing its neck like a metronome.  The frogs keep the ticks in check at night; the waders prune the frogs by day; nothing ever booms enough to crash.

9. Lichen & Light: The Optic Choir  
On exposed boulders grow lens-lichens: crusts studded with microprisms of silica.  They harvest light not for photosynthesis but to project it.  Under the right angle these lichens throw faint circles of rainbow several metres wide—living sundogs that drift over the plateau face.  The technicians use them as calendars; when the first full circle appears, it is time to radio for the relief crew.

10. The Plateau Itself  
Finally, admit the possibility that the whole mesa is alive.  Sensors sunk through the rock seldom agree with one another.  Some report pulses of heat migrating like fever through stone.  Some record low hums that accelerate during cloudless hours.  The under-skin, the pillars, the vines, the fish, the turbines, the lichens, even the outpost cabins—all shed ions and organics into the constant seep.  Everything dissolves fractionally, and the runoff carries that dissolved catalogue into fractures below.  What collects there may already be editing the plateau toward ends of its own: lengthening fissures, re-routing pressure, coaxing pillars to tilt their crowns.  If so, the youngest inhabitant is the one wearing boots and high-vis vinyl.  The oldest is the land, learning its alphabet grain by grain.

When you leave by airship, peel away through the cloud roof, and the roar fades under the propellers, you carry with you damp gear, fungal spores, a ringing in your ears—and the knowledge that nothing up there, not water, not metal, not flesh, ever really stops living.
