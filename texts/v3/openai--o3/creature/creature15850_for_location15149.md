Who Lives Here  

I. The Uncounted Lives  
Before the bipeds ever coaxed the grove into colonnades, the cavern was already crowded. Life, in this place, is a long continuum of mineral, gas and will; where one category ends and another begins is an argument still under way.

1. Sub-Basaltic Microforms  
   • Frost-lichen: not a lichen at all but a two-cell consortium that quilts the basalt ceiling from within, digesting glassy rock atom by atom and exhaling the trace gases the larger organisms require. Their waste heat is so slight that only a billion of them together can be felt, yet without that warmth the spindle seeds could never have germinated.  
   • Rust-chewers: iron-oxidising archaea that gather in the salt rings of every mesa. They knit themselves into lace so fine that moonlight could pass through, if moonlight ever found its way here. During the forty-minute dim of each chemiluminescent cycle they glow a faint infra-red, invisible to eyes but sensed by many hunters.

2. The Spindle Plants (Order: Voltagorales)  
   Each grove is one organism—roots graft underground into a single electro-chemical parliament. The fronds are merely mouths and the resin beads, eyes. Age thickens a trunk until the centre starves and hollows; those hollows become airshafts, then streets, then cathedrals. A grove never dies; it just adds rooms.

3. The Wind-Gliders (Genus: Lysaptychus)  
   Hand-span rays, cartilage stiffened by silicate plates, wing-skin glassy and mirrored. They court by semaphore flashes, mate mid-spiral in the updraft, and deposit strings of eggs into flute-vents. Chilled air incubates the clutch for seven years; chicks emerge already gliding, never having known stillness.

4. Basal Grazers (Family: Cryosphagidae)  
   Dog-sized tortoise analogues with shells of interlocking quartz scales. Jaws drip perchloric acid; each bite etches stone. They pace in mathematical circuits, guided by magnetite nerves that sense the electromagnetic moan of the wind-organ. Two hundred of them can prune an entire basin of fallen fronds in a decade—lightning speed for this ecosystem.

5. The Stalking Vines  
   Predators of movement. A vine threads microscopic hooks through thin layers of dust, waits for centuries; when vibration transmits—grazers fighting, a biped running—it catalyses stored perchlorate, puffs a frost of oxidiser, and lunges a metre in an hour: a killing sprint by local reckoning. Everything that moves knows the taste of that grey fear.

II. The Artisans: those Small, Clawed Bipeds  

1. Anatomy  
   • Height: knee-high to a human, though no human has visited.  
   • Integument: overlapping scutes of dark horn laced with silica fibres; they read the cavern’s temperature shifts through these plates as if through skin.  
   • Limbs: four in total. The rear pair end in broad gripping pads for basalt; the fore pair divide into three pruning claws and a tactile tongue-digit used for spelling messages onto one another’s shells.  
   • Respiration: lungs lined with perchlorate foam. Oxygen is rare, so they run their metabolisms on nitriles and trace chlorates, exhaling chlorine dioxide in glittering fumes that snap into salt on contact with the dry air.  
   • Lifespan: measured by wind-shifts. One full modulation of the great hollow pillar—about sixty surface years—is a long life.

2. Senses and Speech  
   Sound is useless; the organ-wind drowns all. Vision helps, but light is fickle. Instead they employ three channels:  
   • Shell-tapping: fingers drum prime-number rhythms that cut through resonance. A greeting is 2-3-5; alarm is 11-13-17.  
   • Biolume script: symbiotic glands under the jaw smear resin across skin that then fluoresces for exactly one pulse cycle. Conversations drift, luminous, down streets before winking out.  
   • Pressure glyphs: in the dark of root-chambers they press carapace to carapace and exchange a language of increments in blood pressure—poetry by heartbeat.

3. Culture  
   A. Architecture by Persuasion  
      Nothing is cut; every spindle trunk is convinced. Sap-tappers drink from a growing branch, stealing charge so it bends toward shadow or light. Over seasons a hallway twists itself open, satisfied that this is its own idea.  
   B. Time-Keeping  
      The resonance-pillar hums a fundamental that dips a cent flat each spring as outer caverns cool. They do not name days; instead they name tones. “We met when the pillar sang low-third.”  
   C. Subsistence  
      • Resin beadery: they harvest only those beads whose glow is about to expire, drinking the stored chemical light as warmth.  
      • Grazer husbandry: juvenile grazers are lured with salt bricks, fitted with mirrored panniers grown from glider chitin. They wander, collect frond-fall, return by magnet sense; the panniers contain dust, spores, errant minerals—a wandering agriculture.  
      • And, rarely, they eat silence. When a flute-vent crusts over and falls mute, the bipeds gather, pry the plug, and inhale the hush that pours out first. To them it is medicine.

4. Reproduction and Family  
   Sexes are sequential: shell color drifts from iron-red to cobalt over decades; cobalt adults brood. Brooding occurs in thermal niches where ceiling crystals focus spindle-light. Eggs, translucent disks, are glued to the living walls of child-crèches and left to interpret the hum of the wind as lullaby. No parent meets its offspring until the young can tap 2-3-5 unaided.

5. Art and Story  
   Their myths claim the cavern is the lung of a buried beast; every whistle-vent is a bronchus, every spindle plume a cilium. To tend plants is, therefore, to soothe the sleeper so it will never wake and crush them. Legend also warns of “water,” a substance said to drown sound and rust thought—an unthinkable terror.

III. Other, Stranger Tenants  

1. Echo-Leeches  
   Once every few centuries the resonance of the roof harmonises, focuses enough acoustic energy to melt pockets of salt into glass. Within weeks translucent worms occupy those pockets, drinking vibration through latticed jaws. Each worm is a silence so pure that the bipeds treat the creatures as relics: wearing one around the neck grants a private moment of quiet amid the roar.

2. The Orrery Crabs  
   Orb-bodied crustaceans that map air-currents. By carving shallow furrows into the dust in synchronous circles, they trace pressure lines the way humans sketch star charts. Bipeds consult these living diagrams before pruning, lest they detune the wind-organ.

3. Surface Echoes  
   Very rarely, thin light from an upper fissure spills one clear shaft—and ultraviolet spores drift down in it, alien. They germinate into pale fans that survive a single spindle cycle, then flake into glittering ash. The bipeds gather that ash and store it in sealed basalt jars, believing it to be dreams of somewhere faster.

IV. The Weft: How Lives Interlock  

Nothing here is solitary. The wind keeps every membrane vibrating, every crystal ringing, every mind thrumming. Grazers spread spores; spindles shelter bipeds; bipeds prune spindles so their glow can feed gliders; gliders die, their fall fertilising mesas where rust-chewers wait. Even predators like the stalking vine are guides—its slow pursuit reminds all souls of Rule 4: whatever hurries soon dies.

V. A Glimpse in Motion  

Picture an ordinary half-night:  
The chemilume is on its thirteenth minute of brightening. A wave of tur-quoise climbs the grove like dawn breaking underwater. Gliders lift, mirrors winking an algebra of hunger. Below, adolescent bipeds in cobalt shells chase after juveniles of the herd, hanging new panniers while elders tap a rhythm that syncs with the standing tone. Overhead the ceiling whistles in ragged chords; a sudden silent gap announces an echo-leech’s feeding. For three heartbeats everyone pauses—astonished at the hush—then the roar floods back. Life resumes, unhurried, inexhaustible.

That—multiplied by millennia—is who lives in the Descent: a choir of stone-slow chemistries, patient architects who carve no stone, beasts of mirror and acid and tuned shell, and the omnipresent wind that both deafens and sustains them all.
