LIFE IN THE CHURN-UNDER

I.  Beginnings: the invisible nations  
1.  Vaporflesh bacteria  
    • Shape: gas–filled polyhedra twelve microns wide.  
    • Trick: coat themselves in a monomolecular film of hydrophobic silicates; they float on steam the way pollen rides the wind.  
    • Work: split sulfurous vapor and free the trace heat; the tiny exothermic pops form the background warmth that keeps cold pockets rare.  
    • Gift: when a colony finally cools it drops like lace and crunches underfoot, the first snow the Churn-Under ever knows.

2.  Ferro-lichens  
    • Two bodies, one organic, one not: filaments weave through oxidized iron whiskers precipitated straight out of dripping stalactites.  
    • Their metabolism sounds like distant sandpaper—slow rasp as they file trace metal from the air.  
    • They are eaten only by silence beetles (see III-c); everything else fears the metallic aftertaste that lingers for days.

II.  Architects you never see  
1.  Silica-mycelia (briefly named in the dossier) are not threads but rivers of non-Newtonian fluid stiffened by quartz nanorods.  
    • They advance in pulses synchronized to low-frequency geologic groans; a wall’s vibration is the drum that tells them when to surge.  
    • Every tunnel they leave is a lung: pores remain open, breathing heat upward and moisture downward. The entire warren is therefore a single colony, miles wide, still deciding what shape its body should be.

2.  Gyre-spores  
    • Each glass bubble contains a vortex of static charge that rips electrons off rock as it rolls.  
    • When stepped on, the charge grounds through whatever touched it; filaments blossom, knitting stone dust into nacreous armor around the offender’s foot.  
    • Shale-runners intentionally stamp on fresh drifts to shell their growing young; Flintkin children dare one another to do the same and compare scars years later.

III.  The middle swarm  
a)  Skitterwakes  
    • Anatomy: four-winged hexapods; the “abdomen” is a living hourglass where inhaled radon dust glows, then decays, then is spat out as glitter.  
    • Behavior: ride thermal columns until they strike a ceiling of photoliths, scrape crystal dust with rasping palps, then fall in shimmering rain.  
    • Life cycle: adults seen by travelers are transient. The true organism is the communal “reef” glued high on buttresses—sugar-glass honeycombs stuffed with wingless, eyeless queens. Every migration you glimpse is merely the reef shuffling workforce.

b)  Shale-runners  
    • Skeleton: ribbon of cobalt cartilage; joints click magnetically—hence the Morse-code chatter that guides them through darkness.  
    • Skin: loose, peels in sheets every week; discarded pelts wrinkle into scrolls eaten by nearly everyone for cobalt salts.  
    • Social: paired livers allow them to metabolize almost anything; in famine they become scavengers, in plenty strict herbivores of mycelial foam. A runner that has tasted meat is odor-marked for life and barred from mating burrows.

c)  Silence beetles  
    • Color: impossible black; they drink photons, leaving a temporary after-image on the retina.  
    • Utility: Flintkin keep cages of them to hush sleeping hollows. Each beetle absorbs wavelengths we would label “sound” as well as light through phased elytra. Too many in one spot, and you feel pressure in the ear like diving under water.  
    • Downside: if a cage is smashed, the sudden release of stored noise detonates like a depth charge.

d)  Caecilian dreadknots  
    • Beak: three sections join at a point, twist apart like a drill chuck. They do not chew: they core out cylinders of rock, swallow them whole, and later vomit neat piles of feldspar-flour baited with pheromone. Shale-runners arrive, feed, are swallowed in turn.  
    • Sense: trace concentrations of lithium ions—each creature’s “sweat signature.” Travelers who carry clay jugs of high-lithium pool water can mislead them for hours.  
    • Mating: two dreadknots braid; their skins fuse, trade stem cells, then part. The scar band is whiter than the pink flesh, readable like tree rings.

IV.  That which everything else orbits  
Tremor-matriarchs  

One animal per biome of the Churn-Under, perhaps six in all, never meeting.  

Body plan:  
• Thirty to sixty meters of telescoping segments. Scales are ceramic lamellae grown like teeth in conveyor rows.  
• Neural net is hydraulic; thought pulses in peristaltic waves down the body, so a question asked at the head might not be answered until the tail reaches the place where the words were spoken. Flintkin shamans test bravery by posing riddles to the mouth and waiting days for the tail to flex in reply.  

Ecology:  
• Diet: everything. They sieve condensate for bacteria, graze reefs of Skitterwakes, and ram striated rock to drink melt. The slurry they excrete is sterile but rich in silicates; fresh ground for Gyre-spores.  
• Symphony: each matriarch maintains a chord—a triad of infrasonic notes. Cavern denizens memorize these like weather reports: A dive note means stability; a rising third predicts eruption. When two chords overlap, plate tectonics itself warps; stone between them foams and new passageways yawn open.  
• Death: has not been observed. Flintkin myth claims they do not die but surface, shatter into meteor showers, and seed other worlds with embryonic warrens.

V.  The Flintkin – speaking people  
1.  Physiology  
    • Stature: knee-high to an adult human; spine triple-arched like a paper clip for shock absorption.  
    • Dermis: stippled with subdermal flakes of obsidian implanted at adolescence. These anchor muscle but also refract heat inward, conserving energy.  
    • Blood: copper-rich; smells like burnt coins.  

2.  Language  
    • Base layer clicks tongue-palate, carries through drip and hiss.  
    • Overlay is gesture: wrists circled in hot twine to leave contrails for the watcher’s eye because light shifts too fast for bare-hand signing.  
    • Only proper nouns are sung; melody pins identity in a geography that erases scents and footprints.

3.  Lifeways  
    • Hearth: wherever a silica-mycelial lung has just evacuated. The empty space stays warm for a week—enough to birth, fledge, and move on.  
    • Craft: every tool anticipates loss. Knapped glass knives crumble; Flintkin grind the pieces into throat-lozenges that ease heatstroke, nothing wasted.  
    • Food: larvae of Skitterwakes smoked in radon vents until jewel-bright; dreadknot core-flour leavened with bacterial foam.  

4.  Social geometry  
    • Bands of thirteen. Twelve walk; one rides a litter of woven runner-skins, eyes fixed upward to read the photolith constellations and track time. Rotate the role daily; everyone sees the sky at some point—even if it is only a ceiling pretending to be sky.  
    • Law: “When rock moves, debt resets.” Any promise annulled by geographical change is forgiven. Thus betrayal is rare; the landscape does enough of it for everyone.  

5.  Cosmology  
    • Creation story: First came Heat. Heat exhaled Steam; Steam condensed into Voice; Voice sang Rimstone, and Rimstone is still singing, carving chambers for new verses. The Churn-Under is therefore a throat. Living well means harmonizing with the throat, never speaking a note that clashes.  
    • Aspirations: entropy is exile. The worst fate is cold preservation. Flintkin deliberately dismantle the cairns of the dead so the stones can find new songs in new walls.  

VI.  Edge dwellers and rumors  
1.  Photolith shepherds – small squid-like organisms inhabiting the glowing crystal itself, swimming through fractures filled with molten quartz. Rarely squeeze out; when they do their bodies cool into brittle glass within minutes. Flintkin record finding perfect statuettes of them and argue whether they were ever alive.  

2.  Echo-leeches – immaterial parasites shaped only by reflected sound. They cling to travelers generating steady rhythm—bootsteps, heartbeat—and thicken the echoes until the host can no longer hear predators. Survive a week, then burst in a clap that can stun Skitterwakes out of the air.  

3.  The Pale Ledger – rumored procession of blind Flintkin carrying basalt tablets that record every death by tapping characters into still-pliable stone. Scholars chase them, hoping to learn the sum of all endings. No one ever catches up: ground behind the Ledger freezes, preserving their text, then liquefies days later, erasing it.

VII.  What it means to be alive here  
• To breathe is to sign a contract with the heat: it will animate you, but it will also melt your footprints.  
• To eat is to participate in sculpting: every bite of mineral becomes muscle that shifts the caverns when you leap.  
• To die is to cool—and cooling is the only permanence the Churn-Under recognizes.

So the Churn-Under seethes with citizenship: microbes that draft the laws of chemistry, insects that weave with radiance, serpents that write tunnels like calligraphy, giants whose pulse waltzes with tectonics, and Flintkin who translate all of it into story, song, and knives meant to snap.  They do not merely live in a place; they live as the place, each heartbeat another syllable in a lithic epic still being composed underneath the blind crust of the world.
