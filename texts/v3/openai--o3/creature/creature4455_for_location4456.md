WHO LIVES HERE  

1. The City-Body  
The plateau’s dominant resident is not a people but an organism: a single, ancient polyfloral colony the Junara call Uru-Mant.  Three main genotypes—reed, broad-leaf, and lignum-vine—share one vascular system.  Sap analysis shows identical sugars moving through every stem, whether it looks like bamboo, cedar, or strangler fig.  
• Age: Cores taken from dead root tips suggest at least 9,000 seasonal rings.  
• Behaviour: Uru-Mant breathes.  At dawn its surfaces uncurl, thinning the boundary layer so wind can strip out yesterday’s carbon-dioxide load.  By nightfall the same leaves laminate together, trapping warmth for the amphibians that nest inside.  
• Memory: Wound sites never regrow identical tissue.  A notch made fifty years ago is still visible as a faint chevron.  The Junara treat these marks as scripture, reading the bark like a chorded narrative of seasons, storms, and ancestors’ choices.  

2. The Junara (People Within)  
Numbering roughly two hundred, they regard themselves as circulating cells rather than tenants.  
• Physiology: Broad nail-beds for clinging to slick cambium; lungs with unusually large surface area (confirmed by visiting medics) that let them breathe cold, saturated air without coughing.  Children develop inner-ear musculature strong enough to counter the never-ending sway—they can run full tilt across a line of spring-boards that buckle under each footfall.  
• Dress: Nothing dead.  Tunics are coaxed from living feltleaf which thickens where rubbed and thins where vented; after three years the garment is clipped off, rooted in compost, and a new skin is taught to grow.  
• Social Pattern: Five occupational “beats,” each named for a body rhythm.  
  ­- Pulse keepers – patrol the root ways, pruning edema-knots so sap continues to circulate.  
  ­- Breath callers – climb to the crown before dawn, slitting transient vents so the day’s wind can enter.  
  ­- Nerve binders – weave creeper cabling that transmits tension messages across the canopy; a falling limb in the west tugs a line in the east, warning children away before the crack finishes echoing.  
  ­- Dream singers – oral historians who recite the bark marks at winter solstice.  
  ­- Skin menders – practice “graftcraft,” pressing compatible seedlings against storm scars until they fuse.  
• Law: The single exile crime is flame.  A transgressor’s name is cored into a sapling that is then bent outward over the cliff—held alive but forever outside the shared enclosure.  

3. Fog-Swimmers (Microfauna in the Air)  
On bright, cooled mornings the mist itself crawls with life.  Each cubic metre contains about 30,000 flagellate algae called sky-kelp.  They photosynthesize on the fly, riding updrafts until too heavy, then exude droplets and sink to lower leaves where slugs eat them.  Their constant rise-and-fall gives the fog a grainy sparkle the Junara read as tomorrow’s weather: a glittering fog means high pressure, dull fog a coming deluge.  

4. Insect Choirs  
a) Latch-Cicadas – torsos shaped like harp frames; they clamp to reed walls and stridulate directly into the plant’s air canals, turning whole halls into resonators.  The Junara time their communal meals to the cicadas’ quiet molts.  
b) Glass-Beetles – sheathed in translucent chitin through which their gut flora glow neon green; they are the primary dispersers of luminous yeast.  
c) Weather-Moths – four seasonal morphs.  The “Rain” morph has hydrophobic scales that shingle together, letting it fly through downpours to pollinate night-blooming funnels.  

5. Amphibian Plumbing  
Glider-frogs (the ones whose gutteral pump is the plateau’s alarm clock) double as living valves.  Each colonizes a hollow node of reed; by flexing its webbed feet it can shutter or open the reed’s vent, regulating humidity for its own egg-mass and incidentally for the people who sleep in adjacent chambers.  A well-tuned transit hall contains a dozen such frogs, and the airflow rises and falls like a breathing flute.  

6. The Swift Predators  
There is no apex beast, only specialists of the ambush instant.  
• Blink-Ocelot – dappled fur the color of unmixed lichens.  It coils around a stair-trunk, waits for vibration, strikes through its own tail-loop, and is gone.  Average chase distance: 1.3 metres, measured by claw marks.  
• Needle-Heron – the plateau’s only regularly visiting flyer large enough to spear mammals.  It skims the cloud ceiling, folds suddenly, and spears prey on the bough.  The Junara pay it a toll of stone-snails so it ignores their arboreal goats.  

7. The Arboreal Goats  
Weight: 2–3 kilograms; four digits with reversed knee articulation so they can hang head-down without tiring tendons.  Milk tastes of cold smoke and cucumber.  Goatherds do not pen them—they weave “salt-lace” (minerals crystallized onto a living net of vine) along approved corridors, guiding the little ruminants like beads on a string.  

8. The Hidden Colossus  
Beneath the moss terraces sleeps Droma, an earthworm 70 metres long, blind, older than Uru-Mant itself.  Seismic logs show it surfaces once every nineteen or twenty years to gulp a fresh roll of schist and excrete a ribbon of micronized mineral the plateau plants treasure.  The Junara calendar is keyed to Droma surfacings; a “long year” is the interval between appearances.  

9. Symbiotic Microbes  
• Ember Yeast – thrums a weak chemiluminescence where sap sugars run high.  Every doorframe is actually a live yeast baffle: step inside and pale amber light exudes for exactly as long as your body heat remains.  
• Gut Weavers – bacteria living in Junara intestines that spin nanofibrils from digested cellulose; excreted, the fibrils are harvested, twisted with spider silk, and return as climbing ropes.  Outsiders find the practice revolting; Junara children find outsiders’ rope shame amusing.  

10. The Quiet Dead  
Space is so valuable that even death is assimilated.  Corpses—animal or human—are fed to the “Mortuary Rind,” a shelf fungus the width of the plateau’s rim.  It grows exactly one new ring per body, allowing a visual census of all who have ever lived here.  The rind glows violet until mineralization is complete, then dulls to slate.  At dawn, widows walk the rim, naming the new rings.  

11. Voices Without Flesh  
Ask any Junara and they will tell you the wind talks.  Researchers brought phonographs; nothing conclusive.  But windpaths do repeat melodic patterns, especially where reed corridors narrow to organ-pipe throats.  The locals leave queries—bundles of seed, puzzles of knotted vine—dangling in these throats.  Some mornings the bundles are gone, replaced by solutions no one can replicate.  The Junara attribute this to “The Unbodied,” entities neither plant nor animal that manifest only as cooperative turbulence.  Skeptics note that answers always benefit the plateau’s water balance.  Whether intelligence or emergent physics, something listens.  

12. Visitors  
Because fire is exile, traders from the drier plains below must surrender all flints at the cliff ladder.  They bring salt bricks, obsidian, and news of politics that matter only until the next rain erases the story from bark.  Some stay, accept the damp silence, and within two generations their descendants sprout the broad Junara nail-beds—a living visa stamped in keratin.  

WHAT THE PLACE WANTS  
Every inhabitant, from sky-kelp mote to Junara matriarch, shares a single imperative: keep the flow moving.  Sap, air, water, information, memory—anything that lingers stagnates and invites rot of the wrong kind.  Motion is worship.  The plateau rewards it with unimaginable fecundity, punishes stillness with gentle, osmotic obliteration.  

Stand here a month and small lichens will sketch your boot prints.  Stand a year and vines will lace your ribs.  Stand a lifetime and you will not stand at all; you will be folded, remixed, sung of in frog-valves and bark chevrons, living forever not as an individual but as a continued eddy in the single, crowded bloodstream of the City-Body on the cloud-washed stone.
