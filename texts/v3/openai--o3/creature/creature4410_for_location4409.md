Who lives atop the still-new stone is not a single folk but an assemblage of lives, each pared down to what the plateau will tolerate and nothing more. Walk the tilted tableland from south brink to north and you will meet them in layers, the way one meets fossils inside a cliff face—first the obvious hoof and hand, then the subtler workings that keep the surface calm, and finally the presences no one can point at yet everyone allows for.

1. The High People, or Keirri

Body  
• Tall by valley standards but thin as tent poles, ribcages flared like bellows from a lifetime of thin air.  
• Hair and eyes dark enough to reflect the sky’s unbroken blue; skin the pale taupe of weathered pumice. When visitors first arrive they think the Keirri never sweat—really the droplets wick off before they can bead, stolen by the same hunger in the air that drinks the river dry.  
• Fingernails grown deliberately long and filed to chisel edges: an extra tool for teasing fibers, lifting stone flakes, popping moss crusts.

Dress  
• Undyed nettle-fiber cloth, heavy and draping; color comes from copper dust rubbed in while the weave is still damp, an effortless metallic lustre that will never tarnish here.  
• Goat-hide sandals with soles notched to grip the glassy obsidian plates. No heel: silence is status.

Speech & Memory  
Nobody writes. They claim there is no need—memories do not blur. A child can recite the exact phrasing of a lullaby heard once; two negotiators pause the gong’s second note and resume the debate three days later on the same breath. Their language, Ket, reflects that: verbs uninflected by tense, sentences built in mirrored halves so the end repeats the beginning and the sense may be entered at any point without losing the thread.

Social Shape  
• Seven Ridges, seven pragmatic clans: Stonewrights, Cloud-herds, Water-wardens, Gardeners, Tortoise-keepers, Gong-casters, Rope-spinners. A person may apprentice to any craft, but the ridge of birth names the cadence of her footsteps—a subtle code by which elders measure balance.  
• Governance is a rotating “Still Circle” of fourteen adults who agree on almost nothing but are required, by law, to reach unanimous decisions. The debates can run for months, conducted in sign when the plateau is listening.

Daily Work  
Dawn: every able body walks the runnels gathering pearl-beads of condensed water with a squeegee of willow bark. Mid-morning: blades are struck from last night’s obsidian core, handles wrapped, edges tested on goat hair. Noon: the long hush; food is cold stinging-berry mash and whatever a cliff-goat has yielded. Afternoon: repairs—always repairs, never improvements. Dusk: the Funicular Descent, when six muscled haulers winch grain and strangers up from the cloud shelf far below. Night: story-weaving, a communal sculpting of meticulous oral dioramas that will petrify in shared memory.

Belief  
They worship nothing, yet venerate everything that refuses to move: the unmossed flanks of basalt columns, the mirror pools, the single roasted grain a novice must hold beneath her tongue from gong to gong without swallowing. The plateau’s immobility is understood as a covenant; to break it would be to invite the unfinished violence of its birth back into the stone.

2. The Agreeable Beasts

Cliff-Goats  
• Bone-white horns corkscrewed forward like augers; when two clash the sound is porcelain struck by a mallet.  
• Hooves excrete a resin that glues and unglues in micro-seconds, letting them stand on glassy inclines. The Keirri harvest the dried resin flakes—it softens nettle fibers and waterproofs sandals.

Spiral-Shell Tortoises  
• Each the size of a kitchen hearthstone. Scutes overgrow in logarithmic spirals and carry an ecosystem of moss, dye-lichen, even small flowering plants.  
• Live two centuries, possibly more. Once a decade they haul themselves to Keirr’s plaza; elders kneel, place palms against the shell, and “listen.” No outsider has discovered what is heard.

Flicker-Bats  
• Wings refract sunset into smoke-violet halos. Internal membranes studded with mica granules—reason unknown.  
• Hang in clusters above mirror pools, never touching the water; to fall in would trap them within the unbreaking ripples until someone lifts them clear.

Finger-Fish  
• Blind, lateral lines hypertrophied into whisker-like frills.  
• Skin secretions prevent water stagnation; a single fish in a cistern keeps it sweet for months. They are returned to the pools after service, minus one scale taken as tax.

Absent Birds  
No nest, no dropping, no dawn chorus. Even feathers blown across the rim turn back of their own accord. Children collect the near-miss feathers and braid them into “maybe-wings,” toys that must be unmade before the next gong.

3. The Smaller and Stranger

Copper Wraiths  
Invisible motes that haunt every metal surface. In the lowlands they oxidize whatever they touch; here, lacking whatever reagent the plateau withholds, they drift hungry and harmless, visible only as sparks when two hammered-copper blades strike.

Scree-Weavers  
• Arthropods the length of a knuckle, body a single obsidian chip. They extrude silica threads and stitch loose gravel into stable quilted mats. Gardeners seed them along terrace edges; left alone, they pattern the slope in fractals that keep soil from wandering.

Ghast Moss  
• Breathes so slowly a single cell’s cycle takes a human week. Its mat of dark green drains sound—stand on it and even your heartbeat grows distant. Patches are left undisturbed near the gong tower, amplifying the strike’s bronze.

4. The Uncounted—Echoes and Hypotheses

Plateau-Born  
From time to time a Keirri child is born with pupils shaped like the plateau’s rim: an almost-square squiggle. Such children need no air-rest, can hold a memory not only perfect but projecting: elders say they foresee the way an event will be recalled, and steer actions toward the most elegant future remembrance. They often become the plateau’s ambassadors to the lowlands, walking archives who never write but can recite six generations of trade promises without error.

Stone-Kith  
When fog covers the crown, silhouettes glide among the basalt fins: bipedal, tall, jointed wrong, limbs flexing along fault-lines like hinges. No prints, no spoor, just a child’s lost toy found placed upright on a ridge-top, chipped into perfect stillness.

Mirror-Pool Doubles  
Stare into one of those motionless waters and eventually you see yourself arrive, kneel, and stare back from the other side. Travelers panic; locals wave at their doubles and walk on. The reflection never precedes the real body, it only lingers after. Some say the doubles graze on abandoned possibilities, keeping the plateau thin enough to stay balanced.

5. Relations With Outsiders

Caravans arrive by balloon or by the funicular’s perilous lower terminus. Lowlanders trade hammered tin, parchment, and gossip; they take away obsidian blades, goat resin, and berries that never spoil. Most stay three days at most—after that, memory begins to harden, making them feel trapped inside their own first impressions.

A handful remain. They shed watches that no longer tick, forget calendar years, and trim their sentences until the silence between words does more talking. After seven winters a newcomer is considered plateau-woven; her past below is treated as legend, perhaps true, perhaps not, but in either event complete.

6. Lifespan of the Plateau Itself

Geologists insist the land must resume weathering one day. The Keirri politely disagree. In their cosmology the plateau is a bloom of spacetime folded upward through common rock, a petal of foreverness. Everything living here is not merely adapted to stillness; it is enlisted to maintain it. Goats scour fresh rock so lichen never grips, scree-weavers quilt slides tight, humans halt water before it can dig. Even memory joins the effort: a past that will not degrade is another buttress against change.

Should any resident—goat, moss, or person—begin to forget, to soften, to drift, the plateau would feel it like a loosened stone in a dam. Perhaps then erosion would restart, cliffs would blunt, air would thicken, copper would finally green. Until that shudder, the inhabitants stand watch, each in their measured orbit, each a rivet in the ship of stone that sails forever on a tideless sky-sea.

That, then, is who lives here: lungs and hooves and shells and echoes, bound together by the single, adamant idea that the instant of the mountain’s rising can be prolonged into a world entire, provided every creature keeps perfectly, impeccably still—except for the soft, essential beating of the life inside it.
