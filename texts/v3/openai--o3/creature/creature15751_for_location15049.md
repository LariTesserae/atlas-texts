Whoever you expected to meet in the Altitudinal Abyss, you will not.  
No hoofprints score the mud, no wings cut the gloom. The plateau’s only unquestioned inhabitants are plants and water. Yet the place is not un-lived-in. Three kinds of presences occupy it, each drawing so little from the world that earlier surveyors wrote “fauna: none” and moved on.

1. The Kelp-Trees (alive in a way nobody anticipated)  
Stand long enough beside a trunk and you will hear the pulse in it: a syncopated thrum like distant machinery. That is not mere sap. Each tree houses a column of colloidal protoplasm that migrates seasonally up into the over-sea, then back down into the exposed meter of air. The column carries ions, stored memories of light, and—most surprisingly—signal.

Roots from different trees splice themselves together in the cracks underfoot, lacing the mesa into one vast rhizome-network. When a cataract crashes onto the stone frames and makes the net-drums boom, the kelp forest replies. Leaves flex, water jets through internal siphons, mosses shiver open. Over decades the trees have tuned themselves to the plateau’s ringing: they can distinguish direction, interval, even rhythm. Two rapid repetitions of a B-flat → rest → low E produce a coordinated opening of stomata all across the basin, a gesture rather like inhalation. Other patterns trigger spore release or mass withdrawal of fronds. Slowly—tree-slowly—they are composing answers to the plateau’s own seismic music. They talk in hydraulics and hold their parliament in the inch of breathing air we share with them.

2. The Lithosei—Stone Eaters that Never Leave the Rock  
Between thunderclaps you may hear a gritty crunching directly under your boots. The sound belongs to creatures slimmer than matchsticks, mineral worm-things that live embedded in the basalt shelves. Their flesh is chelated iron, their blood a trickle of brine drawn through micropores. They advance maybe a centimeter each year, headfirst, rasping quartz grains into carbohydrate analogues with enzymes borrowed from an ancestor that was once a lichen.

Lithosei need no sunlight and never penetrate the surface; that is why nobody has ever photographed one. Yet take a hammer to the rock face, split a piece just excavated by yesterday’s waterfall, and an ocher smear will vanish before your eyes—necrotizing on contact with oxygen. What matters is the tunnels they leave. Those capillaries amplify the plateau’s natural resonance until a single footstep can ring for kilometers. The Lithosei are, quite literally, carving the place into a better ear for the forest above.

3. The Absent Builders—Present Only as Acoustics  
The triangular frames and their taut kelp-nets are not abandoned architecture: they are inhabitants, fossils of intention still performing the behavior for which they were shaped. The makers—call them the High Tenders—left centuries ago when the over-sea thickened into its current paradox. No bones, no tools remain. What abides is the discipline they taught the plateau.

Each frame is an adjustable instrument. Lintels can be levered to new angles; cords can be re-braided by anyone with patience and plant-fiber. The High Tenders tuned these drums to the valley’s organ notes the way a sailor trims canvas to a wind. When the people vanished, the trees inherited the conversation. The drums strike; the forest answers; the stone transmits. An ecosystem that once included vertebrates now circulates their role as pure information.

So—what lives here?

1. Networks of kelp that use sound the way bats use echo, mapping and deciding inside a shared vascular mind.  
2. Sub-basalt grazers that sculpt the very acoustics the kelp require.  
3. A ghost species whose only surviving tissue is architecture, still flexing and booming in the dark.

Together they produce a composite organism: Plateau-Water-Plant-Stone. Remove any layer and the system stops breathing. The over-sea would sag, the cataracts would misfire, the forest would fall silent, and the mesa would become just another altitude where nothing lives.

But as long as water hangs and stone sings, life persists—invisibly, collectively, at a volume too low for hurried ears.
