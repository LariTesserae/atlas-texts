Who lives under the breathing ceiling is less a single species than a slow-knotted ecology of minds, bodies and manufactories that regard one another as obligate neighbors.  The people you would call “citizens” are certainly present—descendants of the surface engineers who first bored the caverns—but they are only one chord in the chord-stack that sounds here.

1. The Human Strains  

   a. Ring-Folk  
   • Appearance: Tall in the way vines are tall—long femurs, narrow shoulders, pale foam-coloured skin that blushes green where capillaries run close.  Generations of clinic-white daylight have bleached melanin almost away; instead, carotenoids from a vegetable-heavy diet tint the epidermis faintly chartreuse.  
   • Gait & dress: They walk flat-footed and soft-kneed to keep sound from ricocheting down corridors.  Tunics are felted from cellulose produced by utric broom pellets, laminated against the dryness with resin batched from the root exhalers.  Everyone owns an air-mask, worn guerrilla-style around the neck, half clothing, half courtesy: if your oxygen tab is paid up, you exhale through a micro-scrubber so the ceiling need not.  
   • Senses & manners: Hearing is prized above sight; children are taught “cavern cant”—a signing dialect performed with two fingers against one’s own sternum so the contact sound carries only inches.  They speak aloud only when machinery overpowers them.  
   • Society: Each concentric ring is its own cohort, like annual rings in a trunk.  The Core Rings (oldest) are labor guilds and archive houses.  Mid Rings are the manufactoria and dormitories.  The Newest Ring is still being bored; its residents are young families and audacious entrepreneurs who bet their fortunes on unclaimed wallface.  
   • Occupations:  
     - Woodwrights coax latticewood into bridges and doorframes by running shaped current through rebar scaffolds.  
     - Sap-surgeons travel with induction knives and copper catheters to relieve pressure boils in the ceiling.  
     - Lamplighters—really photoperiod engineers—reprogram the lenticular nodules so festivals can have dawns and dusks.

   b. Tunnelborn  
   A recessive minority conceived entirely underground.  Reduced parietal sinuses, enlarged tympanic membranes, and a tapetum-like sheen on the retina make them better adapted to the static daylight and echoing dark.  Surface cousins find their eyes unsettling—silver pupils ringed by jade—but here they are considered beautiful, like polished ore.

2. Co-Workers That Happen To Be Plants  

   a. Latticewood Proper  
   One organism, innumerable bodies.  Genetic analysis says every buttress vine is a clone graft of the same master stock implanted when the first excavations began.  Under strong EM fields the root crown generates spike potentials that look, to an oscilloscope, suspiciously like a slow Morse.  Woodwright apprentices swear the beams sometimes answer back.  Whether that counts as speech is a matter for symposiums that have lasted fifty years.  

   b. Photokeratin Moss Hives  
   Carpet the façades in ordered survey rectangles.  Each hive is an R&D lab breeding new pigments or growth inhibitors at the request of civil engineers.  Moss-keepers, usually stooped elders with quartz hand lenses, patrol on silent skates, scraping samples into crystal vials and tucking payment—condensed water beads—into the mats like tips left for a busker.

3. The Domestic Machines  

   a. Brass-Rod Trams  
   No true AI, just valve logic and good memory metal, yet crews treat them as shy animals.  You tap the control rod three times before boarding so the tram “knows” your gait and won’t jolt.  Children leave willow-resin candies on the cow-catcher; if they’re gone at shift’s end, everyone swears the tram enjoyed them.  

   b. Muscle-Cord Winches  
   Splice of myocyte tissue and kevlar braid, vat-grown to lift kiln doors.  They hum when idle, a subsonic purr felt more than heard.  Winch-whisperers keep them calm with rhythmic stroking of the sheath; a panicked cord can snap itself in two like a spooked horse.

4. Free-Roving Animals  

   a. Resin-Gnats  
   Six millimetres, bronze carapace, wings tuned to ultrasound.  They are janitors of the ceiling’s sap leaks; without them the place would smell like a wounded pine forest.  In courtship they choreograph in perfect cubes—tiny, living holodisplays.  Lovers borrow the pattern for wedding dances.  

   b. Utric Brooms  
   Bio-engineered from bladderwort stock.  Hexapodal, glossy as shellac.  They aren’t pets but something like municipal street sweepers.  When two meet they cross throat cilia, exchange the day’s mineral content, then swap directions without fuss—as if handing off gossip only they understand.  

   c. Glider-Lizards  
   Patience given wings.  Membranes veined with copper pigment that flashes when the ceiling-lamps shift to “dusk.”  Their only sound is the hiss of scale against air.  Folklore claims that seeing one launch above you means your birth-ring will vote in your favor at the next council.

5. The Quiet Guests  

   a. Aerospores  
   Part fungus, part sensor network.  They drift, settle, sample chemical traces, then lift again on convection, carrying micro-reports back to ceiling roots.  Miners test for them: if your headlamp shows no glittering spoor, you are beyond the plant’s sight line and must tread as though on thin ice.  

   b. Acoustic Mycelia  
   The “ears” of the city, woven through wall interstices.  They eat echo.  A scream will vanish a metre from your lips, absorbed into fungal labyrinths that convert sonic energy to faint bioluminescence—dim blue pulses people read like weather signs.  You do not curse the walls unless you want the walls to remember.

6. Intelligences Unsettled By Categories  

   The Breathing Ceiling itself—call it the Canopy, call it the Mother Root—is legally “infrastructure,” religiously “guardian,” biologically “plant.”  It decides where balconies bud, which abandoned corridor gets resealed with green cement, when to loosen the vents so newborn Tunnelborn get a waft of moist air on delivery day.  There is an elected Council that represents human interests to the Canopy’s caretakers, but everyone knows negotiation is mostly theater.  The ceiling is slow, and it is winning the long game of shaping the city to its comfort.  People simply adapt—gratefully, resentfully, often both at once.

7. What Outsiders Notice  

   • The hush, first—footsteps like drumbeats even when you tiptoe.  
   • Then the smell: stone dust braided with cold chlorophyll.  
   • Finally the gaze.  Every passerby looks at you the way one listens for a creak in timbers: politely, intently, measuring whether your presence will jostle the load-bearing rhythm of their world.

In sum, who lives here are cooperatives of flesh, chloroplast, piston and spore—organisms that have discovered the cheapest path to survival is to braid themselves into one another’s future.  Humans call it a city because that is the only word they have for a place full of addresses and obligations.  The ceiling, if it thinks at all, likely calls it a garden; and the moss, the trams, the gliders, the winches, all go on composing their small, tireless verses in the long, green poem of the underworld.
