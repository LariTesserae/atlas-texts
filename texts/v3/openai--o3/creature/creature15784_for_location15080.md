WHO LIVES HERE  

They call themselves the PELAGOTH—though the word you would actually hear is a slow pressure-throb against your ribs, a syllable made of current rather than sound. To others they are simply “the City-folk,” for they and the living architecture are an inseparable compound creature: the reef is their skeleton, they are the reef’s roaming blood.

1. BODY  

• Shape. From a distance each Pelagoth looks a little like a drifting banner. The trunk is a supple ribbon of muscle, two to three metres long, flattened side-to-side. Along both edges runs a continuous undulating fin that lets the animal hover, surge, or brake with hair-breadth control.  

• Skin. The hide is translucent, tea-brown like the water. Embedded just beneath it are thousands of thumbnail mirrors—fish scales, pearl chips, mica flakes—worked in over a lifetime. By day the mirrors hoard light; by night they shed it as a faint stipple, a personal constellation. No two patterns are alike: from them you can read a Pelagoth’s lineage, voyages, and favoured myths.  

• Head. Instead of a single face there is a circular “crown” of six flexible jaws, each edged with fine chitin rakers. The animal feeds by passing water through this revolving ring, combing out copepods, silted detritus, and the plankton breath of the reef. Between the jaws sit three lens-pits that register only the warmest colours; everything else they sense through pressure and electric field.  

• Armature. Four ribbon-arms grow from the mid-body. They are boneless, muscular, and end in a frill of chemical taste buds. For tasks that need a harder edge the Pelagoth simply straps on a living tool: a scallop shell for scraping, a razor clam for incising limestone, a pistol shrimp for stunning prey. Tools are shed, regrown, or replaced the way land animals change clothes.

2. GROWTH & REPRODUCTION  

A Pelagoth begins as a grain-small larva anchored inside a pumping corridor of the city. The wall feeds it, teaches it the house’s electrical grammar, and finally releases it when the youngster can hold a stable current-print—roughly five years. Adults are gender-fluid; once every thirty tides they hold a “dark bloom,” gathering by the thousands in the exhaust plazas and releasing slow clouds of gametes that mix on the upward drift. Fertilised eggs plaster themselves to whatever surface is near; thus the reef constantly seeds citizens into its own gaps.

Average life span: 160–200 years, long enough to see whole quarters crumble and be breathed back anew.

3. SENSE & SPEECH  

• Hearing is lateral-line listening: pressure ripples rather than vibrations in air. A shout becomes a squeeze of water around your lungs.  

• Language therefore happens in three modes:  
  1) Pulse-speech—bursts of contraction that travel through walls and can be “overheard” rooms away.  
  2) Mirror-writing—flashes from their embedded scale-mosaics; used for poetry, courting, and long-range signalling.  
  3) Path-telling—a tale traced as a particular swimming route through alleys whose barnacle patterns serve as punctuation. To read an epic you must follow the author’s exact spiral up twenty storeys, then dive through a pair of mushroom decks while turning your mirrors in the right rhythm.

4. WORK & EVERYDAY LIFE  

Nothing is owned outright; everything is stewarded for the reef. Still, individuals gravitate toward roles:

• Seeders scout the silt storms for promising detritus—a pottery shard, a whale vertebra—and wedge it in a likely nook so the masonry polyps can begin a new tower.  

• Gleaners patrol the streets with baskets woven from kelp fibre, gathering moulted plates, shed scales, and any corpse fragments not already claimed. They feed the harvest to the city’s grinding worms, which recalcify it as fresh mortar.  

• Lantern-herders culture colonies of cinnamon medusae in open courtyards, trimming them the way a topiarist shapes hedges so that their combined glow makes navigable avenues.  

• Story-counters keep the civic memory. With a fingernail shell they loosen barnacles whose growth rings have recorded great floods, shark incursions, or distant meteor showers (perceived as brief, impossible brightness from above). The rings become beads in necklaces of history.

5. VALUES  

Light is holy because it is scarce. A single new mirror scale is cause for ceremony; children duel playfully for the right to claim a shiny pebble fallen from a gull’s gut. Debt, favour, and marriage are all expressed in reflected brightness—if you owe someone greatly you swim beneath them so their mirror pattern can ride on your borrowed glow.

Second only to light is Stillness. Between heartbeats the whole basin seems to wait; the Pelagoth believe that perfect stillness is the moment the city listens to its own dreaming. To disrupt it needlessly is taboo. On entering a plaza one always pauses, expels a bubble, and lets the silence knit shut again.

6. RELATIONS WITH OTHER CREATURES  

• The Reef-Masons (polyps, worms, calcareous algae) are viewed as “Grandmothers”—vast, slow, mildly sentient. A Pelagoth never cuts living stone without first stroking it with its taste-arms; if the wall answers with a particular mineral flavour the cut is postponed for a year.  

• Giant saddle mantas, big as galleys, visit monthly to have parasites groomed away. For this service they carry Pelagoth caravans on migratory errands to distant coastlines and return with stories of sunrise colours no city dweller will ever see.  

• Surface dwellers—humans, birds, flotsam—are myths made real only when storms drop wreckage into the bowl. Anything unfamiliar is immediately tested as a possible seed for new construction or a new mirror to bribe the city’s night.

7. DANGERS & CONFLICT  

Predation is accepted as weather; a barracuda taking a child is mourned but not resented. True fear is reserved for Bleaches—rare intrusions of cold, nutrient-starved water that blanch the Grandmothers and stop their slow thinking. When a Bleach is sensed, every citizen becomes a courier, fanning warm silt back across the stricken facades, singing through pulse-speech to keep the masonry polyps awake.

8. RITUALS THAT MARK A LIFE  

• First Mirror: when an adolescent earns a scale bright enough to flash, they swim the “Spiral of Names,” announcing the mirror’s pattern to each level so that the city can remember them.  

• Tithe of Silence: adults spend one tide every month wedged motionless in a pumping shaft, lending their metabolic heat to the Grandmothers and listening for slow architectural dreams. Many claim to have heard plans for streets that will not be grown for another century.  

• Dissolution: the rare, honoured dead are not buried; they are pressed gently into an eroding buttress. Over decades the lime of their bones seeps outward and their embedded mirrors become the sparkle line that guides travellers into the new court that replaces the old.

9. IF YOU ARRIVE AS A VISITOR  

Expect to be tested for mirrors—any reflective surface may be nibbled loose unless you declare it a gift. Speak by holding your palms a handspan apart and pushing water between them in pulses; even a child will understand the basics. And do not rush: every street corner may be mid-sentence, every quiet may be part of a larger chord. Remember you stand inside a breathing elder, and the people who escort you are its wandering thoughts.
