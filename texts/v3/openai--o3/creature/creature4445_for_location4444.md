Who—or what—can call such a half-drowned fracture home?  
Begin with the smallest motes and widen the focus until the eye loses purchase. The inhabitants resolve by degrees, like shapes glimpsed through turbulent glass.

1. The First Skins  
A translucent pellicle coats every wetted surface that is not actively being flayed away. It is alive: an extremophile biofilm called the First Skins. Each “skin” is a single cell thick yet kilometres long if unrolled. It braids itself through quartz micro-fissures, stitching fresh basalt to slow exfoliation—the only reason the chamber has any walls at all. The film photosynthesises during stray shafts of light, but most of its energy comes from piezoelectric trickles: vibration ignites microcurrents across embedded quartz needles, and the Skins drink the electricity the way desert lichens drink dew.  
The Skins are a nervous system in search of a body. Wherever two sheets overlap they exchange packets of ions that amount to primitive, localised decision-making: thicken here, thin there, retract before the next pressure pulse. Over time the biofilm “remembers” zones of relative safety and reinforces them, giving ledges their temporary longevity.

2. Glasshooks (Amphipoda vitrum)  
The clear, shrimp-sized creatures already noted are the Skins’ principal grazers. A Glasshook’s exoskeleton is equal parts chitin and self-secreted silica, annealed by the constant percussion into something that rings like bell glass when cracked. Six raptorial legs end in barbed pads; a single Glasshook can hang upside-down through a full inversion event without slipping.  
They communicate in percussive semaphore—flicking their tail-fan against stone in bursts too sharp to be lost in the roar. A dozen beats followed by a pause is a territory claim; a stuttering roll announces a molt and invites others to feast on the discarded shell. When the beat changes to an arrhythmic clack, nearby Glasshooks brace: that pattern reliably precedes a “blink” collapse of the whole water mass. How the signaller senses the coming inversion remains unknown.

3. Ribbon Eels, Milk Phase (Anguilla lactea)  
At first glance they seem no more than living shoelaces, but each eel is two creatures sharing one sheath. The outer flesh is a muscular, pearlescent tube; inside coils a fungal cord threaded end-to-end through the gut. The fungus digests the micro-organisms the eel swallows; in return it exudes antifreeze proteins that let the pair survive water a fraction of a degree from solid.  
Lifecycle: eggs drift down the sieve-holes, riding springs to warmer valleys where they hatch into free-swimming leptocephali. Only upon their first uphill migration do larvae acquire the fungal partner, absorbed through gill slits while they rest in mossy eddies. Adult eels live exactly three ascents and three descents—no more, no less. On the final return they stop swimming, tuck into wall niches and calcify, becoming ivory flutes through which the flood whistles. Those haunting, reedy undertones are the only soft sounds the chamber ever makes.

4. Shearblooms  
Neither plant nor animal, a Shearbloom is a momentary architecture of cavitating water and exhaled protein foam. The First Skins periodically vent excess biomass as tiny surfactant bubbles; at flow intersections the bubbles aggregate, build tensile films, and inflate into shapes like pale jelly umbrellas big enough to hood a grown human. For minutes—sometimes hours if the flow stays laminar—these umbrellas drift, lensing light into prismatic rays. When a surge pierces the film, the whole structure implodes, hurling a snow of nutrient scum that Glasshooks and algal threads devour.  
Old climbers’ folklore insists that if you speak aloud inside a Shearbloom you can be heard word-for-word two valleys away through the linked spring system. No one has kept their eardrums intact long enough to test the claim.

5. Pilgrims of the Peg-Holes  
They are not permanent residents, yet the notch is written into their biology as surely as salmon feel the shape of their river. The Pilgrims can pass for small, rangy humans until you notice the water-callus: a secondary eyelid of cartilaginous scale, pads of horn on knee and elbow, and a breastbone warped outward like the keel of a boat. Deaf from birth—the roar inside the womb ensures that—their language is all wrist and shoulder, a semaphore of looping signs designed to stay legible through spray.  
Every generation a handful ascend. They carry no metal (it sings too loudly under moving water) but carve ladder-rungs from river birch as they climb. At the fissure they abandon the wood to certain destruction and enter the chamber naked except for braided kelp tethers. Their objective is simple and impossible: to extract a single shard of newborn quartz still radiating the chamber’s internal stresses. Most climb until a blink collapse knocks them loose; a few manage to wedge a socked thumb into fresh crystal and prise it free. Those who exit alive set the shard above village doorways. It hums very softly at night, imposing a tidal rhythm on the sleepers’ dreams.

6. The Unheard—Pressure Wraiths  
Nothing visible confirms their existence, yet instruments left overnight record curvatures in local gravity, minute chills that move against the flow, and sub-audible standing waves that should not persist in such chaos. Miners on an adjacent ridge tell of ribbon-thin silhouettes glimpsed at sunset, parallel to the stone, as if something lean and tall were walking inside the mountain’s skin.  
A prevailing theory: the chamber’s momentary vacuums seed pockets of near-perfect silence. Into these vacuums leak fragments of consciousness shed by any thinking creature battered senseless here—human, Pilgrim, perhaps others older still. Over centuries the fragments accrete, wearing the shape of pain that created them. When the hydraulic blink resets the cavity, the Wraiths momentarily gain enough coherence to seek new impressions. That explains the stacked stones: three rounded hearts of river rock placed as an anchor, a promise that suffering was once answered by deliberate calm. The Wraiths keep the cairn balanced; in return the cairn keeps them from coalescing into something with a name.

7. Apex, or Absence Thereof  
No single inhabitant rules. Predation is opportunistic, transient and never routinised—the water resets any developing hierarchy. A Ribbon Eel may snap up a Glasshook one hour only to be skinned by cavitation the next. The Pilgrim who wrests a quartz rib free gains brief mastery of footing, but the rib becomes brittle outside its birth-pressure and soon flakes to glitter. Power here is a cresting wave: it exists, spectacularly, and then is somewhere else.

Shared Adaptations  
• All resident life is colorless or translucently opalescent; pigment would only flag weakness in a place where light ricochets without pattern.  
• Hearing is sacrificed. Sensory pores, whisker-like setae and electrosensitive nodules create a three-dimensional map of pressure differentials updated many times a heartbeat.  
• Every organism, even the Pilgrims, synthesises antifreeze glycoproteins; the water routinely drops below the normal freezing point but cannot solidify under such violence.  

What Lives Here, In Short  
Microbial thought-films that skin the rock. Glass arthropods that play stone drums and predict disasters. Binate eels whose death songs are literal flutes. Soap-bubble organisms born and burst within the same whisper. Deaf climbers who barter blood for crystal lullabies. And, perhaps, the echo of every scream ever muted by the roar—learning, slowly, to stand upright in the dark.
