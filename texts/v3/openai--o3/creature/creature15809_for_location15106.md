Who (and what) lives in the Basin of Hollow Calls

1. The Ridge People – Children of the Perpetual Drone  
Appearance  
• Height: 60–75 cm when standing straight, though a permanent crouch keeps the crown of the head scarcely above a human’s knee.  
• Skin: a pebble-textured hide the colour of wet slate; individual scales are actually enlarged epidermal cells saturated with silica, lending the whole body a dull shimmer. The skin beads water on contact and sheds it in sheets during vigorous shaking.  
• Head: wide, almost manta-shaped. No external nose, only two vertical slits tucked behind the jaw hinge. Eyes are reduced—silver lenticels like halved pearls that see only luminosity and motion. What dominates the skull is the tympanic crest: a sail of cartilage, ridged like a bat wing, that begins between the shoulder blades and overarches the crown before ending in a forked “fin” above the brow. The crest is a living tympanum; every gust, boom, or footstep around the wearer becomes a ripple of pressure across that sail.  
• Hands: two fingers and an opposable “thumb-hook.” Joints articulate in any direction; a ridge person can brace a hand flat on rock, then lift its entire body sideways without shifting that palm.  

Lifecycle  
Eggs—each the size of a clenched fist—are glued to the undersides of naturally sonorous arches. The hum of passing wind vibrates the embryos continuously; without the droning massage they fail to develop neural synchrony and hatch deformed or mute. At three months the young wriggle free, drop into the flood film, and follow the loudest sound they can locate—usually the communal nesting mounds. Adolescence passes in six Basin years (≈18 standard months). Lifespan: 30–35 Basin years.

Senses & Mind  
The auditory cortex occupies half the brain. Ridge people can map a landscape by echo alone, identifying individual bellow-worms by timbre from kilometres away. Vision is supplementary—useful on rare clear days or inside the orange glow of thermal vents. Their spoken language is not so much “spoken” as oscillated: throat sacs inflate until they resemble miniature bellows; air is pulsed in modulated bursts that carry through stone as much as air. A ridge person can whisper to another by placing palms on the same rock slab and fluttering the finger pads.

Culture  
• Shelter: overlapping plates of collected bellow-worm cartilage, clamped together with chitin pins. Fungus fibre swells when wet, closing every seam; when the clouds briefly part, the huts loosen to vent moisture.  
• Industry: resonance-crafting. They quarry thin sheets of the local granite, then gnaw vein-lines through it with fungus acid, tuning each sheet like a tongue-drum. Such plates become cookware, incubators, even surgery tables: vibration heats, stuns, and sterilises.  
• Foodways: 70 % of calories arrive as skitter-prawns. The rest is cartilage marrow, glass-eel fat, and distilled fungal liquor that tastes of cold pepper. A single standing arch makes an excellent still—liquids placed at the node of two crossing harmonics separate into clear distillate and thick fungal lees within hours.  
• Art & Lore: stories are sculpted into multi-note motifs rather than recited. A traveller returning from afar may strike a slab three times at pitches 318 Hz, 245 Hz, 318 Hz: every adult instantly understands “storm-silence-storm,” shorthand for the year’s eerie quieting of the flutes. The Basin’s epic—the Symphony of First Water—spans six nights and is “played” on a ring of wind hollows while the audience lies belly-down, ears pressed to the stone.  
• Taboos: muting the Basin is sacrilege. Plugging more than half the holes in a natural arch is punishable by exile.

2. Bellow-Worms – The Moving Organs  
Physiology  
Imagine a two-metre fire hose of meat, each annulus wrapped in a collar of calcified cartilage. Within every collar lies a resonant cavity; contracting muscles squeeze air along a spiral trachea, forcing it through slots in each ring and producing the Basin’s famously layered roar. Colours run charcoal to chalk, forming irregular banding that camouflages the worm among salt-lined ripples.

Feeding & Behavior  
Bellow-worms do not bite; they stun. A pod (20–40 individuals) will circle a shallow pan, synchronising their pulses until a thunderclap rolls through the film like an invisible hammer. Skitter-prawns float to the surface, twitchless, and are vacuumed up by the worms’ ciliated gullets. After feeding they slither onto the driest swell available, basking in the tremor-heat of arch music to aid digestion.

Reproduction  
Pods merge at the season of Bright Mist (when the nocturnal cloud-glow peaks). Two worms entwine head-to-tail; their tracheae align and the combined air-column booms a pure subsonic note that resonates inside the female’s body, liquefying a mantle of nutrient tissue around already-fertilised ova. She then extrudes this living custard into the shallowest pan she can find. Ridge people hear the sub-bass mating note from tens of miles away and mark their calendars by it.

Ecological Role  
1. Predator and inadvertent farmer: their shockwaves aerate the pans, promoting fungal bloom.  
2. Mobile quarry: after death, their cartilage rings are scavenged by nearly every tool-using species in the Basin—chiefly the ridge people, who treat a fresh carcass like a wholly furnished hardware store.  

3. Skitter-Prawns – The Hissing Surf  
Morphology  
Six legs, five eyes, zero pigment until maturity. At the final moult they develop a nacreous sheen that refracts the pewter light into soft rainbows—a side-effect of thickening the exoskeleton against sonic predation. Gills double as million-bristle combs that rake the fungal film, sifting out nutritious nodules.

Behavior  
When barometric pressure falls, the surface tension of the pans slackens a fraction. Sensing this, whole prawn hordes pinch off bubbles beneath their carapaces and launch themselves as self-contained hydrofoils, achieving brief, buzzing flights over the mirror water. The mass take-off sounds like applause shuffled through gravel.

Lifecycle & Economics  
Life expectancy: nine months. In that time an adult female lays 10,000 eggs, most devoured by glass eels or her own kin. Ridge people harvest the yearly megaswarms by stretching eel-gut mesh across “boom frames”—rectangles of cartilage that they plant in front of an oncoming pod of bellow-worms. The shockwave drives prawns straight into the net, already tenderised.

4. Glass Eels – Ghosts Beneath the Salt Skin  
Physiology  
Entirely translucent save a silver thread of spinal cord, these eels spend 95 % of their time inside liquid-suspended silt. Their skin exudes an anticoagulant mucus that turns any cut of meat placed in it into a gelatinous slurry—one of the few natural tenderisers the ridge people cannot yet replicate by sound alone.

Survival Trick  
When the atmospheric pressure plummets before a nasty squall, water in their vascular system boils at body temperature. To keep from embolising, glass eels surface en masse to gulp air, forcing the bubbles into reinforced swim bladders. Two hours later they burp it out, deflate, and dive.

5. The Unsilent Mold – Mother of Lichen Lace  
Technically not mold at all, but a nested colony of cyanobacteria, extremophile yeast, and silica-loving mycelia. Each strand etches micro-flutes into the stone as it expands; wind across these pores produces whispers far too high for ridge-person hearing but easily tracked by skitter-prawn predators. Thus the very act of feeding upon the mold writes fresh music into the Basin—life engraving its own dinner bell.

6. Things Only the Old Cloud Sees  
a. Pressure Shades  
Not organisms in the biological sense. They are standing waves of air density that form where two arch-songs cancel. Walk through a node and you feel an instant, localised silence; hair lifts, stomach drops. Ridge children dare one another to sprint across them at dusk, emerging with voices temporarily pitched an octave lower due to cochlear fatigue.  
b. The Lonely Note  
Once a decade a single natural flute faults—perhaps a stone pin snaps, perhaps a portion dissolves—changing its pitch so that no other arch harmonises with it. For several seasons the Lonely Note wanders, Dopplering in and out of existence as winds shift. Animals track it uneasily; ridge elders speak of previous Lonely Notes that coincided with meteor falls, stranger tides, or the burying of an ancestral queen. Some call it an omen, others the Basin’s way of tuning itself.

Relations and Interdependencies  

1. Ridge People ↔ Bellow-Worms  
Symbiosis verging on domestication. Pods ignore ridge foragers who approach from a respectful angle—bent low, crest flattened, emitting a soft 95 Hz rumble taken to mean “kin.” In return the ridge people clear fungal mats from the worms’ favourite basking swells, preventing fatal overgrowth in their spiracles.

2. Bellow-Worms ↔ Skitter-Prawns  
Predation keeps prawn numbers in check; without it, the prawns would strip the fungal mat in a single season.

3. Skitter-Prawns ↔ Unsilent Mold  
The prawns’ grazing would annihilate the mold but for the salts dissolved in their urine, which feed a new wave of bacterial growth. A prawn swarm leaves behind concentric halos of richer colour that enlarge the feeding ground for the next generation.

4. Ridge People ↔ Everything Else  
The ridge folk take only what the Basin replaces quickly: prawns, worm cartilage, eel fat. They speak of the “Third Silence,” a prophesied moment when the arches fall quiet forever; till then, every harvest is an act of stewardship meant to ensure the choir continues.

Rumours Carried by the Wind  
• A clan far to windward claims to have bred miniature bellow-worms as living musical instruments—lap-sized pets that purr lullabies.  
• Somewhere under the deepest mirror pan lies a cavern where drainage thunder turns to literal heat; survivors tell of entering misty tunnels and finding stones sweating liquid fire.  
• The oldest arch, so wide a hundred ridge folk can lie head-to-toe within its shadow, has drooped a hand-span in living memory. Mathematic-priests calculate it will touch the ground in 40 Basin years; no one agrees on what new chord that impact will strike.

To Visit or to Dwell?  
For an outsider, the Basin is claustrophobic despite its openness: light diluted, air always vibrating, sleep rationed in snatches between gusts. But for the beings born here, the ceaseless roar is as warming as sunlight. A ridge mother crooning over her egg-clutch, a bellow-worm basking in the chorus of its own kin, a sheet of fungal lace writing micro-hymns into the rock—each is perfectly at home. The Basin of Hollow Calls is not merely a place they inhabit; it is the instrument they have become, and every heartbeat, scrape, or whisper is one more note in its unending score.
