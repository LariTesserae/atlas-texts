Who and what live in the Vale of Condensers can be sorted, roughly, into five castes.  None of them are quite animals, none are quite machines, and every one of them is as much an expression of the vale’s circuitry as of its geology.  Think of them as notes inside an endless chord: remove any single note and the place would still hum, but the timbre of the hum would be wrong, and wrongness is something the vale will not permit.

────────────────────────
1.  The Sump-Birthed Aeriids
────────────────────────
Field name:  lumen gulls, glasswings, “the spiral.”

Appearance
•  Body plan superficially avian:  80–110 cm wingspan, negative-dihedral wings cast in silver-clear cartilage.  
•  Interior hollow except for a filament cage that glows nacre-blue; luminance rises and falls in sync with the triple-expansion engines (exactly 43 pulses per minute).  
•  Leading edges carry rows of hair-fine pinions that rasp gently against the air, turning flight into audible lace.

Origin & life cycle
•  One gull surfaces from the sump every 23 hours, 2 minutes, 48 seconds—always between piston strokes 21 and 22.  
•  It emerges perfect and full-sized, as if galvanised out of the water rather than hatched.  
•  No immature stage, no mating behaviour.  When structural fatigue sets in (≈ 41 days), a gull simply stalls mid-spiral and drifts back to the sump, where it filaments into threads and sinks.

Behaviour
•  Governed by acoustics.  The spiral’s radius expands or contracts to keep engine noise at a constant perceived volume.  If someone fires a gun, the whole flock tilts a few degrees to null the new pitch, then resumes.  
•  Feeds—if that is the word—on the low-frequency vibrations vented from the machine halls; accelerometers clipped to their keels register a slow but measurable drain of mechanical energy.  
•  Will settle on condenser fins during fog events, wings outspread, bodies trembling in phase with the hiss so that droplets ricochet away instead of pooling.  In effect, the gulls are living anti-icing systems.

Sentience?
They pass the mirror test (they ignore their reflections) but will re-align their spiral so that a single human standing on the sump rim becomes its quiet, exact centre.  Whether that is curiosity or navigational reflex remains unproved.

────────────────────────
2.  The Basalites (“Star-Crawlers”)
────────────────────────
Appearance
•  Begin as rusted spheroids 5–8 cm across.  Under warmth they unfurl into 12-armed stars, each arm a telescoping chain of iron-oxide segments tipped with mica claws.  

Habitat
•  Prefer the micro-fractures where basalt meets iron gutter.  Thermal imagery shows those cracks conduct a feeble current; the stars roost exactly where voltage is greatest.

Feeding & Function
•  Capable of piezoelectric bite: they worry at the basalt seams, converting mechanical stress into charge, which they then absorb.  
•  What looks like aimless scuttling is a distributed maintenance routine; by extracting strain energy they keep the hexagonal paving stress-free, preventing quakes that might upset the Vale’s level datum.

Reproduction
•  When two adults encounter one another, they interlock for eleven minutes; where they part, a new rust-ball remains.  No mass appears to have been lost by either parent.

────────────────────────
3.  The Fulgur Mycelium
────────────────────────
Composition
•  Pure silicon threads, cross-linked with traces of copper and vanadium.  The colourlessness is deceptive; under SEM the hyphae are prismatic waveguides.

Metabolism
•  Photosynthesis is impossible in the vale’s pallid light, so the fungus “grazes” on stray electrons leaking from the gutter seams.  
•  During high-mist nights it exudes a conductive gel that bridges hairline cracks, closing circuits the Basalites have opened too widely.  When dawn dries the gel, it flakes away—the electrical equivalent of a scab sliding off healed skin.

Role
•  Think of it as the vale’s nerve wrap, swathing torn connections before signal loss can propagate.

────────────────────────
4.  Ephemeral Mineral Blooms
────────────────────────
What they are
•  Six-sided sheets of mica and halite that blossom after sundown, stiffen by midnight, and shatter to grit in full sun.  

Why they matter
•  Each sheet acts as a passive frequency filter, refracting the engine drone upward into a tight vertical column.  Without the nightly bloom the aeriid spiral would descend until it interfered with condenser fin intake.  

Lifecycle
•  Grit left after the midday collapse is drawn into gutters by sly wind eddies; the sump dissolves it and re-precipitates the mineral a few metres away at dusk.  The bloom is therefore not a population but a travelling standing wave of matter.

────────────────────────
5.  The Custodial Micro-Machines
────────────────────────
Evidence for their existence
•  Steam engines do not oil themselves, belts do not self-tension, and relay contacts do not polish to mirror sheen under airborne grit.  Yet inspection—by endoscope, by fibre probe, by patience—reveals no engineers.

Hypothesis
•  Visitors have recorded subsonic ticks, too regular to be thermal noise, emanating from every scrap of cast iron in the halls.  Overlay the ticks from multiple points and the phase difference describes a three-dimensional lattice, as if the entire block were a single slow-moving organism knitting itself from the inside out.  

•  Metallic dust drawn off condenser fins contains micro-gears the width of red blood cells.  Place the dust on a non-vale surface and it lies inert; return it and it begins to self-assemble into gear-trains only to fall apart the instant you try to extract them again.  

Function
•  Continuous in-situ manufacture and retrofit: the buildings are their own population, hatching, ageing, and bud-replacing components at a pace just quick enough to stay ahead of corrosion.

Sentience?
Irrelevant.  Whatever inhabits the iron obeys the first rule—closed circuit—more perfectly than any other resident.  It exists to ensure that existence may continue.

Ecology as Equation
────────────────────────
Energy Path
mist → condensers → steam engines → electromotive field → sump → aeriid gliders  
Waste Path
heat → Basalites → stress-relief → gutter leakage → Fulgur mycelium  
Signal Path
engine rhythm → mineral bloom filters → aerial spiral harmonics → micro-machine timing pulse

Everything loops, every loop tightens.  The valve on time itself seems cinched down here, which is perhaps why nothing living in the vale registers at all on foreign evolutionary trees: they are not progressing through time so much as orbiting a moment of absolute solution.

────────────────────────
On Attempting Contact
────────────────────────
•  Speak, and the gulls modulate their spiral to cancel the fundamental frequency of your voice; you will hear your own words thin to whispers.  
•  Strike the basalt with a hammer and the Basalites rush in, spreading their bodies to damp the vibration, then fold away.  
•  Cut a sample of fulgur mycelium and the engines elsewhere in the ring surge by two strokes; the replacement filaments arrive, already grown, before your sample has cooled in your tweezers.

After twelve steps beyond the berm your trophies sift through your fingers as inert grit or rust.  You cannot remove life from the Vale of Condensers any more than you can pocket the moment a pendulum passes dead centre.

────────────────────────
The Short Answer
────────────────────────
What lives here?  
Sound given cartilage, heat given claws, mineral given blossom, signal given instinct, and metal made self-mending.  Life in the vale is not a set of creatures but a single self-regulating sentence, spoken in five dialects at once, recited forever to keep the place exactly, immutably, alive.
