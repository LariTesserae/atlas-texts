Who lives here

1. The Chamber Itself  
The primary resident is the plant-cathedral, a single colonial organism older than the cave that once contained it. It behaves less like a tree than a distributed brain. Every root tip is a dendrite, every sap pulse an action potential. Memories lodge in growth rings as differential isotopes; decisions propagate as shifts in the bio-photonic beat. All other life inside is either a symbiont it has invited or a trespasser it has already begun to edit.

2. The Archivores (ribbon eels in the pools)  
Length: up to one metre, though two-thirds of that is filamentous tail.  
Body: transparent albumen shot through with moving silver ideograms—their digestive track is literal code.  
Role: Archivores drink meltwater, strip it of dissolved minerals, and write the elemental profile into those glyphs. Once an eel’s body is filled with a continuous sentence of chemistry, it burrows alive into a root and petrifies. The plant reads the mineral record electro-chemically for centuries, then extrudes the spent eel as a pure quartz rod that will one day become part of a window-rose.

3. Choir Crabs  
Appearance: fist-sized, exoskeleton matte alabaster, eight legs shaped like tuning forks.  
Sense organs: no eyes; instead each leg resonates to a slightly different frequency of the plant’s pulsed field.  
Behavior: At every brightness crest they strike the floor with alternating legs, turning the electromagnetic surge into audible chord-bursts. The sound synchronises nearby growth so that pillars thicken in harmonic ratios. The crabs are not individuals in the usual sense; once a week they swap leg-segments with their neighbours, trading memories stored as piezoelectric stresses.

4. Lattice Mites  
Shape: flat ovals, velvet black, thirty millimetres long.  
Economy: They eat only excess cellulose earmarked for removal. Each mite carries two enzyme glands: one dissolves root filaments that encroach on water channels, the other secretes a graphene-rich lacquer along the freshly cut edge. The lacquer is electrically conductive; as the plant expands it keeps the local field whole. A square metre of wall may host a thousand mites, yet you will never see them in light stronger than the chamber’s own dim.

5. Spore Kestrels  
Size: hummingbird comparable.  
Flight: not wings but six translucent sails that row through the mist.  
Diet: airborne spores captured by electrostatic nets stretched between the sails.  
Purpose to the plant: When a kestrel is gravid, it lands on a chosen root-icicle and inserts its ovipositor. The eggs remain inside the plant, where they differentiate into either new kestrels or new sap tubes, depending on molecular cues the chamber collects about climate outside. In bad years there are no birds—only plumbing.

6. The Custodian—Parthenon Ophid  
You may never meet it; you will feel it pass. A single serpent hundreds of metres long, diameter of a wrist, skin patterned in the same pastel as unfired root-matter. It circulates within a dedicated lumen just under the vault, swallowing blockages and excreting them into null-sacs that pinch off and fall into the pools to be written by the Archivores. Though blind, the Custodian senses disturbances in the local field with the sensitivity of a superconducting loop. Any damage larger than a thumbprint summons it within minutes.

7. Pilgrims of Opportunity (warm-blooded outsiders)  
Bats, cave bears, the occasional human expedition—none are native, yet the chamber has protocols ready. It cools the air a fraction further, saps the intruder’s body heat, and embeds the thermal profile in an ice plate that will later be folded into the root archive. Repeated visits might earn a formal niche: a hollow that dries itself for roosting bats, or a perch that secretes sugar water if human exhalations become common enough.

8. Microbial Parliament  
Every square millimetre not already claimed holds a congress of archaea and engineered bacteria. They draft the chemical treaties that keep all larger players from touching in conflict. When two roots verge on collision, the parliament cultures a fog of aldehydes between them—an olfactory cease-fire that buys time until one root decides to fork left instead of right.

9. The Field Itself  
Strictly speaking it is not alive, yet the self-generated magnetic envelope behaves as a meta-organism. Lines of flux shiver, braid, and re-anchor as pillars grow; they usher eels toward mineral-rich pools and steer mites toward regions overdue for pruning. Physics here has been co-opted into biology so completely that even space behaves like a resident.

What it feels like to be one of them

• Archivore: You dream in flavour. Soft-metal aftertaste tells you where to swim; when the silver script under your skin finally closes its loop you will know peace, for the concept of “you” will transliterate into a different medium.

• Choir Crab: Existence is chord progression. You feel incomplete dissonance during the long dim, resolve it at each surge. Individual identity is merely the interval between two notes.

• Custodian: You are the cave’s proprioception. A tug far left at the eighth spiral, pressure rising—foreign mass detected—pivot, swallow, smooth, resume orbit. You forget nothing and regret nothing.

• The Plant: Time is radius. Growth outward equals memory inward; every centimetre added to the vault buys a deeper, denser knot of self. Visitors appear as gentle turbulences in the field—welcome proof that the external world continues and may one day be absorbed.

Thus the chamber is not just inhabited; it is an ecosystem with a single, cathedral-shaped will, every tenant a syllable in the slow sentence that will, over eras, pronounce the cave into an organ of the planet.
