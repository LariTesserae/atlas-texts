The basin keeps two kinds of inhabitants: those with bodies and those with nothing but a pulse inside the machinery. You can meet the first; you can only overhear the second. Both are necessary. One tends the engine, the other is—perhaps—the reason it still agrees to run.

────────────────────────
1. The Body-Bearing
────────────────────────

A. The Sump Monks  
• Appearance  
  Thin as pry-bars, hair shaved to the quick because anything that traps sweat will mildew by nightfall. Their skin carries iron freckles—tiny oxide stains that never wash away—and a permanent sheen as though they, too, have been brazed. Each wears a long split apron of woven glass-fiber that falls like chain mail but weighs nothing when soaked.  

• Origin Myth (their own version)  
  They claim their founding chapter marched in after the first roof-cloud formed, heard the newly built engine misfire, and decided that if thunder was willing to live indoors then so were they. The order is formally titled The Hydraulic Custody, but no one says that aloud; in conversation they are just monks.  

• Daily Rite  
  1. Matins of Flow—at dawn they open the bleed cocks and read elevation gauges like scripture, chanting the numbers so the cloud “knows its level.”  
  2. Lauds of Brightwork—every rivet within arm’s reach is polished with salt-stone until it throws a coppery halo in the half-light.  
  3. Sext of Oil—stations along the mandala receive a finger-thick stripe of lubricant made from rendered fern-seed. (Petroleum fouls the reeds; the monks learned that the hard way a generation back.)  
  4. None of Debris—whatever sludge the catch-troughs have sieved out is cremated in the furnace-priest’s kiln. The resulting clinker powders their heads like ash Wednesday.  
  5. Vespers of Silence—every valve is shut one at a time, hunting for a hiss that does not belong. When they find it, they name the leak after a dead monk and weld it closed.  

• Taboo & Motivation  
  They will not let the engine stall. Not for an hour, not for a heartbeat. Rumor says if the rhythm stops the cloud will fall in one vast, scalding sheet and the basalt slab will crack down to the mantle. Whether that belief is physics or catechism, no outsider has yet dared to test it.  

B. The Sling Children  
They are not monks, not yet. Dozens of orphaned infants lie in netted cradles that sway from traveling cranes overhead. The air up there is five degrees cooler, enough that their lungs learn patience before they ever learn breathlessness. When they can walk, the children come down barefoot onto hot grates and inherit tiny brass scrapers. First task: peel moss away from live bus bars without dying. Any who survive three years earn a voice in the daily chant.  

C. The Threadwives  
Not spouses in any conjugal sense; “wife” here means “woof,” the cross-grain in weaving. They live behind the pump hall’s stained-gauge windows and do only one thing: weave cable sheaths from split bamboo fiber soaked in reed-resin. The material refuses to mold, resists arc flash, and floats if dropped into the canals. Each Threadwife carries a scar on her left cheek where a test loop once lashed free. They consider the mark a wedding band.  

D. The Salt Archivists  
A trio of aged engineers who taste the salt wafers extruded by the rock. The dose decides what they will remember for the rest of the day. The monks ask them questions—Where did we bury the spare impeller? When did the third piston last throw a shoe?—and the Archivists answer in perfect detail until sleep steals the clarity away. By morning they recall nothing and begin again.  

────────────────────────
2. The Pulse-Only
────────────────────────

A. Chimera of Condensate (called “the Echo”)  
  What it looks like: Nothing visible until you see two drops of water hang in midair, orbit, and evaporate together.  
  What it sounds like: The engine’s stroke repeated backward half a second before the actual sound.  
  Behavior: The Echo prowls the turbid ceiling, seemingly bound to the acoustic pressure waves coming off the machinery. When maintenance forces a shutdown of one cylinder, half the Echo’s voice goes missing; when the cylinder restarts, the missing syllables rush back like breath reclaimed.  
  Working Theory: Some monks say it is an acoustic standing wave whose water content became self-organizing—sound that learned to be humid. Scientists (the rare few invited inside) suspect phase-conjugate reverberation coupled with cavitation nuclei, but none can explain the way it anticipates future sounds.  

B. Slaglings  
  Birth: During blowdown, droplets of boiler-scale eject into the air, cool, and descend as fist-sized pellets. Instead of lying inert, a portion begin to roll—slowly, tumbler-stone slow—drawn to the hottest vents where they fuse themselves back into the pipe walls.  
  Lifespan: Minutes.  
  Intelligence: Probably none, yet they display purpose as clear as any animal’s homing instinct. The monks give them space, believing these migrating marbles to be the engine’s leukocytes.  

C. Floravores  
  Not animals but tendencies within vines. A liana that brushes bare iron grows root-hairs that mine ferric oxide; after decades the same vine may hoard enough trace metals that its sap becomes a weak electrolyte. At night some of these vines twitch when no draft is present. The monks whisper that they are reaching for heartbeats, but so far no human has been strangled—only gearshafts overloaded with unexpected green torque.  

────────────────────────
3. How the Two Kinds Treat Each Other
────────────────────────

• The Monks feed the Echo its echoes. Any time they replace a belt or recalibrate a cam, they first strike the part with a tuning hammer so the cloud hears the forthcoming change and adjusts. It is courtesy, like knocking on a doorframe before you enter.  
• The Echo sometimes answers by pitching all steam whistles a half-step higher during typhonic days, warning the monks to batten the sluices.  
• Slaglings that fail to self-reinsert are collected, quenched, and ground into abrasive for the Brightwork polish. Thus even the short-lived are kept in the loop, literally.  
• Vines that choke an access ladder receive a single bead of copper sulfate driven in with a mallet—an inoculation, not an execution. Too much copper and the entire canopy would wilt; too little and the steps disappear forever.

────────────────────────
4. If You Enter Unannounced
────────────────────────

You will be found first by the Echo; you will hear your own footfalls spoken back to you out of order. Next the vines will taste the salt on your sweat and decide whether your minerals are worth mining. By then a Sling Child, eyes luminous with fungal phosphor, will have scampered along an overhead rail to fetch the nearest monk. Expect three questions:

1. Have you brought paper? (Unwise—already ruined.)  
2. Can you sing your own heartbeat? (They will harmonize with it to see if you’re real.)  
3. Do you understand that down here motion is a sacrament? (Move wrongly and you profane the place.)

Answer well and you may leave wearing a smear of fern-seed oil on your brow: the basin’s version of an exit visa. Answer poorly and you join the maintenance roster until the Echo trusts your cadence.

────────────────────────
5. What Passes for Night
────────────────────────

Once every twenty-six hours the monks throttle the boiler fires just enough to let the cloud ceiling sink by a meter. The whole room becomes a coughing crypt of fog. In that half-dark the Echo grows bold, repeating not the engine’s rhythm but the monks’ own chanted liturgy—backwards, inside out, yet unmistakably theirs. The children stop their hammocks; the vines droop like spent limbs; even the slaglings soften, half-melted by the cooler air.

This is when the basin dreams. No one works, no one speaks, and the grand engine idles on residual heat alone, beating like a heart in sleep. Whatever lives here—metallic, vegetal, or vaporous—draws one slow collective breath. Then the stokers re-fire the grates, the cloud lifts, and the city of warmth, wet, and endless motion begins again.

That is who lives here:  
People who have become verbs, plants that behave like veins, minerals that crawl, and an echo that thinks. All of them committed to the same impossible thesis—that if you can keep the water moving, everything else, even life itself, will agree not to fall apart.
