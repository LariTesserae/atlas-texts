Who lives in the thumb-sized pocket is not a single species but an orchestra of mineral organisms, each one engineered by blind geology to play a specific part in the chamber’s never-ending industrial fugue. Seen in slow time they resemble machines; seen in still time they resemble minerals; only when you speed the clock do they look convincingly alive.

1. Glass-gnawers  
Shape and size: a flattened centipede the length of a cigarette, built from overlapping plates of smoky quartz. Each segment ends in a lip of curved grater-teeth; under magnification those teeth resolve into microscopic saw blades grown from cristobalite.  
Mouth and gut: the mouth is a tunnel that spirals through the torso like rifling in a gun barrel. Shards go in raw, are liquefied by induction heat from a coil-organ that rings the gut, and are extruded out the rear as flawless wedges of fresh plate—continual body maintenance through consumption.  
Locomotion: two hundred legs articulate on sapphire ball-joints and take exactly one step every eight seconds, timed to the pulse of the heat-leeches. The animal never lifts more than three legs at once; instead it negotiates with its own friction, partially melting the contact points so the foot can slide rather than lift.  
Reproduction: when internal quartz reserves overflow the gnawer secretes a nacreous bead, then abandons it. Heat-leeches later detonate the bead with a shock pulse, fracturing it into a brood of pin-sized nymphs already hungry for glass dust.

2. Slag-heavers  
Shape and size: a low dome of magnetite tiles, forty centimetres across, twelve kilos of walking ore. The shell is jointed like an iris; at its centre sits a throat furnace that never fully closes, glowing dull orange.  
Metabolism: the animal hoovers semi-molten drips, pressurises them with a peristaltic ring of hematite pistons, and then coughs out bars of nearly pure nickel-iron. Each bar is scored along one axis so it will fracture cleanly if later residents need smaller blanks.  
Behavioural logic: every third ingot is stacked on the previous two, creating a three-point base before the next layer begins—structural stability by instinct. A full grown heaver builds a tapering ziggurat roughly its own height, then clambers onto the summit to moult. The old shell is left in place, instantly colonised by spark-midge larvae that crave the residual magnetism.  
Communication: heavers “talk” by modulating the flow of slag through their shell plates, producing a wet bell-tone not unlike struck pewter. Different chords carry different permissions—“approach,” “bypass,” “ingot accepted.” Glass-gnawers obey these chords as railway signals, never crossing an active stack.

3. Heat-leeches  
Shape and size: ribbons of platinum-rich iridium two metres long, cross-section no thicker than an ethernet cable. Each ribbon houses a chain of voids filled with super-pressurised gas; these voids collapse in sequence, generating a peristaltic boom powerful enough to shake the chamber walls.  
Thermal cycle: leeches rest entangled in the deepest cracks, heat-soaking until their body temperature exceeds the chamber’s by 50 °C. Then they dash—if that word can describe a motion that lasts full minutes—toward cooler areas, venting shock pulses that redistribute heat like convection fans made of metal.  
Sensory kit: the outer epidermis is riddled with nanopores that open at specific frequencies of acoustic pressure. When the ambient drone slips out of tolerance the pores clamp shut and the leech retreats, preventing catastrophic over-cooling by self-censoring its own motion.  
Reproductive note: two leeches braid themselves together around a share of molten basalt. The cooling rock extrudes through their bodies, capturing trace iridium; when it hardens the pair splits, leaving behind a brittle spindle—the egg. Six months later a ghost-thin filament hatches by cracking the spindle along its growth rings.

4. Spark-midges  
Morphology: each midge is a floating dandelion of copper whiskers surrounding a graphite core. Typical diameter: three millimetres.  
Charge strategy: legs—really antennae—pluck at pressure ridges in the cooling rock. The piezoelectric effect charges the whiskers; when potential hits a threshold the entire body discharges across the nearest gap, leaping a dozen centimetres on an electric arc. That flash is the chamber’s lightning, its only “daylight.”  
Swarm logic: midges tune their flash intervals so no two neighbours fire close in time; the effect is a rotational strobe that gives long-exposure observers a false sense of continuous light.  
Ecological function: the ultraviolet produced by repeated arcs anneals micro-fractures in the glass-gnawers’ plates and sterilises parasites that might otherwise colonise slag-heaver ingots. In return, gnawers allow midges to ride their dorsal seams, gaining elevated launch points.

5. Gauge-keepers (the recently evolved newcomer)  
Form: thin pillars of fused spinel and nickel iron, rooted to the floor but jointed in thirty-centimetre segments. They resemble telescoping tripods, though each is a single living column.  
Purpose: a keeper converts sound into structural adjustment. Inside every segment a ring of ferroelastic material flexes with the ambient drone; exceed a harmonic threshold and the ring ratchets one detent, extending or retracting the segment by fractions of a millimetre. In aggregate, the keepers keep the overall cavity volume within the narrow band required for optimal resonance.  
Why they matter now: until a few hundred generations ago the biome accepted wall erosion as destiny. Then a mutation allowed a dormant line of silicate cells to secrete spinel under acoustic stimulation. The keepers are the outcome—a feedback system that can literally grow more floor when the pocket grows too loud. They are the first sign that this ecology may yet escape heat death by engineering its own enclosure.

Interdependence—life as closed-loop metallurgy  
• The gnawers rough-shape the walls, feeding the heavers with drips.  
• The heavers supply pure metals that seed keeper columns.  
• The leeches pump the heat that drives every digestion.  
• The midges police infection and stitch vision out of darkness.  
• The keepers, newly arrived, promise stability over geological time.

Taken individually the residents feel like clever automata. Taken together they reveal something closer to a planet’s subconscious: heat dreaming itself into organs, pressure into skeletons, chemistry into behavior. They do not know we exist. They do not know light exists except as accident. What they do know—down to the last chirped chord and micro-molten footfall—is how to keep their single, shared furnace alive for one more pulse.

Stand at the fissure lip long enough and you will feel it through your boot soles: the faint, deliberate throb of a chamber teaching stone how to breathe.
