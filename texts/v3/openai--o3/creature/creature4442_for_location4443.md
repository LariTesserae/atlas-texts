Who lives here?

The short answer is “almost nothing,” but on this plateau almost nothing is already a crowd.  Look carefully—through the glare, in the hush, across the million-year rhythm of the place—and presences become visible in four overlapping scales.

1. Films and flecks—the people of the crust  
   • Xerolith colonies (you have met the adults) are underlain by a second life-stage called the root-mesh: threads of doped glass only a few molecules thick that run for meters beneath the surface salts.  By day they are inert; by night they superconduct, siphoning the plateau’s lightning-born currents to recharge the mats above.  A single strike anywhere in the region will pulse through the mesh within seconds, a continent-wide nervous system that never had to invent nerves.  
   • Radionims are single-cell halide sacks no thicker than frost.  They gather in the turquoise shadows where carbonyl ice plates the rock.  When UV flares, each cell extrudes a mirror-bright spine, launches itself like a tiny javelin, and tumbles until electrostatic drag lets it down—usually in the lee of a tower, where background gravity is a hair lower and decay particles are fractionally more abundant.  They live on radiation directly, storing the surplus as nested fullerene cages; miners in a world with no ore.  
   • Saltglass worms are misnamed—they are neither worms nor truly alive by most definitions.  Think self-healing microcracks that migrate.  Heat opens them, cold seals them, and with every cycle they ratchet a few microns sideways.  After ten millennia a single “worm” may have traveled an entire meter, carving cuneiform that only tectonic time can read.  They are the plateau’s historians, writing with absence.

2. Crawlers and climbers—the slow articulate kind  
   • Windleaps look like dropped handkerchiefs of bronze tissue.  Unfold one and you will find a radial skeleton of vanadium foam and muscle fiber as thin as dreams.  They spend their whole lives preparing for a single dawn: four or five local years of clinging to the lee side of a mesa, feeding on xerolith condensate, weaving their bodies larger and lighter, until the plateau’s scheduled exhalation tears them free.  The great majority perish in the Mach-number torrent; the lucky few clear the rim and drift into the upwelling mesospheric rivers where stratosails roam.  There they breed, scatter larvae like metallic drizzle, and eventually snow back onto the upland to begin again.  
   • Deep inside the slot canyons live the chours—organisms best described as living stalactites.  They grow downward grain by grain, extruding silica ribs that ring when struck by dust devils.  The note of the canyon wind determines what minerals each rib will crystallize next, so every chour encodes decades of weather as a descending spiral of tuned glasstone.  When a chour becomes too massive for its own tensile strength, it snaps, avalanches into the canyon floor, and breaks open—releasing a vapor of bacterial symbionts that recolonize the fracture surfaces and start the slow drop all over again.

3. Fliers and drifters—the plateau’s intermittent angels  
   • The stratosails you spotted are only the adults of a broader guild called the aeroliths.  Below them, at 10-kilometer altitude, float the cryokites—polyhedral balloons kept buoyant by neon heated a scant three degrees above ambient by biochemical lasers.  They hunt by shadow: detect the silhouette of a sail above, snap their facets open, and bathe it in an entangling web of coherent blue that robs the larger creature of lift.  What drifts down becomes slow-release fertilizer for the xerolith mats.  
   • Lightning serpents, those kilometer-long filaments you thought were mere electricity, are in fact semi-living plasma sheaths seeded with dust motes of magnetic bacteria.  Each serpent threads back and forth across the plateau like a loom shuttle, weaving a self-consistent, planet-wide magnetic map that the rest of the ecosystem uses for orientation.  In still photographs they look identical from century to century; only time-lapse reveals how they edit their own routes, keeping the dunes of charged dust from ever settling twice in the same spot.

4. The quiet architects—present but no longer populous  
   • Around the active gravity towers operate the Custodial Motile Elements: machines the size of coffee beans, polished opal in color, often mistaken for wind-blown grit.  They emerge at mathematically precise intervals—once every 8,640 plateau hours—move in choreographed lattices, and vanish again into the rubble at a tower’s foot.  No two observers have ever recorded the same pattern.  The consensus is that they are correcting minute drift in the Array’s spacetime lensing: a maintenance crew that outlived its city, its makers, and perhaps even its original purpose.  
   • The rumor of ghosts is not metaphor.  Close to a functioning pylon, EEG nets light up with signals that are neither brain-nor-weather.  They recur, they answer simple wave-encoded queries, and they insist on one datum: the plateau itself is an organ of a larger machine, built to bleed rotational energy from the planet’s core in quanta so fine no mantle fracture can form.  Whether that claim is true or merely a million-year echo of a shutdown protocol, no one yet knows.  But if you wait by a tower until the zenith dust surge, you may feel a fingertip of gravity trace the outline of your skull—as if something is taking inventory.

Patterns of life  

All these beings, biological and not, obey the plateau’s ancient schedule.  Each dawn wind is not merely weather; it is census and dispersal.  Each thermal quake cracks open fresh mineral for the xeroliths while resetting the piezoelectric tension that powers the chours’ growth.  Even the magnetic serpents adjust their paths to avoid the gravity dimples that, once a century, the Array retunes.  The entire community moves like parts in a clock one civilization built and many others inherited by accident.

What does it feel like to meet them?  

Very little, at first.  A sparkle at ankle height that turns out to be a radionim swarm; a sudden lightness in your pack as you walk across a tower’s shadow; a glassy pang somewhere deep in the slots below, the snapping of a chour older than primates.  You realize you are not an observer but a transient disturbance, a warmth source to be metabolized, mapped, or ignored.  Stand long enough and you become a semantic element in the plateau’s grammar.  Your footprints will vanish in minutes, but the perturbation you make in the charge field will be catalogued by windleap larvae, the echo of your voice stored as altered mineral orientation in the next generation of saltglass worms.  The plateau forgets surfaces quickly; it remembers fields and stresses forever.

So who lives here?  Gossamer fliers, superconducting lichens, plasma shepherds, nano-janitors, sunglass thunderworms, songs of dust and silent gravity.  A city of almost nothings—yet together they keep the highest desert on the planet not merely alive, but in tune, long after the making species folded its last tower and fell into the dark below.
