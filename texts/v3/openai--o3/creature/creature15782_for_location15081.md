What lives here is almost nothing, and in that almost nothingness it has learned to parcel out its own definition—cell by cell, heartbeat a thousand times slower than ours.  You could wait a week with your face against the slate and swear nothing changed, but if you could lend your sight to a strand of time the length of a century the chamber would look as busy as a marketplace: roots inching, films creeping, genes trading hands like gossip.  The denizens are all smaller than eyelash-tips, yet each has found a way to endure the chill, the sparse light, the iron tang that would ulcer a larger creature’s gut.  Taken together they fill three roles: the tall, the flat, and the drifting.

1.  Thread-grass (Zostera tenuis subsp. vitrea)  
   • Shape and stance  
     Picture a meadow miniaturized until the tallest stalk barely reaches your little finger.  Each blade is tubular rather than ribbon-flat, walling in a capillary so fine that even under ten metres of pressure the sap lifts with ease.  The grass has no beds of soil; instead, its roots knit through the rust-clay band at the bowl’s rim, using ferric oxides the way terrestrial cousins use sand—an anchor more chemical than mechanical.  

   • Colour and chemistry  
     In open air the leaves would be green; here they read as dilute amber, almost transparent.  The factory-sourced carbon blacks crowd the water with particles that steal part of the red spectrum, so chlorophyll has ceded first place to phaeophytin and strange violets unknown on land.  The blades therefore glow sepia under daylight but sink toward violet when an electric lamp intrudes.  No animal comes to graze them, yet the grass still wages war: it weeps a thin exudate rich in phenols, poisoning any younger blade that dares sprout too close and giving every stalk a personal exclusion zone the width of a thumbnail.  

   • Rhythm of living  
     Growth: one millimetre every ten days in spring-tinted months, half that across the deep winter.  Reproduction: a single capsule at the leaf’s base, shedding two seeds per year.  Half the seeds germinate; half of those succumb to their mother’s phenol halo; what survives has already learned thrift.  A stand of twenty stalks therefore represents perhaps two hundred seasons of negotiation.  

2.  The film—also called smoke-lichen, mirror-skin, or simply the grey  
   • Constitution  
     Underhand, the slate feels bare, but peel it with a scalpel and you lift a membrane 15–20 µm thick: a sandwich of three microbial guilds.  
       a.  Cyanobacteroid Iota-13 clads the upper 4 µm, harvesting the same softened daylight that feeds the grass.  The cells are puckered, lentil-shaped, with a lensing mucilage that focuses photons into the chloroplast zone the way a cat concentrates sun through a window.  
       b.  Ferridissolvens rubra lives immediately below, oxidising Fe2+ weeping from the marl.  As it does it lays down a gel of ferric hydroxide, giving the slate its deceptive glassy polish—every shimmer is actually billions of iron jackets excreted cell by cell.  
       c.  Carbovora subtilis, the deepest layer, breaks the phantom oils so fine that conventional microbes cannot bite them.  In doing so it excretes acetate and trace dimethyl sulphide: fertiliser and faint anaesthetic for the strata above.  

   • Metabolism in slow-time  
     A full cycle—from photon to stored glycogen to exhaled CO₂—takes thirty-six hours at nine degrees Celsius.  Each cohort of cells divides once a fortnight, shedding husks upward into the water column.  The husks drift for minutes and then settle back: the chamber’s way of snowing.  Over decades the accretion would thicken, but every century or so the film suffers a catastrophic molt: a slight tremor, a rare wind-driven pressure surge from the surface, anything that tips the slate by a millimetre shears the membrane away like dried skin.  It drifts out through the fissures, and the regrowth begins.  Thus the bowl polishes itself.  

3.  The drifters, or library of motes  
   • Virions, plasmids, and gene-chips  
     Where no animals graze, predation retreats to the microscopic.  The chamber contains an invisible cloud of viral particles—the only hunters permitted by the chemistry.  Each carries one or two auxiliary metabolic genes harvested from former bacterial hosts.  When a virion pierces a cyanobacterium it rarely kills outright; instead it installs its borrowed gene, reprogramming the cell to tolerate yet another trace toxin from the factory hinterland.  Over centuries the library has become encyclopedic: one out of every ten Iota-13 cells now contains virus-derived code, giving the film a memory of insults never personally suffered.  

   • Exchange ceremonies  
     Once or twice a year, when barometric pressure outside drops far enough, the slow seep quickens.  Fresh drifters arrive, and others depart.  The bowl’s microbiota respond by releasing plasmids—tiny rings of DNA—as decoys.  Incoming viruses waste themselves on the empty loops, sparing the resident genomes.  By the time pressure restabilises, the exchange is over and the population has copied any useful novelty into safe harbour.  

Hidden tenants in the wall  
The slate itself hosts endolithic archaea, so deeply recessed that sampling them means grinding the rock.  Their metabolism is almost theoretical: they oxidise trace hydrogen produced by low-level radiation in the mineral, dividing perhaps once a decade.  They matter only because they are old.  By matching mutation rates to known radiolytic flux, geobiologists calculate their lineage began before the upper valley drowned.  If anything in the chamber remembers river-days, it is these grain-locked cells dreaming beneath the polish.

What passes for behaviour  
None of these creatures swim, hunt, flee, or court.  Instead they practice three gestures:  
1.  Thrift—hoarding photons, electrons, nitrates as if siege were permanent.  
2.  Replacement—sloughing slow death outward so that the young, genetically half-mutant, inherit the light.  
3.  Listening—chemical receptors forever tuned to any new molecule that arrives down the fissures, ready to press it into metabolism or defense.  

What they make of you  
A warm-blooded diver or probe is, to them, an impossible summer.  Your suit sheds convection currents a hundred times stronger than anything the chamber normally feels.  The grass tips bow minutely.  Heat blooms from your lights, and every cyanobacterium above your shadow throttles its photosystem to keep from bleaching.  You may stay an hour; they will need a week to return to equilibrium.  The virions, scenting alien proteins, ghost toward you but find no purchase.  When you leave you carry a microgram of film on your gloves: a diaspora of chance.  Given sweeter water, higher light, and a touch of iron, it could pave another rock exactly as patiently somewhere else.

The net balance  
Add all carbon fixed in a day, subtract all respired, and the figure is almost zero—perhaps a fingernail’s mass saved against entropy every month.  That margin is life here: the tiniest wedge driven between geology and chemistry, prising them apart just far enough for the concept of organism to persist.  Nothing with muscle could survive on so thin a profit.  Nothing with nerves could bear the delay between stimulus and reward.  Yet for folio-thin grass, for iron-jacketed cells, for viruses that regard decades the way we regard minutes, the bowl is spacious, well lit, and reliably provisioned.  They are its congregation, and in their still, recursive industry the nave remains not vacant but quietly, intricately occupied.
