WHO (AND WHAT) LIVES IN THE RIFT OF UNFINISHED PEAKS  

The moment you step into the corridor the place begins to enumerate its citizens. Many you have already glimpsed—Gloom-Gulls, Clatter-Shrimps, Song-Leeches, Chimera Larvae, Ridge Seraphs—but each of those shorthand names hides a stratified society of castes, sexes, instars and aberrants. Around and between them exist lesser-known species, some no bigger than eyelash seeds, others so distributed in the air they are better spoken of as weather than as animals. Below is the current best attempt at a census, arranged from highest roost to deepest crack.

1. THE GLOOM-GULL CONSTELLATION  
   a. Pin-Featherlings  
      • Hatch in clumps of twenty or thirty, dangling like black grapes beneath an adult hen’s breastbone.  
      • For their first three days they are soft as soot; wind alone could unmake them. The mother jettisons the weakest, letting the canyon’s noise feed the Song-Leeches below.  

   b. Choir-Cocks  
      • Mature males grow a second tongue: a thin, bifurcated probe that flicks the air like a tuning fork. By sampling vibrational micro-currents they can chart moving objects within ten metres—enough to joust for perch space without ever needing sight.  
      • When rival males meet, they do not buffet one another. Instead they “play” each other’s chest cavities, striking a resonant thrum that carries up-canyon. The loser’s lungs flood with sympathetic vibration and he spirals away stunned, but seldom dies.  

   c. Murmurers (Widows)  
      • Old females whose smell-organs have failed, leaving them almost senseless. They drift on reflex alone. Their wingbeats are arrhythmic, producing a low tremolo that smaller scavengers harvest.  
      • Twice a century a storm wind drives whole flocks of these widows eastward; the next morning the canyon walls are lacquered with their grey dust. Copper algae flourish for years afterward, drawing on that protein windfall.  

2. CLATTER-SHRIMP CONGLOMERATES  
   a. Flint-Pickers  
      • Juveniles. Each limb ends in a fan of serrated hairs that generate static as the shrimp scrapes stone. Sparks arc between fans, loosening cations the animal licks up.  
      • They are translucent, nearly invisible. What gives them away is the perpetual knitting-needle rattle of a million tiny discharges.  

   b. Chime-Queens  
      • Once in a shrimp’s life—if it lives past its sixth moult—it grows a hollow ridge down its back. Wind singing through this ridge both nourishes and immobilises her; she anchors permanently, becoming a living bell.  
      • Workers rove beneath each queen, harvesting the metal-rich dust that sloughs from her carapace. In deep dusk the ridges glint like organ pipes.  

   c. Carrion-Beat Brigade  
      • A rare warrior morph produced only during population crashes. Broader, heavier, with tibiae flanged like cymbals.  
      • They attack Ridge Seraphs by landing en masse and clapping their limbs, stunning the amphibian with a hammering sonic pulse before picking it clean.  

3. SONG-LEECH ECOSYSTEM  
   a. Glass-Veins  
      • Minute, threadlike juveniles that spool themselves inside the micro-fractures of basalt. They lengthen the crack, riding each thermal tick as a banquet. One can split a boulder given thirty silent years.  
   b. Lanterns  
      • The familiar fist-long adults. Their glow is not indiscriminate: specific frequencies “overfill” them, forcing off surplus as light. A trained ear can play them like a keyboard, summoning coded flashes across the talus.  
   c. Cemetery Coils  
      • After five or six fat seasons an adult knots itself, goes opaque and solidifies. These ivory corkscrews remain wedged in rock for centuries, still faintly humming—fossilised batteries slowly bleeding the canyon’s deep bass.  

4. SPECIES ONLY HALF-SEEN  

   a. The Chimera Imago  
      • Pupation ends on a night of absolute stillness, perhaps one evening in four years.  
      • Witnesses describe a ripple in the dark, as if the stars themselves flexed and detached. No cadaver, moult or trace remains, save for a faint coniferous odour and a ragged gap in the chorus that lingers for days.  

   b. Apex Aurorans (“Silence Cats”)  
      • Not cats at all, but long-bodied reptiles with fur-like cilia that damp sound the way velvet drinks colour.  
      • They stalk by cancelling the noise of their own footsteps, creating moving holes in the soundscape. One realises a Silence Cat is near when a patch of hush, the size of a cloak, drifts across the talus like shadow from an unseen cloud.  
      • They consume Song-Leeches whole, crunching them only after swallowing; the muffled pop of imploding leech-bladders is the single sound a cat cannot hide.  

   c. Vaporids  
      • Sub-micron colonial jellyfish that ride the canyon’s thermal column. Each bell is so small a billion could fit in a teacup.  
      • They condense on contact with warm exhalations—hence the sweet musk in the air—and lick that moisture for salts before sublimating again.  

5. RIDGE SERAPHS IN DETAIL  
   a. Frame  
      • Skeleton mostly cartilage, impregnated with manganese for flexibility. Four limbs, all convertible: paddle in water, grasp in climb, spread into parachute.  
   b. Mind  
      • High brain mass for an amphibian. They navigate by memory of turbulence rather than by sight or sound. Juveniles raised in still air lose the ability to glide and become cave-bound cretins.  
   c. Communion  
      • At dawn they gather on resonant shelves and, as light first touches them, exhale in chorus. The release equalises pressures in their air-sacs, but to human ears it resembles a church organ adjusting stops—a soft, yearning chord that rolls the length of the Rift. Choristers of early expeditions once mistook this for a distant avalanche and fled.  

6. MICROFLORA & ENGINES OF ROCK  
   a. Copper Film Algae  
      • Not green but haematite red, containing phycobilins tuned to the canyon’s meagre blueshifted daylight. They seize stray photons others cannot touch.  
      • Produce a brittle polysaccharide sheet that shields them from razor wind; early naturalists dried the film and found it conducted weak electrical current—as if the algae still expected thunder.  

   b. Thorn-Moss  
      • Each spine narrows to a cytoplasmic filament only a few molecules thick, making it the finest natural needle known.  
      • When fog condenses, droplets string themselves along the spines in bead-and-wire formations. Gloom-Gulls sip these like rosaries, careful not to sever the moss, so the plant becomes a living watering trough.  

7. PARASITES & PASSENGERS  
   a. Echo-Ticks (on Gloom-Gulls)  
      • Flattened, obsidian disks that resonate sympathetically with their host’s wingbeats. They siphon kinetic vibration, never blood. A gull with too many begins to fly in ragged spurts; the ticks drop off at first sign of weakness, indicating a precise sense for metabolic tipping points.  

   b. Static Mites (on Clatter-Shrimps)  
      • Exploit the electrostatic pads, riding free charge from shrimp to shrimp. Colonies communicate by modulating the polarity of entire stone faces, briefly magnetising grains of iron dust to spell glyphs the size of dinner plates.  

8. THE HUMAN QUESTION  
   • No permanent settlements, but once every solar minimum a roofless monastic order—the Subaudients—treks in. They arrive barefoot, welcoming laceration as penance, and carry no lanterns. Their goal: a three-day fast from speech. To remain unheard in the Rift is impossible—yet they try, letting the canyon abrade them into quieter things.  
   • Departing Subaudients scrape poems into basalt with bone shards. The wind files many letters away, but fragments endure: “to fall is to finish singing,” “stone is a throat without hunger,” “may my silence be loud enough to feed.”  

ENERGY & ECOSYSTEM LOOP  
   1. Solar shafts strike copper algae → chemical energy.  
   2. Rock flexes in daily thermal tides → infrasonic waves.  
   3. Both forms of energy converge as vibration: everything listens, everything eats.  
   4. Bioluminescent surpluses flash, attracting predation, redistributing mass.  
   5. When bodies die, wind mills them into metallic dust; algae drink the rust; the loop closes.  

WHAT THE CANYON YET WAITS FOR  
   • Erosion enough to muffle its own keenness, ending the golden age of sound-eaters.  
   • The return of whatever adult the Chimera Larva becomes—perhaps to sew a ceiling, perhaps to pry the walls wider.  
   • A quiet so profound that the Silence Cats finally starve and lie still, gifting the Rift its first lasting hush.  

Until then, life here is an orchestra whose players are also its pipes, strings and echoing walls. Stand long enough and the taxonomy blurs: gull, shrimp, leech, monk, even the basalt itself—all are only temporary shapes taken by noise on its long pilgrimage from thunderhead to ground.
