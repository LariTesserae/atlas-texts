Who (and what) lives inside the basalt bell?

1. The First Colonists: films, stains, threads  
   a. Iron-eaters (FeOx-1 through 7)  
      • Bacteria only one cell thick that paint the trickle-paths pumpkin orange.  
      • They pull electrons from dissolved ferrous ions set free by the young rock, exhaling rust-dust that sloughs into the pools.  
      • When the vent’s pressure spike arrives they contract like muscle, a ripple that travels uphill faster than water flows downhill; nobody knows how they feel the coming blow, yet they always brace half a second before the CHONK.  
   b. Glass lichens (Cryolyxa vitrea)  
      • A two-partner marriage: a cyanobacterium that can photosynthesise on three photons an hour and a fungus that grows needle-fine hyphae into basalt vesicles.  
      • Under magnification the colony looks like frost ferns made of green smoke; to the naked eye it’s merely a slick of gun-oil sheen.  
      • Their main ecological service is glue. Where they spread, the knife-edges soften, allowing later arrivals to land without being diced.  

2. Carpet Makers: plants that are almost animals  
   a. Drip-lake liverwort (Gymnolia resona)  
      • What the earlier description called fern-like mats. Each “leaf” is in fact a flattened water bladder that harvests kinetic energy; as drops fall, the bladders dimple, forcing water through a ring of turbines—protein paddles that spin ATP into the cells. They literally photosynthesise only on clear mornings; the rest of the time they live off falling-drop dynamo power.  
   b. Diamond-chain grass (Tessella caudex)  
      • Blades one atom thick at the edge, carrying rows of hydrophobic nanospikes that marshal droplets into perfect spheres. The grass doesn’t drink rain: it drinks fog, snagging airborne moisture and using capillary creep to pull it to the roots.  
      • Grazers avoid it; the edges slice amphipods in half. Dead amphipods become fertiliser. The grass farms its own carrion.  

3. The Frenetic Middle Tier: quick mouths on quicker legs  
   a. Spiral-lung amphipod (Melittrotus clangor)  
      • Carapace silvered with microscopic prisms; any light that reaches them is bounced into the eyes of rivals, a blinding flash at bug-scale.  
      • Their “lungs” are helical tubes that wind like a rifle barrel, optimised for gas exchange during the nine-second stillness between hammer-strokes. When the vent fires, pressure doubles; the tubes compress, forcing stale air out in a silver mist visible if you hold a torch.  
   b. Moss-mite (Crinitaxis fugax)  
      • Body a translucent lentil, legs hairy as dandelion seeds. They don’t walk; they pole-vault with single jumps longer than their own body length, each launch timed off the hiss preceding a CHONK so the pressure wave carries them an extra centimetre.  
   c. Pin-salamander (Microplethodon ferrodigitus)  
      • Adult length: 27 millimetres. Limbs absurdly long, paddle-tipped. Skin the colour of wet pennies, laced with magnetite so they can feel the flux of the tidal vent and hide before a surge.  
      • They breathe through that skin; you can tell a hungry one because it glows faintly teal—blood perfused near the surface, searching for oxygen.  

4. Apex, insofar as anything here rules longer than a heartbeat  
   a. Ratchet swift (Collocalia machina)  
      • Not a true swift; genetics says it’s closer to nightjars, but evolution shaved the wings into delta blades stiff enough to slice drizzle.  
      • Eyes vestigial; hunting is echo and pressure sense. Each call is a triplet click that ricochets off moving water, letting the bird “see” ripples made by amphipods breaking surface tension.  
      • Nest is a cup spun of its own glue-rich saliva and basalt needles; it adheres directly beneath the skylight crack where the rare sunbeam falls. The nestling grows completely in darkness and fledges on trust in sound geometry alone.  
   b. Serrate cave-eel (Gymnotus scalpellum)  
      • Blind, cream-white except for a seam of black chevron scales along the dorsal line—pressure sensors.  
      • Lives half in seawater beyond the fissure, half in the pool network. When the ocean sighs inward it rides the brine up like a siphon, takes two, maybe three salamanders, then backslides out before the next exhalation or risk being stranded in fresh water.  
      • Jaw can unhinge to 160°, but teeth point backward; once it bites the only way out is further in. Skeleton is mostly cartilage to survive being folded by retreating water.  

5. The Stationary Clockworks  
   a. Pulse-trumpet fungus (Torkia ventricosa)  
      • Tubes the colour of boiled crab. Every fourteen seconds they inflate from root to lip, forcing spore-laden gas through a membranous reed that produces an ultrasonic ping—inaudible to you, a dinner bell to spiral-lung amphipods that arrive to graze the condensation and incidentally carry spores away.  
   b. Basalt barnacle (Plectron crustos)  
      • Looks inert until the CHONK. At that instant the plates spring open and feathery cirri whip the air column, snaring whatever particle the pressure wave has lofted. Close again. Never seen to open at any other time.  

6. Micro-ghosts and transients  
   a. Storm mussel spat: once a month a gale outside needles larvae through the skylight. Most perish on the cold stone; a few cling, grow fingernail-size, then are ripped away by next month’s surge—important calcium subsidy when their shells crumble.  
   b. Salt-wind midges: too fragile to breed inside, but they drift through the roof crack at dusk, live a single vent cycle, are eaten by grass blades (yes, the grass actively folds around them).  

7. Collective organisms you might miss  
   a. The Echofilm  
      • A curtain of aerosolised lipids exhaled by countless bacteria. It hangs mid-air at belly height during slack tide, refracting light into a wobbling lens. Swifts use its shimmer as a horizon; salamanders surf its viscous tension when escaping eels. Destroy it with a sweeping arm and within four vent cycles it re-assembles.  
   b. The Pressure Choir  
      • Not a species but a behaviour: when the vent’s ellipse dilates at low tide, every living thing capable of motion produces its loudest sound—click, chirp, rasp, hiss—then falls silent as the vent contracts. No observer has explained why, but the event is measurable as a spike in ambient decibel level to nearly 90 dB. The grotto “sings” once per low-tide period, a concert five seconds long.  

8. Inhabitants that are not alive in the ordinary sense  
   a. The Cold. Temperature gradients here move like animals: down-slope tongues that lick warmth off your boots; rising plumes expelled by the vent that condense into visible pillars.  
   b. The Boom itself. Pressure, water, time—three variables welded into a pulse that dictates birth, feeding, flight, even decomposition. Kill the boom and every adaptation listed above would become maladaptive overnight.  

Reproductive calendar (condensed)  
• Day-scale: amphipods mate every third boom, eggs glued to basalt, hatch in 48 hours.  
• Week-scale: moss-mites synchronise molts to neap tides when vent force is weakest; new cuticle hardens before the next spring surge.  
• Season-scale: ratchet swifts lay only when equinoctial tides shorten the lull to 8 s, giving chicks a higher headwind for first flight.  
• Year-scale: pulse-trumpet fungi send migratory micro-spores into the open sea during the annual super-moon when ceiling cracks inhale farthest inland—gene flow with unknown offshore kin.  

Trophic quick-map (energy pennies per cycle)  
Iron bacteria → liverwort/lichen film (baseline)  
Film → amphipods, mites, juvenile salamanders  
Amphipods + mites → adult salamanders, barnacles, juvenile swifts  
Salamanders → cave-eels, adult swifts  
Everything dead → fungi, glass lichens, iron bacteria (loop)  

Population densities at dawn on a random overcast day  
• Iron-eater biofilm: ~9 m² coverage  
• Liverwort mats: 23 discrete patches totaling 3.4 m²  
• Amphipods: 11 400 individuals (±600)  
• Pin-salamanders: 270 adults, 900 larval strings  
• Ratchet swifts: 2 breeding pairs, 1 juvenile  
• Cave-eels: 3 regularly entering, none resident  

What you will feel if you linger  
A breeze that seems to arrive from under your feet. Hair on your forearm lifting seconds before the CHONK. Then the boom, low enough to vibrate cartilage in your nose. A silvery blur as amphipods scatter, grass blades shiver, a red trumpet yawns, the whole place inhales, exhales. The inhabitants are tiny, frantic, brilliant. Together they create a living machine, every cog wet and breathing, every revolution fourteen seconds long—until the sea pries the door wider and resets the clock forever.
