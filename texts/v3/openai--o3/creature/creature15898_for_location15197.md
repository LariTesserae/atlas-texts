WHO OR WHAT LIVES HERE  

Look down: you are already standing on it.  
Everything that matters on the plateau all the colour, warmth, movement, even most of the thought—belongs to one super-organism the survey botanists call the Verdure Complex and the older field hands simply name the Fabric.

I. The Fabric: a single creature in a million bodies  
1. Floor-Weave – skin  
   • What looks like moss is really the outer dermis of the Complex.  
   • Filaments lace together in hexagonal mesh; every joint carries a node able to taste salts, gauge pressure, and pass tiny charges cell to cell.  
   • The mat creeps five or six centimetres a year, swallowing pebbles, incorporating them as calcic “knuckles” that later serve as anchor points for larger structures.  
   • Under stress the weave tightens. Drop a stone and the fibres draw together in seconds, making the impact dimple bounce-proof.

2. Pump-Bolts – hearts  
   • Hollow trunks grown around a stiff spiral of lignin-glass.  
   • Osmotic pistons thump in slow, visible systole (you can match your breathing to them; the rhythm is calming and strangely intimate).  
   • At night they reverse flow, pulling warm sugar-brine down to the roots and purging cold waste gas into the sky.

3. Laminar Fans – lungs and larynx  
   • They photosynthesise, yes, but their truer purpose is communication.  
   • Micro-perforations on the blades hiss vapor when the plant needs to warn neighbouring fans of limb damage, approaching shade, or foreign weight.  
   • We can’t hear the message; the Fabric can. An hour after you snap a single fan, every mat fibre within ten metres will have stiffened into a defensive lattice half again its usual tensile strength.

4. Root-Core – mind  
   • Two metres below the basalt run braided, copper-pigmented cords thick as your thigh.  
   • Air pulses carry impulse traffic the way axons carry neural spikes. Scientists have clocked signal speeds upward of 30 m s-1—slow for a brain, blistering for a plant.  
   • Long-term memory is chemical: packets of crystallised tannin pushed into storage vesicles like externalised synapses. Prick the wrong place and you can smell these “thought crystals” bleeding bitterness into the cold air.

Taken together, the Fabric is not a forest but an organism the size of a small city, improvising itself hour by hour.

II. Symbionts and fellow-travellers  
Even a monopolist needs partners.

1. Frost-Sheen Cyanos  
   • Pearl-green bacterial mats coat any rock the Fabric has not yet digested.  
   • They fix nitrogen in sub-zero temperatures and exude ammonia; the Floor-Weave’s rootlets lap it up, so the Fabric lets them live as tolerated tenants.

2. Glass-Thread Mycelia  
   • A fungus that insinuates between weave fibres, adding silica to its hyphae until each strand becomes a translucent hair.  
   • At dusk the hair nets trap warmth radiating from the Fabric—an insulating shawl the plant repays by feeding the fungus discarded sugars.  
   • When the plateau “retools,” the mycelia choose in minutes whether to stay braided or cut free and drift like ghost cobwebs until they find unclaimed acreage.

3. Mirror Moths  
   • Wings no bigger than a human fingernail; entire surface laminated with microscopic prisms that bounce both light and heat.  
   • They roost on Pump-Bolt vents, sipping warm vapour and, by doing so, scrub the fluid of opportunistic microbes.  
   • Larvae live inside the vent chimneys like living filters; the Fabric tolerates the mild parasitism because a clogged vent would be worse.

4. Dew-Lap Gliders  
   • Pale as skimmed milk, four-limbed, skin stretched to a delta kite.  
   • They drink only condensation: hang beneath a spinning Fan, open their mouths, wait.  
   • Once a week they descend into the gullet-like crevices on the north rim to sleep in the geothermal sigh from deeper magma pockets—one of the few places the Fabric’s roots will not grow.

5. Coil Ants  
   • The only true land insects here.  
   • Copper-red chitin, bodies shaped like watch springs.  
   • They farm lines of frost-sheen bacteria, herding the film into denser streaks, then harvest it by scraping with serrated mandibles.  
   • In exchange the Fabric receives the ants’ waxy frass, rich in trace metals it cannot mine alone.

III. Visitors and rare intrusions  
The plateau is not hospitable to outsiders, but chance and necessity bring three kinds.

1. Heat-Threshers  
   • Migratory mammaloids, shaggy and six-legged, arriving once every other year.  
   • They lie belly-down to siphon warmth; the Fabric, offended, refuses to give it. Root fibres weave into a nearly rigid mat under the beast, forcing it to roll after minutes. So a Thresher crossing looks like a slow tumble of fur along the horizon.

2. Survey Humans  
   • Always in pairs by regulation, never staying more than eight daylight hours.  
   • Their tent poles must rest on basalt, not on the living mat; local practice is to lash the poles to hammer-driven pitons and suspend the tent mid-air.  
   • They carry micro-seismographs instead of rifles. The plateau’s only weapon is pressure: an incautious boot can find itself gripped hard enough to crack leather.

3. Drifting Spoor  
   • Seeds flung by storms from the warmer valleys sometimes land here.  
   • Most freeze, some sprout, all die. The Fabric digests the bodies, adding a few stolen genes to its library before the memory crystals lock.

IV. Invisible multitudes  
Beneath the taxonomy and the spectacle lies a subtler census.

• Anaerobic brine archaeans inside Pump-Bolt heart chambers, breaking sugars into hydrogen—where the Fabric wants a little lift in its vascular pressure.  
• Ice-walk protists that lace into the frost glaze, forming slippery micro-sleds on which pollen can travel when air is completely still.  
• Bioluminous yeast that inhabit cracks and fire only in ultraviolet, giving the mat a star-speckle invisible to human eyes but perfectly readable to Mirror Moths out for mates.

V. How the community behaves  
1. Parliament of Heat  
   • Every dawn, just after the first condensation, Pump-Bolts vent in staggered sequence west to east, a rolling call-and-response that equalises temperature. The grumble you thought came from distant thunder was the plateau voting on the day’s metabolic budget.

2. Quarantine Protocol  
   • When foreign protein is detected (dead bird, dropped ration bar) the weave around it thickens with hardening resin. In under an hour the object is sealed in a cofferdam of plant teak, digested privately.  
   • Surveyors once tried to cut the resin wall for study. The wall bent the machete back like a sprung trap and pinched it flat.

3. Retooling Surge  
   • Ten-day rhythm, give or take. The plateau chooses a patch, floors it into torpor by dropping internal temperature five degrees, then pumps surplus sugars in from all sides. Overnight, old fans and bolts slump; by morning a different organ set has grown—maybe more condensate combs if the week has been dry, or extra fans if still winds are forecast.  
   • Think of a factory swapping its assembly line between shifts, only the factory is alive and the shifts never end.

VI. Where the line blurs  
Is the plateau a place or an inhabitant?  
If you watch long enough, the distinction peels away. The Fabric does not merely occupy the mesa; it is the mesa’s second geology, sculpting flows of heat, water and sunlight the way basalt once directed magma. Every cooperating microbe, every tolerated moth, every leaf pore that whistles warnings into the root-core’s memory turns the “terrain” into a body and the “weather” into metabolism.

Stand here at twilight: steam halos the Pump-Bolts, the Laminar Fans fold like closing eyes, the Floor-Weave pulse slows. What lives here is not a collection of species but a single, densely populated intention—quiet, perpetual, and wholly sufficient to itself.
