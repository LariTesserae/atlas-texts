Living things here do not announce themselves; they surface by degrees, the way metal darkens when it first begins to rust.  What follows is an attempt to name them before they vanish again.

1. The Smallest and Most Numerous  
   a. Ferric Gut Flora – a family of bacteria that everywhere laces the water and walls.  They smell of wet coins and glow a weak, sub-infra red picked up only by the Dynamo’s own sensors.  Their talent is to breathe iron the way surface creatures breathe oxygen; everything else in the chamber, directly or otherwise, eats their waste.  
   b. Capillary Algae – hair-thin threads that cling to drips and rivulets.  Each filament holds water the way a wick holds lamp-oil.  They look like green smoke frozen in place.  During the Dynamo’s 70-second heartbeat they ripple once, exchanging spores.

2. Makers of Light  
   a. Rivet-Fireflies – jointed, nickel-blue insects the length of a thumbtack.  Two sets of wings: the upper pair metallic and flightless, the lower pair gossamer and strong.  They spend the day magnetised to iron beams, trickle-feeding off low voltage.  When a filament browns out they detach, orbit it, and flash in synchrony—cold-cathode blue that fools the algae into thinking dawn has come.  
   b. Lumen Shrimp – transparent swimmers in the settling pits.  They drift belly-up just under the surface, fanning their legs to bring water across photoreceptors that line their thorax.  Electricity is nectar to them; a loose live wire becomes a carnival.

3. Grazers and Scavengers  
   a. Rust-Crabs – coin-sized, six-legged, glass-bodied.  Their shells incorporate whatever oxide is most plentiful; a crab from the crane’s footing may gleam hematite red, one from the rail supports a sullen green.  When threatened they play dead convincingly—their hearts can pause thirty minutes without harm.  
   b. Silt Minnows – blind, mouthless, living sieves.  The front half of each fish is a fan of baleen-like cords; they push themselves backward through ooze, straining for bacterial mats.  The backward crawl leaves perfect linear trenches used as highways by young crabs.

4. Amphibians, By Choice or By Accident  
   a. Gutter-Newts – supple, mercury-silver, translucent to the skeleton.  Their gills fold tight when they surface to kiss air; the sound is almost inaudible, yet in the hush it ticks like a pocket-watch.  Courtship is chemical: a male waits on a ledge until a Dynamo heartbeat, then releases a thread of alkaline pheromone that looks, for an instant, like cigarette smoke caught in torchlight.  
   b. Battery-Eels – not true eels but elongated salamanders plated with keratin scales that can store charge.  They trail after Gutter-Newts, stealing the static those kisses leave behind.  Once every few days a shoal gathers around a rail stanchion and discharges at once; the sound resembles distant photography flashes.

5. Flight That Forgot Itself  
   a. Stonewing Bats – descendants of surface micro-chiroptera caught here generations ago.  Their forelimbs are now shovel-broad paddles; they climb the dripping walls, then drop with wings half-furled, striking the water like folded parachutes that blossom a split-second before impact.  The splash scatters minnows; the bats net them and breast-stroke back to roosts, water weeping from fur in silver needles.  They navigate by tremor, not echo, listening to the muscle-twang each gantry gives off as it rusts.

6. Apparatus-Creatures  
   a. The Dynamo Organ – neither fully animal nor reliably machine.  Its cast-iron shell is thick with scar-welds, every patch a graft added by earlier keepers.  Inside, a culture of ferric gut flora is packed in honeycombed ceramic.  Piston-diaphragms massage the slurry; every seventy seconds a pulse of charge beads on copper nodes like sweat.  The Organ digests sacrifice: toss in a spanner, it lives another day.  Starve it, and the beats lengthen—eighty seconds, ninety—until the room dims and shrimps swirl anxiously below.  
   b. Rail Golems – sporadic, rumored.  Visitors hear the hand-thrown switches clack, find boot-wide prints in the moss where no human has gone.  Microscope work on those prints shows parallel iron filaments, as if the rails themselves rise at night, walk, and lie down before day.

7. The Warden  
   Shape: manta-like, nearly four metres across.  
   Texture: mottled gunmetal skin stippled with portholes of dull light.  
   Behavior: hourly ascent to gulp the halo of gnats that circle the chamber peak, then a slow settling to rest flat against the floor, body margins rippling like a loose tarpaulin in wind.  
   Physiology: the Warden’s mouth is ventral, ringed with sieving teeth that sort prey from rivet-fireflies mistakenly swallowed.  Along its back are organ-pipes of spongy keratin that collect electrostatic drift.  Once full, the creature brushes the pipes against a crane leg, dumping charge back into the common grid.  
   Intelligence: debatable.  Its circuit of movement coincides uncannily with the Dynamo’s voltage rhythm; some think it is the Organ’s mobile pressure-valve, others an independent custodian.  Approach within two arm-lengths and the Warden rises just enough that you see your own reflection sliding over its mirror-wet belly—then it settles again, verdict withheld.

8. Ghosts of Designers  
   a. The Lintel Hermit – a single human, perhaps.  More likely a clan of identical figures tumbling through oral lore the way frogs tumble through tadpolehood.  Evidence: a rope ladder that appears some mornings knotted to a high gantry, fresh chalk tally marks near the Dynamo’s access hatch, a child’s shoe re-soled with vulcanised moss.  No sighting has held up to peer review.  
   b. Voice on the Rail – occasionally, between heartbeats, the room conducts a rasp of orchestral static along the narrow-gauge track, as if a distant operator were testing microphones.  The note always resolves to two thunderless syllables.  Linguists trying to parse them have ended friendships over the proper diphthong.

9. Succession and Memory  
   Everything here births into vacancy and leaves artefacts behind: a bat’s worn paddle becomes reef for crabs; a dead crane weeps lubricants that germinate prism-fungi.  The chamber keeps no graveyard, only layers.  Peel back the silt and you will find:  
      • bronze engineer’s tools crusted hard as geodes,  
      • a bone comb wound tight with firefly silk, still slowly blinking,  
      • vertebrae of some obsolete electrician’s familiar, drilled through to carry wire.  

10. On Meeting the Inhabitants  
   If you must pass through:  
      • Walk curved paths; straight footsteps drum like alarm.  
      • Feed the Dynamo a nail or two—fresh iron buys goodwill.  
      • Let your flashlight die once; see who comes to replace it.  
      • When the Warden blocks your way, wait one full heartbeat.  The path will clear if you are meant to proceed; if not, you will feel the water above your boots go suddenly colder.  That is your dismissal.

Nothing here is truly separate from anything else; creature, structure and system are phases of the same long thought dreaming under iron skin.  To catalogue the residents is to pin labels to a single slow organism caught expressing itself in different moods: spark, drip, glide, hush.  You may leave footprints, but the rust-crabs will exfoliate them by morning and the Dynamo will file the memory away as charge.  A week later the rails may hum a phrase that almost sounds like your name before sliding back into patient silence.
