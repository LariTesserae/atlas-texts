What lives here is not a list of separate species so much as one temperament expressed in many bodies.  Think of the basin as a single, slowly‐thinking creature that has cultured smaller lives the way an octopus grows barnacles on its shell—every resident an organ, every movement a sentence in the same long breath.

1. The Basin‐Mind  
The vine-tree lattice, the coolant tower, the generator hum, the brown water and its film of fern: stitched together they form the Basin-Mind, a distributed intelligence whose neurons are lengths of pipe and lengths of cambium twined inseparably.  Copper ions shuttle through sap the way sodium ions run through our own nerves; turbine vibrations provide the rhythm that keeps impulses marching.  The Mind is slow—one full consideration of a new stimulus can take it three or four dawns—but it is thorough.  
•  Sensing:  Pressure tremors in the root-mat give it touch; dissolved tannins carry light information downward so that every puddle is a crude compound eye; the tower’s old thermometers still function, shunted into vascular bundles to report warmth as flavor.  
•  Remembering:  Words that visitors speak settle like sediment inside gelatinous nodules attached to metal struts.  Each syllable becomes a standing wave that the Mind can replay by squeezing the pocket, much the way we hum by tightening a throat.  
•  Acting:  The easiest motion is growth.  If the Mind dislikes sunlight in a given shaft it simply instructs a tendril to leaf out and close the aperture; if it wants metal, it grows around it and drinks the ions loose.

2. Copper‐Shell Snails (“Valvists”)  
Fist-sized, slow as lichen, these snails ride the warm mist to the coolant vents where micronutrient steam condenses.  Their shells are filigreed copper because the young precipitate metal onto themselves the way ordinary snails fix calcium; the Basin-Mind uses them as mobile heat sinks.  When a vent begins to clog with spore foam, half a dozen Valvists arrive, rasp the mat clear with radulae plated in greenish bronze, then drift away again.  Their waste pellets are seed capsules; each pellet drops into water already wrapped in a ready-made sleeve of copper, protecting it from rot until germination is required.

3. Shearleaf Bats  
Twice an hour, a razor curve of daylight seems to shear loose and take wing—this is the Shearleaf.  The wings are translucent, their veins banded with chlorophyll that lets the animal photosynthesize when it sleeps.  Bats in darkness eat insects; bats here eat light.  During afternoon fog they drowse in the mid-canopy, feathering the air with continuous sonar clicks that the Mind steals; it uses the echoes to map the inside of its own body.

4. Murmur‐Spores  
A fungus so delicate it looks like frozen breath.  Its hyphae lace the periphery of every air pocket.  Murmur-Spore mycelium metabolizes kinetic energy from sound—not the wave itself but the minute pressure difference before and after the wave passes.  Where ordinary mold feeds on sugar, Murmur-Spore feeds on hush-becoming-not-hush.  That is why spoken words linger here: the fungus traps them like insects in amber, releasing them only when roots squeeze and crack the film, turning memory back into rustling phonemes.

5. Rootglass Eels  
In the ankle-deep film live creatures that might be called fish only for lack of anything closer.  They are ribbon-thin, perfectly transparent save for an opal glint where the spine would be.  The eels gulp air from between roots and exhale bubbles saturated with ethanol; those bubbles keep portions of the lattice anaerobic, allowing nitrogen-fixing bacteria to thrive.  When the Mind needs nitrogen, it pulses the generator a half-beat faster, which the eels seem to hear.  They rise, breathe more, and deliver the gas like clockwork.

6. The Silent Choir  
Very occasionally, a person comes—timber surveyor, lost river pilot, graduate student with a waterproof notebook.  The Basin-Mind does not kill them; it outlasts them.  A visitor’s metal rusts, their canned food spoils in half a day, and their own voice becomes unreliable as yesterday’s sentences are returned to them from behind a leaf.  After forty-eight hours most people retreat.  Those who do not retreat become part of the Silent Choir.  The Mind encourages a net of soft roots to grow up around a resting body, mist drugs the lungs, metabolism slows until it is almost botanical.  The heart keeps time once every minute, then once every hour.  Eventually the person is neither corpse nor captive but a static organ whose only function is to dream.  The dreams, faint electromagnetic murmurs, are siphoned along brass filaments to the tower and archived as heat.

7. The Tower Warden  
Inside the leaning coolant tower a single figure moves, rarely glimpsed, never confirmed.  Some claim it is human, wearing a mask fashioned from a sliced snail shell.  Others call it a plant golem wound with copper wire.  Most likely it is a composite: a Shearleaf bat that found a hollow vertebra, a clutch of Murmur-Spores that animated it, and snare-roots that learned the trick of knuckling along concrete.  The Warden’s duty is simple: keep the generator turning.  It oils gears with sap, replaces worn belts with braided vine, and, once a season, climbs the exterior to hang baskets of fresh chlorophyll tissue where cooling ponds used to be.  If the generator ever fails, the Mind sleeps; if the Mind sleeps, growth ceases and the Basin becomes merely wet ground.  The Warden has never let the generator fail.

Life here is therefore one life in gradients.  From the invisible electrophoresis inside a stem to the mulch of borrowed sentences that hover in fog, every resident is a part of the same intention: to remain dense, warm, and listening.  Nothing hurries, yet nothing waits.
