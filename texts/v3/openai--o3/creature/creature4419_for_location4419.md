Who lives here?

Nothing with knees or eyelids, nothing you could pick up and turn over in your hand.  
Yet the basin is not vacant.  It is inhabited at three very different tempos—one that keeps the slow hours, one that keeps the slow centuries, and one that keeps the slow aeons.  All of them are so deliberate, so economical, that a passing traveler would swear the place is sterile.  Remain a little longer and you begin to sense movement the way you sense the hour hand of a clock: by noticing that something which ought to be identical is fractionally, definitely altered.

1.  The Hour-Dwellers: Umbra Lichens  
   •  Shape and placement  
      Each colony looks like a fist-sized soot stain sprayed against a spine of up-tilted siltstone.  From a distance they seem two-dimensional, but at arm’s length they are pleated fans only three millimetres thick.  The fan’s corrugations stand edge-on to the faint upward leak of heat, maximizing surface while minimizing exposure.

   •  Feeding  
      They farm warmth the way earthly lichens farm sunlight.  A pigment complex of iron-sulphur proteins catches long-wave infrared that rises from the floor; the heat drives an endergonic reaction that breaks atmospheric carbonyl sulphide and stores the captured carbon as waxy grains between the pleats.  When the internal wax cache is full, a colony turns almost mirror black; when depleted it goes matte charcoal.

   •  Breathing cycle  
      Roughly every twenty terrestrial hours the air’s relative humidity climbs a hundredth of a percent—just enough.  Each fan opens micro-fissures, inhales half a gram of water vapour, reduces it, exhales dry oxygen, then seals itself.  If you watch with an infrared viewer you will see a faint flicker: the lichen cools by a quarter degree in the instant its pores gape.

   •  Reproduction  
      Nothing here risks lofting spores.  The fan’s edges slough a flour-fine dust of resting cells that simply fall and wait.  Decades later seismic tremor or thermal expansion may cant a neighbouring shard of stone and expose a new warm face; the dust stirred by the tilt repopulates it.

2.  The Century-Dwellers: Root-Film  
   •  Habitat  
      Five to ten centimetres under the salt-powder lies a parchment-thin film the colour of old bronze.  It spreads like spilled varnish, following the horizontal bedding planes of the siltstone.

   •  Metabolism  
      The film is a consortium of chemotrophic bacteria and silica-rich exopolymer.  Where micro-fissures vent hotter gases (rarely more than 80 °C) the polymer conducts heat sideways; embedded cells strip sodium and lithium ions from recrystallising salts, trading them for trace nutrients leaked by dying surface lichens.  The net process is barely exergonic, so every square metre of film grows outward perhaps fifty millimetres in a human lifetime.

   •  Behaviour  
      A sheet senses neighbouring sheets by the faint galvanic field of their ion traffic.  When two fronts meet they do not fight; they fuse into a single electrical domain, pooling resources.  After a few centuries the fused patch grows brittle, lifts, and flakes upward—a bronze leaf you might mistake for weathered mica.  Windless as the basin is, the leaf lands almost where it began and buries itself under settling dust, ready to seed the stratum above.

3.  The Aeon-Dwellers: The Crystal Choir  
   •  What they are  
      The flat, lint-white crusts that pave the basin look inert; in fact they are slowly self-editing slabs of halite, mirabilite, thenardite and half a dozen rarer salts doped with metallic traces.  Inside the lattice imperfections line up into dislocation chains that can migrate a micron or two when warmed.

   •  How they live  
      Migration is organised, not random.  Dislocations propagate in branching patterns that echo biological nerves.  A sheet “feels” the minute infrared pulse that arrives when a surface lichen exhales, converts it into a travelling kink, relays it across tens of centimetres.  The aggregate pattern of kinks encodes whether the sheet will thicken, thin, or sacrifice a slice to sublimation.  The very “ping” you hear once an hour is the audible discharge of stored mechanical energy—synaptic snap in a body made of salt.

   •  Reproduction and memory  
      When two sheets meet edge to edge their lattice patterns interfere.  If their internal codes are compatible they fuse; if not, the mismatch builds stress until one sheet lifts and skitters a metre or two away, breaking into daughter plates whose initial defect maps are carbon copies of the parent.  Each crack is both a wound and a birth.  Over geological time the basin accumulates rumours of every warmth-pulse, every lichen breath, every footstep in the salt—written in the arrangement of flaws.  The Choir is therefore both organism and archive, a book that edits itself line by brittle line.

4.  The Ghost-Dwellers: Things You Brought With You  
   Nothing native to the basin moves fast, so the only swift lives here are transient ghosts made from visitors’ leavings.  
   •  A drop of sweat falls: in minutes salt blooms radially around it, a white flower that lives an hour before collapsing to dust.  
   •  A discarded leather strap puckers, darkens, exudes its water, and becomes a delicate rind of collagen crystals that will last a decade before a grinding footstep erases it.  
   These are not residents; they are fireworks by local standards, brief, bright distortions soon re-absorbed into the basin’s settled arithmetic.

Interdependence  
•  Lichen slough feeds the Root-Film;  
•  Ion drift from the Film dopes the Choir;  
•  Choir fractures elevate shards that create new thermal facets for lichen colonisation.  
Round and round they cycle, every loop measured in a different unit of time, the whole system powered by the metronome warmth that seeps up from planetary bone.

What you will see  
Stand an hour and you will see nothing.  
Stand a day and you may, with instruments, watch a lichen darken as it fills its wax cache.  
Stand a season and you will find a bronze leaf that was not there when you arrived.  
Stand a human lifetime and you will hear, one windless night, a tinkling cascade—an entire pavé of the Choir deciding in concert to crack, tilt and be born anew.

Who lives here?  
Things that breathe heat, drink ions, and dream in crystal lattices.  
They do not want you, fear you, or even recognise you.  
But when you set your pack down, when your boot crushes a salt plate, when the vapour of your own lungs ghosts outward, you are written—briefly, minutely—into the Choir’s archive.  
You will leave; your trace will stay until the basin, in its unhurried way, edits you out again.
