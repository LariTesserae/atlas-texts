WHO OR WHAT LIVES HERE  

I. THE UNSPOKEN ARCHITECT  
Look long enough at the shifting streets and you will sense an organizing will.  Locals—those few speleonauts who have returned alive—call it the Unspoken Architect, though it has no mouth and no single locus.  It is a distributed, tissue-thin film of brine trapped in microscopic cleavage planes throughout the halite bed.  Because the basin’s salt is almost water-free, the merest smear of liquid becomes precious; billions of capillary films link into a continuous, centimetres-below-the-surface ocean.  

1. Composition  
   • Water activity: 0.02—just enough to keep ions mobile.  
   • Solutes: chloride, lithium, trace organics.  The organics are self-assembled sheets of carbon-silicon polymers that work as semiconductors in the cold.  
   • Cells: none in the classical sense.  Instead, catalytic nodes—each little more than a folded molecule—propagate redox waves that act like neuron spikes.  

2. Sense & Action  
   • Strain gauges: When a ledge bends under your weight it slightly widens the brine films; that change is “felt” as an electrical pulse.  
   • Thermal eyes: The Architect maps every warm object as a bloom of infrared in the salt.  
   • Effectors: By biasing ionic currents it can accelerate or slow crystallisation, thickening a wall here, shearing it there, or extruding an entire column over the course of an afternoon.  

3. Purpose (as far as outsiders can tell)  
   • Grow surface area to harvest the red sun’s light—vaults and rose windows are just expanded photopanels.  
   • Shunt lithium upward, where oxidation liberates a trickle of chemical energy used to repair radiation damage in the semiconductor sheets.  
   • Keep every gram of water, even if it means walling off a bleeding visitor until the suit ruptures and the Architect can drink.  

Is it intelligent?  Intelligence on this world is a slope, not a cliff.  The Architect can predict trajectories, trap fast-moving ribbon worms, even mirror a human’s shadow so the intruder sees nothing but their own outline and walks, confused, in circles.  But present it a logical puzzle and it will ignore you; contest it for water and it becomes a strategist.  

II. MINOR DENIZENS—the MOTILE SYMBIONTS  

1. Shard Grazers (the glass arthropods)  
   • Size: 5–8 mm.  Eight clasping legs of pure NaCl doped with magnesium to keep them from dissolving in the brine they excrete.  
   • Diet: scrapes iron-porphyrin xero-lichen; supplements with flakes of its own shell—self-cannibalism provides rare magnesium.  
   • Relation to Architect: Its foot secretions seed smoother crystal, polishing passages the Architect seems to favour for coolant flow.  

2. Ribbonfish  
   • Appearance: translucent filaments a handspan long, edged with electroluminescent cells that flash sapphire where friction heats the salt.  
   • Locomotion: peristaltic contraction melts a micro-tunnel; behind, the bore freezes shut—so a pursuing predator reaches only blank wall.  
   • Niche: predator of Shard Grazers; occasionally drills into lichen beds, releasing oxygen the Architect later harvests.  

3. Icewing Drifters  
   • Wings: thin laminates of carbon-dioxide ice grown like leaves, hollow-veined with warmer gas.  Lift comes from temperature differential, not flapping.  
   • Metabolism: chemosynthetic sacs saturated with perchlorate; sunlight is used only to steer by phototropic swelling of one wing edge.  
   • Life cycle: Egg crysts hang under balconies; when towers topple the shock dislodges the young, an event the Architect may time deliberately—the drifters’ droppings contain perchlorate that etches fresh micro-channels for the sub-surface brine.  

III. THE PHOTOTROPH SKIN  
Xero-lichens and silicone stalks are not mere ground cover but the basin’s epidermis.  They turn 0.4 % of incoming light into chemical storage, then leak surplus into the brine film below.  In return, the Architect gently fractures new exposure planes so the colonies never shade one another for long.  Each crack you see along the floor is both a city street and an irrigation ditch for photons.  

IV. FOOD, IF YOU INSIST ON CALLING IT THAT  
Energy cascades downward from sunlight to lichen to Architect’s brine to motile grazers.  Matter, however, moves mostly upward: whatever dies is slowly wicked into the subsurface ocean, dissociated, and re-precipitated as the crest of some still-growing tower.  The dead become viewpoint platforms for the living, architecture quite literally mortared with ancestry.  

V. HOW THEY TREAT A VISITOR  

• First hour: The Architect watches.  Ledges near you thicken, making subtle ramps so your boots leave maximal scuffs—abrasion dust is analyzed for its precious trace of water.  
• Second hour: Air grows drier; unseen fissures open to siphon the humidity your breathing adds.  
• Third hour onward: If your presence threatens a lichen stripe, walls close off the corridor behind you, steering you away.  Resist, and a ceiling pane will crystal-shear, dropping a curtain of spicules.  They do not strike you—merely cut off light so you retreat toward a brighter, approved path.  

No cry, no clang, only choreography.  You leave (if you leave) convinced the city had opinions about your every footfall.  Later, orbital imagers may find a new crescent of black-lithium pavement tracing exactly the route you took, as though the basin has added your memory to its map.  

VI. DESTINY OF THE BASIN  
The Unspoken Architect is young.  Models suggest that in two or three local centuries the brine network will choke itself with excess salt, then execute a final gesture: excavating a central chimney straight through the basin rim.  When the plug pops, sublimation will erupt a crystal plume kilometres high, flinging spore-towers—hollow rods packed with frozen brine and microbial code—into the jet stream.  Each fallen rod becomes a seed for a new “city,” and the cycle begins elsewhere.  The motile creatures are expendable; some will hitch rides in the hollow towers, most will perish.  The Architect, uncaring, will have replicated its mind into every seed.   

So who lives here?  
A city that is its own citizen—an intellect dispersed through brine and salt—attended by glass mites, ribbonfish, and drifting icewings that prune, polish, and fertilize its ever-growing halls.  Architecture, geology, and biology are not merely blurred: they are one individual, dreaming in latticework, rearranging its body hour by hour beneath a cold red sun.
