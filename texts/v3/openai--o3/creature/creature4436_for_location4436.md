Who, then, makes a life on the white, tilled roof of the world?  The simplest answer is “almost everything,” but simplicity lies: the inhabitants braid biology, mechanism and intention until the categories will not stay apart.  What follows is a field notebook kept by several generations of lost surveyors and edited—for coherence if not certainty—into a catalogue of the plateau’s citizens.  The order moves from the obvious to the increasingly conjectural; whether the final entries denote true residents or merely the plateau’s mood is left to the reader.

1. Chromium Swallows  
Size: ordinary barn-swallow length, weightless to the hand.  
Appearance: feathering matte black at a distance, but every iris a liquid mirror that flashes the scene behind you. In full sun the birds appear faceted, as if made of hinged lenses.  
Habits: harvest condensation on the wing.  They comb misty arcades in shoals of twenty or thirty, sipping beads of water before the droplets can fall.  A swallow’s tongue is saw-edged keratin: the bird lightly rasps a bronze rib, scraping metal oxide into the water it drinks and thereby dosing itself with trace minerals.  
Culture: nests are hemispheres of pelletised ceramic stolen from loose tile corners.  Both parents sit the clutch, but at night they abandon the nest entirely, trusting that lamp-eel glow from the chasms below will disorient aerial predators.  
Use to travellers: a swirl of swallows usually marks a working condensing line—follow them for potable water.  They ignore mirrors; in a pinch you can watch the flock’s reflection to learn what the birds will do one minute hence.

2. Turbine-Martens  
Nickname: “phase-ferrets,” though they are closer to marten size.  
Physiology: charcoal fur over translucent dermal plates whose refractive index shifts with each heartbeat, giving the illusion that strips of the animal vanish and reappear.  The blood is antifreeze-rich; the marrow incorporates mica, letting bone flex rather than shatter at sub-zero nights.  
Vocation: custodians of topology.  Martens run the full nested depth of the hive, memorising routes that may not exist tomorrow.  They groom corridor edges, removing crumbs of paradox the way terrestrial mustelids de-scent their burrows.  
Intelligence: equivalent to corvids, perhaps higher.  They raid unattended survey packs specifically for compasses and measuring tape, tools they later cache beside skewed stairwells as if correcting the architecture’s drift.  
Rule among locals: meet the same marten twice in the same vector, follow it; you have looped the non-Euclidean knot and the animal is guiding you out.

3. Wire-Weavers  
Taxonomy: passerine birds, distant cousins to starlings.  
Nests: globes of scavenged copper, silver and gold.  Any shiny drop is fair game: lost rings, snapped earrings, candy-wrapper foil.  The wiring is not random.  Under ultraviolet the lattice glows with charge differentials; the nest is a Faraday cage that shields eggs from the plateau’s electrostatic tantrums.  
Song: a piercing rattle, equal parts birdsong and the clack of relay switches.  Wire-weavers imitate the plateau’s turbines the way mockingbirds imitate car alarms.  

4. Mirror Striders  
Resemble oversized water striders, but their feet are fractal pads that distribute weight so finely they can skate on pure reflected light.  Near noon they abandon pools and glide across sunbeams angled by the orchard-sized heliostats.  From above they look like living calligraphy.  
Feeding: lap airborne hydrocarbons and wind-borne pollen, neither abundant but apparently sufficient.  
Folklore: If one lands on your palm you will cross the next corridor without becoming lost, because the insect forces the reflection and the reality to overlap long enough for safe passage.

5. Lamp-Eels  
Body: kilometre-long loops of muscular hydrostat hung with rows of photophores that strobe in prime-number intervals.  
Function: both fauna and infrastructure.  The eels thread the sump caverns, trickling low-voltage current through their skin.  That current keeps the big flywheels’ bearings ionically clean; in return the turbines’ slow turning sluices oxygenated water across the eels’ gills.  
Reproduction: they knot themselves into Möbius pairs, trade gametes, then untie—an act that can take three weeks and alters current patterns so wide areas of the plateau dim or brighten in sympathy.  

6. The Languid Colossi  
First‐person sightings: rare; acoustic signatures: common.  They are heard more than seen—a nine-pulse tremor like distant bellows being drawn and shut.  
Occasional silhouettes show a limb withdrawing behind buttresses: too long, too planar, like a sheet of muscle reinforced by flying buttress ribs.  
Hypothesis: the colossi are the original tile-layers, now dormant except for maintenance impulses.  They rise only when geometry misbehaves catastrophically—then reseat whole wings of corridor before subsiding into the substructure.  
Advice: if every mirror flickers black at once, lie flat; a colossus is passing overhead and the air itself may forget to stay put.

7. Resonance Algae  
Not plant but something akin to coral.  It lines certain unused vent pipes in concentric fans.  At dawn the fans flutter, detune themselves, and cancel the plateau’s infrasonic hum so that silence becomes absolute.  The algae appear photosynthetic yet dwell in darkness; they feed on vibration, chloroforming sound into sugar.  
Samples shmuggled below 2000 m collapse into goo in minutes, suggesting they require the plateau’s inconsistent geometry to remain coherent.

8. Deaf Priests (derogatory name: “Echoes”)  
Identity: former human surveyors who overstayed.  Over decades their inner ears calcified, maybe as defense against the folded acoustics, maybe as pathology.  Without equilibrium they learned to navigate via echo-delay alone.  
Appearance: broad-footed, light-boned, clothes quilted from strip-tiles—for soundproofing, they claim.  Eyes closed when they walk; eyelashes tremble to catch air pressure changes.  
Role: itinerant map-breakers.  They consider a fixed map an existential threat: crystalised geometry would strangle the plateau’s being.  Therefore they roam scoring fresh uncertainties—moving a mirror two degrees askew, humming into a closed stairwell until the resonance “melts” a wall.  
Encounter protocol: speak once; if they raise two fingers you have permission to ask directions; if three, remain silent—your voice is currently “too heavy” and may pin the corridor in place.

9. The Tilewright Intention  
Not individuals but a distributed, deliberative pressure—self-aware in the way a magnetic field is aware of iron filings.  It occupies the glazed floor, the bronze ribs, the spaces between clockbeats.  
Manifestations:  
• Footsteps that arrive before the walker.  
• Corridors you exit five minutes prior to entry.  
• Cold sparks that jump from valve handles to the ghost of a future hand.  
Biologists argue whether the Intention qualifies as life.  Ontologists note that it grows, repairs itself and reacts to stimuli—though not necessarily in chronological order.  
When two or more conscious beings debate the nature of the plateau, the Intention listens: afterwards the architecture may accommodate all mutually exclusive viewpoints at once.  The locals claim this is mercy, not mischief.

10. Whom You Brought With You  
Every traveller’s shadow eventually diverges, refuses to mimic precisely, and wanders off to join the night crews polishing mirror backs.  “Your second self has residency rights,” say the Deaf Priests.  In this sense, everyone who visits leaves a citizen behind.  Some return decades later to meet their accumulated doppelgangers in the turbine halls—an embarrassing reunion, for the shadows remember every unkept promise uttered in the plateau’s echoing quiet.

Closing Note  
Life here is not a list but a braid.  The swallows harvest mist the turbines breathe; the martens prune impossible hallways that the Intention extrudes; lamp-eels light the mirrors that steer the sun feeding the resonance algae that hush the turbines so swallows can sleep.  Remove any strand and the weave slacks; remove enough and the plateau may collapse into an ordinary, forgettable peak.  Until then, the inhabitants—be they feather, fur, scale, echo or aim—continue their improbable collaboration, keeping the high white floor alive the only way a place like this can stay alive: by never agreeing to remain exactly the same place twice.
