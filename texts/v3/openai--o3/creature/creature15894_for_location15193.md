Nothing that lives here looks familiar at first glance; the pocket is too new, too cold, too hard for anything designed for sunlit valleys. Instead it has been colonized by pioneer biologies that use the mountain’s own instabilities the way most creatures use food and firelight. Seen over weeks, three very different orders reveal themselves.

────────────────────────
1. The Wall-Moss Conglomerate
────────────────────────

Think of an iceberg re-imagined as vegetation: a pale, almost bluish tissue spread thin over the rock face, yet ninety-five percent of its substance hides inside fractures and microscopic pores. The green felt we see is only the breathing skin of a sprawling organism that threads the whole pocket together.

• Architecture  
  Hyphae—transparent, quartz-stiff tubes—enter the stone along existing cleavage planes, then secrete carbonic acids that propagate new cracks in the direction of heat and nutrient flow. In effect, the moss slowly quarries its own root bed, carving capillaries in which meltwater and mineral dust can circulate.

• Metabolism  
  Photosynthesis is supplemental. The main calorie source is chemolithotrophy: the hyphae strip electrons from ferrous ions in fresh basalt, exhaling faint rust that streaks the walls like old blood. Lightning strikes, when they happen, are jackpots; the current races along the wet rock and supercharges every hyphal filament, letting the organism fix months’ worth of carbon in a single white flash.

• Behavior  
  Vibration is both information and nutrient to it. A falling slab sends a pressure wave through the rock that the hyphae interpret as thunder through bone. Where the wave decelerates—the near-surface spots that will likely shear loose next—the moss thickens, reinforcing future habitat. To an outside observer the pattern looks prophetic: green halos form exactly around tomorrow’s rockfalls.

• Reproduction  
  Once a year, during the coldest week, the moss releases a mist of magnetite-coated spores. The iron dust makes them electrically sensitive. They drift upward, cling to the cliff walls, and wait for the next lightning fork to fuse some of them into embryonic mats. Others are carried off on the downdrafts to seed neighboring cracks.

In a sense the conglomerate is the pocket’s nervous system. Everything that follows grows in the ecological afterglow it produces—thin films of ferrous sludge, pockets of decaying sheath, the faint warmth of exothermic mineral digestion.

────────────────────────
2. The Shardlings
────────────────────────

Where the moss is the resident engineer, Shardlings are the restless tenants. They are animal—loosely—and always in motion, yet almost impossible to detect until they fracture.

• Morphology  
  Picture a brittle starfish distilled into five translucent digits, each digit a wafer of silicate reinforced with collagen strands. Centered where the arms meet lies a lentil-sized gas bladder. A Shardling weighs less than a maple seed; when it flexes its arms the wafer-blades generate autorotative lift, letting it glide on the rising thermals that surge after every rock impact.

• Birth and Life Cycle  
  They gestate inside the wall-moss. Chemical cues from the host trigger moss tissue to fold into little alveoli, each nuo-green pocket cradling a gelatin egg. A hard knock—thunder, water hammer, or the boom of a detaching boulder—bursts the alveoli and flings the hatchlings into the air like shrapnel. The newborns are immediately airborne, twirling upward through the rubble mist.

  For two to four days they drift, sipping aerosolized nutrients: moss exudate, rock dust, trace metals dissolved in the never-ending drizzle. Their bodies remain soft until their first lightning encounter; the flash heat anneals the silica in their arms, leaving them rigid and glass-bright.

• Feeding  
  A Shardling never lands by choice. Its ventral surface weeps a chelating mucous that absorbs ions straight from the mist. The arms beat not only for flight but also for filtration: each spin drags air across the mucous sheet, concentrating the sparse calories of this newborn canyon.

• Death  
  The same annealing that hardens them dooms them. After three, maybe five cycles of heating, the arms craze with micro-fissures. One more sonic boom—and the wafer palms snap. The creature falls as glitter, five separate glass darts. Where they land, the shards leach silica and protein that the wall-moss drinks up, closing the loop.

  Every flash of sunlight you think you glimpse out of the corner of your eye down here—those tiny sparks that vanish before the brain can focus—are almost certainly Shardlings, pinwheeling between oblivion and the stone sky.

────────────────────────
3. The Echo-Eaters
────────────────────────

Below the dripping shelves, in the temporary blackwater pools, live the only organisms here that mae a sound of their own. You never see them, only feel them: a braided tremor across the soles when you stand on a ledge above their water.

• Physical Form  
  Echo-Eaters resemble ribbon worms but are part mineral, part flesh. Their skins are lattices of goethite crystals embedded in gelatinous muscle. The crystals act as resonators; when struck by incoming acoustic energy, they vibrate and convert that vibration into faint electrical current, which in turn fuels the worm’s metabolism.

• Habitat Dynamics  
  Because pools appear and vanish, the worms can desiccate without dying. When water drains away they exude a snot-like wrap that seals in a teardrop of moisture. In this torpor they can endure a year of drought. The next cascade re-hydrates them, and they unroll like measuring tapes dropped into the basin.

• Diet of Sound  
  Their favorite meal is the multi-tone funder of a new rockfall. One worm anchors its tail in mud, lifts its head, and opens a slit running nearly its whole length. Inside, membranous folds ripple like vocal cords in reverse, absorbing low-frequency rumbles that travel through the water and stone. A large enough detonation can swell a worm to twice its slack girth in minutes.

  Researchers (there have only been three) who lowered hydrophones into the pools recorded what they thought was submarine machinery: syncopated whining, gearlike revs, sometimes a grinding crescendo that made the headphones shiver. All Echo-Eaters, feasting.

• Reproduction  
  They broadcast tiny seed-crystals during storms. Each seed is a pinhead of iron oxide wrapped in protein. Most lodge uselessly in cracks, but a handful settle in new puddles, germinate, and weave their first soft tissue around the mineral heart.

Once or twice a decade, when two or more Echo-Eaters grow very large in the same basin, they knot together into a single cable. The fusion gradually stiffens until the pair become a rod of stone-flesh cemented across the pool bed—a permanent tuning fork the wall-moss will later colonize. Thus the worms gradually armor the basin floor, reducing erosion and ensuring their descendants a longer-lived auditorium.

────────────────────────
How They Interrelate
────────────────────────

1. Wall-moss excretes ferrous sludge → seeds both Shardlings (via nutrient pockets) and Echo-Eaters (via iron-rich pools).  
2. Shardlings aerosolize moss exudate while gliding, giving the moss access to air-borne resource feedback loops. Their shattered bodies feed the moss directly.  
3. Echo-Eaters anchor pools in place, slowing scouring erosion; more stable water means more surface for moss colonization, which in turn means more hatcheries for Shardlings.  

The three orders, taken together, behave like a single organism broken into mobile, sessile, and fluid-phase organs. No predator disturbs them. Their only enemy is time—time that will widen the crack, warm the light, invite wings and teeth and roots thick enough to drink their cisterns dry. Until then the cathedral pocket belongs to them, a triune colony forging life from bare echo, lightning, and the metallic taste of brand-new stone.
