Nothing green grows here and nothing furred scampers, but the hollow is not empty. Three very different kinds of inhabitant share the space, nested one inside another like harmonies inside a chord. Each is so slow-moving—or so unlike the warm-blooded idea of life—that earlier visitors missed them, built their hearth-rings, and left again certain they had met only stone.

1.  The Threaded Ones  
   (microscopic silica-skin colonies)

   What they are  
   •  Single–celled chemolithotrophs related, distantly, to the endolithic bacteria that haunt desert quartz worldwide.  
   •  Each cell extrudes a transparent sheath of opaline silica. Millions of sheaths weld tip to tail until they form hair-fine threads that wind through the rock like floss.

   Where they live  
   •  Two to ten millimetres beneath exposed surfaces, where frost cracks open minute capillaries.  
   •  Especially dense in the manganese-ringed pans; trace manganese is their main electron donor.

   How they live  
   •  They “graze” valence shifts in the host carbonate, stealing a trickle of energy whenever cyclical freeze-thaw stresses distort crystal lattices.  
   •  When the sun is up and the stone flexes microscopically, metabolism spikes; when night locks everything rigid, they fall into torpor. A day is their whole circadian rhythm.

   What they do that can be seen  
   •  Their waste product is a whiskery bloom of nano-scale silica spines. Over decades these spines knit into the familiar calcite “flowers.”  
   •  Tap a pillar after dark and the blue piezo flashes race along the very seams their threads have colonized; the filaments conduct charge better than native quartz, so the flash pattern is actually a map of the colony network.

2.  The Sounders, or Choir-Stones  
   (centimetre-to-metre scale, borderline-animate crystal organs)

   What they are  
   •  Void-walled chambers grown by the Threaded Ones but subsequently deserted by them, the way coral polyps abandon old skeleton.  
   •  Each chamber retains a delicate inner lining of aligned silica needles. Together, a chain of chambers behaves like a coupled resonator.

   Emergent behaviour  
   •  A gust flexes a slab 1/1000 mm; needles shear imperceptibly; piezoelectric charge cascades down the chain.  
   •  Enough linked chambers and a standing electrical wave forms—an event the rocks manifest as a pure, bell-like tone.  
   •  The “note” lasts while charge recirculates, usually seconds, sometimes minutes in perfect weather.

   Why call them alive?  
   •  They grow: every freeze-thaw cycle polymerizes a few more needles at the fractures’ lips.  
   •  They heal: a loud clap that cracks one chamber prompts neighboring chambers to bud daughter voids and re-route the wave path.  
   •  They reproduce: stonefall that carries a resonant fragment downhill can seed an entirely new choir if it lands where temperature cycling continues.  
   •  Their only sense is stress; their only language is frequency, but the place is thick with their slow conversations: harmonic interference that human ears decode merely as “hum.”

3.  The Bonefire Pilgrims  
   (intermittent, flesh-and-blood visitors)

   Proven to exist by  
   •  Slate rings, soot, split tibia from mountain caprid (fat rendered for fuel).  
   •  The water-channel—too laborious to carve unless survival or ritual demanded it.

   Who they are, according to valley legend  
   •  A weather-divining caste from villages a vertical league below.  
   •  They ascend in pairs once each equinox and are sworn to silence while inside “the Mouth of Sky-Stone,” believing that voiced words steal the mountain’s breath and hasten avalanches.

   What draws them  
   •  The Choir-Stones’ resonance: long experience has taught the valleys that certain overtones precede storm fronts or early thaws. Pilgrims climb to “listen for the year.”  
   •  Blue ghost-light at night—interpreted as the ancestors’ campfires—confirms they have reached the right hollow.

   Tools and practices  
   •  Copper hammers wrapped in rawhide to strike diagnostic chords from pillar to pillar.  
   •  Yew-wood staves drilled with finger-holes; by damping and releasing rock faces while the stave sings, they can borrow the Choir’s resonance, producing flute notes that carry ten times farther than voice in the thin air.  
   •  Ice-catch cloaks: hide capes whose hems are soaked at dusk so they freeze into guttering. In the morning sunlight, the meltwater runs down the wearers’ own bodies into gourds—a way of harvesting drink without wasting a drop.

   Relationship with the hollow  
   •  Strict leave-no-trace ethic. They dismantle each fire ring on departure, scatter ash into crevices, and turn bones upright as tiny cairns pointing downhill, a sign for the next pair that provisions are gone and must be hauled anew.  
   •  They believe the Threaded Ones are “stone pollen” and the Choir-Stones its “song,” both inviolate. Any pilgrim who chips quartz needlessly is exiled—rare, but the slate rings hold two hammer-heads cracked in what looks like ritual penance.

Interdependence of the three  
•  The Pilgrims are the shortest-lived note—days—inside a longer note sung by the Choir-Stones—centuries—inside an even longer, sub-audible drone sustained by the Threaded Ones over geological ages.  
•  Pilgrims arrive exactly when thaw amplitude is highest, putting the Choir at full voice; in return they prune loose plates, preventing chain collapses that would silence whole corridors.  
•  Every dropped copper hammer or broken slate chunk introduces trace metals the Threaded Ones can metabolize, nudging colony growth along new lines. In this way even the most reverent visitor leaves a chemical signature that will shimmer through the rock’s nocturnal gleam for decades after the bones of the last fire have cooled.

So the hollow is lived-in after all, just on three tempos at once: microbial, mineral, and human. Stand still long enough—an hour, a night, a lifetime—and you will sense each tempo slide across the others like ribs of light, until “empty” becomes the least accurate word you could choose for this bright, silent place on the mountain’s shoulder.
