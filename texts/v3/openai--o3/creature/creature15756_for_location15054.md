Who - or what - lives in the Hydrosmith’s Ampule?  
Below is a naturalist’s roll-call, tier by tier, beginning with the things too small to see and ending with the one resident that cannot be collected at all.

I. The Makers of Breath (0.1 µm – 2 mm)

1. Glistenfilm  
Single-celled chromophytes that plate themselves with mirror flakes of iron-oxide. By day the plates angle light into the cell; by night they pivot flat to shed accumulated heat. When a flake tears free it falls like glitter through the column, seeding every surface with oxygen factories.

2. Threadforge Consortium  
A tri-party biofilm of bacteria, archaea, and rust fungi. Bacteria drill into any free ferrous metal, archaea reduce the leached ions, and fungal hyphae knit the ions into nano-wires that conduct a faint current back to the colony. Seen under magnification, the seafloor is spider-webbed with these living circuits.

II. The Fixed Green (2 cm – 3 m)

3. Fan-ferns  
Fronds like lace parasols, anchored to latticework railings. Each leaflet is hollow; during the 18-hour photoperiod it inflates with O₂, making the entire plant stand erect and stiff. Lights off, gas vents and the fan collapses, shedding a snow of spores that bottom feeders prize.

4. Heliohelix Ropes  
Bundles of translucent tubes that twist into cables ten meters long. Interior cilia pump captured bubbles tipward; when a rope has stored all it can carry it kinks, snaps, and the upper half rockets toward the stopper, releasing a white plume that resembles a breach in surf.

5. Shankweed  
Looks like lengths of frayed electrical tape flapping in the convection streams. The “fray” is reproductive tissue; when grazers nip a strand the severed scrap curls into a seedpod and sinks until it snags on another bit of metal.

III. The Upper Glow (0 .5 mm – 4 cm)

6. Silver Needlers  
Bristles with eyes at both ends, each eye seeing forward and back simultaneously. Propulsion comes from ion pumps along the lateral line; no tail beat, just a marble-run of bubbles. A school turns as one because each fish can see what the fish behind it has already decided.

7. Filter Efts  
Larval amphibians that will never metamorphose; their genome has discarded adulthood as unnecessary luggage. They graze standing columns of plankton, secrete powdered bone as ballast, then jettison the ballast in panic bursts to slingshot away from predators.

8. Skitterwings  
Insects in function, jellyfish in phylogeny. The umbrella is reduced to a sculling keel; six chitinous “wings” row like oars. Entire swarms live in the clear convection jets, riding upward drafts until lights-off flips the current and drops them like hail.

IV. The Middle Terraces (2 cm – 25 cm)

9. Shutter Clams  
Half their mass is muscle devoted to slamming the twin shells shut. The impact stuns small pursuers and also shakes loose rust flakes that the clam then filters for trace iron. Each shell grows the embossed letters of the plate it first colonized; museum collectors covet specimens that spell a recognizable word.

10. Skeletal Shrimp  
Transparent except for a ladder of calcium nodes that look like vertebrae—hence “skeletal.” They orbit any source of falling detritus, trimming particles to uniform size. Where they abound the water column is as evenly screened as sifted flour.

11. Clangers  
Crabs whose dorsal carapace mimics whatever machine part they have most recently worn as armor. A Clanger might appear to be bearing a fly-wheel, a cotter pin, even a length of chain. They hammer scraps against hardpan not to break them open but to listen: metallic resonance tells a Clanger whether the scrap still contains edible lubricants.

V. The Lower Dusk (30 cm – 1 m)

12. Spine Eels  
Ribbons of cartilage threaded through with copper ions; every pulse of the lighting circuit writes a ghost current along the spine, letting the eel sense the coming on/off cycle seconds before it happens. They migrate by corkscrewing upshaft at Lights-Off, downs haft at Lights-On, swallowing anything slower than the spiral.

13. Valve Hermits  
Descendants of shore crabs that long ago forsook natural shells. They occupy hand-sized check-valves, gauging interior volume by stroking the cavity with hairs on the third walking leg. When growth forces eviction, the crab leaves a scent trail that younger hermits follow in reverse— a used-valve market in perpetual circulation.

14. Turbojellies  
The nearest thing the ampule has to an apex predator. Umbrella folded, they resemble spinning grenades; unfolded, a blinking chandelier. The blue chem-flash is not light but an exothermic temperature spike that parboils prey in the instant before ingestion. A reef of half-digested bones and metal tags grows wherever a mature turbojelly chooses to die.

VI. The Unboxed Resident (scale indeterminate)

15. The Reciter  
No specimen, no photograph—only evidence. When the ampule is placed in acoustic quiet, a thread of Morse-like taps emerges, coded in micro-stutters of pressure that run the length of the glass. Patterns quote the rhythmic signatures of pumps, clams, Clangers, even human heartbeats recorded by stethoscope against the wall. Aquarium-quieted Shutter Clams have been observed answering in tempo. The Reciter may be:

a) a colony mind of Threadforge nano-wires,  
b) residual software in the corroded coils,  
c) nothing more than patterned echo between ribs.

Whatever it is, it never pauses longer than six beats—the Hydrosmith’s unbroken metronome, keeping time for a toy ocean still inventing ways to live inside its borrowed bottle.

These are the inhabitants: from oxide-plated microbes up to a rumor made of pulse and echo, all of them bound to thirty centimeters of tropical brine and a gravity that points the wrong way—but none, so far, aware of the world beyond the warm glass that holds their sea in its palm.
