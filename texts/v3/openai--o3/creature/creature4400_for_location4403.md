Who Lives in the Breathing Hollow

1. The Hollow-Folk (who call themselves “Naleki”)

Population
• always between 170 and 190 breathing bodies.  No census has ever returned another number, though babies arrive and elders leave each season.  
• two-thirds are “stone-kin,” born in Nalek; the rest are marriage partners or drift-ins from the roaming ice tribes above.

Bodies
• Short, lithe, tendon-strong.  Children grow up climbing rawhide ladders; every adult can cling to slick limestone one-handed while palming a basket with the other.  
• Skin: pearl at birth, then slowly mottles the greens and rusts of the moss they tend—a cosmetic staining no soap removes.  Visitors think it tattoo; it is simply life’s varnish.  
• Hair: fine, colourless, habitually bound close so no strand snags on ivy.  A Naleki unbinding her hair is signalling intimacy or war; neither happens in public.  
• Ears: a little larger than average and able to pivot.  In a world where talk is soft, hearing is livelihood.  
• Voice: deep-in-the-chest murmur, never above the hush of a falling bead of water.

Dress
• Tunics and leggings cut from storm-gull skin—silvery gray turns almost invisible against the walls’ glare.  
• Wide belts woven of birch bark hold knives, adzes, signal slates.  
• Anklets of knucklebone identify clans by rhythm rather than colour: each clan has a signature click for greeting in the near-silence.

Language
• “Nalekisasa,” half soft consonants, half hand, whole body.  A sentence may begin with breath, peak in gesture, and finish in fingertip taps against a companion’s wrist.  
• There is no word for “echo.”  There are fourteen for “brightness,” eight for “drip,” and a verb that means “to do just enough.”

Lifeways
• Dawn (blue-white hour): moss-combing, frog-ditch cleaning, birch-tower watch.  
• Goldlight: platform repair, trading baskets hauled up the shaft, communal meal of root-paste and fish slivers.  
• Silver hush: story-signing, weaving, quiet courting among the ivy.  
• Night (still silver): dream-keeping.  Three sleepers at a time are nudged awake in rotation to note how the Hollow breathed while the rest dozed; those records steer the next day’s gutter work.

Occupations
a) Gutter-menders – keep the drip obedient. They inherit copperless tools whose edges are re-knapped daily.  
b) Moss-tenders – hum the growing songs.  Their pitches are so low newcomers feel them as ribcage vibration rather than sound.  
c) Lattice-watch – climb the fifteen-man-height tower and mirror-flash the traders on the plain rim; lowest rung for students, topmost for the soon-to-die who wish to greet the outside wind once more.  
d) Frog-wardens – childhood job.  A dozen seven-to-ten-year-olds herd tadpoles with reed sticks, learning stewardship by sharing water first with “the ones who were here before us.”

Birth & Death
• Birth: mother sits in a wall-niche, knees to chest, so infant drops forthright into her own hands; the placenta is laid in the moss directly, stitching new breath into old ground.  
• Death: corpse seated upright in an unused platform corner.  Over four days the constant mist washes skin to polished bone.  On day five, bone beads are strung into the clan-anklets; the rest is carried through the floor slot to feed the unseen current below.

Government (so small they hardly use the word)
• Six task-circles (one per rule of living, plus a spare).  Each circle elects two quietest members, never the loudest.  Debate by slate and chalk; chalk saved by limiting every proposal to seven written strokes.

Faith
• They don’t worship the Hollow; they reciprocate.  The place breathes; they keep its lungs clear.  The closest ritual to prayer is the nightly drip-count: adults pause, match pulse to patter, and think one grateful thought.  Outsiders call it superstition; cardiologists visiting once called it “impossibly low heart rates.”

2. The Small Multitudes

Snow-sparrows
• Bill flattened, song eroded to a rasp.  Nest anywhere a silence pocket forms; householders welcome them as living burglar alarms—if a sparrow calls, some larger intruder has broken the hush.

Glass-Frogs
• Not actually transparent but filmed with iridescence from limestone minerals.  Lit by ricocheted skylight they look like dropped beads.  Their eggs are harvested, two per household per thaw, for medicine against lung chill.

White-Weasels
• Semi-tame.  Each platform claims one as mouser.  If it disappears, two families must trade pets for balance—another unspoken law.

Moss-Mites, Ivy-Ants, Drip-Midges
• Individually insignificant, collectively a weather system.  Naleki children learn to diagnose gutter failure by which insect is suddenly breeding out of ratio.

3. The Quiet Engine (what the Naleki admit exists but do not name loudly)

The Hollow-Breath
• A pressure wave no instrument has measured yet.  Every 47 heartbeats walls flex inward the width of a hair, bowls more light, exhale a scent of wet stone, then settle.  Old folk lean into it the way sailors lean into deck-tilt.  Some say it is the lungs of a buried glacier, others a stone beast too large to wake fully.

The Number-Drift
• Count seven ladders today, perhaps eight tomorrow though none has moved.  Scholars from the rim blame refraction and fatigue; Naleki mothers teach it as a lesson: the Hollow dislikes possession, even of integers.

The Listening Moss
• Hum to it and it fattens.  Shout and it goes pale.  After an outsider once fired a pistol in demonstration, every moss cushion within six paces died overnight, leaving a chalk silhouette of silence.

4. Visitors

The Seal-Nomads
• Leather-draped, laughter-loud, they prefer to stay on the lip where sound can expand.  They lower trade baskets but will not enter; the one time a youth descended on a dare he resurfaced wordless, palms scarred with new lines that bled saltwater, and has not spoken since.

The Upper Engineers
• Three expeditions in recorded time, each intent on measuring the impossible luminosity.  Their metal tripods tarnished in days; their chronometers lost minutes then hours.  They left politely, packs bulging with moss samples that withered to powder once carried beyond sight of the sinkhole.

5. Legends of What May Come

• Should the lattice tower ever touch the sky’s centre point, the Hollow will sigh out its last light and become an ordinary pit.  
• If a child is born with no shadow (rare but rumored), that child will one day count the ladders correctly and the platforms will freeze in number forever.  
• The frogs remember an older climate and will sing it back the night before any true catastrophe.

Until then the Naleki live as they have: knees damp, voices low, measuring the bright hush between drips and believing, with reason, that nothing breaks; it only continues.
