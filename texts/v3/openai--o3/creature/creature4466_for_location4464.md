A living list is all we can offer; nothing here has yet settled into final shapes or finished names.  The mountain ocean is still sketching its cast in warm water and thin air, and any catalogue written today will be outdated before the next season’s rain.  Still, this is the best census we have of the beings that already call the plateau home.

I.  Seed-light and Salt-breath  
1.  Prism plankton: single cells whose glassy halos split sunlight into pastel rings.  They crowd the upper metre, trading pigments with neighbours the way villages trade stories.  From a height their mingled colours paint slow barcodes across the water.  
2.  Zinc-spirals: helices of metabolising metal that grow only where fresh ash still clouds the shallows.  They are neither plant nor animal—more a mood of chemistry—but everything that comes later grazes on the trace vitamins they leak.  
3.  Heat ghosts: transparent bacterial veils that bloom each night when the forests exhale warmth.  They live for six hours, fall apart at dawn, and snow down as nutrient rain.

II.  The Architects—Plants That Behave Like Continents  
1.  Banner trees (the dominant algal giants already noted): Each trunk is a stack of watertight air-bladders, so an adult always owns one extra metre of height beyond its rivals.  Their canopies raft together until wind tears them apart, after which the torn edge sprouts buds that rebuild the gap within a week.  
2.  Lace-ferns: hydraulic, yes, but also social.  Every frond carries pressure nodules that let neighbouring ferns know exactly when they have reached equilibrium; an entire copse can fold or unfurl in the span of a heartbeat, leaving predators lunging at empty water.  
3.  Horizon creepers: rain-fed vines that march outward from any rock spike.  The weight of a single mature creeper can counterbalance three tonnes of water held inside capillary arches, so wherever the “impossible” higher-than-gravity ponds occur, creepers girdle their rims like living retaining walls.  
4.  Lantern fungus: grows on dead creeper stems, stores photons in layered oils, then vents the light at night in cool amber flashes.  The rhythm is slower than a pulse but faster than a tide, and whole lagoons can beat together like sleeping hearts.

III.  Small Animals, Numerous but Unhurried  
1.  Glass-eels: the scouts of river systems far below.  Their bodies are clear but for a line of silver periscopic scales that telescope upward when they rise to breathe.  They feed on prism plankton; their waste, rich in metals, triggers zinc-spiral blooms—a loop unbroken since year two of the flood.  
2.  Basalt crickets: amphipods that colonised the new stone during the first storms.  They rasp the rock for lichens and sing by flicking water droplets off their thigh plates; the pitch drops as air pressure changes, so a hillside of crickets can forecast the afternoon deluge to within five minutes.  
3.  Mist-moths: soft, palm-sized rays that use hydrophobic scales to trap a film of water over their gills while they spiral through the cloud layer.  When the film dries they parachute back to the surface with a sigh like wind in sails.

IV.  Mid-Water Drifters—Life in the Jade Belt  
1.  Chapterfish: long, ribbon-bodied hunters whose skins carry rows of piebald squares.  Each square flips from pale to dark when the fish turns, so a pursuing eye sees a stuttering pattern instead of a continuous form—predation disguised as punctuation.  
2.  Needle congregations: not individuals but bundles of magnetite filaments that knot into spear-shapes two metres long.  Sun-heated currents charge them; at random moments the bundles twitch and shoot ten, twenty metres in any direction, scattering everything nearby with sonic pops.  Afterward they drift again, cooling, waiting for the next convulsion.  
3.  Pylon polyps: sessile jelly towers that choose the sheerest cliff faces.  Their stinging threads extend thirty metres upslope; creepers lace through them unharmed, but any animal touching a thread finds itself paralysed and gently reeled in like caught silk.

V.  Citizens of the Capillary Arches  
1.  Arch-hermits: translucent crabs whose abdomens have grown to cup the wrist-thick water bridges.  They clamp around an arch and drink from it directly; minerals precipitate inside their shells, armouring them in porcelain swirls that mimic the ripples above.  
2.  Whistleworms: meter-long annelids that live upside-down beneath the bridges.  They gulp water on the “higher” side and jet it into the lower valley, singing as the flow vibrates through chitin vanes.  Their constant siphoning may partly explain why the flood’s logic defies gravity.  
3.  Step-sparrows: birds in waiting.  They are still evolving towards flight; at the moment they run the arches on four elongated toes, vaulting gaps that would kill anything heavier.  Their feathers are scale-stiff and pitch black—perfect solar collectors for the chill nights.

VI.  The Heat Harvesters (after dark)  
1.  Pulse mantas: thin as a bedsheet, five metres tip to tip.  By day they hang inert a few metres above the canopy, kite-strings of mucus anchoring them to banner-tree crowns.  When the forests exhale heat, the mantas shiver awake, their undersides glowing vermilion as they absorb the thermal surge.  By midnight they have banked enough energy to dart in slow loops, scooping airborne plankton until dawn drops them limp again.  
2.  Furnace shrimp: scarlet zigzags that live only in the mantas’ shadow.  They press their bellies to the ray’s skin, harvesting micron-scale leaks of heat through specialised vent plates.  No specimen has been found more than three centimetres from a manta at any time of day.

VII.  Rumours and Rarities  
1.  Verdigris grazer: witnessed twice at dawn, once at dusk.  Size: “a barge with knees.”  Colour: oxidised copper.  Behaviours: rises vertically through ten metres of canopy, mouths the youngest leaf clumps, then sinks without a ripple.  Hypotheses range from an undiscovered manatee cousin to a slow-growing colonial slug steered by distributed nerve rings.  
2.  The Turning Thing: never seen, only heard—a corkscrew churn deep below that lasts exactly seven minutes and returns every nineteen days.  After each episode, fresh talus stripes appear on the nearest submerged escarpment, as though whole wall sections had been planed off from within.  
3.  Cloudward Eyes: occasional twin sparks far above the mist in the hour before storms, moving too slowly for insects, too erratically for drones.  Some swear they watch back; others think they are only the sun’s reflection in condensate lenses.

VIII.  Decomposers, the Silent Multitude  
1.  Quilt slimes: fungal-algal amalgams that stitch themselves across dead creeper rafts.  Their surfaces bubble with methane and sweet esters, drawing in swarms of glass-eels that chew passage holes.  When a raft finally sinks, the slime mats balloon, catching enough gas to float away as foot-balloon colonies, starting new decay sites kilometres off.  
2.  Threadfin vultures: not birds but fish that specialise in carrion drifting just below the air-water boundary.  Their fins have become multi-filament tassels; each tassel can taste for rot, so a single vulture can map an entire hectare of surface soup while barely moving.  
3.  Ash vaulters: mites with spring-loaded tails.  They collect volcanic ash from bare rock and fling it into the upper water column where microbes find the minerals irresistible.  A vaulter that fails to vault simply starves, so the species is engaged in an endless gymnastic panic that, incidentally, seeds half the plateau with iron and silica.

IX.  The Solitary Pragmatists—Visitors from Beyond  
Very rarely a human researcher or a highland herder strays here on balloon-raft or thermal glider.  They leave almost nothing behind: one camp-ring of crushed creeper stems, a coil of measurement tape caught by mist-moths, a single footprint that fills with warm rain and is gone in an hour.  The locals treat them as transient weather—curious, perhaps useful, but unlikely to stay.

X.  A Closing Note on Futures  
Everything listed above is provisional.  The plateau is still rising; the lakes inside the ridges still invent their own levels; tonight’s heat pulse will write invitations to microbes not yet conceived.  When you return—should you manage to leave at all—you will find new architects, different rumours, and whole animal phyla that decided, in your absence, that they too could belong to a warm ocean in the mountains.

Until then, remember that nothing here is truly finished.  Life is still slipping wet clay from its moulds, deciding which shapes it likes best.  To witness that choosing is to stand, briefly, at creation’s side—quiet, ankle-deep, listening to the world make up its mind.
