The Hollow-Folk  

1. What they call themselves  
Among their own rafters they are simply the Many—an old word meaning “interwoven lengths of reed.” Outsiders from the marshes say sievekin or chalk-lungs. They answer to either without offense, as long as the speaker keeps a respectful hush.

2. Bodies made for damp corners  
They look like people thinned by weather. Skin: river-pearl pale, stippled with darker freckles along the ribs where shirts stay wet longest. Hair: wiry, grows in uneven shocks that frizz the moment mist touches it; youths slick theirs flat with eel oil so wind can’t snag on ladders. Toes spread wide, the middle pair webbed halfway to the first joint. A newborn’s fontanel stays unsealed for nearly two years, pulsing faintly when storms pile pressure over the bowl; midwives judge a child’s future sensitivity by that tremor.  

Ears are the most telling part. Each lobe ends in a thin, flexible flange that can be folded tight or fanned open. When they work on high scaffold they fold them, to dull the roar of slate cracking. When they walk the gutters they flare them, feeling for the minute hiss that means a vent has unclogged below.

3. Dress and ornament  
Clothing must dry fast or never dry at all. They weave tunics from split reed skin and marsh-grass bast, then coat the cloth in a whisper of salt-ash that keeps mold from rooting. Color comes late: indigo traded from upriver, ochre ground from abandoned hollows, thin green streaks boiled out of willow bark. No garment is sewn closed; sleeves tie on with rawhide so a soaked piece can be peeled away before weight drags the wearer off a walkway.  

Metal is scarce, flaunted sparingly. A thumb-sized iron stud at the collarbone marks a house elder; two mark a craft-judge; three, set in a triangle, are worn only by a gutter-ward—a person licensed to hammer new routes for water. Children beg to touch the studs, as if iron might rub off on their fingers.

4. Tongue and timbre  
Speech seldom rises above library hush. Their consonants are glottal taps, vowels short and curtained with breath. What outsiders take for shyness is calculation: every unnecessary syllable is another droplet shaken loose from a beam. For distance they use arm-drums—tight eel skin over hollow willow rods. Two strokes mean “weight shift,” three quick taps “incoming surge,” and a rolling palm “all clear.” At night the drums are traded for hand-mirrors. They flash the frog-light back and forth in blinks that travel roof to roof like moths.

5. Households, lines, agreements  
A platform and everything that hangs directly beneath it make a “tier.” A tier may host three birth-lines and twice as many lodgers. Who sleeps where is fluid; who tends which gutter or stilt is not. Every dusk, just before the light goes completely grey, the tier council squats on its driest plank and recites the Seam-list, a litany of every joint that felt soft that day. Those with two free hours volunteer repairs; those with only one hour trade eel meat or slate shards for someone else’s labor. No gift is weighed by size—only by time promised dry.

6. Food, work, small economies  
Stomach staples:  
• Tubers from the rim terrace, sliced thin and salt-smoked so they keep.  
• Eel, cubed raw and pressed overnight between sheets of nacreous salt until firm.  
• A lichen wafer called shelf-bark—ground moss mixed with willow ash, spread on a hot slate rib, and peeled off once it curls.  

Work flows with the water table. High table: scraping salt, planing new slate knives, turning carpentry spindles. Low table: reed harvest, eel tending, throat-well patrol. The salt-glow scraped from cistern rims buys indigo, iron dust, and once a year a whetstone big enough for every knife in the Hollow to kiss.

7. The way lives unroll  
Infants spend their first months in hanging cradles, rocked by any breath of wind that sneaks past the ridge. The rocking teaches balance. By three, a child is trusted on the lowest ramps with a moss knife. By seven, they can swim blindfold through the flooded crawlspaces beneath the oldest tiers.  

Courtship is a matter of shared chores. A pair (sometimes a trio) signs their intent by installing a new rain-lip under an elder’s roof—if the lip keeps the wall dry for one full storm, the union is blessed. Funerals are held swiftly: body bound in reed mats, weighted with broken slate tools, and lowered into a throat-well on the ebb tide so it drifts out beneath the shale roots. Two nights later, kin listen at the shaft for the cavern’s answering belch, then dissolve the household of the dead, boards and all, into spare timber.

8. Listening to the bowl  
They trust the Hollow the way sailors trust a fickle sea—by knowing every mood swing. The crack of a ribsnap means evacuate the tier above; a rising fog-sheet that folds three times signals deep tide and the hour to shutter cistern intakes; water that mirrors sky predicts stone-needles will bloom before dawn. A gutter-ward keeps a toadstone (the smooth core of a throat-well pebble) in her mouth when she makes rounds. If the stone tingles against her teeth, air pressure is wrong and she orders new vents drilled.

9. Dreams, stories, quiet dreads  
Children fall asleep to the Tale of the First Rake: how a god-beast dragged its bronze claws across the seabed and left the bowl behind, then slept so long that rock forgot to finish healing. Adults no longer believe the beast returns, but they knock knuckles on sill beams whenever a shale wall weeps more than usual.  

They worry about silence. Too long without drip, creak, or sigh and everyone migrates instinctively toward the throat-wells, as if to be near the only heartbeat left. In their dialect death is called the Still; love, the Lean-across; wisdom, the Clear-gutter.

10. Outsiders’ view  
Marsh traders find the Hollow-Folk confusing: so many bodies in so small a place, yet each time traders arrive another narrow ledge has grown, another roof joined to its neighbor, all without argument. The marshers tell stories of night markets where frog-light glows in bottled mist and slate blades split river water like silk. They rarely stay beyond a tide cycle; the constant whisper of water against plank feels to them like a crowd mumbling secrets.

Yet the Marsh knows: if storms ever rise enough to drown the ridgeline, the only folk still breathing when the sky calms will be those of Sieve-Hollow, balanced on lash-poles, ears flared, reading the ripples for the next place the earth decides to be born.
