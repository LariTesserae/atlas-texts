Whoever lives here has accepted two articles of faith: that height is dangerous bravado and that water—present, absent, or merely imagined—makes the only laws worth obeying. Everything that follows has been shaped by those convictions.

1. The Lateral Folk (plants that behave like animals)

• Hushreed  
Looks like an ordinary sedge until you touch it: the leaves exude an oil that numbs skin in seconds. The plant does this for the same reason other things here stay low—every stem it raises must not be worth biting. Swamp-antelopes know to graze around a hushreed tuft as if skirting a firepit.

• Knuckle-mangrove  
Its girth is never more than a wrist, yet its root-system sprawls the diameter of a house, propping the trunk on dozens of flexed “knuckles.” Roots fracture clay and drink the seepage that wells up through the faults. When drought drags the water table down, the knuckles simply kneel, following the retreat like pilgrims descending a staircase.

2. The Mud-Breathers (things that perfected the in-between state)

• Reliquary killifish  
Fat as a finger, skin mottled to match rusted earth. At the first hint of desiccation their gills grow a rind, sealing moisture in until rain returns—which may be seven days or seven years. Generations overlap inside the mud: grand-parents in suspended animation lie centimeters beneath swimming great-grand-offspring.  
Cultural footnote: on those rare nights when thunder drums the basin, you can hear the buried fish coughing awake, a fragile clicking that passes through mud like Morse code.

• Silt-cradler shrimp  
Occupy the palm-sized scallops in old sandstone. They hoard air under their carapaces; flip upside-down to let the bubble caress their gills, then right themselves to browse algal fuzz. A single puddle might contain five hundred—an opalescent choir of minuscule belly-up dancers.

3. The Air-Owners (who spend fuel to reach a handspan above everyone else)

• Glassneedle dragonflies  
Bodies transparent except for a slim copper back-stripe that absorbs sunlight so fiercely the abdomen steams on cool mornings. Watch two males duel: they rise perhaps thirty centimeters, higher than anything vertebrate ever bothers with, and the sun paints brief sword-flashes along their spines before they settle, exhausted, onto the next mirror of water.

• Spoonwader  
Bird the color of a dried reed. Legs so thin you see blood pulse inside the shins. Their feet are webbed not for swimming but for load-bearing on soft crusts; they distribute weight like snowshoes. The beak ends as a flexible spatula: drag, close, lift, and an entire pan of insect larvae comes up as if peeled from a griddle.

4. The Quiet Ruminant

• Basin antelope (“ankle-wolf” to travelers who hear hoof-clicks before they see shape)  
Back hardly taller than the sedges; horns no larger than thumbs; eyes huge and sea-green. This is the only resident mammal bold enough to sip standing water—it lifts one forefoot, waits for the mud to sigh and settle, then drinks from the vibration-stilled patch, a talent learned from watching frogs. At night the herd (never more than eight) beds on islets, flanks pressed to damp stone that bleeds warmth.

5. The Unseen Apex

• Marrow-worm  
Looks like a root until it moves. Blind, jointless, as long as a man, it tunnels just below the surface, mouth ringed with stubby nubs that feel for the metabolic heat of other organisms. When it finds a shrimp-pool or a frog-clutch, the soil around the prey collapses into a sinkhole. Locals might speak of dunes that walk; here, it is the earth that feeds on itself.

6. The Atmospheric Stragglers

• Seep-whistlers (cyanobacteria mats)  
Where two seeps meet, a pale film forms. As the morning sun climbs, tiny oxygen pockets pop in rapid succession—high enough in frequency to sit at the edge of human hearing. To the basin’s inhabitants this is the “dawn bell,” signalling the hour when predators with keen eyes become futile and mud-breathers can risk surfacing.

7. The Fossil Choir (residents too old to notice they are still alive)

• Choirstones  
Porous sandstone knobs whose inner chambers stay wet all year. Thread-thin luminescent worms live permanently inside, scraping biofilm from their home’s damp walls. On windless nights the stones glow faintly, a peppering of green constellations inverted upon the ground. Amphibians use these stars the way sailors use the sky, triangulating their return to ancestral pools.

Patterns of living

• Ownership is temporary. A puddle belongs to whomever can outlast it in sleep. The moment it refills, the title passes to whoever wakes first.  
• Noise buys privacy: frogs chorus not to attract mates but to shield individual voices. Predators can isolate a lone croak but are defeated by a crowd.  
• Anything spent on verticality must be paid back in lightness—stems that hollow, bones that air-inflate, bodies that molt weight like coins.

What is conspicuously absent

No miners, no nest-builders above ankle height, no creatures that stake fences. The land is too fluid for permanent architecture; claims dissolve the way mica flecks do—glint once, submerge, reappear elsewhere.

In sum

The basin is not empty after all. It is simply occupied by tenants that understand the lease: Keep low. Trade places politely. Speak softly unless a chorus is required. And always feel for water before dreaming of the sky.
