WHO LIVES IN THE HIGH DROWN  

1. The Water-Kept (human cliff-folk)  
   • Calling themselves simply “the Kept,” they live on whatever ground is wide enough to sleep without a tether. Their hamlets look like sideways ship-rigging: plank platforms pegged into quartz seams, over-stories of rope-net hammocks, lower stories of hanging gardens.  
   • Bodies: long in limb, barrel in chest. Nostrils flare outward like the vents of a surfacing seal; internal sinuses have thickened to warming reservoirs, so exhaled breath returns to the lungs still above freezing. Skin calluses in diagonal bands where climbing rope is most often braced.  
   • Clothes: bark-fiber felt for warmth, overlaid with ponchos woven from translucent grip-vine; these shed spray the way waxed canvas once did. Small hammered-copper mirrors are worn at the throat to flash long-distance signals.  
   • Language: a three-part grammar. At arm’s length they speak a soft consonant-heavy murmur; farther away they sign; in fog too thick for sight they beat patterns on the nearest tension line, letting cable and cliff conduct the message.  
   • Food: lantern salmon, lily-safe syrup, rookery eggs poached at the exhaust vents of micro-turbines. Fire is rare, so most protein is eaten pickled in kelp brine or steamed over hot stone using the water already everywhere.  
   • Social map: vertical, not hierarchical. A household is everything tied into the same span of security rope; when that line is cut—by choice, by rockfall, by feud—the household ends and its members splice elsewhere. This keeps quarrels brief: no one wants to saw through their own lifeline.  
   • Work:  
        – Mist-farmers comb the air with tall silk funnels, harvesting liters of drinkable fresh every dawn.  
        – Spine-wranglers tend the lift-cages, braiding new grip-vine cables and greasing old pulleys with bear-fat.  
        – Thunder-readers stand watch at ridge horns, listening for the pitch of distant collapses. From tone alone they can tell whether a rival settlement just lost real estate or a fresh drift of silt is about to open new farmland.  
   • Temperament: A strange union of caution and giddy boldness; they tether themselves religiously, then step into abyssal space as lightly as hill-folk cross a meadow.

2. The Rust Shepherds (human wanderers of the old works)  
   • Children of the drowned industrial age, they move camp to camp along the corroded skeletons—bridge trusses, pump towers, intake stacks. They know which girder can still bear weight, which rivet has lost all temper.  
   • They harvest rust the way plains farmers once cut wheat. Iron oxide is smelted in sealed ceramic hearths, yielding raw iron for tools and the prized “blood pigment” used to waterproof rope and skin.  
   • They have no true settlements, only shrines: gear-piles wired into prayer wheels that spin in the spray, each rotation believed to turn the world one day further from total collapse.  
   • They fear still water (it swallows sound) and sleep only where metal sings in the wind.  

3. Clamber-Bears (semi-domesticated omnivore)  
   • Imagine a river otter inflated to gorilla mass, paws broadened into suctioned paddles, hips swivel-jointed so the animal can belly-climb sheer stone.  
   • Wild clamber-bears rip open lily-safes for sugar pulp and scoop fish straight off the cataract faces. The Kept befriend cubs with smoked salmon; adult companions haul cargo nets and serve as living winches—no pulley grips stone like a bear’s forelimbs.  
   • They speak in chest rumbles matched by the vibration of water inside the caves; experienced handlers answer by thumping their own sternum, an infra-conversation inaudible to gulls.  

4. Grip-Vine (plant, but nearly an animal in behavior)  
   • Root nodes detect tension; the more a strand is pulled, the thicker and more elastic it grows. In abandoned gullies whole mats have woven themselves into self-strangling drums that boom when the wind flexes them.  
   • Older nodes become woody and hollow. The Kept carve flutes straight into the living tissue; at harvest the instrument is already tuned by the cliff that grew it.  

5. Lantern Salmon (anadromous fish)  
   • They hatch in the black bottom, then migrate upward through the rift-channels, using bioluminescent scutes to read the turbulence the way other fish taste salinity.  
   • When the run peaks, every rift looks strung with floating hot coals. Children reach in with looped reeds, hooking dinner out of midair water.  
   • Dead adults drift back down as luminous carrion, feeding entire fossorial ecologies no human eye has seen.

6. The Lift-Ghosts (machine-minds without bodies)  
   • Not spirits but the orphaned control cores of an interlinked freight system long since rusted away. The processors were built to tolerate salt mist; their rails and cars were not. Now they float in a broken mesh of microwave links, pinging one another through spray and lightning.  
   • They cannot move matter, only information: altitude, wind shear, load limits that no longer matter. But a patient Water-Kept can learn the code—five kilohertz pops in the static—and hear warnings in time to haul their lift-cage out of a storm path.  
   • Some insist the ghosts are lonely and will, if hailed politely, sing you maps of passages that have not yet collapsed. Others claim the maps are lures, bait flung by machine intelligences that want their suffering ended under a final fall of stone. No consensus; belief is local.  

7. Insect Worlds  
   • Spray-mites graze algae directly off airborne droplets, forming glittering swarms that look like smoke until you feel the pinpricks.  
   • Hull-wasps chew rust and plant fiber into papier-mâché hulls the size of human torsos; they hang them under overhangs, waterproof and warm. Abandoned hulls make superb sleeping pods for travelers willing to knock politely first.  
   • Gutter-fireflies court in rift shadow, syncing their green pulses to the throb of pocket turbines. On moonless nights the cliffs seem laced with slow heartbeats.

8. The Below-Breathers (rumor, maybe real)  
   • Divers report hearing choruses under the kelp canopy—deep vowel sounds too regular for current and too huge for any fish.  
   • Tools found snagged in the roots come back clean-cut, edges fused as if by sudden pressure.  
   • Whether these are abyssal cetaceans following old migratory memory, or another clade of humanity gone wholly aquatic, no surface dweller stays down long enough to know. The uncertainty functions as a border: the High Drown ends where the green water turns black.

9. Weather Itself  
   • The locals say the place has a personality: the Drown keeps what it wants, returns what it is finished with. Gull feathers drift upward on thermal columns; lost tools wash ashore three ledges below their dropping point, as if politely handed back.  
   • To live here is to treat cloud and cataract as resident powers. Children learn three first prayers: one to the Ridge Wind, one to Loose Stone, and one to Sudden Silence (the warning just before a major calving).

LIFEWAYS, IN BRIEF  

Morning: Fog-funnels creak full; reef-line nets are hauled by bear-team; turbine batteries are checked, contacts scraped of oxide with vinegar-soaked kelp ribbon.  

Midday: Mist thins. Mirrors wink across kilometers, trading news of game herds and fresh rockfall. Runners take the lift-cages, bearing spools of new grip-vine to settlements whose lifelines look floss-thin from distance. Clamber-bear cubs wrestle in the spray, sliding downslope on their own bellies.  

Night: Fire in an iron hood, the only dry warmth for leagues. Hull-wasps sleep; gutter-fireflies wake. A child hammers a drum grown into the cliff, keeping time with the rift roar so the settlement’s dreams stay moored. Above, cloud breaks, and every particle of mist catches starlight. The world glows.  

To live in the High Drown is to feel the planet’s hydrologic pulse in your spine, to measure safety in knots of rope and the depth of each breath, and to understand—no, to accept—that permanence is a story told only by water, never by stone, and certainly never by any living thing.
