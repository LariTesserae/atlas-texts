Who lives here is life at the tempo of cooling stone.

1. The chamber itself: The Basilica Colony  
Everything green you see—fern-trees, cable-vines, coin-scented blossom-sacs—belongs to one clonal organism that botanists, back when there were botanists, filed under the placeholder name Thermophyta basilica.  Think of an aspen grove that learned to stand upright in tropical heat, then swapped sugar for rust and photosynthesis for chemosynthesis.  What looks like a forest is in fact a single individual spread out in tens of thousands of stems.  

• Body plan  
  – Aerial organs: The five-metre “trunks” are only breathing spars: hollow, warm to the touch, corrugated like bellows. They pull geothermal air past capillary beds that strip out oxygen, water vapor and iron micro-particles.  
  – Support: True mass lies beneath the basalt glass. A root-knot the size of a grain silo grips the rock columns and the old steel footings of catwalks, plating itself in magnetite as it grows. That black-steel rind is almost as hard as the surrounding basalt.  
  – Metabolism: Fe²⁺ + O₂ → Fe³⁺ + heat. The oxidation is catalyzed by nickel-porphyrin pigments that glow dull orange when the chamber goes briefly dark; the plant is its own ember.  

• Sense organs  
  – Vibrofronds: paper-thin leaves fanned flat against the air like satellite dishes, tuned not to light but to sub-audible vibration. Their petioles twist until the leaf’s long axis aligns with the thump of the turbine two storeys below.  
  – Ferrite hairs: microscopic whiskers that detect changes in local magnetic flux. When a power cable overhead shorts out, every fern-tree angles an inch toward the burst like curious snakes.  

• Reproduction  
  – Temperature-gate spores: Each stem stores a stack of graphite-grey spore plates under its crown. When the air hits 42 °C the plates flex, snapping open and launching paired spores that spiral upward on their own exothermic lift. No animals required—heat itself takes the role of pollinator, and the spores’ corkscrew shells drill into cracks of fresh rock where condensation will later collect.  

2. The Secondary Flora: Attendants and Opportunists  

a) Lamp-Moss (Luminaria cuprata)  
A carpet only two millimetres thick, but brighter than any leaf. It embeds copper stolen atom by atom from oxidising wires into a mesh of chlorophyll-shot crystals. The crystals fluoresce green when struck by sodium light, and pale aqua during arc-lamp flashes, turning the floor into a tidal pool of shifting colour. Lamp-moss is the Basilica Colony’s scout: its rhizoids burrow ahead of the main root front, tasting for metals in concentrations as low as one part per billion.  

b) Kiln-Vines (Scoria constrictor)  
These are the cables draped over the flopped drum kiln. They are fast, by cavern standards—able to creep an entire centimetre per hour when iron is plentiful. Every vine tip extrudes an acid droplet, clear as glass, that etches steel into a ferrous soup the plant can drink. When two vines meet they splice vessels and share whatever metals they have dissolved, forming a circulatory network that reaches from catwalk to catwalk.  

c) Foliate Siphon-Puffs (Aeris piriforme)  
The bladder-shaped sacs that breathe out the smell of pepper and wet coins. They are epiphytes clamped onto the fern-tree lattice. Their interior is a negative-pressure chamber lined with iron sulfide plates. Each exhalation forces moist air across those plates, feeding sulfur bacteria that colonise the lining; the bacteria pay rent by fixing nitrogen for the host plant. Siphon-puffs open only during the brief moments the distant ventilation fans sigh; the pressure wave is both clock and trigger.  

3. The Invisible Majority: Microthermal Guilds  

• Siderophages—iron-eating archaea that live in brine films only a few molecules thick along the basalt’s microfractures. They share plasmids freely with the Basilica Colony, peppering the plant’s genome with prokaryote tricks for heat tolerance and redox chemistry.  

• Thallotherms—lumpy, tar-coloured lichens that cling to the underside of catwalk gratings. They hoard manganese and, when overloaded, shed pebbles of metal-rich tissue that clink onto the glass floor like dropped ball bearings. Fern-roots collect those pebbles for later digestion.  

4. Motion, such as it is  

If you stood in the dark for a full hour you would notice that the fern-tree beside you has leaned a degree nearer the basalt wall, and that the trumpet mouth of a tubular vine has swallowed the bolt it was only kissing before.  No creature here scurries, but the entire chamber is forever in a slow unison crawl toward food sources—heat vents, rusting steel, leaking copper.  The locomotion resembles glacier flow more than animal stride: tissues at the rear mineralise into rock; tissues at the front bud fresh and soft, always advancing.  

5. Intelligence, maybe  

Every stem of Thermophyta basilica is wired into every other by skeins of conductive lignin shot through with magnetite. Electrochemical pulses travel those trunks—measured once at about three centimetres per second.  Patterns repeat, feed back, damp out, start again.  Researchers proposed the colony was computing something, perhaps mapping heat plumes, perhaps echo-locating the turbine heartbeat.  Then the grant money ended, the catwalk lab was abandoned, and the plant went on thinking its glacial thoughts without witnesses.  

6. Outsiders  

No animal lives here now, but bones of three species of bat and one species of rat have been found embedded in the basal moss.  They were probably accidental arrivals through ventilation shafts.  Their skeletons are iron-plated now, part fossil, part garden trellis.  In cross-section the rat femurs show that the plant infiltrated the marrow canals with red rootlets, used the calcium, then moved on.  Even unintended guests are recycled into architecture.  

7. Population count  

One.  An empire of trunks and vines and mosses, but genetically a single inhabitant, still young, still experimenting with its mineral palate.  Return in a century and you will find pillars sheathed in magnetite, catwalks swollen into ironwood ridges, and the submarine hatch knitted shut by a seam of green-black bark.  The Basilica Colony will have advanced perhaps two more metres, but in those two metres it will have swallowed every remaining bolt, every last glimmer of copper.  Nothing here dies; it is only promoted to structure.  

That is who lives here: a cathedral-sized, rust-eating, heat-loving singular organism cultivating attendants, digesting relics, and tilting its innumerable leaves toward the quiet machinery still thumping in the stone below—as if it already knows that, when the turbine finally stops, it will have to learn to make its own heartbeat.
