Those who make their homes in the basin are fewer than outsiders assume—five-hundred and some change at the last census-board—but a single inhabitant is never only one creature.  Each person walks in consortium with slower, smaller, stranger lives that borrow their warmth, their tools, their patience.  To describe the residents you must keep widening the lens: begin with the bodies that speak, then notice the colonies that whisper inside their cuffs, then feel the trunks that lean a fraction closer every time someone passes.

1.  The Hollow-Folk  

Name.  They call themselves nothing so grand; “folk of the hollows” is merely the label they enter on shipping manifests.  River charts mark them as the Bent Basin Cooperative.  Travelers shorten both to “Hollowers.”

Physique.  Years of damp chill have thinned their subcutaneous fat but thickened their skin.  Knuckles, heels, and the napes of their necks are plated with a translucent keratin that sheds water the way the pillar-birch sheds smoke.  Hair grows sparse on the crown but dense behind the ears, where it wicks condensation before it can dribble into collars.

Breath.  Lungs learn to sip; conversation happens in low, measured phrases so as not to stir the moist stillness.  Children practice “fog counting,” exhaling through pursed lips and timing the swirl.  Anyone who can keep a plume coherent for ten seconds is judged ready to operate a boiler.

Dress.  Outer layers are woven from ribbon-fungus that colonizes fallen leaves high in the canopy.  When cured it flexes like suede but never soaks through.  Inner layers are cotton imported upriver and traded for sap-resin casks and lichen polish.

Work.  Almost everyone keeps two trades: one metallic, one vegetal.  
•  Sapwrights tune the single-piston engines, then spend afternoons braiding repair sutures for wounded bark.  
•  Lathe-hands mill brass bushings before climbing out to thin heavy limbs the exhaust has overheated.  
•  Apprentices rotate monthly between the foundry’s glow and the canopy’s dusk so no eye grows unbalanced.  

Speech.  Their dialect retains river jargon—bend, reach, slack—but has grafted arboreal verbs onto hydraulic nouns.  “Let the line meristem” means “give the pipe room to grow with the tree.”  Outsiders find them cryptic; insiders find outsiders loud.

Customs.  
•  At shift-change every tool is stroked once with a pad of peroxide moss that eats the first bloom of green rust.  The foreman inspects the rag; if it comes away clear the crew is dismissed.  
•  Weddings occur at night, inside an unplugged boiler whose warm sides ring like a bell when tapped.  Vows are tapped in code so quiet that the officiant feels them through the metal rather than hears them.

2.  The Companion Biota  

Metal-Lichen.  Verdigris in texture though not in chemistry, it seeds itself in the micro-scratches left by files.  Rather than corrode the steel it forms a sacrificial skin, metabolizing trace oxides and exuding a waxy film that smells of peppermint and solder.  Tools last twice as long under its care, which is why no one polishes down to bright mirror.

Sap-Aerobes.  Pale threads that drift in boiler exhaust, land on skin, and knit into lacework around pores.  Completely benign; some say they filter particulates and give the wearer an indefinable “enginehouse tang.”  Guests are offered a steam-bath on arrival so the aerobes may settle gently rather than find their own way.

Torch-Frogs.  No larger than a thimble, skin the color of hot iron.  They lay eggs on cooling crucibles, attracted by the copper salts that condense there.  At night they chorus in the octave just above turbine hum, fooling newcomers into thinking a gasket has failed.

3.  The Pillar-Birches Themselves  

Sentience.  Ask three Hollowers and receive four opinions.  The consensus is that each trunk is a lung, each canopy a limb, and the entire ring an organism whose body circles the basin like a bracelet.  Whether that organism thinks, or only reacts, is immaterial; either way it answers.  

Language.  Growth is grammar.  A warmed exhaust-flue invites an enveloping knot of wood: a question.  Let the flue cool and the knot relaxes: an answer accepted.  Keep it hot and the wood contracts to choke the pipe: a rebuke.  Sapwrights read these movements the way shipwrights read hull stress, and claim to know when the bracelet is pleased.

Service.  Once a year, at the first slow pulse of autumn, the trunks exude crisp sheets of bark that peel free like parchment.  Residents harvest the sheets before dawn, press them flat between iron plates, and trade them upriver as waterproof map-stock.  No one remembers asking the trees for this gift; the timing simply coincides with the Cooperative’s need for export goods.

Internal Weather.  The upper lattice traps morning sun, condenses it into cloudlets, then rains it back as the factory drizzle.  Children chase the falling beads and bite them in mid-air, pretending to sample sky brewed by trees.  Adults know the drizzle carries minute sugars: the birches’ version of pollen, perhaps, or a quiet method of feeding the ground that feeds their roots.

4.  Others Who Graze the Edge  

Slinkers.  The rumored predators of the open plain are real, but they come only to the periphery.  Long-bodied, glass-furred, they press one ear to the earth and listen for the thrum of turbines.  When a sap engine idles they retreat; when it resumes they circle closer, curious rather than hostile.  Their paw-prints mark the mud in the morning, erased by noon drizzle.

Wind-Strays.  Gusts that detach from larger weather systems and lose themselves under the canopy.  Because nothing here is dry enough to raise dust, they wander almost blind, probing with eddies, tasting boiler exhaust for direction.  Children fly ribbon-fungus kites on calm days to give the strays something to tug, then let them escape back to the plain.

5.  Population, if You Count Every Pulse  

•  532 humans on the last roll.  
•  Roughly six million individual tools, each home to a bloom of metal-lichen.  
•  147 pillar-birch trunks, collectively one bracelet-organism.  
•  Uncounted torch-frogs, though the foundry keeps a ledger of egg clutches as an informal barometer of furnace health.  
•  One continuous, whispering fog that condenses where it pleases and might, in its wandering coherence, deserve the courtesy of being called alive.  

To live in the bend is to accept layered identities: flesh and bark, iron and lichen, breath and cloud, all nested like those sap-powered dynamos inside the breathing wood.  Step onto the ironwood boards and you are immediately part of the count, radiating just enough warmth for a pillar-birch to notice and, perhaps, lean a fractional millimetre nearer.  From that moment forward the basin remembers your shape.
