They are not plants and not quite animals in any terrestrial sense, but for convenience I will speak of “creatures” and place them in loose guilds, working upward from the thin chemovore crust to the giant, silence-making hunters that sometimes pass overhead like eclipses.

1. The First Trophic Layer – Things That Eat Stone and Air

a. Oxide-raspers (family Ferrivora)  
Appearance: dull porcelain plaques, five to twenty millimetres across, grown together edge-to-edge so that a cliff face or valley floor looks tiled. The living body is the thickness of a fingernail; beneath it sits a honeycomb of empty sub-cells that once stored waste salts.  
Method: a molecular file on the ventral surface scrapes iron oxides from basalt a few atoms at a time, feeding lithovorous bacteria trapped in the plaque’s gel interior. Once a hexagon empties the rock immediately under it, the plaque detaches, flips like a coin in syrupy slow motion and re-seats itself a millimetre away. Millions doing this in imperfect synchrony make the continuous, distant-surf part of the valley’s roar.  
Note: they do not reproduce individually. Every few years whole mosaics shear loose, crumble in the drizzle and release bacteria that flow away to seed new plaques elsewhere.

b. Knot-worms (order Salicola)  
Appearance: yarn-balls of colourless vermicelli, each filament a separate body, braiding and un-braiding around its neighbours.  
Method: osmotic siphons draw brine from the pale salt veins. Internal symbionts extract energy from chloride oxidation, exhaling faintly luminous chlorine gas that leaks through pores. The gas flashes brief green when stray droplets hit it, so a resting colony blinks at the pace of shallow breathing.  
Behaviour: when a knot-worm mass grows too large it pinches into halves like bread dough; the severed bundles ooze apart and crawl in opposite directions at a speed of perhaps two centimetres a day.

c. Veil filament (genus Chemora)  
Appearance: wet smoke at knee level. In reality trillions of two-micron fibres that tangle the valley’s fog.  
Method: chemosynthesis from sulphide traces in the mist. Every fibre terminates in a luminous organelle that discharges excess electrons as a pulse of blue. Those pulses, though dim, are the earliest “phrases” of the valley’s light-language and serve as a metronome for many higher organisms.

2. The Grazing Stratum – Living Pavement

a. Coin-shells (order Cirraterra)  
Appearance: glossy, graphite-black domes the size of shirt buttons, packed so tight a boot heel cannot descend without crushing a dozen. Each bears a frill of cilia along its perimeter; these comb the filament veil, rake oxide scrapings and sweep knot-worm crumbs toward a radial mouth.  
Synchrony: the cilia beat in travelling waves that propagate for kilometres, so the whole ground, viewed from above, looks like wind on grass. Those waves are not random—they carry alarm, invitation to mating areas and even topological information (“sudden void two metres east”).  
Defence: they cannot bite, so their single tactic is density. When a predator thrusts among them they surge upward into a temporary, knee-deep fluid of shells that jams proboscides and dulls stabs.

b. Porous whorls (family Absorptiliidae)  
Appearance: coiled, semi-rigid tubes that look like fossil nautilus shells inflated with gel. They begin life no wider than a toothpick but may grow to thigh girth.  
Method: absorb the collective waste of the coin-shell carpet—mostly nitrate-rich droplet fallout—and convert it to sweet proteins. Those proteins leak back through the shell walls as a slow exudate that other creatures lap.  
Ecological role: the whorls are the valley’s pantries and its loudspeakers. When full they tense and resonate to the background roar, producing basso pulses that advertise plenty. The hunters listen.

3. Mobile Predators of the Ground

a. Tripod transfusers (genus Trikana)  
Appearance: jointed “Y” shapes, knee high, two rear limbs for stalking, one fore-limb ending in a hollow stilettto. The body proper is no more than a clasp of cartilage riding the limb intersection like a rider on stilts.  
Senses: vision almost nil. Instead they track gradients of carbonyl scent emitted by alarmed coin-shells.  
Feeding: a transfuser plants its spike vertically, injecting an anticoagulant cocktail. Twenty seconds later it drinks liquified organs. Because the transfusion lasts longer than the kill, fights are silent; only afterwards does a transfuser vent surplus gases in a bark so sharp it appears to crack the air. These barks are the single most recognizable mid-range note inside the valley’s roar.  
Social: never more than one per fifty square metres, but they relay information through chained barking—an impromptu, shifting “radio network” that maps food abundance and the passing of greater dangers.

4. The Fog Occupants – Things With Wings and Alters of Sound

a. Facet-bats (order Prismata)  
Appearance: kite-sized membranes of charcoal skin strung between angular cartilaginous spars. The “head” is a disk of mirrored facets—each a tympanum rather than an eye.  
Navigation: emit bursts of ultrasonic clicks rich in sulphur overtones; returning echoes modulate the charge in sub-cuticular crystal lattices, producing sheen shifts that flash messages to their peers.  
Diet: midair plankton of newly hatched knot-worm larvae and veil filaments. During the two-hour misting phase the air blooms with that plankton, and the bats swarm until the roar peaks in a storm of whistles.  
Language: the bats do not stop calling even when resting. Their chorus acts as sky-lid to the biosphere; ground creatures gauge vertical safety by its volume. A sudden hush above means something large enough to silence facet-bats has entered the fog.

b. Courter swarms (class Phaeophota)  
Appearance: clouds of twenty-millimetre, translucent medusoids kept aloft by hydrogen generated in their gut sacs.  
Lifecycle: live only eight hours. They hatch, rise, search for chemoline strings broadcast by unrelated swarms, merge, exchange gene coils, then dive into water pans to dissolve into nutrient slurry that feeds ribbonfish.  
Light code: their mating display is the most complex photic language known here—seven colours, three pulse widths, and constructive interference patterns. Facet-bats steal fragments of this code to ornament their own conversations; tripod transfusers appear instinctively attracted to particular patterns, following them the way cats chase laser pointers.

5. Aquatic Guilds – The Pans

a. Ribbonfish (genus Lacelima)  
Appearance: one-metre flat eels that can unspool into ribbons five metres long and a centimetre wide, then contract again like concertinas.  
Hunting: lie across the mirror-still water, indistinguishable from reflections. When condensation drips, the impact ripples tell them where the surface broke; they leap so abruptly that observers rarely see motion, only splash.  
Symbiosis: carry colonies of veil filament on their dorsal surface. When those colonies flash distress, ribbonfish reverse-thrust with their entire length, fleeing before the shock wave of an incoming apex predator reaches the pool.

b. Bladder-beasts (order Resonantia)  
Appearance: partially submerged orbs three metres wide, skin stretched drum-tight over internal gas bladders. Only a few dozen exist at once, each occupying a favourite pan.  
Metabolism: drink brine, crack it into hydrogen and chlorine, vent chlorine through stuttering valves that create the thunderless booms travellers mistake for geological activity.  
Purpose of the boom: twofold. First, advertises ownership—no two bladder-beasts tolerate the same pool. Second, drives prey (ribbonfish, courting swarms) outward where the beast’s tangle of filamentous feeding arms can sieve them.  
Aging: when reserves of trace metals run low a bladder-beast cannot keep its skin taut; its final boom is a flat, heartbreaking cough, after which it sinks, decomposes and fertilises a whole new corral of chemovores along the basin floor.

6. Apex Predators – Keepers of the Silence

a. The Black Strider (species unknown, called “strider” for lack of better)  
First sign: every voice within two kilometres drops out. The roar slackens into a whisper. Even mineral fog dims, for the strider’s dermis absorbs nearly every photon and much of the broadband noise.  
Shape: witness reports vary. Some swear to see a thirty-metre giraffe silhouette stepping once per minute; others describe a manta the size of an airship sliding just under the fog; one expedition’s lidar suggested a lattice of separate bodies choreographed as one.  
Feeding: not predation but harvest. When the strider passes, living things simply lose cohesion—shells peel apart, tripod transfusers buckle and deflate, facet-bats rain down like ash. In its wake falls a gentle snow of unstructured protoplasm, already half-digested.  
Theory: the strider may be the basin’s ultimate recycler, accelerating entropy to keep biomass moving. Its presence every few weeks prevents any single lineage from monopolising space or resources.  
Communication: silent, but compasses spin violently in its vicinity. Some researchers posit that the predator uses a magnetic lattice to tear molecular assemblies, explaining both the navigational havoc and the shredding of tissue.

b. Veil Mothers (family Chemora magnus)  
Relation: not true predators but overwhelming colonisers. Several times a century a knot-worm super-colony and a veil filament bloom synchronize, mating to form a kilometre-wide sheet of living gauze that lifts off the valley floor and drifts for days. Underneath it, light from every other creature refracts into twilight carnival hues, drawing in crowd after crowd until the sheet sags under their weight. When it finally collapses everything trapped inside becomes food for the merged bloom.  
Travelers’ rule: if the gray fog above you brightens into pastel pearl, run upslope immediately.

7. Parasites and Hitchhikers – Including You

a. Egg-film lichen (symbiote between Porous whorls and facet-bats)  
A transparent, gluey smear that adheres to any warm surface. Each smear holds up to a thousand ovoid packets—eggs of grazers, tripod transfusers, even veil filament spores—all arranged by unknown logic. The film stays quiescent until moistened by the basin’s drizzle. Anyone passing through acquires a coat; anyone leaving exports the ecology.

b. Mind-glass (rare silicaceous fungus)  
Grows inside cracks of a traveller’s goggles or lenses. Feeds on skin oils, exudes prismatic scales that bend light just enough to delay perception of motion by a fraction of a second. Explorers afflicted report the infamous “time-pockets,” though the world is normal and only their sensory pipeline is warped. Removing mind-glass requires either fire or the hungry mouthplates of coin-shells.

8. Meta-Ecology – The Valley as Superorganism

The inhabitants above do not form a food chain so much as a symphonic machine engineered by selection for one purpose: continuous conversion of geothermal salts to distributed, self-propagating sound. Noise is their bloodstream, light their nervous impulse. Remove any guild and the roar’s harmonics shift; too many shifts and condensation patterns alter, starving the chemovores that anchor everything else.

That is why even the Black Strider stops tearing when the decibel fall threatens to dip below a threshold. It moves on, the chorus resumes and the basin exhales relief in a hiss of renewed drizzle.

Who lives here?  
• Stones that grind themselves into mouths.  
• Shells that march in collective poems.  
• Tripod drinkers who bark semaphore across kilometres.  
• Mirror-faced bats that weave echo and light into airborne nets.  
• Pools that breathe like lungs and clap like cannon.  
• And, towering above, an interpreter of silence that edits life back into raw ingredients.

If you stand quietly—not too quietly—and wipe the fog from your visor, you will realise the whole valley is one breathing creature, and every beating wing, scraping jaw, pulsing gland and percussive bladder is a moving cell inside its body. Whether you count yourself a parasite or a partner will depend on how swiftly you learn to sing.
