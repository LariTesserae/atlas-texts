Who lives under the shale slope depends on how strictly you mean the verb to live.  Down here animation, memory and intention distribute themselves among stone, water, light and the things that move between them.  The list that follows begins with the truest citizens—those that could not survive a single breath on the surface—and ends with visitors who merely borrow the cavern’s mannerisms for a while before slipping back into ordinary causality.  Think of it less as a food-web and more as a conversation whose speakers change languages between sentences.

1.  The Cavern-Body  
The first inhabitant is the place itself.  Fault-light, gypsum skin, tides of barometric air—taken together they behave like a single slow animal.

•  Sensory organs: tension lines in the crystal sheets register the weight of every footstep; deviations in refraction announce each flutter of a gutter-bat.  The cavern “hears” by feeling how light breaks.  
•  Nervous system: hairline cracks branch and heal in response to stimulus, storing events as stress patterns.  A visitor leaning on one wall writes a bruise into that memory.  
•  Metabolism: calcium sulphate dissolves, redeposits, dissolves again, releasing minute heat that powers the local convection cells—enough energy to keep water globes circling and gravity uncertain.  

Nothing else down here is free of that slow, flexing will.  When the exit slit migrates or gravity leans sideways, it is the Cavern-Body repositioning its bones to make more comfortable sense of itself.

2.  Prismata (crystal embryos)  
Seen only when the sun spurts clean through the entrance crack, the Prismata look like thumb-sized shards hovering in the brighter pockets of air.  They are not yet mineral, not yet organism—still deciding.

•  Behaviour: drift toward convergence points where ricocheted rays overlap, bathing in maximal luminosity.  There they elongate by less than a millimetre per day.  
•  Feeding: absorb photons; extrude sugars laced with silicate dust that rain invisibly from their basal edge.  Blink-shrimp graze on those sugars, so the cavern’s food chain begins with light caught and partially remembered as candy-sand.  
•  Fate: most wink out when the daily barometric tide dims the cavern.  A few anchor to the wall and crystallise into permanent gypsum prisms—nerves added to the Cavern-Body.

3.  Blink-shrimp (Aeropenaeus lentus)  
Already noticed by every visitor, yet seldom understood.

•  Physiology: six glassy legs secrete a film that repels liquid water but loves humidity, letting each shrimp surf on an oval cushion of mist.  
•  Diet: Prismata sugar, condensation minerals, the occasional water globe.  Their forelegs are capillary straws able to sip a sphere without bursting it.  
•  Reproduction: when humidity spikes, two shrimp spin a tiny torus of mucus that catches loose ions; larvae hatch as bead-sized pearls that grow by layering secretions, eventually unfolding into adults and stepping out of their own shells.  Empty larval husks drift like jewellery until sublimated.  

4.  Gutter-bats (Chiroptera pluviorum)  
Sculpted for silence.

•  Echolocation band: 42–45 kHz, just beneath the frequency that makes human teeth hurt.  To the Cavern-Body those calls are hammer-blows; the walls re-tune themselves fractionally after every hunting pass.  
•  Wing chemistry: collagen filaments plated with mercury microflakes.  In bright surge the wings mirror light and make the bats momentarily invisible, a defence against the Thread-wolves.  
•  Social unit: not a colony but a quorum.  They roost alone yet synchronise sleep phases; wake one, all wake.  Every dusk they perform a census lap, counting each reflected squeak, and will abandon the site if numbers fall below thirteen.

5.  Thread-wolves (Phoberia gyrosa)  
Neither worms nor wolves but colonial annelids that borrow both names.

•  Construction: up to two hundred translucent worms spiral around a common lumen, composing a rope thicker than a thumb.  A leading coil serves as mouth, a trailing tassel excretes binding silk that quick-patches footfalls in the soft floor—erasing evidence of anything edible.  
•  Hunting algorithm: distribute, encircle, recombine.  Individual worms answer only two pheromones—spread and join—yet the colony’s completed braid can change direction mid-flow, granting an illusion of foresight.  
•  By-product: abandoned braids fossilise into delicate limestone cords miles older than the species, which geologists will someday misclassify as root casts.

6.  Root-thread Fungus (Mycothrix lunaris)  
The only true plant analogue.

•  Trophism: chemolitho; digests calcite, excretes calcium oxalate that strengthens overhanging stone.  In effect the fungus is the cavern’s structural engineer, propping the ceilings it slowly devours.  
•  Signalling: pulses of faint bioluminescence run along fibres when seismic tremors approach, warning the Cavern-Body seconds in advance.  Gutter-bats read these pulses and evacuate early.  
•  Symbiosis: silver moss grows exclusively on dead fungal strands, using the oxalate residue as scaffold.  Moss fronds unfold only when light concentrates enough to photosynthesise faster than they dehydrate.

7.  Hydrospheres (water globes)  
Officially “inanimate,” though the taxonomy panel that said so never spent a night here.

•  Cohesion skin: laden with surfactant proteins leaked by Blink-shrimp.  The spheres heal rips and even split to dodge collisions, behaviour suggestive of quorum sensing.  
•  Memory ring: each time a globe contacts stone it leaves a travertine halo; those rings guide the next orbit, a lithic feedback loop that looks suspiciously like learning.  
•  Emotive display: during low-pressure tides globes dim with dissolved gypsum dust, as though sulking; when the light flares they clarify again.  

8.  The Clayfold (Tile Apparition)  
Not permanent residents but stubborn squatters from the surface.

•  Origin: six river-mud hexagons folded until angles meet exactly 120°.  The arrangement behaves like a sundial for refracted light instead of direct sun.  
•  Occupants: extremophile bacteria bred in the folds; they flash-calcify gypsum dust into microscopic armour.  Every barometric tide they migrate along the folds, turning the Clayfold into a live map of humidity vectors.  Whoever built it may have meant to harvest that data—but never returned, or did not leave whole.  
•  After-effect: the Clayfold’s precise symmetry exerts a local correction on physics; for two metres around it, gravity and time run almost standard, a resting bench for frazzled senses.

9.  Transients: Surveyors, Runaways, Sleepwalkers  
Humans appear, flicker through a half-life of astonishment, and go.  Those who linger longest acquire adaptations:  
•  Sweat beads that will not fall but orbit the skull like satellites.  
•  A habit of pausing mid-sentence because syntax rearranges between tongue and ear.  
•  After several days, corneas emulsify slightly to reduce glare, leaving eyesight crystalline and prone to prism-ghosts even back in daylight.  

Most leave before the cavern writes them permanently into its autograph.  A few—one in twenty, the gutter-bats seem to know which—never find the slit again.  Perhaps they resolved too cleanly with local rules, and the Cavern-Body kept them as new organs.

10.  The Yet-To-Be  
Every micron the chamber expands creates an acre of prospective ecology.  Geologists might forecast cave pearls, troglobitic spiders, dripstone cathedrals.  They underestimate the place’s ambition.  What lives here tomorrow will not be limited to biology or mineralogy but some third grammar of becoming, already rehearsing in the unsteady glint that fills the air.

For now, these are the inhabitants: stone that deliberates, water that remembers, light that fattens itself into prisms, and a quickcast of translucent animals that skate the mutable medium between.  They do not much mind your presence; they mind only whether you are part of the story the cavern is trying to tell.  If you listen, really listen, you may hear the next draft begin.
