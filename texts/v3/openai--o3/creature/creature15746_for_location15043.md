They are not a single people, or even a single kingdom of life, but a stacked congregation—layer on layer—each riding on the patience of the stratum below it. Start low and work upward. Nothing here makes sense if you do not first meet the ground itself.

1. The Root-Skin  
What looks like vegetation is not colony but creature. Miners logged it decades ago as “moss-vine aggregate,” but the later botanists who autopsied fallen pieces concluded it is one individual, a sheet organism whose countless mouths and lungs have fused into a single continent of flesh. Its sap runs in braided capillaries the width of a hair and the length of a football field; in places two adjacent cords beat in synchrony as if sharing a heart. Every six or seven metres a lobe thickens into a hump no higher than a man’s knee: these are the animal parts, pale diaphragms that flex in time with the tidal pressure changes the fans create. The whole carpet breathes by concertina—drawing air down into internal crypts when the pressure drops, letting it exhale when the pulse rises. In total darkness the Root-Skin can slow until a single breath takes an hour; under a lamp, the rhythm climbs to almost mammalian speed.  

It feeds primarily on the film that condensation washes off iron. Where pipes sweat, its papery trophy-roots creep upward to lap the brine. You can hear them if you press an ear to the metal: a soft ticking, like mice in a barrel, as calcium nodes rasp loose rust. A week after a girder first weeps, it is already furred in translucent suckers.  

The organism is deaf, but not indifferent to sound. Vibration steers its growth the way sun steers a tree’s crown. Tie a brass gong to a pylon and the Root-Skin will, within two seasons, raise a veiny blister up the post until it reaches the source of shaking, then sheath the metal in a damping pad of tissue. To live here is to crave quiet in the bones.

2. Drip-Kin (fauna that move among the roots)  
a. The Lurker-Salamander  
Colourless as cracked ice, skin patterned only by the silver tangle of lymph, it lies submerged in the water mirrors until the surface skins over with new film. When a bead falls hard enough to pierce the seal, the animal’s orbitolith ear registers the ripple and it lunges, not for prey but for the hypoxic bubble just released. Dissolved oxygen is its true diet. Each lungful buys it another hour of torpor.  

b. Fan-Moths  
Two nearly identical species, distinguished solely by the pitch each clings to. One rests on ducting that resonates at 55 hertz, the other on plates that drone at 59. Their scales are less pigment than diffraction grating; they read the pressure waves in the steel and align until their bodies lie in a node of silence. Courtship is performed by minute modulations of wing angle that cancel the local vibration for an instant—an acoustic wink visible only to another moth’s mechanosensory hairs. Eggs are laid in the airstream itself, coated in spider-silk parachutes; most vanish into the cavern’s permanent wind, but enough snag on the Root-Skin’s blades to begin again.  

c. Coal-Hoppers  
Indistinguishable from droppings, they wait on the lattice of catwalks where warm downdrafts meet cold updrafts. When a human footfall shakes the grate, a hopper springs, vanishing into the negative shadow. They store kinetic energy in a spine of magnesium-rich cartilage that flexes all day under the turbine pulse, then releases in a single, perfect parabola. If caught, they liquefy—literally melt—as though the idea of stillness were fatal.

3. The Salt Priests (caretakers)  
Once there were crews on salary; now there are six volunteers who descend irregularly from the surface settlement two kilometres west of the old shaft mouth. They call themselves priests not from faith but from habit: every job that must be done forever acquires ritual. Their faces are windburned, but the rest of them has the waxy pallor of things that spend most waking hours under amber lamps. Each carries three tools only:  
• a blocky hand-crank starter the size of a lunchbox,  
• a canvas satchel of rust flake and kiln-dried salt,  
• an oil chalk that writes even on sweating metal.  

Their covenant is with Rule 4: the fans must not stop. Once a month, sometimes less, sometimes much less in summer, they walk the full line of twelve rotors. Where a shaft moans, they smear pinches of salt into the bearing grease—salt to draw the moisture out, salt to keep the iron honest. If a rotor hesitates below two revolutions a minute they lean in with the starter, throw a shoulder against the flywheel, and chant counts in a rhythm older than the mine: “One-and-lift, two-and-lift,” synchronising breath with gust. It is not the push that matters—one person could not hope to budge that mass—but the declaration to the metal that motion, once named, must continue.  

They sleep in the corrugated hut, hung in hammocks so the Root-Skin cannot clutch their sweat. All night the turbine bass thrums up through the bolts, through spine and salt-stung sinus, until their dreams pulse like sonar. In notebooks they track lamp failures and new rises of buttress-tissue. Now and then they redraw the light map and, with a portable winch, shift a fixture a dozen metres to keep any one section from blooming too thickly. They know how quickly the Root-Skin could smother a stalled fan. They also respect the bargain: let the great lungs breathe and the green continent will spare the narrow catwalks that humans require.

4. Things Without Names  
Between the categories are inhabitants only partly glimpsed. An oily ribbon that surfaces in overflow freshets and is gone before a second lamp can be lit. A thunderhead of spores that rises when two domes of Root-Skin meet and bruise each other. The Priests speak of a dry, papery bell they sometimes see hanging beneath a catwalk, flexing in the draft—neither plant nor animal, but reactive architecture the Root-Skin builds around pure tone. Touch it and it collapses into confetti the colour of old bone. Whether seed or sensor, nobody knows; the Priests write it down, date it, and walk on.

5. The Cavern Itself  
No list of residents can omit the sound. The 45-hertz fundamental, the shuddering subharmonics, the glacial modulation as temperature drifts—not background, but organism. The Root-Skin entrains to it, the moths court to it, the Priests take vows to it. When an earthquake five years ago shifted one fan on its mounts, the note dropped by a quarter-tone and every lobe of the Root-Skin on the south side blistered in the same week, as if scalded. The Priests laboured three days to wedge the housing true again; when the old pitch returned the blisters flattened, and an exhale rolled through the hall bigger than weather.

So who lives here?  
• A single vegetal leviathan sprawling like continental shelf, breathing in paragraphs.  
• Three small animals perfectly edited for silence, each writing its own footnote on motion and stillness.  
• Six brittle, blinking humans who believe that keeping a rotor turning is the same as keeping history intact.  
• And a note—low A, somewhere around fifty cycles per second—that has learned to inhabit matter the way a pearl inhabits a shell.

Together they make a slow ecology whose only true currency is continuation. Nothing is born for hurry; nothing dies before being heard, if only by the pressure-sensitive leaves of the Root-Skin or the carved, salt-bitten ears of the Priests. Life here is a pact with inertia, drafted in condensation, countersigned in rust, and read aloud forever by the voice of turning steel.
