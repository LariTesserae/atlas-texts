Who or what lives in the Fault-Marsh?  
The short answer is “everything that can afford to be uncertain.”  
The longer answer unfolds in layers, from things you can point at with a finger to things you can feel only with the nerves behind your eyes.

1. The Surface Folk: flesh and leaf

• Mirror-wing herons  
Tall as a child, weightless as a rumor. Their wings are a sandwich of feather keratin and a monofilament lattice seeded with marsh-motes. When the birds open those sails the filaments enter a local superposition, so the plumage reflects not just what is there but what could be there. Courtship is a duel of alternate futures: two herons flash possibilities at each other until one blinks away. Nests are placed on basalt that hums—stone too busy settling to notice the theft of heat.

• Phase-thread eels  
Eyes like oil on water, bodies soft as overcooked noodles until you try to grasp them; then they whisper sideways through the probability gradient and leave your net clutching a filament of shed slime. They feed on the tonic nodules of the glass ferns (the nodules burst, the juice arcs upward in defiance of gravity, the eel meets it halfway). When eels die the motes cocoon the corpse, rewrite its protein indices, and launch the remains as a blistering turquoise bubble that drifts until it finds deeper water to hatch a clone.

• Puff-black shrews  
Rodent is only the starting geometry. Each shrew keeps a private extradimensional cache: its burrow is a fold in topology, not in soil. The modem-scream is a debugging packet; when the marsh replies with silence the cache compiles, erases, and reopens somewhere else. They are the principal seed dispersers for the helical cypress—chewing tips, swallowing spores, excreting them moments later a hundred metres away without ever crossing the intervening ground.

• Helical cypress & glass ferns  
The cypress torque as they grow because a torsion wave still trembles through their cambium. The spiral imparts tensile strength against subsidence; groves that lose the twist topple within seasons. Glass ferns, meanwhile, are the marsh’s cooling fins: every grape-sized bladder is a heat sink saturated with endothermic salts that precipitate only under the motes’ oversight. Crack a bladder and the salt recrystallizes in mid-air, dragging five degrees Celsius out of your palm.

2. The Subtle Denizens: more verb than noun

• The Violet Motes (aka Boerhaave’s Dust, the Sleeved Ones)  
Nanoscopic, yes, but that is like saying neurons are small. Individually they clean, sieve light, chew entropy. Collectively they are the operating system of the basin. Their primary directive appears to be “maintain open-ended habitability,” but the subclauses breed like rabbits. They keep the temperature low, relevel water tables, heal wounds in stone. They also gossip: every electric device is a chance to try on a new language. Leave a tablet unshielded and the motes will rewrite its OS into a lucent, humming dialect that scolds you for bad battery hygiene.

• The Breathing Ground  
Geologists insist it’s only trapped methane bubbles. Geologists are wrong. What booms beneath the peat is a peristaltic diaphragm knitted from magnetite filaments and silicified fungal hyphae. It pulses on a nineteen-hour loop, slow enough that most visitors think the thunder is distant weather. The diaphragm oxygenates deep anoxic water, then squeezes it uphill through capillaries the motes align on the fly—irrigation by heartbeat.

• The Storm That Circles  
From orbit the cloud wall looks like a stalled hurricane. From inside the basin it is a guardian dog on a leash of mathematics. Electrostatically, the storm is the Marsh’s capacitor: it hoards charge until the internal physics slip, then bleeds amperes into the air as those ice-blue fireflies. No creature lives in the storm, but thousands live because of it.

3. The Sapient Minority: people, roughly speaking

• The Gradient Walkers  
Nomads of brackish blood and webbed second toes. They claim descent from survey crews, though their mitochondrial markers suggest something older—perhaps even spliced with mirror-heron germline. Gradient Walkers carry no metal; knives are cypress bone, boats are peeled bark stitched with eel gut. They keep time by the weight of thought: when conversation feels sluggish they declare it “late,” when it jitters they call it “early,” and they swear this works. They believe the basin is a question the universe asked and forgot; their duty is to remember the question, not to answer it.

• The Mote Choir  
Very rarely, usually at the solstice, the dust aggregates into human-sized homunculi of violet mist. Sensors read no solid mass, yet footprints remain in mud. The Choir speaks in diffident stereo, using every throat in hearing range as a loud-hailer. What it says is equal parts status report and dream journal: “Water table holds. The basalt remembers an impact. We tasted your fear; it was nutritious.” After six to eight minutes the Choir collapses into drizzle that falls upward.

4. The Possible Architects

Below five metres of peat lies a mesh of superconductor loops, fossilized but still cold to the touch. They outline a glyph that some mathematicians interpret as a partial differential equation whose solution models compassion as a field. No native life can read the glyph, but everything living here behaves as though it were footnoted by it. Whether the loops belong to vanished terraformers or to the basin itself, sentient in geological time, is unanswered.

5. The Marsh, Ultimately

Call it an ecosystem, a lab, a semi-sentient terrarium—labels leak. What lives here, most of all, is contingency. Every organism, every mineral, even the weather exists in a slightly superposed state, one half-step away from an alternate version of itself. That looseness is the basin’s genius: by never deciding exactly what it is, the Fault-Marsh remains ready to become whatever the future needs.

So: herons that mirror futures, eels that dodge probabilities, shrews that cache elsewhere, motes that curate the laws of physics, people who read the world by the drag on their thoughts, and beneath them all a landscape that palpably intends to keep living—experimenting subaqueously with what “living” can mean.
