WHO LIVES HERE

1. The Brinefolk – human, but only just  
They are the descendants of the first extraction crews: a few thousand people stretched over five centuries of steamy dusk. Generations in the basin have braided a culture as sinewy as the levees themselves.

Physiology  
• Skin: clay-red to iron-brown, stained by ferrous dust that never truly washes out. Children are born paler; by adolescence the mineral patina has fixed, and the finger-whorls darken like wet pottery.  
• Salt pores: many adults carry a faint rash of crystalline bloom at the hairline and elbows—a benign adaptation, osmotic glands that leak brine so kidneys are spared the endless salt. In the hot season they scrape the crust with obsidian scalpels, a communal grooming that leaves mosaic scars treasured as family signatures.  
• Voice: low, reined in. The air is dense; anything shouted travels far. Gossip here is conducted in glottal clicks and hand-flash code learned from dronelines. Visitors describe a city of mimes.

Dress  
Clothing begins with the same glassy fabric that wraps the condenser towers. Filaments of fused quartz and copper mesh are woven on slow treadle looms, then dipped in latex skimmed from skimsail eggs. The result is supple, heat-reflective, and impervious to the fungal rust that devours organic cloth by sunrise.

Family & Dwelling  
A “house” is a stack of three platforms:  
• The Cool Deck, lowest, half a metre above the brine, slatted so mist can breathe through. That is for sleep.  
• The Work Deck, loud with lathes, baby pumps, and pickling vats.  
• The Dry Deck, highest, sheet-roofed in corroded tin, reserved for fire, stories, and the scraping of salt pores.  

Families reorganise every ten years when the old pilings are surveyed; households swap members like parts between engines so each platform keeps at least one pipewright, one cook, and one licensed mist-reader.

Economy  
Condensate is coin. It is collected in tear-shaped bladders of silvered gut; they clink soft as wind-chimes. One adult needs six drams a day to stay kidney-sure. Every trade recites both money price and water price. (“Three nails, two drams.”) Children learn to count on a rigged abacus where each bead weighs exactly one sip.

Guilds  
• Pumpwrights (ocher scarves) maintain the rhythmic heartbeats of Carrow Mire. They know the lullaby that keeps pistons stroking evenly through the night; if a note is sung off key the whole quadrant can water-hammer itself apart.  
• Lamplighters (white-sooted hands) harvest the copper netting from dead droneline cords, re-spin it into wicks, and patrol the viaducts lighting the amber eggs.  
• Skimsailers (oil-black teeth from chewing kelp tar) drift after the manta-things, shaving dew from steaming backs. Their punts are near-silent, and so are they; jokes about gills behind the ears persist.  
• Clicker Shepherds (hollow anklets made of chitin rings) corral crustacean swarms toward the evaporation pans where the animals are pickled alive for meat and plate.

Belief  
The faith of the basin is Distillation. Anything pure must once have been impure and pressured. Birth is called First Flash; death is called Final Condense. Corpses are sleeved in boiler cloth, weighted with salt bricks, and lowered into the brine where ferro-bacteria jacket the bones in iron lace. Each generation sends divers to retrieve an ancestor’s reliquary skeleton and rivet it into the levee—family and seawall reinforcing one another.

2. The Companion Species  

a. Clickers (Order: Decapoda ferrosalis)  
• Size: palm to forearm.  
• Carapace: clear keratin over iron nodules; when young they glitter like comet soot.  
• Behaviour: migrate by the million, always upwind so exhaled CO₂ guides them toward basalt weepers rich in trace metals. Their clicking is joint cavitation; a swarm sounds like hail on tin.  
• Use: meat, armour scutes, and an algal paste from their gut that flavours every basin stew.

b. Skimsails (Class: Chondroskathoi)  
• Form: dish-shaped, 2–3 m across, charcoal grey with glints of cobalt. No true mouth—just a muscular vacuum slot on the underside that inhales both water and prey.  
• Locomotion: four fin-tips row in slow figure-eights, barely breaking surface tension; hence “sailing.”  
• Special organ: dorsal condensation plate kept cooler than ambient by evaporative pumping. Each dawn beads of near-pure water pearl there. Removing a plate kills the animal, taboo under Rule Two, so the Skimsailers practice “dew-milking” with horn spoons while the creature drifts on.

c. Dronelines (Genus: Sibilia)  
• Looks: centimetre-long midges with quartz wing-ribs and abdomens extruding silicate thread.  
• Life cycle: egg in salt foam → larva in mist film → imago in air. Adults never land; they anchor by extruding cable, knitting themselves into harp-like arrays that drink vibration.  
• Role: pest control and communication. Residents pluck tuned strands to signal across districts—an aerial telegraph audible only through the bones of the listener’s skull.

d. Unseen Giants  
Miners hear them first: tidal groans vibrating through bedrock. “Basalt eelpups,” say elders—worm things the girth of subway tunnels, eating iron, excreting magnetite pearls later dredged up and sold as compass hearts. No surface witness has confirmed them, but sometimes in fog the levees shudder as if something vast has rolled beneath.

3. The Microbiological Choir  
Every puddle is a parliament. Ferro-bacteria build rust cathedrals one micron thick. Arsenic algae bloom crimson then bleach to white gypsum filigree. Mists rebreathe exhalations of sulfate goblins that ignite when lightning dares strike. The Brinefolk do not name these citizens; they give them verbs—because colonies act rather than abide. “The clay is peeling” means the bacteria are migrating. “The weeper sweats” means the halophiles have unzipped another basalt vein.

4. How They Touch Strangers  
A visitor is offered a ceramic bead of distilled water. Accept it and you must reciprocate with equal mass—metal, story, whatever can survive the climate. Decline and you are marked “brief.” Briefs are given wide berth; they rustle the quiet, and predators favour novelty.

The second gift is silence. A guide will walk you along the viaducts saying nothing while the city’s pulse infiltrates your hearing: piston hymn, clicker tide, the sigh of towers condensing breath for thousands. Only when you mimic their soft tread will anyone speak again.

5. Forecasts  
• Next Sulphur Bloom: nineteen days. Skimsails will swirl like ash in updraft, and Brinefolk couples will lie on Cool Decks inhaling the narcotic reek—tradition holds that children conceived under yellow sky inherit clearer kidneys.  
• Levee Renewal: three months. Families will argue which ancestor skeletons may be pried loose for resealing. Bones too thin to reuse are powdered and scattered in evaporation pans where clicker larvae will eat them, closing the loop.

6. Closing Image  
Picture a twilight hot as breath. On the horizon the iron islands glow red, not with fire but with the reflection of furnace doors just opened for the night shift. Between those scattered embers the marsh quivers: millions of glassy insects stringing harpwire, shoals of translucent crustaceans skittering like living rain, manta-shadows skimming, and amid it all the Brinefolk—quiet figures padding across catwalks under sputtering amber lamps, each carrying a small silver bladder that sloshes with the currency of their continued existence.

In the hush you hear the rule obeyed by every inhabitant, mineral or mortal: hold your shape, but only as hard as this warm, breathing world will allow.
