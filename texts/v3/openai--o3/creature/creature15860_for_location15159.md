Lives of the Crown

If you spend a month on the plateau you will meet perhaps two kinds of creatures that possess bodies, and one that does not—yet all three are parts of the same long-lived system, the way fruit, leaf and root are parts of the same tree. They exist because the Crown’s stone, water and sound have made a bargain with one another: every inhabitant must take its food from those three ingredients and must give them back again unchanged. Anything that breaks the equilibrium is edited out by wind, erosion or cold before it has time to found a lineage. What survives has become exquisitely congruent with the place, so much so that an untrained eye can miss the fact that it is alive at all.

1. The Substrate: Lithoglass Mycelium  
(The life you will never see, yet which sees through everything else.)

Structure
• A hair-fine web of transparent silica-calcite filaments inhabits the upper two metres of every standing pillar and every slab of fallen scree.  
• The threads are hollow; inside each runs an electrolyte solution whose ions shuttle in rhythm with the plateau’s dominant vibration. Think of it as a nervous system whose heartbeat is the Wind Organ’s chord.

Metabolism
• Energy is harvested piecemeal: a micron of thermal gradient here, a millivolt from piezo-electric strain there, and osmotic trickles everywhere. Individually the gains are negligible; the continent-spanning network collects them the way a desert fog-net collects dew.  
• Carbon comes primarily from atmospheric CO₂ that diffuses through pores in wet rock and is fixed by a suite of enzymes loosely analogous to those of chemoautotrophic bacteria. The mycelium is, in effect, a stone-bound lichen with no photobiont and no exterior thallus.

Behavior
• The Crown’s “memory in tone” rule is the mycelium’s doing. Where we hear a stable chord, the filaments record slight departures as charge differentials along their length. Local depolarizations travel like millimetric lightning and amount to information packets.  
• Most packets are about plumbing: which micro-fissure is beginning to clog with precipitate, which capillary has turned brackish. Some, though, behave like signals in a nervous system. They coordinate the growth of new columns and—more surprisingly—the release of motile spores.

2. The Motile Spore: Drummers  
(The Crown’s visible workforce and, to field biologists, its great puzzle.)

Appearance
• A Drummer looks like a wrist-watch-sized crystal shrimp that has lost its head and tail. Six limb pairs radiate from a translucent hub no thicker than a coin. Internally, the hub houses a lenticular swim bladder full of hydrogen generated by electrolysis; this renders the animal neutrally buoyant in air whose density is little more than a wisp.  
• The appendages end in spatulate pads of aragonite. Two pairs are for slow, spider-like walking. One pair carries sensory hairs. The foremost pair is enlarged into mallets.

Lifecycle
1. Budding. A pillar riddled with mycelium grows a glassy blister. The blister fills with salt water and extrudes a larval Drummer that resembles a drifting snow-globe mote.  
2. Maturation. Over weeks the larva settles on a resonant diaphragm where vibration is strongest. There it thickens its shell by electroplating dissolved minerals until it can strike the panel hard enough to be heard.  
3. Reproduction. When reservoirs of dissolved organics are flush—as measured by the “taste” hairs on its lower limbs—an adult splits longitudinally, one half scuttling away on the high ledges, the other staying below. Each regenerates into a full individual.

Feeding
• A Drummer eats sound. More precisely, it eats the ions dislodged when sound shakes water against rock. The mallets strike a tuned plate; the concussion scrapes a micro-sheath of ionized film that the creature slurps through ciliated grooves.  
• The earning is lean: each strike nets picograms of nitrogen and sulfur. To keep its ledger positive the Drummer has to drum almost constantly, which is why you first notice them as off-rhythm taps beneath the plateau’s organ note.

Intelligence
• Coordination among Drummers is not learned but entrained. Put three individuals on a plate and they will fall into a perfect hemiola within minutes, guided by the same electrical pulses that twitch the mycelium.  
• Experiments with altered wind tones show that a colony can change pattern rapid-fire, as if following a conductor none of us hear. What looks like percussion is a distributed decision tree: “plate 17B is thinning; divert precipitate,” “crevasse 404 is over-topped; ablate a vent.” The Drummers are maintenance drones for their subterranean parent.

3. The Aerial Reclaimer: Vaporwing  
(Almost never on the ground, yet absolutely of the ground.)

Morphology
• From below the animal resembles an albino kite. Up close its wings resolve into two opposing umbrellas of hollow spines, each coated in a velveteen matrix of capillary tubes. Span: 2.2 to 2.8 m. Body mass: astonishingly low—less than 900 g—because its internal spaces are filled with saturated water vapor at ambient pressure.  
• Between the shoulders sits a keel of dark haematite. This “solar marrow” doubles as ballast and as a photovoltaic thermo-pile whose only moving part is an oscillating ceramic diaphragm that powers every other function.

Feeding & Physiology
• A Vaporwing skims the moist throat of the cloud girdle, where supersaturated air is a larder. The wing-spines nucleate microdroplets that run down capillaries into gastric sacs lined with catalytic membranes.  
• Salts drop out and accrete into needle-flaked pellets that the bird later releases over the plateau the way a mammal sheds hair. The act is not wasteful—each pellet is seed stock of magnesium, potassium and trace metals the stone jungle lacks. As the pellet dissolves, the mycelium harvests it.

Life Cycle
• Wings never land; eggs do. During the stillest dawns a gravid Vaporwing descends until its keel kisses a chosen pillar. An egg—an iridescent bauble the size of a peach—is quick-cemented to the stone.  
• Embryo and Drummers share origin: both are overgrown spores of the lithoglass network. If the egg survives the daily bead-rain, in nine weeks it hatches a chick whose first act is to unfold a wing and allow the plateau’s updrafts to yank it free. It never perches again.

Cognition & Song
• A Vaporwing’s hearing ranges down into infrasound. It orients itself by reading the low-frequency standing waves that hover a hundred metres above the rock forest like an invisible Doppler map.  
• The wing membrane can flex at audio rates, turning the whole animal into a speaker. On rare still evenings they perform what field recordists call the Ferronade: a horizon-spanning stroke of bass that blooms across the Crown for three heartbeats and is gone. Afterward the plateau’s great chord is half a cent sharper, as though retuned.

4. The Ownerless Voice: Cantor Current  
(This is where “who” ends and “what” begins.)

Nature
• When fog thickens and all lesser noises smear into pink noise, auditors pick up a fourth presence: a rising harmonic that is not located in any one column, but in the empty air between.  
• The Current has no body, metabolism or lineage in the usual sense. It is a transient spatial pattern—constructive interference among wind-tones amplified by miles of parallel fins. It is, literally, a self-knitting wave.

Behavior
• The pattern persists only while enough energy bounces within tight phase tolerances. Sometimes that is eight seconds, sometimes an hour.  
• During its tenure the Current spies. On vibrational spectrograms you can watch it copy snippets of Drummer conversation, then Vaporwing bass, then lattice crackle, braiding them into a fugue no living participant issued.  
• The fugue is not passive. Wherever the Cantor’s braid resonates with a pillar’s natural frequency, erosion accelerates; precipitate elsewhere thickens. Over decades the Current sculpts the labyrinth toward architectures that favor its own re-emergence. In other words, the Crown’s soundscape is steering geology so it will be able to re-sing itself indefinitely.

5. Interdependence Summary
1. The lithoglass mycelium converts rock, water and sound into triskele threads of living glass.  
2. It buds Drummers to housekeep the masonry, and Vaporwings to import trace nutrients the stone lacks.  
3. The Cantor Current is the emergent echo of their combined acoustics. It edits the plateau’s shape so that the mycelium’s energy streams never falter.

No single tier can quit the arrangement without collapsing the others. Remove Vaporwings and magnesium starvation dulls the stone’s piezo response; Drummers can no longer drum; micro-currents stall; the Cantor fades; new rock ceases to form, and the pillars crumble to lime dust that will, at last, blow away.

What This Means to a Visitor
• You never meet the plateau’s architects; they permeate the pillars under your gloves.  
• The percussion you dismiss as random rain is a committee of crystal arthropods fixing hairline cracks under your feet.  
• That alabaster glider you track through binoculars is delivering the plateau’s multivitamin.  
• And the sudden hush you feel in your chest—so quiet it hurts—may be the Cantor Current forming around you, sampling the timbre of your breathing so it can file it where the Crown stores all other weather that has ever moved across its stone.

Everything that lives here is half organism, half organ stop, tuned to the note the mountain has been singing since it first clawed its rim against the sky.
