Rootriver Warrens  

1. The shape of the underworld  
Trace any spring on the Canopy Altiplano downward. The trickle widens, darkens, and at last joins a drowned labyrinth the plateau folk never truly see. The Warrens are that labyrinth: an aquifer the size of a minor ocean, honey-combed through ferric limestone by the Matriarch’s descending roots. Picture a ceiling of braided wood two hundred metres thick, from which a million white umbilicals hang like chandeliers. Between those cords lie vaulted chambers—some bone-dry, some half flooded, some drowned to the crown—and all of them shift imperceptibly as growth pressure bends the stone. Cartographers complain that by the time a map is inked, the river that justified it has already found another wall to eat.

Floor strata are memory in hard copy. Upper vaults show red silt—wind-blown dust that fell through broken cambium plates centuries ago. Lower galleries gleam with greenish marcasite and the ghost of old seas. At the very deepest level you find mirror-slabs of primordial glass where lava once cooled against groundwater. The Matriarch’s thinnest filaments etch cursive grooves there, spelling—some say—whatever she dreamed while the world was still cooling.

2. Light and colour  
No sun lives here, yet vision never quite surrenders. The roots exhale sugars into the water; chemotrophic algae drink the spill and return blue-violet glow enough to sketch shoreline and limb. Copper ions leaked from Click-Eater carapaces tint the algae, so every pool glows its own weather—turquoise where arthropods scraped, amethyst where none have passed for years.

Where chambers rise into air pockets, stalactites of crystallised sap drip sparks. Warreners shave the drips into arrowheads called silent-stars; one tossed across a cavern is light enough to read by for two slow breaths before its chemistry gutters out.

3. Hydrology and climate  
Temperature sits at twenty-five degrees and barely budges. What changes is pressure. Twice a day the Matriarch’s great vascular beat swells every root tube. Water displaces stone, seeks the only yieldable path, and whole rivers reverse course for an hour. This is Hearttide. First beat: waters draw inland, baring mossy shelves for harvest. Second beat: a pulse comes roaring back, foaming with white exudate the Warreners skim for sugar. Hearttide is clock, calendar and oracle; a child learns its throb in the womb.

Humidity is total; fire is myth here as well. Yet sparks are rumoured. A cave strike between flint beds during Hearttide slack can flash-boil pockets of methane, sending mushroom clouds of steam up the same faults that surface folk call springs. Now and then a Heshut hunter—refilling her canteen—feels the ground cough warm rain and swears she smelled smoke no story accounts for.

4. The sound that is mostly absence  
Above, sound is thickness; below, it is fugitive. Water kills airborne vibration, and stone eats what little survives. The Warrens therefore feel hollow, poised on the edge of a gulp that never swallows. Ears adapt: a traveller learns to read ripple-talk, the aliasing of distant drip into standing wave, the way boot-sole splash throws echoes off root wood and comes back saltier or sweeter depending on algae density.

When noise does arrive it is monumental. A single arthropod resin-bomb exploding topside drops a thunder-bolt down every flooded chimney; stalagmites ring like bell-metal; sleeping fish convulse. Then the silence returns, heavier for having left.

5. Biota in the dark wet  

• Root-Lung Nodules  
Organs the size of amphorae bud off main roots. They rhythmically gulp water, strip it of dissolved oxygen and vent the surplus gas into upper air pockets. Their cuticle is thin parchment; prod one and it hisses an hour’s breathing air. Warreners patch leaks with wax, not wanting to wake the Matriarch’s scab reflex: she can flood a chamber overnight if she believes a root-lung fatally wounded.  

• Mirrorfish  
Scales are perfectly specular; the animal erases itself by reflecting whatever faint light exists. Lateral lines read electromagnetic pulses from roots, letting the fish draft along sap currents the way gliders ride thermals. They school beneath human rafts on night passages, believing hull-knocks are courting drums.  

• Hooded Cephalopods (“Veil-Rays”)  
Flattened octopods with wing spans of three metres. Skin webbed between arms forms a funeral-shroud silhouette; chromatophores pulse concentric rings that mesmerise Mirrorfish until a quick tuck folds prey inside the living cape. Veil-Rays cannot pierce air; they drown if a tide throws them into a dry vault. Warreners carve their beaks—transparent chitin tougher than obsidian—into ritual hair combs.  

• Candle Fungus  
Pale club shapes as long as a man’s arm. They hoard nitrates during the dry beat of Hearttide, then ignite slow chemoluminescent combustion when water returns, shedding flakes of salt and light for exactly four days before collapsing into nutrient slurry. Entire pilgrim processions are timed to walk by fresh candlelight; the dead are left where the last flames die, their lungs filled with glow spores so a passer-by knows whom not to disturb.  

6. The Warreners (people)  
Name themselves Yaruk, “those who drink echoes.” Skin always slick, hair combed with Veil-Ray chitin into backward crests that shed water like the fins of fish. Eyes almond-wide, pupils horizontal ellipses—better to read the difference between up-glow and down-glow.

Physiology  
• Bone mass low; children weaned in floating cradles so ankles never learn the tyranny of full gravity. A Yaruk on the plateau can leap twice her height—but may break ribs coughing the thin, hot air.  
• Tympanic membrane oversized; a second, inward-facing drum sits against the Eustachian tube and senses hydraulic pressure. The two drums in concert give three-dimensional sonar good to forty metres through stone.  
• Kidneys align along the spine like beads, ten instead of two, shunting excess water to sub-cutaneous sacs. Before trading journeys to the surface they drain those sacs via hollow reeds, lightening their weight and gifting the plateau folk precious litres.

Settlement pattern  
Villages cling to upside-down stalactite roots—hollowed, braced, hung with nets. Each is named for the reverberation a hand-clap makes under its porch. The capital is Dub-Dub-Taar, whose echo double-strikes the ear like twin heartbeats. Streets are suspended wood slats; beneath them slow current glows blue. Above every doorway hangs a waterproof bowl: thin ceramic acquired from Heshut traders. One downstroke of a palm over its rim summons a tone unique to the household—doorbell, identity badge, marriage announcement all in one.

Culture  
• Writing is ripple-script: pebbles dropped in encoded cadence, read by eye as interference patterns across a lamplight pool. Scholars travel with wooden tubs; history conferences look like children skipping stones in solemn silence.  
• Law is bound to Hearttide. Crimes judged during the ebb; sentences carried out at the flood—often literal washing-away of taboo objects into depths no diver touches.  
• Highest art is breath-blown rootglass. Gather sap before it sugars, bubble it, anneal it in steam vents. Finished panes ring when struck by water; Yaruk mosaic walls play chords as tides climb them.

7. Rules that keep lungs full  
• Count the pulses: two hundred and twelve between first and second Hearttide beat. Miscount, you’ll be in a crawlspace when the back-rush fills it.  
• Light is costly; never waste glow on footsteps you can memorise by toe.  
• Surface resin burns under water if mixed with fungus oil—but the flame is invisible, heating the water around it. Boil a Veil-Ray by accident once and you will pat the scalds on your palms for life.  
• A hanging root that vibrates faster than your wrist vein is about to grow. Do not tie boats, nets, lovers or pedigrees to it.

8. Strange phenomena  

Blue Drowning  
Every generation the algae population spikes. Oxygen supersaturates the water; fish gasp, then float belly-up, literally poisoned by breath. Warreners mark the year by indigo stains on ceiling rock—the colour never leaches. Famine always follows because Veil-Rays lose prey and turn on village larders.

Rootquakes  
When a plateau column topples, its root net yanks hard miles below. In the Warrens a corridor might slide two body-lengths sideways, doors sealing behind it as if embarrassed by the misstep. Yaruk engineers carve stress gutters—slots in the stone that guide such lurches into sacrificial pillars which explode inward, sparing homes.

Mirror Bloom  
Rarely, a Veil-Ray mating frenzy churns silt until it coats every surface in powdered mica. The whole cavern becomes a fragmented mirror; candle fungus multiplies each spark a thousand times; disoriented predators beach themselves. Children are permitted to play drumming games in the open for those few safe nights, their laughter ricocheting like silver minnows.

9. Trade, memory, kinship to the plateau  
The Warrens send surfeit water upward through fault chimneys; the Heshut call that gift Spring-Mother and repay with pollen cakes wrapped in leaf-sail membrane. Resonator bowls swap hands: Heshut record day-stories in the glaze; Yaruk fill the same bowls with tide percussion. A traveller can descend reading history and ascend listening to it, the two narratives curling around one another like double helix.

Nor is the kinship only human. Click-Eater larvae wash down every wet season. In the dark they metamorphose into Shard-Shrimp: translucent, copper-veined, edible. Yaruk fishers herd them with low whistles then basket them in root-cages. Days later, fragmentary drumming from plateau heights tells of empty pollen caches; a balance, unspoken, is maintained.

10. Things that dream almost like people  

• Sap-Scribes  
Spirals of woody tissue grown by the Matriarch expressly to monitor mineral quotas. They extrude lignin glyphs the way squid jet ink. Yaruk archivists break off the curls, dry them, and read quarterly resource accounts direct from the plant’s own bookkeeping.

• Echo-Gulls  
Not birds but globs of bubble-gel seeded with Soundleech protists. They drift up fault chimneys, reach dry air, pop, and spill their colony. The silence they carry is gift to plateau predators, scourge to the Yaruk who birthed them. So every full moon a deputation climbs to the springs, begging Heshut silence-sharpers to burst stray gulls before they mate with raptor shadow.

11. What it feels like  

A. To be a root-lung: pressure your oldest friend, water your only language. Stone hugs you close, then relaxes; in that exhale you loose oxygen that will become a child’s gasp far above.  

B. To be a Yaruk courier on the Ebb beat: pole skiff down a corridor wide as legend, glow-light stroking cave roof. Heart counts pulses without help. Somewhere past cliff shadow a mirror-fish trails like a thought you forgot to think.  

C. To be a Veil-Ray hunting: fold the world inside your cape; feel the panicked Morse code of scales against skin; taste copper shadows; know the ceiling is a myth told by air-breathers.  

D. To be a candle fungus on the fourth night: burn with slow inevitability, casting your own corpse as lantern. The children will sing while you die; you are glad to go bright.

12. Closing image  
When the second Hearttide beat subsides, water stands still as held breath. For perhaps three heartbeats the whole Rootriver Warrens becomes a lens. In it gleams the upside-down reflection of the Matriarch’s continental crown—seen not by eye but by pressure, colour, thought. Above, the plateau folk crouch at springs, hearing a hush where insect choirs should roar. Below, the Yaruk float in boats that do not rock, tasting silence as sweet as sap. It is the same pause, the same shared prayer, spoken once in thunder, once in whisper, binding trunk to root, canopy to warren, world to itself in one long, resonant chord.
