The Winded Steppe

Follow the river’s absence north-east, where its stolen waters seep underground and the channel itself climbs out of the plain, a pale scar of silt that hardens to shale.  Travel three days along that desiccated scar and it lifts you onto a tableland so broad the horizon has to arc to fit.  Rain forgets this height; clouds shear apart on the escarpment’s rim and tumble away as ragged foams.  Day heat brands the soil, night cold anneals it, and what remains is a land that hums with stored weather.

The ground is flat, almost surgically so, yet it is ribbed lengthwise by thousands of parallel wind-belts—narrow lanes two or three metres across in which the air forever travels the same direction, never quite touching its neighbours.  Between belts lie static corridors of vacancy, lull-strips where dust falls straight down and collects in shallow furrows.  No one remembers what first braided the atmosphere like this; some swear it was the last, indignant sigh of the desertifying river, others that the plateau’s iron core magnetises the sky.  Whatever the cause, the belts run unbroken for kilometres and give the whole plateau the look of an infinite loom already warped with invisible thread.

Vegetation has adapted by choosing sides.  In the belts themselves thrive the spindle-grasses: clean white stalks that lean into the wind until each resembles a taut bowstring.  Their leaves end in backward-facing hooks that catch airborne grit, threshing it for the trace minerals locked inside.  Between the belts, in the lull-strips, grow the repose-shrubs: squat cushions the colour of unbleached wool, fleshy with stolen moisture, unpleasantly sweet when bruised.  Each shrub extends fine root-hairs into the lee of the nearest belt, drinking the cool drawn downward by constant airflow.

From a height—one of the old telemetric pylons that still stud the steppe like vertebrae—you see a shimmering weave: bright wind lanes alternating with matte stillness, the whole surface flickering as the spindle-grasses pivot to keep their aerodynamic poise.

Into this oscillating quiet, people have inserted themselves as patiently as solder in a circuit.

1.  The Belt-Weavers

Name.  They accept “Weavers,” though their own tongue renders it closer to “those who knot the air.”

Lineage.  Many are descendants of floodplain farmers who trailed the vanished river uphill, looking for the source of its departure.  Others arrived later: runaway millwrights, sky-survey deserters, a trickle of Hollowers seeking relief from perpetual damp.

Physique.  Shoulders narrow, lungs long.  The Weavers do not grow taller than one hundred and fifty centimetres; any extra height would topple them in the belts’ knifing gusts.  Skin is cross-hatched with tiny raised welts where wind-borne sand has scoured away hair follicles.  They call these “thread-marks” and read them like a diary of storms survived.

Dress.  Two layers: an inner mesh woven from repose-shrub bast, porous enough to equalise pressure, and an outer carapace of spindle-grass culms lacquered with resin.  The result is light, stiff, and whistles at certain angles, allowing its wearer to hear the wind’s temper before it escalates.

Speech.  Every sentence begins with a directional.  To omit it is to speak without gravity.  A Weaver greeting a friend might say: “Leeward-eighth, I missed your silence,” which means, roughly, “Standing here, I have come from leeward lane eight, and in that coming I noted your absence.”

Shelter.  Homes occupy the lull-strips.  Each begins as a waist-high earth mound tamped into a shallow oval.  Along the windward edge the builders drive a row of spindle-grass ribs, bend them crosswise, and lash them into a biconvex vault that parts the air like the bow of a ship.  Fine sand accumulates against the rear curve, sealing the dwelling in a blanket of dust that thickens a finger-width per season.  After ten years a house is mostly dune; its inhabitants live in a cool silence broken only by the click of airlocks.

Food.  In the belts they plant cotton: a dwarf cultivar whose bolls cling tight and never quite open, guarding the lint from windburn.  In the lulls they raise gourds inflated with compressed air instead of water; pierce one and it sighs itself flat, leaving behind a sweet, dry rind.  Though cotton is their main export, it rarely travels as raw staple.  Instead the Weavers spin it on treadle-driven flyer wheels geared to the belts’ own momentum, turning the wind into constant tread.  The result is a yarn already pre-tensioned, famous for keeping its twist even in swamp humidity.

Work.  Three crafts dominate:  
•  Airsteading—mapping the shifting edges of each belt, pruning stray grasses that threaten to snarl neighbouring lanes.  
•  Galebinding—tying helical ribbon windsocks that, when planted in a belt, extract torque for small generators.  Each binder can read an unspooling windsock the way a river pilot reads ripples.  
•  Threadkeeping—the slow, meditative task of coaxing cotton-yarn into wide webs called haul-sheets.  These are folded, weighted, and slid down-slope over days toward foreign markets.

Customs.  
•  Children learn balance by standing in a lull and leaning into the adjacent belt until the gust holds them upright.  A misjudged lean means an embarrassing tumble across the strip; elders watch, betting quietly on who will turn a fall into a roll.  
•  During Dead-Calm—the scant hours each year when all belts pause together—the Weavers emerge from their mounds, unlock tall brass harps, and let the stillness ring the strings with its own dry fingertips.  The harps are dismantled an hour later, before the plateau remembers to breathe again.

2.  The Companion Currents

Ribbon Winds.  Narrow bands of air so thin they can thread a needle’s eye, yet so forceful they can flense paint from metal.  When two intersect they twist into a rope-vortex that leaps the lull-strips in search of new belts, snatching hats and inattentive birds.

Dust-Flares.  Static corridors sometimes spark, lofting clouds of charged silt that glow violet at twilight.  These flares sterilise insect eggs in exposed soil, a nuisance the Weavers mitigate by burying gourds a handspan deeper than necessary.

Peregrine Fog.  Once or twice a month, moisture-heavy breath escapes the pillar-birch basin far below, hugs the escarpment, and arrives as a single migrating cloud.  It slides up the tableland’s ramp like a ghost tidal bore, condenses along belt edges, and ices every spindle-grass in scintillant rime.  The Weavers harvest this rime before sunrise, shaking it into glazed amphorae.  By noon it is liquid water of a delicacy they cannot otherwise taste.

3.  Fauna, or Near Enough

Grain-Gnats.  Each no larger than a comma, transparent except for a black thoracic stripe.  They hover in static corridors, bobbing like punctuation in an unseen sentence.  When a belt shifts course the gnats rearrange within minutes, spelling out new patterns that veteran galebinders consult before sewing a windsock.

One-Step Kites.  Quadrupeds in theory; practically, they spend life aloft, tethered to the ground by prehensile tails hooked into spindle-grass stems.  A kite pup takes its first and last step simultaneously—climbing the stalk, unfurling membranous flanks, and letting the belt carry it horizon-long.  Weavers track wind velocity by noting the angle of kites’ dangling shadows.

Metal-Tooths.  Rust-red beetles that chew galvanised wire.  They infest abandoned telemetric pylons, worrying at bolts until whole sections fall.  When a pylon collapses the beetles migrate in a clattering swarm, sounding like hail on an empty tin roof.

4.  Technology, Such as It Is

Wind-Looms.  Imagine a vertical fence whose slats are hollow reeds mounted on gimbals.  Air passing through spins each reed; the twist is transferred by gut-cord to a lateral beam, which reciprocates a shuttle across a stretched warp of cotton.  A single adult can tend six looms by strolling back and forth, oiling joints with repose-sap, and singing timing chants audible only within that particular lull.

Torque-Sills.  Shallow pits lined with slate, half-filled with braided rope.  Belt air diverted through ceramic vents turns buried paddles, winding the rope under tension.  When the steppe falls still, Weavers hitch carts to the rope’s free end and flick a bone release; the rope uncoils, towing the cart across kilometres of lull before it slackens.

Hope-Flumes.  These began as accidental artefacts.  Haul-sheets dragging cotton southward carved low channels in the dust.  Over decades the channels polished to hardpan, and cotton friction ionised the air above them, creating faintly luminescent corridors that point unerringly toward the basin.  Night caravans follow the glow downhill, arriving just as dawn fog begins to climb the escarpment behind them.

5.  Commerce & Kinship with the Hollow Basin

What the Steppe Sends Downhill  
•  Bolt-cotton already spun into half-twist skeins, prized by Hollower reed-loomers for its refusal to slacken in humidity.  
•  Resin panels peeled from repose-shrubs, impermeable yet breathable; basin engineers use them to gasket sap-pipes.  
•  Shattered pylon iron, magnetically tempered by a century of sky-stress; re-forged in pillar-birch foundries into lathe beds that never warp.

What the Basin Sends Uphill  
•  Bark parchment—waterproof, wind-proof, used here to sheath haul-sheet leading edges so they skate rather than drag.  
•  Lichen polish, which Weavers daub on spindle-grass ribs to keep them supple against sand-scour.  
•  Fog itself, arriving unbidden, a courier with no manifest.

Cultural Exchange  
A handful of young Hollowers apprentice each year as galebinders, trading mist-breath patience for belt-listening nerve.  Likewise, steppe adolescents descended to the basin often return after a season able to measure wind by ear alone, having learned that stillness can be as articulate as gale.

Shared Superstition  
Both peoples believe the river survives somewhere underground, carrying messages between root and dust.  Hollowers swear the pillar-birches lean fractionally north-east, listening for buried surf.  Weavers claim certain dust-flares taste faintly of tannin, as if the water below dreams of its aldered banks.

6.  Census of Everything That Stirs

•  1,124 Weavers across the plateau’s mapped third.  
•  Approximately 8,000 wind-looms in continuous or intermittent operation.  
•  19 standing pylons, 47 fallen, 0 entirely abandoned—every heap of scrap houses Metal-Tooths worth harvesting.  
•  One river in exile beneath the crust, which those who kneel can sometimes hear, ticking like a faraway metronome.

Stand in a lull at dusk.  The belts on either side blur to satin, haul-sheets skate downslope trailing cotton perfume, and over the rim of plateau the basin’s night fog appears, a pale tongue tasting the dry roof of the world.  Somewhere inside that fog a candle behind breathing bark answers the same dusk, and for a moment the distance between damp hollow and winded steppe narrows to a single shared inhalation—moisture lifting, breeze descending, two small civilizations exchanging their proofs of life in the only language both can trust: the slow barter of water and wind.
