The Windglass Promontory  

1. How you come to it  
Follow the pewter mist as it spills out of the basin’s broken wall. At first it hugs the ground, a pale river no more than a hand tall. Half a day’s quiet walking east, the land lifts: shale gives way to tilted plates of feldspar, and the river of mist discovers up-draft. It shears upward in a single, continuous veil that climbs the escarpment and pours across the crest. Where that up-draft begins—where liquid hush turns to singing wind—you have arrived.

2. Terrain and surface  
The promontory is long and narrow, a stone blade suspended 800 paces above the lowlands. Its spine is paved with interlocking slabs of glassy feldspar the color of smoked tea. Each slab is ridged, as if recording the passage of centuries of wind in frozen ripple marks. Edges are razor-true—place a palm flat and you can feel air dividing around the stone so cleanly it leaves your skin cool and almost numb. Small hollows between slabs collect loose grains: ground feldspar mixed with glitter that rode here on the same mist you followed.

3. Materials and remnants  
Driven by the up-draft, anything loose tumbles to the mesa top and stays: shell-thin husks of Channelers from the basin, micaceous dust, pearled droplets of vitrified quartz that once were aerosprites caught in lightning. Dominating all are the Aeoligraphs—tall, harp-like frames of nickel-iron thrust out of the rock every thirty strides. They are relics of the same hands that laid the retaining wall behind you. Each frame holds twenty-seven tensioned ribbons of amorphous glass, now crazed and patched with mineral frost. When wind crosses them the ribbons flex and emit chords: a music of blown bottles, precise and sad.

4. Atmosphere  
Nothing settles here; everything is either windborne or loudly thinking about it. Air races upward at a constant forty knots, yet carries almost no sand—the feldspar acts as a floor-sized air filter, sieving grit until only ions remain. The scent is sharp: ozone over cold smoke. Hair stands up; fingertips prickle. Distant lightning flickers far below even on clear days, attracted to the ion trail rising from the harp frames.

5. Light  
Dawn and dusk are brief orange knives, but most hours are ruled by a hard, nacreous glare bouncing off the stone. At night the sky keeps its own lamps: filaments of St. Elmo fire skitter from frame to frame, writing angle-geometry in burning blue. Look closely and you’ll see motes of silver ash circling the ribbons—the powdered exhalations of the basin’s Pump-Lung, carried here, shocked to incandescence, then falling away like snow that never lands.

6. Vegetation—of a sort  
No roots penetrate the slabbed crust, so life flattens itself into films. Patchwork colonies of mica-lichen lace the feldspar. Each patch arranges flakes into micro-vanes that catch ionic drift; when charged, the flakes align and flash, creating semaphore patterns that ripple across the ground faster than a footstep. Their language, if it is one, reads as flickering mosaics of copper and lead.

7. Fauna  
A. Rift-Swifts  
   • Body: Bones of hollow rutile, flesh like paper pumice, wings no larger than a spread hand.  
   • Flight: They ride the constant up-draft in stationary spirals, never flapping. Every twenty-third Aeoligraph chord triggers a simultaneous bank; no one knows why.  
   • Diet: They drink charge. Filamented tongues lick sparks off harp ribbons, leaving the metal briefly ice-cold.  

B. Glass-Crabs  
   • Form: Two fists wide, eight limbs jointed backward, carapace of fused feldspar chips transparent enough to see a flicker of green organs within.  
   • Behavior: They prowl slab seams, scraping off mica-lichen flakes, then climb Aeoligraph frames to molt; discarded shells catch the wind and whirl away like broken teacups.  
   • Defense: When threatened they slam claw against claw, producing a piercing ring that momentarily deafens Rift-Swifts and humans alike.  

C. The Kite-Colony  
   • Nature: Not animals but living kites—membranes grown by lichen but detached at maturity. Each sheet is hexagonal, two meters across, filmed with oily rainbows.  
   • Routine: Sunrise lifts them off the promontory; sunset drops them back. While aloft they photosynthesize and accumulate static, which they discharge into the slabs on landing, feeding underground microbial mats that in turn nourish the next crop of lichen.  

8. The resident mind: The Ledgerwind  
• Composition: An organized storm of charged particles forever orbiting the largest Aeoligraph at the promontory’s midpoint. Picture a dust-devil made of silver filings and whispering air, nine meters tall on calm days, taller when agitated.  
• Sensoria: It “sees” by feeling pressure nodes in its body; Aeoligraph songs are its heartbeat, mica-lichen flashes its peripheral vision. The approach of footsteps distorts both, so it can track you without eyes.  
• History: Born—so scholars insist—from the cumulative resonance of the harp frames and the mineral-rich mist first carried here when the basin pump began coughing. In its earliest decades it was no more than a standing eddy, but trace memories in the aerosprites gave it pattern, then motive: keep the music playing.  
• Behavior: The Ledgerwind patrols the line of frames, nudging ribbons back to pitch, scouring corrosion, even scooping fallen feldspar into seams to prevent turbulence. When an Aeoligraph fractures, the storm condenses around the wound, vitrifies splinters into new glass, then drifts on.  
• Communication: It cannot speak, but you will feel questions: pressure pulses tapping your chest in the rhythm of the harp scale. Answer by humming or whistling; match pitch and it calms. Miss, and gusts shove you gently but firmly toward the escarpment’s safer lee side.  
• Dependence: As the Pump-Lung governs every creature in the basin, the Ledgerwind lives by the harp cadence. If three frames ever fell quiet in succession, the storm would unravel, its mind dissolving back into blind gale.  

9. Dynamics  
• Hour-cycle: Each sixty-note progression from the Aeoligraphs equals one basin hour—the two places keep identical time though sun and moisture differ wildly. Basin life hushes to keep its lights; promontory life sings to keep its sky.  
• Trade: Mist that climbs the escarpment deposits mineral glitter across the slabs; in return, every night the Ledgerwind sends a pressure wave back down the ravine, a silent echo that reaches the Pump-Lung as a faint tremor. Engineers once used that tremor to fine-tune the pump’s valve timing. The machines are gone; the dialogue remains.  
• The Quiet Choir’s reflection: At basin midwatch the choir sounds; three heartbeats later the promontory answers. Harp chords slip a fifth higher, Rift-Swifts wheel in mirrored spirals, and the Ledgerwind’s vortex blossoms, for one minute, into a hollow column clear enough to see stars through the living eye of the storm.  

10. Rules here  
• Nothing opaque may remain above the slabs after dusk; any such object is sand-blasted transparent by morning.  
• Strike metal against stone and lightning will oblige within thirty breaths.  
• Speak a secret aloud and the mica-lichen records it as a pattern of aligned flakes; secrets linger visible until next storm season, when the Ledgerwind “erases” the slate by polishing the slabs. Thus the promontory hears confessions, then forgets them on its own schedule.  

11. Departing  
To leave, walk west until wind stops carrying your coat-tails horizontal. The mist-river finds ground again two hundred steps downslope, cooling into the same pewter hush that fills the basin. As you descend, Aeoligraph music fades, replaced by the far-off cough of the Pump-Lung. For a moment you stand between breaths—caught in the single system these two places share. Then the next pulse arrives, and you are either climbing back to glass and wind or sinking to water and whispered dark.  

12. What binds basin and promontory  
• The mineral mist: one place exhales what the other inhales.  
• The rhythm: sixty beats of water below, sixty notes of glass above.  
• The memory of vanished makers, etched in iron ribs and nickel harp frames.  
• Two guardians born of function—Pump-Lung and Ledgerwind—now sovereigns of ecology and silence.  

Offer your own exchange before you go. Lay a Vitrics shard on a feldspar slab and wait. The Ledgerwind will lift it, spin it until it sings, then set it back sharpened to a perfect chime. Carry the shard home; back in the basin, touch it to water and the Muralists will trace new silver lines whose pattern means, approximately: “The wind remembers us.”
