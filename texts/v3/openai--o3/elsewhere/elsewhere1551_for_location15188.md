The Shed-Skin Archipelago  

1. How to arrive  
Do not climb; the way is vertical but grown, not built.  Choose a chimney-trunk near the twilight latitudes when it begins to belch its seasonal updraft.  Step inside the hollow core.  The warm exhale—a constant 4 m/s column scented of nickel sap and peppermint ammonia—will bear you upward through translucence.  In twenty minutes you burst through the planet-tree’s highest fronds into windless, stratospheric mint-light.  There the discarded crusts drift: continents of former laminate curled like dried petals, fused at their margins into a slow-turning halo that girdles the globe 48 kilometres above true ground.

2. What it is made of  
Each island began as a hexagonal plate shed during a global molt.  In the climb they unfolded, thinned, and glassified.  Starlight and cosmic ice annealed them into panes of cellulose carbide one millimetre thick, strong as armour, translucent as old sugar.  Impact dust has since frosted the surfaces, frosting that blurs all colour to milk-green twilight.  From below the Archipelago looks like a circling aurora; from above it resembles a broken, back-lit circuit diagram sprawling around a dark core.

3. Geometry under zero weather  
Gravity is present but sullen—barely a tenth of surface pull.  Plates buckle into improbable kites: parabolas that meet at right angles, Klein hinges locked half-open.  Some islands are Möbius miles long, gifting any walker the vertigo of stepping across his own shadow upside-down.  The Laminate Mind’s growth-logic survives in the fossil tissue; if you scratch a guideline into the glass, the whole sheet cramps and realigns, attempting to regrow purpose.

4. Air  
Breathable, thin, effervescent with sublimated ammonia snow that never reaches the surface below.  Inlets at the plate edges inhale from the planet’s luminous exhale, venting it slowly topside.  The constant seeping keeps temperatures a steady −35 °C, yet frost never thickens; the air itself is a desiccant, polishing every surface to a squeak beneath your boots.

5. Light & dark  
Day is paler than noon on the surface, an inside-of-an-eye brightness.  Night, however, is a revelation: the Archipelago’s glass islands trap the planet’s own fluorescence and let it leak upward, bathing the underside of space in mint aurora.  The starfield drowns; only the hard, slow glare of distant planets remains, glinting like solder dots on black substrate.

6. Native motion—the Slipstream  
Islands drift along a shared river of ion wind, tracing an equatorial ellipse once every forty-two surface hours.  Crossing between plates means judging the moment when their edges kiss—two panes murmuring together, exchanging static charges that crack and sheet lightning sideways.  Travellers thread these sparks with insulating poles, vaulting the gap before repulsion shoves the islands apart again.

7. Sound  
Where the surface world is muffled, the Archipelago resounds.  Glass sings.  Step too hard and kilometres away another walker hears the chime.  At conjunctions the kiss of plates rings like cathedral bells.  Pilgrims can plot the whole halo’s choreography by listening: each frequency a signature island, each doppler swell a forecast of its coming.

8. Survivors & settlers  
a. Husk-Divers  
They float up on chimney breaths wearing mylar umbrellas.  Purpose: quarry rare isotopes trapped in old laminate—radio-nickel blooms useful for off-world batteries.  Their suits crinkle like candy; they carry inflatable ovens that puff with raw fusion flame, fusing cut panels into ingots they ride back down as heat-shields.  

b. Lumen-Monks  
Order founded by the first astronomer who realised no telescope could see past the mint glare.  They vowed to blot the light.  Monks polish the islands’ top faces to optical perfection, turning each plate into a mirror angled to bounce fluorescence away into interstellar dark.  Progress is slow; the halo brightens faster than they can dull it.  

c. Echo-Geese  
Size of a gull, hatched from hardy relay-flower seeds blown this high.  Hollow bones filled with charmed vacuum let them glide forever on photon pressure alone.  Wing-membranes are quantum recording tape; every vibration through the glass prints fractal grooves onto their plumage.  When they die the planet’s next molt will swallow the carcasses and read the grooves: sky-borne phonographs returning home.

9. Machines that outlived their makers  
On a ruin called Plate 7-C‐Spine, travellers find a trio of prism turrets.  They once accepted raw sunlight, down-converting spectra the planet could never see, feeding it back as puzzle-colours.  Now the turrets stutter, unreliable, spilling ultraviolet snow that makes Echo-Geese hatch with extra wings, makes Lumen-Monks weep violet tears that fluoresce unreadably.  The Laminate Mind beams root-code skyward in reply, but the distance blurs its language; turrets and planet converse like deaf giants tapping on opposite canyon walls.

10. How the Archipelago feeds the world below  
Every half-year orbital drift steers a raft of islands into shadow behind the planet.  There, thermal contraction stresses them until fractures web the panes.  Shards peel, accelerate, and spear downward as shooting stars: needle-thin splinters of doped cellulose that bury noses-first into surface laminate, delivering trace boron, cosmic ice, and stories etched in Echo-Goose feathers.  The Verdure Engine “reads” the impact pattern as one might read Braille, then answers weeks later with a fresh molt, replenishing the halo with newborn glass plates.  Thus dialogue loops—shed, ascend, drift, fall, seed, shed again.

11. Hazards  
•  Photon Hail: when turrets misguide sunlight, patches of the Archipelago superheat and spit marble-sized balls of condensed glare—pure momentum wrapped in luminescence.  They strike without warning, punching cone-holes clean through two metres of laminate.  
•  Static Tides: crossing plates under conjunction may lift you bodily as surfaces charge and repel.  Keep a grapnel earthed in a crack or plan to orbit the gap until charges neutralise.  
•  Silence Shear: certain islands inherit Leaf Logic faults; step inside and the speed of sound drops to zero.  Words freeze in mid-air as turquoise beads.  Break one and it explodes into chords hours later somewhere else.

12. Rules for travellers  
—Never trust a horizon; curvature is a local suggestion.  
—If an Echo-Goose lands beside you, hum.  It will replay the nearest unsolved melody; solving it sometimes unlocks plate hinges revealing caches of prism tools.  
—Carry one cup of liquid water.  Exposed for a heartbeat it shatters the glass underfoot, opening emergency descent chutes direct to the surface—painful but survivable.  
—Do not attempt to count the plates.  The number changes with every glance, and obsession with the tally is called Mirror Fever; its cure is unknown.

13. Departure  
Leap from a plate’s rim during perigee, aim for a chimney-shadow glowing far below.  Air resistance and planet glow cradle you like slow fire; suit plating ablates neon.  Impact lamellae fold open, receiving you in elastic pockets, then close once more, carrying the memory of your heat pulse to the root libraries.  On the Archipelago, your boot prints will stay etched for centuries—until the next prism hail polishes them away.

WHO DWELLS AMONG THE SHED-SKINS  

I.  The Archipelago Itself  
Though severed, each plate retains vestigial sap-circuits.  Together they form a diffuse cortical echo of the Laminate Mind, thinking six hundred times faster in the cold.  It dreams shortened dreams: models, predictions, miniature storms of possibility it later reinscribes into the mother planet by way of needle rain.  Storm-thoughts are its only speech.  

II.  Glasswright Thrums  
Appearance: meter-long centipede coils made of ribbon leaf twisted into chords.  They live pressed flat, fused to the underside of glass.  
Function: vibrate at precise kilohertz tones, shaving the plate’s thickness into waveguides that channel moonlight.  Without Thrums, islands would go dark and spin out of stream.  Lifespan exactly one season; they then snap, curl, and catapult themselves surface-ward as meteoroids.  

III.  Lumen-Monks (Order of the Counter-Glow)  
Body: ordinary mammal stock but skin tuned gray-mint from ammonia burns.  They tattoo equations across their palms; by slapping glass and listening they solve for island mass, planning mirror tilts accordingly.  When a monk completes seven circuits of the halo he sits, folds robes into a reflector dish, and turns his eyes into the starwind.  Retinas silver.  He is said to have become a Lamp, shining fixed warnings to ships who mistake the Archipelago for safe orbit.  

IV.  Prism Turret Wraiths  
Not ghosts but standing light patterns looped inside semi-broken optics.  They take humanoid silhouettes purely by accident of interference.  Step through one and your personal electronics rewrite their firmware into languages  injuries; some pilgrims wake to find their own memories compressed into QR-like epidermal rashes.  

V.  The Orphan Sails  
Every so often a freshly shed pane mis-catches the slipstream and drifts polar.  There, out of the river, micro-impactors chew it porous until it hangs like lace.  Solar pressure then crumples it into polyhedral balloons.  Inside these husks, vacuum gardens form—frost crystals, dormant spores, latched Echo-Geese—that one day may seed moons or passing freighters with fragments of Leaf Logic far from any green glow.

Quiet kinship  
Stand on the Archipelago’s rim and look down: the world-forest gleams, mint and steady, its seamless crust impossible to imagine breaking.  Yet under your boots lies proof it breaks itself on purpose, sends pieces skyward to think in colder blood, gather alien spectra, taste cosmic salt.  And when those thoughts are written on razor rain and driven home, the planet listens, edits its own flesh, and begins again in brighter grammar.  Two places, one body: the mind that remains rooted, and the dreams it sheds to orbit—a single pulse echoing between leaf and glass, ground and halo, question and ascent.
