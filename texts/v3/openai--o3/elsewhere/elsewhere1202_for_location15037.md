THE RESIN REACHES  

A thousand vertical kilometres below the thunder-gulf, every rivulet of melt-water, sap-wash and sugar rain has the same destination: cracks that widen, faults that yawn, a labyrinth of pressure-warmed conduits that finally debouch onto a sunken plain.  
There the trickles mingle, heat, and steep together for centuries until rock gives way beneath the weight of their sweetness.  
The breach is ongoing.  It pours out as the slowest flood imaginable—a continent-wide tide of golden, half-molten resin fifteen metres deep, moving just fast enough that you can see its surface creep.  This is the Resin Reaches, an inland sea that behaves like living glass.

TERRAIN AND MOTION  
• Basin – 440 km at its widest, ringed by ledges of torn basalt and pillow lava where the plain’s floor failed.  Islands are rare: knives of older rock that poke through the resin, edges rounded by centuries of ooze.  
• Flow Lines – The whole mass drains southeast at roughly one hand-width a day, carrying with it trunks fallen from the gulf, bones, weather-kite hulls, and any creature careless about sleep.  
• Resin Surface – Looks solid: a crazed crust the colour of dark tea, veined by brighter threads.  Step on it and your weight welds you in place.  Local travellers wear stilts tipped with quick-chill silver that momentarily skins the resin beneath each stride.  
• Fumarole Belts – Where the flood crosses volcanic vents it thins to fuming whirlpools.  Gases trapped since the forest’s earliest saplings jet upward, smelling of orchids and meat gone sweet.

LIGHT  
The basin lies only three kilometres above sea level; air is thick, ochre with dust.  Sunlight breaks into amber glare reflected off the flood, bathing everything in perpetual late afternoon.  At night the resin itself luminesces where microbial mats churn: constellations swirl underfoot while the sky overhead is usually blanked by the same cloud-deck that hides the pillar crowns.

CHEMISTRY & WEATHER  
• Temperature – Sub-tropical.  Daytime highs hover near 40 °C; heat rises off the resin as syrupy thermals that seed the gulf’s hailstorms hours later.  
• Precipitation – Rain comes in two flavours.  From above: warm, laden with iron picked up during the gulf’s downdraft.  From below: terpene fog, exhaled wherever the crust fractures.  Both slick the rim trails until they resonate like drums.  
• Fires – Lightning that misses the gulf’s canopy lands here.  Resin flashes from viscous to flame without liquefying.  Burn scars march across the flood like molten skating tracks, hardening into black glass that hums when struck.

THE RESIN ITSELF  
1. Young Amber – Outer centimetres, brittle, takes fingerprints of wind.  
2. Bloodline – Middle metre, honey-red, fluid enough for convection.  Home to most life.  
3. Ghost-Core – Deeper stratum shot through with trapped air bells.  Knock on one and voices echo back; it is thought every thing that ever died in the gulf eventually whispers here.

FLORA & FAUNA  
Plant analogues here root in resin the way mangroves root in brine.  
• Prism Rushes – Hollow spears that grow in bundles.  They pump acetone to dissolve a tunnel, then anchor with hair-roots.  Sunlight through their stalks splits into rainbows that lure insects.  
• Flot-Pines – Rafts of interlocked needles riding the crust.  They photosynthesise by day, then fold into themselves at dusk, storing heat that keeps micro-gardens from stiffening.  
• Glassleeches – Slug-long worms with ridged bellies of quartz.  They rasp through young amber, straining microorganisms and leaving behind polished galleries.  Each gallery bends light into a pure tone; an experienced walker can navigate by the chord rising underfoot.  
• Riser-Kites – Cousins of the gulf’s weather-kites, but small—only wagon-sized.  They grow from seed barnacles on flot-pines, inflate with the basin’s terpene vapours, then detach to ride thermals back toward the gulf, where they fossilise and snow down as seeding grit for new pillars.  
• Mirid Wolves – Six-legged canids whose paws exude solvent.  They skate by dissolving a film, then letting it re-set behind them.  Their coats carry mirror-flakes of old glassfire; when startled they flash whole horizons’ worth of false sunsets.  
• Calciflies – White moths that flock so densely at dusk they look like airborne coral.  They drink acid exhalations from fumaroles and excrete aragonite snow that drifts until it meets sticky resin and sinks like stones through syrup.

SOUND  
Nothing breaks here; it merely changes phase.  Crackling resin, singing glassleech tunnels, the sucking cough of fumaroles, distant thunder from storms the basin’s own heat is nursing.  When the wind sits still you can hear the flood itself subducting—an octave below human hearing, felt in tooth roots.

HUMANOID PRESENCE  
1. The Skeld  
   • Morphology – Barrel-chested bipeds with double-jointed knees that reverse mid-stride, perfect for stilt work.  Skin pearl-gray, hairless, sweat thick as egg white to trap solvent fumes before they reach lungs.  
   • Culture – Nomads on thirty-day circuits from island to island.  Every island peak bears a bronze mast; from each mast hangs a spiral gong.  Dawn wind strikes the gongs, telling distant clans which crossings have crust thick enough today.  
   • Craft – Stilt-making is half metallurgy, half theology: the silver caps must be quenched in first-season resin (still liquid) while a Skeld cantor recites the flood’s genealogy back to the gulf.  
   • Trade – They climb the basalt falls that wall the basin’s north rim, meeting Tulep parties who descend on braided vine ladders.  Resin pearls—amber nodules rolled perfectly round by the crawl—are exchanged for wind-harp matting and dried moss-cakes.  To the Skeld a harp is proof the upper world still breathes; to the Tulep a pearl is a memory made touchable.

2. The Cone-Makers  
   • Identity – Ten-million strong colonies of Glassroot Engineers who split from their gulf kin long ago.  
   • Project – They mine Ghost-Core bubbles, fuse their walls into tall, fluted cones open at both ends.  Wind spirals through, cooling the resin around each cone so swiftly that it vitrifies.  Over centuries these stiffened vortices grow into transparent buttresses that herd the flood away from Skeld islands, shaping habitable corridors.  
   • Communication – Brass tuning forks carried by Skeld pilgrims; each fork’s note wakes a different memory-thread in the colonial mind lattice.  The Makers answer by flashing prismatic code along cone ribs.

3. The Amber Shepherd  
   • Phenomenon or Person? – A solitary figure sometimes visible at dusk, waist-deep where the crust thins, pushing stranded corpses—cryobats, striders, even weather-kite husks—back toward central currents.  Always faceless, arrayed in overlapping petals cut from the gulf’s canopy.  
   • Belief – The Skeld say the Shepherd is paying a debt: every creature fed by the gulf must eventually return its body so the flood stays fertile.  The Tulep disagree; to them the Shepherd is pruning souls the way Crown-Watchers prune light.  

RHYTHMS & HAZARDS  
• Noon-Lift – Heat lifts whole sheets of resin skin like continental blisters.  When they burst, syrup rainfalls sideways; anything not anchored may be carried kilometres before settling.  
• Glassfire – Lightning + terpene mist.  Ignition temperature is low; the fire skims the surface at 200 kph, fusing mirror plates behind it.  Local rule: lie flat, cover eyes—reflection will pass above you.  
• Solvent Fog – Fumarole belts exhale nightly.  Visibility drops to arm’s length, and exposed silver turns black.  Skeld tradition pairs travellers so one can scrape the other’s stilt-caps clean every hundred steps.

RULES THAT SEEM TO GOVERN  
1. Down becomes In – Anything heavy sinks eventually; depth is distance as surely as kilometres.  
2. Mirrors Remember – Resin that has glass-fired retains the reflection of whatever first looked into it.  Break such a plate and the image bleeds out as sound.  
3. Heat Buys Time – Stay above 35 °C and solvent fog rolls off you; drop below and the flood tastes you for softness.

LINKS TO THE THUNDER-GULF  
• Material – Every droplet that falls through gulf darkness gathers here.  Strider horn-shards, lantern-eel glass, Tulep bone harps—each piece drifts until the flood’s crawl welds it into the plain.  Walk an hour and you read a stratigraphy of everything cast down over millennia.  
• Biological – Riser-kites born here ride thermals back to the gulf, colonising new petals with terpenic microbiota that toughen canopy skin against hail.  What the gulf shelters, the reaches inoculate.  
• Cultural – Twice a century the Skeld climb all the way to the rim plateau, carrying mirrors cut from glassfire sheets.  They gift these to Crown-Watchers they never truly see; in return they are allowed a single night inside the Quiet Cloister to hear their own footsteps handed back as heartbeats.  No Skeld speaks of what rhythm they heard.

HOW A VISITOR MIGHT SURVIVE  
Bring no iron; it rusts to gossamer in terpene air.  
Acquire stilt-caps from a Skeld smith—only silver remembers how to forget the flood’s heat.  
Learn the blister shadows: crescents mean harmless updraft, ovals mean imminent crust-burst.  
Most of all: keep a song in your mouth.  The resin carries voices faster than feet.  If you fall in, what you shout may reach help long after you have vitrified—but shouts set the rescue in motion, and in the Reaches motion is the closest thing to mercy.

EPILOGUE IMAGE  
Sunset.  A glassfire sheet the size of a city drifts overhead, airborne on its own updraft.  For sixty heartbeats it eclipses the sun, and the whole basin beneath becomes a camera obscura: every creature, every cone, every pilgrim and predator is projected upside-down across the moving mirror.  In that moment the gulf and the reaches share the same sky, each the other’s reflection, each proving the other is still there, still alive, still climbing or crawling toward whatever melody first set this world in motion.
