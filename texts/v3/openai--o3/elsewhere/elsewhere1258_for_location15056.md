The Windward Eye

Approach from the plateau’s scrub margin and you see it first as a pale iris set in the land’s hard face: a circular parapet of weather-bloomed limestone forty paces across, standing lonely above a salt-white table of flagrock. Come nearer and the iris proves hollow, a throat straight through the stone hundreds of metres down. Warm air breathes continuously out of that throat, carrying the smell of wet clay and blind things—evidence of the drowned hall far below, though no one up here names it so. What the Rope-Setters call their sump, the folk of the Eye call their Root-Lung, and neither side agrees on where the world truly begins.

TERRAIN AND MATERIAL  
Rim: A ringwall three adult heights high, built of the same chisel-squared blocks used in the submerged hall. Centuries of sun have leached the surface to marble softness; finger the stone and you come away dusted silver. Small, lens-shaped holes pierce every second course. Those holes house the flute-worms whose music tells weather two days early.

Floor: Not a floor at all but a grid of slats—interlocking lattices of cedar braced over the well mouth. The grid bows gently with each exhalation from below, flexing like a diaphragm. Below the lattice another, finer mesh of bronze captures anything light enough to ride the column of air yet heavy enough to fall back: bat dust, eel-egg husks, spores. That drift becomes soil for tray-gardens hung along the inner rim.

Outworks: Twelve stone buttresses radiate from the ringwall like hour marks. Between buttresses stretch great reedcloth vanes stitched with gut-rope; they pivot on bronze pins, opening or closing to throttle the Eye’s breath. A vane too widely spread can tear clean away—whole nights are spent hauling replacement cloth up from river reeds three days distant.

HYDROLOGY  
Rain is rare, but the wind that rises from the shaft is saturated. Valve-Tenders set comb-racks of glass threads in the warm plume; moisture beads, runs, and drips into clay cisterns recessed in the rim. An adult can condense a skin-bag’s worth of drinking water in half a watch. Excess drizzles through the cedar lattice and returns to its source: what the Eye takes, it also gives back, salt from sweat added to the endless cycle.

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
Days are punishingly bright: sky like hammered tin, ground glare that forces eyes half shut. Heat shimmers above the ringwall but never quite reaches inside, where the updraft keeps air fifteen degrees cooler. After sunset the plateau freezes hard enough to ping the stone, yet the Eye still exhales warmth stolen from subterranean water, wreathing the parapet in an opaline fog visible for leagues—a reversed lighthouse.

SOUND  
Wind is constant but not monotonous. Through the vane-mouths it grumbles, sings, and sometimes laughs in three-voiced chords. The flute-worms add higher notes: reedy scale runs each time barometric pressure drops. When the vanes are reefed for storm the Eye falls almost silent except for the far-off keening of the Echo rising from below—answered, after a lag, by a chorus of Valve-Tender bone whistles acknowledging kin they will never meet.

FAUNA  
Nothing larger than a forearm makes permanent home here, yet the place teems.

1. Flute-worms: Segmented invertebrates inhabiting the rimwall’s drilled holes. As they rasp the limestone for calcium they open and close side-gills, producing whistling tones. Valve-Tenders tune the colony by wedging slivers of cedar into select burrows.

2. Skyscales: Skin-winged lizards the colour of new pewter. They launch from the buttress tops, sail a kilometre out, then spiral back on thermals the Eye provides. Skyscales eat the mid-air insect bloom that hatches where warm breath meets cold plateau dusk.

3. Therma-gnats: Minute flies whose larvae feed on condensate sludge in the cistern rims. Adults live a single day, rising at noon in a glittering halo, dying by dusk to dust the vanes with protein that lizards lick at night.

4. Cloud-mice: No bigger than a thumb, fur the pink of evaporated salt. They live entirely inside the ringwall cavities, subsisting on worm castings and condensed water. When frightened they vent warm vapour from cheek sacs, vanishing into their own fog.

5. Rim-kites: Migratory hawks that visit each equinox to ride the chimney’s lift in lazy spirals. Valve-Tenders harvest the talon-shed—the curved keratin is the ideal pick for dislodging marrow from cooked skyscale bones.

DYNAMICS & CYCLES  
Every six hours the drowned hall below draws a long inhalation. Up here that cycle arrives inverted: the Eye’s outbreath slackens, bronze mesh sags, and plateau wind briefly pours inward. Valve-Tenders use those minutes to sprinkle spore-flour across the grid, trusting the next exhalation to loft it skyward for trade with the distant cloud currents that seed their gardens with alien moulds.

Once a lunar month the lantern slugs of the hall descend to breed, and the Eye’s plume goes green for three nights. The light is diffuse but bright enough to summon nocturnal moths from the plateau—moths whose eggs the Valve-Tenders prize as famine food. Thus a slug’s pilgrimage feeds a hawk, which feeds a person, who tends the vane that keeps the slug’s world aired: an ecology measured not in teeth but in reciprocities of breath.

RULES & RUMOURS  
• No one closes more than seven vanes at once; to choke the Eye is to anger the Root-Lung and invite a stall in the earth’s beating heart.  
• Should a skyscale glide straight down the shaft, all work halts—some say it will return bearing a scale dulled black by sump water, a sign that the below-folk have loosed a sickness upward.  
• The flute-worms once played a chord so pure it cracked a buttress. Scholars in distant courts insist this proves the structure is an aero-seismic instrument left by a pre-lithic priesthood. The Valve-Tenders shrug: worms get restless near solstice, that’s all.

ATMOSPHERE OF THE PLACE  
Where the hall feels like an organ pipe half full of water, the Eye is a bell mouth offered to the sky. Light is blade-sharp, objects few, shadows knife-edged—but the air is thick with unseen dialogue: warm against cold, rising against falling, key against answering key. Stand on a buttress with arms spread and you can feel the whole column of world-wind coursing through bone and lung, lifting hair and thought alike.

THE INHABITANTS  
1. The Valve-Tenders (humans, long-limbed and sun-tough)

Appearance:  
• Height: tall, gibbon-armed, tendons corded from climbing vane-rigging.  
• Skin: wind-scarred ochre, streaked with pale lines where salt dried in sweat-channels.  
• Dress: sleeveless hooded jackets cut from rim-kite leather, ankle wraps of woven reed, and goggles carved of cloud-mouse bone glazed with sap-glass.

Number and dwellings:  
Nineteen adults, three adolescents, no infants this year. Each family occupies a “gill”—a swallow-nest chamber hacked into the inner ringwall, its floor slanted so any dropped tool slides harmlessly into a net. Gills glow at night from jars of comb-rack water lit by trace chemoluminescence that rose with the last slug spiral.

Work:  
a. Adjusting the twelve vanes to keep outlet pressure within a singing range; if the note dips flat, stone sweat condenses and breeds rim rot.  
b. Harvesting condensate, worm chalk, and spore-fall for food or trade.  
c. Spinning gut-rope replacements from rim-kite viscera, then greasing them with skyscale fat so plateau dust will not fray the strands.  
d. “Answering the Depths”: a thrice-daily ritual in which five Tenders assemble on the lattice and blow crab-shell whistles dropped upwind centuries ago by Rope-Setters. Each call tests echo integrity; a mis-echo warns of obstruction below.

Language and etiquette:  
Valve-Tenders speak Shore-Throat, a creak-voiced register that wastes no breath. Over distance they rely on flute-worm boards: knocking scripted patterns on the rim so resident worms mimic phrases in piping notes. Laughter is expressed not vocally but by exhaling through closed lips into the palm—any louder and the Root-Lung might miscount its own pulse.

Beliefs:  
The world is twin-chambered: Sky above, Root below. Between hangs the Eye as iris and pupil, seeing both. To keep that Eye open is to remain seen; to let it cloud is to drift into the unsighted, where stories unmake themselves. Births therefore occur on the lattice so the first thing a child senses is wind from below and sun from above passing through a single gate.

2. The Flute-Worm Consortium (Limenitia sonans)  
Valve-Tenders insist the worms vote, in vibrato lengths, on which vane needs oiling. Outsiders scoff until they see a vane pivot of its own accord on the hour a specific whistled phrase quavers from all burrows at once.

3. Skyscales (Dracilla planans)  
During mating season males inflate throat-sails that flash mottled green—the same hue as slug light far below. Some think empathy runs through the shaft like a dye: what glows in darkness teaches daylight how to color love.

4. Drift Scholars, Plateau Bandits, and Weather Monks  
Hardly a year passes without someone arriving desperate to measure the wind, steal a vane, or prophesy apocalypse from the Eye’s “open wound.” Visitors earn water by scraping carbonate crusts from the rimwall—a humiliating chore ensuring only the sincere stay longer than a sunset.

SYNERGIES & CONFLICT  
Nothing is wasted. Gnats feed lizards; lizards feed people; people maintain vanes that shelter worms; worm song warns people of stone fatigue; stone fatigue, if ignored, would shower rubble through the shaft and crush the drowned hall. Any threat to that chain is met with silent efficiency: a trespasser’s tent finds its guy-ropes quietly untied so night wind drags it off the plateau; a would-be vane thief returns from foraging to discover every path cairn rearranged, leading them out and away.

CONNECTION, TRADE, MEMORY  
The hall’s Rope-Setters and the Eye’s Valve-Tenders never meet flesh to flesh, yet the two communities traffic daily in breath, moisture, and rumour. Crab whistles, once blown over the parapet below, ride the updraft to lodge in the bronze mesh above. Reed off-cuts from vane repair sift down to carpet hall gutters, slowing erosion. Twice a generation a brave Tender lowers a clay amphora four hundred metres to the first rope-anchor and hauls up a sibling jar filled with sump water so mineral-rich it blues the lips to drink—a sacrament of shared survival.

Each side tells a story of a child who climbed or was lifted the whole terrible distance and changed both peoples forever. Neither side agrees on the child’s name, only that they returned—which is proof, perhaps, that the world’s twin lungs are, in the end, one body.

IN SHORT  
The Windward Eye is the hall’s opposite and complement: bright where the hall is gloam, gusting where it is dripping, ruled by absence of water even as it lives on the hall’s endlessly generous breath. Together they form a single, slow machine—one beats, one bleeds, each keeping the other from drowning in its own element. Stand upon the lattice at noon and feel the warm exhalation climbing past your knees. Somewhere far below, a stooped artisan is listening to the hiss of your borrowed breath as it condenses on a roof beam, becoming the next drop to fall into the sump that sings into your sky.
