The Windward Mirrorfields

They begin where the crust first opens its lips to the sky: a belt of terraces carved into the wind-planed shoulder of the caldera, five hundred vertical metres above the violet grotto that drinks their daylight.  Seen from a distance—and distance is easy here, because nothing tall will volunteer itself as wind-break—the fields look like a regiment of silver blades tilted at irregular angles, each waiting for the moment the sun’s track aligns with its edge.

There is no soil, only pumice.  A footstep makes the whole plateau grit like broken crockery.  The heat that rises each noon slides sideways here, peeled off by a wind that tastes of gypsum and old lightning; you could wear a winter coat at the summer solstice and still feel the bite.  That chill is deliberate.  The first surveyors learned early that ore remembers light best when the air remembers cold: the sharper the gradient, the deeper the memory the stone will later surrender in violet radiance below.

Layout  
The terraces are hewn in twelve ledges, each a hundred paces deep.  Retaining walls of fused ash act as mirrors in their own right; at dawn they take the sun like dull pewter, but by mid-morning their glassy skins flare white enough to dazzle gulls into the crater updrafts.  Between walls stretch panels—trapezoids of beaten aluminium, some knee-high, some three storeys tall—set on ball-and-socket mounts hacked together from old ship’s gimbals.  Everything moves, but silently: the gearing is magnetic, a bracelet of lodestones shuffling past each other under silk-graphite pads.  You can watch a whole rank of panels swivel in one slow inhalation, no clack, no scrape, only a tremor of reflected sun sliding across your boots.

What runs downslope  
Each panel is cabled to a throat-well: an obsidian chimney bored straight through the basalt and glassed smooth by explosive melt.  There are nine such throats.  Peer over a rim and you see day compressed to a coin, falling and falling until the glow shears violet against the cavern ore.  Warm air refuses the descent, but light—narrow-collimated by mirrors—obeys.  So the grotto below reads the fields as one long, steady noon even while frost halos the mouths above.

Water  
Moisture lives in two states only: crystal or cloud.  The wind drags both out of the western sea, squeezes them against the caldera, and forgets them.  Drifts of rime collect in lee pockets; every dawn the Mirror-hands crack the crust, shovel shard-snow into matte black troughs, and angle a spare mirror to melt it.  The melt runs through a maze of copper veins bedded inside pumice.  By the time it trickles into cisterns, the water is blood-warm, ready to descend in insulated flex-pipes as the grotto’s night-shift tea.

Flora  
Photosynthesis here has teeth.  Lichen glass-greens the pumice, forming crusts sharp enough to cut denim.  Scattered between mirrors grow wind-reefs: low mats of siliconised cactus that trap drifting sand and, over decades, glue it into new soil for slower invaders.  Their spines carry iridescent flanges that double as micro-mirrors, shading the succulent core while throwing spare photons sideways.  From a balloon you can read compass points in the way each reef tilts its halo of glint.

Fauna  
Birds come as mistakes.  They ride the thermal column over the crater and stall in the glare, feathers scorching before they claw altitude.  Carcasses do not last; the wind abrades them down to knuckle-bones no larger than cherry pits.  The only native is the mirror-midge, a gnat with wings of transparent mica.  Swarms drift like handfuls of ash; their larvae live on the aluminium oxide that frets panel edges.  Hands tolerate the midges—the insects keep the mirrors self-polished—but sleep with gauze over their mouths.

Inhabitants  
They call themselves Anemostats, part jest, part title.  Up here a human body is a delicate barometer whose joints forecast barometric dips hours in advance.  The Hands, when off duty, walk with arms clasped behind backs to feel pressure changes in the knuckles.

1. Skin bleaches, not burns.  Iron oxide powder is scrubbed on every fortnight: a rust-red sunscreen that stains wrists and throats until the next pumice bath scours it away.

2. Eyes are rimmed in kohl ground from pyroclastic glass.  The pigment diffracts high-angle ultraviolet, leaving irises pale, almost washed of colour—mirror to the near-black of the grotto folk below.

3. Hair is shaved except for a occipital crest braided with foil strips.  In high wind the foil whistles; pitches map to gust velocity.  A silent braid means the air is slack, a danger sign—mirror orientation without steady wind throws heat into random, glass-splitting hotspots.

4. Clothing: laminar.  Inner layer of aerogel felt traps warmth; outer layer is maille of overlapping mica plates that wink like fishscale as the body moves.  A sudden flare across the terraces could be solar reflection or merely a Hand turning their shoulder.

Castes by angle rather than temperature

A. Heliomancers  
   They write with glare.  Using semaphore mirrors no broader than a thumbnail, they flicker instructions up-terrace and down-throat.  A code burst may redirect an entire ledge inside four breaths.  Their lenses double as tinder; any Heliomancer can produce fire at midnight with one well-timed flare bounced off the inner caldera wall.

B. Load-Riggers  
   Muscle and math.  They ride panel mounts during realignment, swinging out over thirty-metre drops with relieving weights of chain to rebalance the gimbals.  Their harnesses bristle with counterweights the size of cannonballs, each stamped with the zero mark of neutral torque.

C. Throat-Keepers  
   Custodians of the nine light-wells.  They live half in darkness, chewing thermistor gum that changes flavour when mirror flux drifts out of tolerance.  A sour bite means a panel’s gone off track; a mint snap, that the grotto’s ore is satiated and you can shutter the beam for an hour.

D. Frost-Brewers  
   They shepherd water in its transient states—snow, slush, super-cooled drizzle.  Their still-tents are iridescent balloons laid horizontal: inside, vapor condenses on cold skins, is funnelled to pot-stills where trace minerals are captured for grotto lungs below.  A Brewer’s pay is measured in effervescence; the richest can pop a cork of their own carbonated melt.

Rules of the high wind

1. What blinds must be banked.  Any surface brighter than code wattage is to be kiln-blackened or sacrificed to the wind.

2. Warmth is debt; pay it downward.  

3. Never point two mirrors the same way unless you trust the one who ties your harness.

4. When the wind stalls, all Hands lie flat.  Salt your eyelids—the midge clouds rise then.

5. A cracked panel is a mercy.  The unbroken pane waits to kill.

Sound  
Wind owns the vowels; consonants are mouthed, not voiced.  Orders travel on heliograph or on kite-drums—patches of hide stretched in the lee of panels, thumped so the vibration carries through the metal skeleton of the field.  Listening against a support you can feel instructions shuttle from ledge to ledge quicker than speech.

Trade down the throats  
Light, warmth, trace magnesium from snow-silt, and stories about stars (which the grotto folk know only as second-hand myths).  In return rise coils green with moss-packing, flasks where steam has condensed sweet with orchid esters, and seeds already dreaming of darkness.  A fist-sized ingot of bruise-violet ore mounted in a frost-brewer’s kettle is the local hearthstone; even on an overcast noon it glows faintly, remembering light its cousins are busy delivering homeward.

Lifecycle  
Hands live long in lung and bone; the air is thin, but pure.  What ends them is loss of footing or sudden lens-flash.  Their dead are not buried—they are bronzed.  Bodies painted in a slurry of copper salts and left on high pedestals.  Over months, the metal skins them, foil and form matching muscle until the statue is hollow and jointed.  Wind turns the limbs.  A terrace might have twenty slow bronze weathervanes, each an ancestor pointing out gust shifts to living kin.

Religion  
The dialect word for mirror and for mercy is the same.  They tell children: “A mirror gives what it gets.”  Evening rites involve facing east, backs to the setting sun, catching its last glare on hand-mirrors and flashing it one by one down the row of throats.  The gesture is thanks to the darkness for accepting so much day.

Visitors  
You may stay if you offer:

• A mineral pigment unknown to their palettes (they paint code on panel ribs).  
• A lullaby suited to winds over forty knots.  
• Proof you can fall three metres, land rolling, and rise unbroken.

Fail these and you may still walk the terraces, but every reflective object you carry will be daubed in soot and your shadow monitored for unsafe albedo.

Connection, in the breath between  
At local noon, when the plateau crackles with light and the grotto two strata down settles into its cool, slow “night,” both peoples share a single sound: the soft click of an abacus bead.  Above, it tallies mirrors fouled by dust; below, leaves trimmed for airflow.  The number need not match—but when by chance it does, a Heliomancer leans over a throat, whistles once, and a Listener far beneath sets their tuning fork singing back through the rock.  The note vibrates mirror-pane and rootlet, gimbal and fungus stair, until the entire world between them thrums as one vast diaphragm, breathing heat into hunger, darkness into light, and back again.
