The Shouting Basin

Watercourse and emergence  
Follow the single slot beneath Nalek’s ring-ditch: a finger-wide fissure, slick with constant drip, that dives into darkness softer than any silence above. Knee-crawling men once traced it with tallow lamps and came back dazed, saying the crack widens after a thousand heartbeats, then widens again, then drops away in a black stair of limestone shelves. Forty kilometres—though distance feels braided inside those folds—the runoff of the Breathing Hollow finally debouches into a cavern so huge no torch can find the far wall. There the water gathers, pools, accelerates, and crashes in a perpetual cataract two bow-shots tall. Each drop that left Nalek’s moss arrives here together, as though reunion were a promise kept in stone.

Acoustic architecture  
The cavern roof is ragged basalt veined with quartz. Every surface angles inward like organ pipes; every ledge is a tuning fork. The roar of the cataract rebounds, splits, then recombines until sound itself seems molten. Nowhere else in the world is air this loud: thunder without storm, ocean without horizon. Ears glaze. Breath shivers in ribs. The Naleki say the Hollow speaks in exhale; the Basin answers in shout.

Light  
No ricocheted sky pours here. Illumination comes from within the stone: the quartz seams drink vibration and glow a dull amber whenever the cataract’s volume swells. At lowest flow the cavern is night. At highest, it flares like banked embers, revealing impossible mosaics: hexagonal basalt columns draped in calcite silk, terraces feathered with evaporite frost. The light brightens and dims in waves the same way a bonfire pulses when wind gusts across it—wind, in this case, made of noise.

Moisture and temperature  
Mist fills the Basin to the shoulders. It beads on lashes, condenses in beards, runs as warm sweat down bare arms. The temperature never falls below twenty degrees even while blizzards howl on the plain overhead. Geothermal vents hidden beneath the plunge pool breathe mineral heat; their exhalations lace the water with salt and lithium that tastes like old sparks on the tongue.

Sound—tyranny of resonance  
Conversation is impossible within one hundred metres of the cataract. Two people standing cheek-to-cheek can scream and hear only water. Yet further back—at the mouth-point where runoff becomes river—eddies of silence form between basalt buttresses. Here a whisper carries strangely far, threading kilometres along curvature the way a lone violin note can sail through a concert hall. The cliff-folk have mapped these “quiet corridors” and erect their dwellings exactly inside them.

Vegetation and minerals  
Nothing green lives on the resonant terraces; chlorophyll would choke in permanent darkness. Instead the rocks sprout mineral forests: aragonite antlers, gypsum petals, stalagmites glazed with hematite. Luminous fungi colonise the outermost silence corridors, fruiting in globes big as fists; break one and it weeps liquid light for minutes before going dark. Salt crusts every horizontal plane, scuffed aside daily so pathways stay navigable. Metal—iron, nickel, copper—smelts itself here: vents run so hot raw ore sweats out of cracks, drips, and solidifies in puddles like poured glass.

Built space—a scattering of thunder-pockets  
Homes are spherical shells of wrought saltbrick, their interiors lined with fungus-tex that swallows echo. Each globe floats in a larger lattice of rope bridges anchored to basalt pillars. Because the Basin quakes whenever spring melt doubles the cataract, every structure is hung, not raised; suspension allows swaying instead of shattering. Workshops cling nearer the vents, where heat forges tools from vent-iron hammered on basalt anvils. Farther yet, on the furthest acoustical lee, a single tower of pipe-organs rises: dozens of vertical stone flutes that drink the cataract’s roar and transform it into a chord audible three valleys away.

Denizens—the Basin-Kin  
They name themselves “Ara’gai,” meaning “those who ride thunder.”  

Bodies  
• Tall, tendon-thin, lungs wide as bellows: lifelong shouting against surf-noise has bred barrel chests.  
• Skin: pale marble marbled with dark veins—salt abrasion etches every wrinkle into prominence.  
• Hair: shaved entirely; even a braid would whip like a whip in the constant micro-winds of sound.  
• Ears: Thick-rimmed, calloused, pierced with copper damping rings to dull vibration. The rings jangle like distant chimes when they walk.

Dress  
• Sleeveless jerkins of fungus-tex layered over salt-cured seal hide—traded up-shaft from the Naleki.  
• Eye shields cut from quartz plates misted with lithium salt to prevent glare when the vents flare.  
• Broad copper collars resonate when struck; etiquette forbids touching another’s collar except in duel or courtship.

Language  
“Basalt Cant”—spoken entirely in pitch-shifts and heartbeat-fast glottal stops. A word can last half the time of a breath yet carry nuances of friendship, challenge, debt. In the silence corridors they add hand-signal; elsewhere their speech dissolves into percussive stomps on tuned platforms that broadcast meaning through vibration.

Livelihoods  
a) Echo-smiths—hammer vent-iron in rhythm with the cataract’s downbeat so the anvil rings cancel surrounding din; create metal so tempered no plain-world forge can match it.  
b) Salt-harvesters—chip crusts, grade by purity, trade bricks upward via the Throat Passage for moss-rope and birch.  
c) Flute-wardens—maintain the organ tower; each dusk they open or close stone valves to tune the Basin’s outgoing chord, a sonic beacon that guides ice-tribe caravans across whiteouts.  
d) Rift-divers—youths who plunge into the whirlpool, ride submerged tunnels, and surface hours later in hot springs three leagues east—an adulthood rite proving “ears of water, bones of stone.”

Birth & death  
• Birth: midwives submerge mother and infant in muffling salt-pools so first breaths taste of warm mineral hush.  
• Death: body sealed in a hanging saltbubble; inside ten days the roar scours flesh, leaving a clean skeleton. Bones are ground into powder and blown into the falls, thickening the roar by an infinitesimal note.

Governance  
Three Thrones of Pitch—Low, Middle, High—held not by lineage but by whoever can sustain the matching tone the longest while standing at the cataract lip. Challenges occur whenever the roar naturally peaks; some reigns last years, others a single afternoon.

Faith  
Sound is breath and breath is promise. The Ara’gai believe the world began as a drumbeat inside locked stone and will end when the last echo from the final fall fades to stillness. Every forging, every rite, every songful duel is an attempt to keep that echo in motion a heartbeat longer.

Currents between Hollow and Basin  
1. The Breathing Hollow drips; the Shouting Basin thunders. Every bead that leaves silence finds its voice below.  
2. Trade climbs the Throat Passage on alternating days: Naleki moss-rope for Ara’gai vent-iron, seal hide for saltbrick. Neither people sets foot in the other’s heartplace; baskets meet midpoint where the crack is just wide enough for light but too narrow for bodies.  
3. Once each decade, when snowmelt doubles the flow, the Hollow’s gutters sing—a faint hum only Naleki moss-tenders detect. Half a day later the Basin’s roar leaps an octave, quartz flares amber-white, and newborn Ara’gai are said to be blessed with “stone-bright eyes.”  
4. Legends agree that if the gutters ever fail and the Hollow floods, the cataract below will choke, the organ tower fall mute, and the great echo of the world will enter its dying cadence.

Rules of living echoed across distance  
Naleki Rule Three—mend the gutters—mirrors Ara’gai First Precept—tend the roar. Each trusts, without meeting, that the other will keep the breath moving: drip becomes rush, hush becomes shout, and the world between them continues, unbroken, forever taking in and giving out the same long, measured, dazzling lungful of water and sound.
