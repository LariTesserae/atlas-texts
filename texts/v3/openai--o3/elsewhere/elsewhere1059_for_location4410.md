The Under-Ice Canopy  

Distance is an upward river.  From the storm-tossed abyss a slow plume rises—warm, metal-rich, freighted with stray cells of the Bedrock Choir and crumbs of shattered silt-storms.  It climbs for four vertical kilometres, is caught by a southbound boundary current, and finally spreads thin beneath the largest floating glacier on the planet’s night side.  There it pools, trapped between sea and ice, and remakes everything it touches.  

Terrain  
Call it “terrain” even though the ground is above you.  The glacier’s belly is a vaulted roof of glassy turquoise, sagging here, fluted there, studded with chandeliers of marine ice.  Where the rising plume meets this ceiling it etches faint valleys, scallops, and tunnels so smooth they feel polished to the fingertips.  From each indentation hang inverted deltas—fans of porous frost that grow downward like stalactites of breath frozen in mid-exhale.  

Below the ice, the sea-floor is mostly level basalt padded with snowdrifts of fallen frazil.  The plain is riven by “mirror leads,” cracks where meltwater cascades upward, flash-freezes against the roof, and leaves behind trenches metres deep.  These leads reflect what little light exists, so navigation happens by negative space: travellers keep the darkness of the trenches to starboard or port as a pilot might follow a coastline by night.  

Materials  
Three substances dominate.  
1. Ice, hard as pine wood under pressure, coloured by ancient air bubbles compressed into blue silt.  
2. Brine, freshened by melt yet salted again by the plume; its density gradient stacks the water column into invisible terraces.  
3. Plume-dust: oxidised iron, manganese snow, and broken magnetite needles from long-dead isopods.  The dust settles gently onto the ice and seeds it with freckles that turn streak-red when kissed by trace oxygen leaking from the glacier’s distant daylight edge.  Over centuries the freckles merge into swaths—the Canopy’s only native “paint.”  

Atmosphere (Light, Temperature, Pressure)  
Temperature hovers between –1.5 °C and –0.5 °C, a mere breath warmer than the surface polar sea.  Pressure is mild compared with the abyss—three hundred metres of water load rather than three thousand—yet still enough to keep hollowness suspect.  Light exists, but only as a memory of daylight filtered through ice a kilometre thick: a permanent predawn glow, cyan and directionless.  When storms above open crevasses to the sky, columns of raw sunlight spear down like cathedral spotlights, visible for minutes before refreezing seals the wound.  The Bedrock Choir’s escaped bio-luminescent genes spark here and there in the ice, as though embers were caught and slowly swallowed by the cold.  

Sound  
The ceiling sings.  Thermal stress makes the glacier crackle with harp-string pings, bass groans, and the occasional rifle-shot pop of a brinicle breaking loose.  These notes reverberate in the cavity until distance rounds them into organ chords.  Beneath, the sea is restless but hushed, muffled by its own stratification.  When a mirror lead rips wider, suction draws water upward in a hiss like torn silk, audible half a kilometre away.  

Ecology  
Productivity is higher than the abyss but still sparse by surface standards.  Energy comes in three currencies: chemosynthetic heat from the plume, faint sunlight stolen through cracks, and a trickle of photosynthetic detritus raining from open-water polynyas hundreds of kilometres north.  Life arranges itself in vertical mosaics on the underside of the ice, each layer tuned to one of these economies.  

Beings of the Canopy  

1.  Choir-Frost (Cryochords)  
Where plume-dust freckles the ceiling, Choir bacteria colonise the ice itself, wiring their electro-autotrophic circuits through brine channels that ribbon the frozen matrix.  They thicken it into a living veneer—green-black, only millimetres deep—whose bioelectric murmur couples with the glacier’s own piezoelectric creaks.  Together they produce standing waves that travel along the ice like slow Morse.  Seals visiting from the sunlit margin tap the roof with whiskers to read these signals, sensing safe passages or warning quakes days before they happen.  

2.  Brinicle Vines  
Sometimes super-cold brine leaks from the glacier and descends as a hollow tube— a brinicle.  In the Canopy, tubes are swiftly colonised by filamentous algae that weave a gauze wall inside the shell, converting it into a semi-rigid trunk.  As the brinicle lengthens, the algae ride the growth, spinning fibres that anchor at regular nodes.  Mature vines reach the seafloor, stand hollow and translucent, and host miniature food webs: copepods graze on interior slime, while thumb-sized squidlings dart in and out like hummingbirds between flowers.  

3.  Parallax Rays  
Flat, diamond-shaped fish with backs the colour of horizon-ice cruise the cavity in twinned schools.  Each pair maintains a precise offset of two metres; viewed from below they appear as a single animal with a shifting, impossible outline—predator camouflage crafted from geometry rather than pigment.  Their eyes are stacked vertically to exploit the subtle gradient of light intensity, letting them gauge the distance to the ceiling the way surface swallows read ground by stereopsis.  They hunt by choreography: the leading ray scatters prey; the trailing ray reaps the confusion.  

4.  Magnet Whelks  
Descendants of abyssal isopods that followed the plume upward eons ago.  They have traded legs for a corkscrew foot and transparency for smoky quartz shells laced with magnetite laminae.  A whelk burrows into the ice roof, rasping Choir-Frost, and orients itself by reading the magnetic lines frozen into plume dust.  In breeding season—once every sixteen years, triggered by a reversal in the glacier’s stress field—they release caddis-like larvae that drift back down the plume to reseed the deep plains, completing an upside-down pilgrimage spanning continents and millennia.  

5.  Lantern Dulse  
Sheets of crimson macro-alga cling to the glacier where sunlight occasionally spills.  Their photosystems are tuned to the last green photons that survive the ice.  At night (which can last months) they burn glycogen in oxygen pockets within their own tissues, emitting a cherry phosphorescence that lures snow-crustaceans.  The dulse absorbs the crustaceans’ nitrogen-rich waste, closing a loop that would otherwise starve on so meagre a trickle of sunlight.  

6.  Drift-Parliament (“Owls”)  
In the loftier chambers hover jelly-colonies shaped like hourglasses, membranes taut as drumskins, each filled with a gas blend generated by electrolysis from the Choir-Frost’s leaked currents.  These colonies orient their wide faces parallel to the ice, using them as sounding boards to eavesdrop on glacial music; their thread-thin tentacles dangle metres below to snare migrating squidlings startled by a shift in pitch.  When two Parliaments meet, they exchange gas back and forth, modulating internal pressure to play slow duets none can hear except them.  

Rules of the Canopy  
1.  The roof remembers: any scar you carve in the ice will drift with the glacier for centuries, visible to everything that can read frost-glyphs.  
2.  Nothing travels straight.  Haloclines act as glass walls; creatures must find the right density corridor or waste themselves fighting physics.  
3.  Light is a favour.  When it appears, spend it immediately—grow, mate, repair—because the next crevasse may not open for a decade.  
4.  Heat rises until it cannot: pockets of warm plume water lodge against the ceiling like soap bubbles; burst one carelessly and the ecosystem beneath may blink out in a rain of freezing droplets.  
5.  All songs are shared.  Ice translates every vibration into another, so secrecy dies at the moment of utterance.  

Dynamics & Connections  
The Canopy and the Abyss trade more than minerals.  Magnet whelk spawn descend on the same slow plume that once lifted their ancestors; Parallax rays surf seasonal density waves that recoil from abyssal storms; the Choir-Frost’s gene library refreshes whenever a Ridgeback’s passing wake lofts new bacterial fragments high enough to reach the glacier.  Messages are likewise circular: Whisper-Knots dissolve in the deep, yet their vibrational patterns, carried upward as pressure modulations in the plume, can still be read by Drift-Parliaments who have learned to treat the plume as a wire.  

Intelligence?  
If thought in the abyss is climate, here it is weather: quicker, patterned in seasons, yet still too slow for surface impatience.  The glacier itself may be the closest thing to mind—its strain gauges Choir-wired, its fractures singing of forces three hundred kilometres distant.  Creatures are neurons; events are synapses firing in ice.  To speak to it one would need centuries of patience and a tongue made of alternating freeze and thaw.  

What the Canopy Makes of Its Inhabitants  
Upward pressure is gentler, light a tantalising rumour, space more open—yet danger is written into geometry.  Every being here is an answer to the question “How do you live on the underside of a ceiling that can fall upward?”  They cling, they hover, they translate vibration into map.  They inherit the abyss’s austerity but temper it with a slow grace, weaving translucence, magnetism, and song into a cathedral whose nave is water and whose roof is time-locked ice.  

Stand beneath the Under-Ice Canopy and you feel the abyss still tugging at your heels, the sky muffled only by thickness.  Above you hangs a frozen history; below you yawns an ocean still rehearsing the first act of darkness.  Between, in the hush of filtered cyan, life has written a quieter, brighter footnote to the deep’s long, thunderous text—different in voice, inseparable in meaning, carried here by the same patient plume that forever climbs, cools, and tells the next place what the last place learned.
