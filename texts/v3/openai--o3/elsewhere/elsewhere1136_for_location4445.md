The Hailstone Tors  

FIRST RISING – WHAT IT FEELS LIKE TO ARRIVE  
All the heat you carried up from Carrow Mire lifts out of your lungs at the cliff’s base, replaced by air that tastes of cracked ice and distant lightning. The climb is vertical history: steps chiselled into basalt that still sweats the old brine, then andesite that smells of struck flint, and finally the pale pumice crown of the plateau. Every ten metres the wind cools another degree until, two hours later, the sweat on your collar has salted over and the hiss of the lowland boilers has faded to a rumour. Up here the world is wide open, bone-dry, and startlingly bright—as though the same sun that only smoulders over Carrow is suddenly allowed to burn clean.  

TERRAIN AND MATERIALS  
The Tors are not hills so much as a shattered rooftop: a field of blunt columns sheared off by some antique upheaval. Gaps between the columns drop straight through to darkness, revealing glassy walls banded in colour—each stratum a different age of flood, ash, or microbial bloom now lifted sky-high. Across the surface sprawl broken lids of obsidian, light enough to clink when the wind flips them, heavy enough to skin a boot. Whenever rain does fall (three, maybe four times a year) it slams into the obsidian plates, shatters them further, and skitters away in pearl-hard beads that plunge into the crevasses before a human tongue can taste them.  

Between the plates grow the things the Torsfolk call frosthorns: hexagonal chimneys of calcite, waist-high, hollow, bell-mouthed. They form where subterranean vapour seeks the cold night and freezes its dissolved salts on the rim, layer after layer, until each horn looks carved from opal. Break one and it rings like crystal; leave it and, by dawn, it has lengthened a finger-width.  

HYDROLOGY AND WEATHER  
There is no surface water at all—only vapour, always trying to leave. Daytime humidity rarely tops five percent; skin chaps within minutes. But at sundown the hot breath from below (the same tide-breath that bathes Carrow) strikes the plunging chill; white fog mushrooms out of the fissures and diffuses across the plateau in what the locals call the Exhale. For one hour the place is under cloud again, only this time the cloud is beneath your knees, an inland sea turned upside-down.  

During the Exhale, condensate drizzles inside the frosthorns and trickles down their throats into limestone cisterns cut by ancient masons. Each cistern holds perhaps a week’s survival for one family. The Brinefolk count water in drams; the Torsfolk count it in heartbeats of fog.  

ATMOSPHERIC ELECTRICITY  
The plateau is a static engine. Dust rasping across glass, vapour colliding with pumice pores, even the friction of your coat—everything here charges the air. Nights flash with sheet lightning that never finds a cloud. Metal objects hum. Hair stands. The basalt eelpups rumoured under Carrow grunt louder under this tension; their magnetic pearls roll to the surface, clicking like dice across the plates until someone nets them with silk loops.  

BUILT ENVIRONMENT – THE STEADINGS  
A settlement is called a Steading: an irregular hexagon of standing stones laced together with copper ribbon. The ribbon is the nervous system—conducting stray charge into buried jars of brine, where it waits until released through ceramic resistors to warm hearth-plates or drive the slow armatures of glass lathes. Buildings lean inward around this circuit, their walls panels of slag wool pressed between frames of alloy scavenged from sky-fallen wrecks. Roofs? None. Except in the fortnight of Stormbirth when real rain hammers down, the Torsfolk prefer to sleep under the constellations their ancestors etched into obsidian tablets and arranged around each cot.  

Obsidian wind-organs crown the tallest pillars. As the night wind threads them they sing a layered chord—thirty-one notes, microtonal, calculated to remind the plateau that people still keep watch. Carrow’s dronelines hear that song and answer with their own sub-audible purr, so that two places converse across the rising thermals without a syllable spoken.  

INDUSTRY AND EXCHANGE  
1. Glass Founding  
   • Fuel: not coal but arc-caps—clay crucibles packed with magnetite pearls from the eelpups. Discharge a stored thunder-jar into an arc-cap and it flares white-hot for thirty silent seconds, enough to melt local quartz into flawless sheets.  
   • Product: sightglass. Transparent, feather-light, flexible when warm. Carrow’s Lamplighters buy it by the ream, cut it into lantern eggs, and trade up three drams per square hand.  

2. Frosthorn Milk  
   • When a horn is still young its inner walls weep a syrup rich in calcium formate. The Torsfolk tap it through reed needles, then bake sheets of it into brittle wafers that fizz on the tongue. During Sulphur Bloom season, Brinefolk swear these wafers are the only cure for the lung-sulk caused by yellow fog. In return they send cured skimsail membrane—an unbeatable sailcloth for kite-rigs.  

3. Gravity Couriers  
   • Nothing moves up the cliffs alive: too dry for clickers, too thin for skimsails. So the traffic is one-way and uncrewed. Parcels—salt bricks, water bladders, glass, letters—are sealed in gourd-shaped pods of pumice. Each pod rides a woven-copper rail that bows out over the abyss, aims its mouth toward a sand delta far below, and releases. Descent takes six minutes, slowed by fluting that whistles in the air. To the Brinefolk the daily airdrop is known as the Stone Rain; children race punts to the landing flats and compete to net the first pod that splashes.  

LIFE WITHOUT PLANTS  
Colour is an accident here. The living things that endure the dryness scavenge pigment from meteor grit and volcanic glass; they come out graphite-black or ignimbrite-red.  

• Rift-goats – triple-jointed, silica-boned, feeding on lichens that survive only in the electric shade of thunderjars. Their horns glow faintly blue at tips: St. Elmo’s fire drawn off the animal’s own heartbeat.  
• Candlemites – translucent moths big as thumbnails, bodies loaded with combustible wax. They nest in abandoned wind-organs; when lightning strikes they immolate, burst upward, and scatter a snowfall of incandescent ash that seeds new frosthorns.  
• Glasscat – a predator so lean its silhouette looks sketched. It hunts by vibration, waiting on a plate edge until footsteps resonate through the bedrock. Traditionally a Torsfolk hunter pounds a copper peg into the ground at camp; the cat takes that easier meal, cutting its gums on the metal, and avoids the family.  

SOCIAL ANATOMY – THE TORSFOLK  
Physiology  
• Skin pale, flaking into mica scales that reflect the sun; an adaptation that came at the cost of melanin centuries ago.  
• Breath shallow. Lung tissue laced with capillary sponges to steal what meagre moisture the air offers. Visitors from Carrow bleed at the nose their first night until their sinuses learn thirst.  

Clothing  
Stems from the same sightglass sent abroad, heat-softened and wrapped in overlapping shingles. From a distance a Torsfolk cloak looks fluid, like a waterfall that forgot to move. Knots are made with spun copper; every junction doubles as a contact for bleeding charge into ground.  

Kinship  
A household is anyone you can keep wet. Literally: cisterns are sealed by braid-seals of personal hair; to admit a newcomer you cut the braid, open the lid, and let their shadow fall on your stored fogwater.  

Governance  
No councils. The plateau is too brittle for argument. Instead disputes resolve by Resonance: both parties stand on adjacent basalt stumps while the wind-organs sound. Whoever can hold a perfect harmonic with the chord—no quiver, no drift—keeps their claim. The loser walks down the cliffs in silence and usually never returns. Some become Brinefolk; some vanish into the cracks looking for eelpup tunnels that lead, they say, clear beneath the sea.  

BELIEF – THE DRY CREED  
If Carrow’s creed is Distillation, the Tors’ is Evaporation. Nothing endures except by letting go. Children are given names that evaporate letter by letter on a slate necklace; they earn each letter back by deeds. Only elders carry full names, and when they die the necklace is ground to powder and blown off the edge into updraft.  

RULES OF THE TORS  
1. Water hoarded beyond need breeds dust-leeches that will drink it for you.  
2. Never mend shattered glass; each shard is a mirror for the next lightning.  
3. A silent organ invites the pounce. Keep the song, keep the plateau.  
4. What falls may be gathered, never chased. (Chasing leads over edges.)  
5. Thunder speaks first; people only answer.  

DYNAMICS—WHAT YOU MIGHT WITNESS  
• Stormbirth: once every three summers the plateau seeds its own thunderhead. Static charge lashes upward, condenses a sky-lake that overturns in fifteen minutes of marble-sized hail. Hailstones ricochet forever among the pillars, fracturing obsidian, carving new slots for wind-organs. Afterward the Torsfolk harvest bucketfuls of hail still fizzing with ozone, shard them into tumblers, and drink until their teeth ache. The surplus they pack in sightglass flasks and sling down the Gravity Rails—gifts to Carrow’s Midshift children who have never seen ice.  
• Blue Frost: a dry freeze that crawls over the plateau pre-dawn. Vapour skips the liquid phase, frosting anything warm—including people. The safe practice is to bury yourself waist-deep in pumice dust; the dust holds yesterday’s heat long enough for the frost to pass glimmering overhead like slow lightning.  

CONNECTION—THE TWO PLACES IN ONE BREATH  
The wind that leaves Carrow’s boilers climbs the escarpment still carrying the taste of salt and machines, and the Torsfolk breathe it grateful: it is the only air on the plateau that is not knife-sharp. In turn, the Stone Rain delivers glass light enough to float on Mire-fog, hail cold enough to calm skimsails in rut, and new chords etched on copper tape for the dronelines to hum. Somewhere in the exchange a balance holds: brine for brightness, salt for song, heat for voltage, silence for height.  

EPILOGUE – STANDING ON A SHEARED EDGE  
It is night. Far below, Carrow’s furnaces open their red mouths and you can trace the city’s archipelago by the way the haze blushes. Between those embers the marshes glitter with the mute commas of droneline wings. Above you, the wind-organs answer with a chord so pure it vibrates the fillings in your teeth. Frosthorns pulse with blue corona, and for a breath the whole plateau seems levitated on its own static. You realise the rule shared by basin and cliff: survive by transforming—heat into steam, steam into fog, fog into lightning, lightning back into the iron that built the levees. Nothing here is wasted; everything only changes tense.
