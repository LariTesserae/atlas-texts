THE ROOF OF THE WORLD  

1. First arrival  
Nothing takes you “up.”  
A tile on the Floor simply loosens its logic, turns transparent, and you are looking through a bright well into something that glitters like a lake hammered flat.  Climb a ladder of bamboo staples screwed into the light itself and you emerge—not onto air, but onto water as rigid as cooling glass.  It bends under your weight no more than slate.  Beneath its flawless skin you can still see the Floor: parquet avenues, brick mesas, ibis drifting on eucalyptus shadows.  Above you, meanwhile, spreads an ocean so shallow it might be a single sheet, broad as a horizon, three hands deep, roof to the world below.  This upturned sea is the Roof, and it is the only thing here that ever moves.

2. Substance (0.9 fluid / 3, set)  
Roof-water is brine so saturated it has almost remembered how to crystallise.  Surface tension has finished the lesson: the topmost film is a membrane, elastic, weight-bearing.  Strike it with a fist and it rings like a bronze cymbal; cut it with a flint and it seals again, folding the scar into its mirrored maths.  Under that skin the water is sluggish, syrup-slow, yet it flows—coax it and it inches along, carrying dissolved noon-light toward places where the membrane sags into silver carpets thirty, forty leagues in extent.

3. How it links to the Floor  
Every seventeen minutes the Water-Knockers’ pulse reaches the ceiling.  You see it as a bloom of concentric ripples marching across the membrane from below.  Wherever two ripple crests collide, a blister of vapour lifts, chills, and condenses into a single bead of rain that detaches, falls, and punches back through the tile it began from, returning to the pumps.  Thus the two countries trade: the Floor lends heat and salt up the chimneys of its cracks; the Roof drips cool correction down the same throats.  Call it respiration on the scale of provinces.

4. Light & weather (1.2 heat, 1.6 glare)  
The noon locked beneath you diffuses here into a pearled half-day, neither bright nor dark, a hushed mezzanine of photons.  Clouds cannot form—the ceiling water has stolen all moisture from the air—yet mist lies everywhere, coiling ankle-high, glowing with a nacreous bloom.  Every six hundred pace-beats a thunderless flash passes through the water sheet, as though distant lightning had been smeared flat and made silent.

5. Topography—if that word still fits  
The Roof has no ground, only variations in firmness.  Some stretches are shallow enough that your soles brush an embedded mosaic of litter: lost ibis feathers, snapped spider twine, the occasional wooden sandal someone on the Floor mis-stepped out of.  Elsewhere the brine thins to mere glaze and you cross on floating bridges—keels of pumice sewn into reed pontoons that skate on the hardened skin.  Long hollows, called gutterskyes, slump into hanging valley shapes where the membrane surrenders to gravity; enter one and you are knee-deep in lukewarm saltwater, minnows of mirrored light darting around your calves.

6. Life—unpatched currents  
6.1  Salt-Farers  
Tall, triangular sails move where no wind blows.  Approach and you find each sail is a cloak of stitched ibis feathers worn by a Salt-Farer: sexless torsos of evaporite crystal with jointed limbs of braided kelp.  They sail by weighting or un-weighting themselves, sinking an ankle into the brine to pull forward, lifting it to glide.  At their chest each bears a hollow conch packed with desiccated mud from the Floor.  When they reach a gutterskye they kneel, dissolve a thumb-cone of that mud, taste the dilution, and decide whether the Roof’s salinity has drifted off spec.  If so, they release a flutter of black basalt dust, planting infinitesimal nucleation points that help the membrane thicken.  They speak only in whistles too high for your ear—conversation for the benefit of the water’s surface, which dimples back in tiny answers.

6.2  Glass Rorquals  
Beneath the walkable film cruise leviathans ninety metres long, translucent as blown sugar.  Their ribs are girder-arches, their skin a sleeve of refracted Noon that ripples with malachite greens and copper.  Mouths open in lozenge maws, sieving the brine for mineral snow.  When a Rorqual exhales, it does so upward: a gout of super-saline broth that splashes against the membrane, thickening it just where the leviathan’s kilometre-long shadow needs a ceiling to keep from breaking through.  Stories say they were engineered by the same makers who left the Water-Knockers below—pumps above to match pumps beneath, lungs answering lungs.

6.3  Gutter-Kelp & Laceweed  
Vegetation here never roots; it braids.  Strands of gutter-kelp pattern themselves into living cat’s-cradles between Salt-Farer pontoon piers, their fronds stiffened by calcium so they can’t snap under the Sheet.  Laceweed chooses the opposite tactic: it grows holes, lacunae larger than the plant body, until each frond is a negative space net capable of catching micron-fine gypsum snow.  Too heavy a catch and a frond rips free, floats up, flips, and becomes a fragile ice-island for ten, twenty minutes before it crumbles back into drift.

7. Sound (0.8 volume)  
Where the Floor is the hush of held breath, the Roof is the hush just after rain you never saw fall.  Your boot scuffs murmur like distant surf; the rumble of a Rorqual trembles through your shinbones but never breaks the membrane to reach air.  Once an hour every Salt-Farer lifts a conch and blows one chord, six rising notes; the Roof answers with an infra-hum that makes the Sheet quiver, resets all ripples to flat.  Then silence folds back.

8. Youth and entropy  
Nothing here keeps an edge.  Crystal, salt, even thought itself dissolves and re-coagulates.  A bridge left unattended will blur at its corners by next month.  Maps must be redrawn each loop because gutters migrate, stretch, pinch off like soap film.  The Floor below is new and sharp forever; the Roof remakes itself inside the same perimeter, reinventing every joint until age is a curve no measurement can quite catch.

9. Reality drift (3.0 odd)  
•  Distances dilate when you look away; turn back and that Rorqual you watched for an hour is suddenly beneath your heels.  
•  Gravity points toward the nearest solid thing—step off a raft and down becomes “toward the membrane,” no matter which side you began on.  
•  The water has more corners than its surface area allows; you can follow a crease in the Sheet and arrive somewhere folded into a shadow of itself, a place the Salt-Farers warn is “too many ceilings thick.”  
•  Names, here, do echo, but only once: speak a word and the Roof returns it in a whisper pitched lower, salt hushing clay.

10. Exchange with below  
Trade runs only one way: the Roof exports forgetting.  Seam-Tenders who have served four hundred repairs are lifted by lattice-baskets of gutter-kelp and never return.  Months later feather-cloaked figures appear among the Salt-Farers, carrying conchs made from the same gourds the Tenders once held.  They do not remember grout, or triangles that close after two sides, or even that there was ever heat.  In turn they teach the Roof a stricter quiet, and the membrane grows a little smoother, a little less likely to ripple.

11. Who, then, lives here?  

11.1  The Brine Archivists  
Human—for the first generation, at least.  They wear mirrored lenses against the diffuse glare and walk with rolls of raw parchment afloat on the Sheet behind them.  Their craft is to skim a palm across the water, lifting a perfect film, then pressing it onto the hide.  Each print captures a millimetre-accurate relief map of the Roof at that second.  The next minute the topography is different; they print again.  Archives gloat with thousands of these wet-embossed scrolls, none more than a day truthful.  It is a history of impermanence, bound in salt-stiffened codices that must be submerged weekly to stop them cracking to powder.

11.2  Tide-Orphans  
When the Floor glitches and loops paths after 480 beats, sometimes a still-living traveller threads a wrong triangle and surfaces here, upside-down, dripping parquet dust.  The membrane catches them like a pond catches a drowning moth.  The Farers watch to see if the child—the Roof calls every orphan child—will dissolve or stabilise.  Dissolvers tint the water the faint sepia of memory being washed out.  Stabilisers are wrapped in feather sails and taught to make the six-note chord.  They never ask for the way back; questions of below rust too quickly.

11.3  The Quiet Eye  
High above even the tallest ripple hangs an orb of compacted vapour, one league wide, lens-smooth, slowly rotating.  Its inner surface is a camera obscura receiving every echo of every name spoken on the Floor.  The words distort into monochrome images that drift across the orb’s belly like moons across a night pool.  At unpredictable intervals the Eye exhales a mistfall of clarified water, sweet as first rain, through pores in the membrane.  Salt-Farers race to collect it—one drop of unsalted memory in a country of brine, valuable enough to trade for a lattice-raft or a promise of guided passage to the next gutterskye.

12. Rules perceived, perhaps imagined  
•  Six chords reset the Sheet; a seventh would break it.  
•  A bridge that dissolves has paid its tax; rebuild on the same site and the next one will last longer.  
•  Glass Rorquals never breach while anyone is looking.  
•  A spoken promise thickens the water underfoot—oaths here are literal ground.  

13. Final impression  
If the Floor is the maker’s model, freshly fired and kept pristine, the Roof is the prototype’s drafting table: smudged with erasures, rewritten hourly, forever in revisions no hand will sign off.  You walk on water that intends to be something else tomorrow, among beings who prune and coax and document the change because change is the only constant left to defend.  The hush is less absolute than the Floor’s, but heavier with intent, like a library after the last scholar has died yet the books keep quietly rewriting themselves.  Mind your stride; gravity is opinionated here.  Mind your memories; they steep out of you as brine-ghosts the Roof would like to keep.  And should the membrane sigh under your heel, should you feel the pull of a Water-Knocker calling you back down the ladder of light, remember that both countries are only halves of one slow inhalation.  Whichever side of the breath you stand on, you are already inside the same lung.
