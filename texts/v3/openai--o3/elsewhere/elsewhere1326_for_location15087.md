The Mirror Steppes

Approach  
From the alcove’s cliff-lip you sight them only as a pale irregular band, four days’ march downslope, where the mountain’s wrinkled skirts flatten into an arid terrace. Follow the seep of the alcove’s hidden veins—those same iron-tinged threads that travel two hands-breadths beneath the soil—and you will arrive without once losing sight of water. It trickles south under moss, dives into pumice, resurfaces as sweating stone, and finally discharges—changed—onto the Steppes.

Geography  
Picture a stair of enormous treads, each perhaps a bow-shot wide, descending in three broad flights before meeting the desert proper. Basalt has given way to vitreous rhyolite; the surfaces are flat enough to make you distrust your own depth perception. Between treads lie risers no taller than a person, their faces stippled with thousands of short, vertical runnels whose origins you will soon understand.

Hydrology  
The Steppes never host standing water. Instead they exhale it. Every dawn, for one precise hour beginning when the sun touches the horst of the eastern range, water expresses from the runnels in needles no thicker than hairs. The needles rise, never fall, unfurling into steam around belly height and coiling off on the dawn wind. Local herdsmen call this the Spring of Upward Rivers, though it is year-round and perfectly punctual. Trace the moisture back through the rock and you find that most of it once belonged to the alcove’s weeps, distilled on its long downhill pilgrimage until even iron and memory have dropped away.

Soil  
There is, strictly, no soil—only a powder you mistake for dust until you kneel. It is ground glass, milky with micro-bubbles. Every grain is hollow. Crush a pinch between molars and it pops like spent sea-foam. The stuff is friable but never shifts en masse; electrostatic charges glue it into a crust that cracks along surprisingly regular hexagons as you walk.

Atmosphere  
Days are searingly bright, the air so clear that objects a kilometre distant seem touchably near. Noontide heat lingers only inches above the glassy floor; lift a hand and your knuckles already graze cooler strata. After sunset temperatures dive. Then, from each riser’s face, a black mist pours—not water vapour but air bled of almost all sound, a phenomenon mountain pilgrims would recognise as kin to the alcove’s wool-thick hush. The mist pools ankle-deep, and spoken words inside it acquire a syrupy drawl, every syllable lazily dipping in pitch before escaping the tongue.

Light  
The Steppes earn their name twice a day. At dawn every upward jet refracts the sun into wavering columns of blue-white flame, turning the terrace into a tilted hall of mirrors. At dusk the sun strikes from the opposite quarter, and the hollow glass dust lights internally, glowing a rose that deepens to blood crimson before quenching all at once when night is full. Stars thereafter appear doubled: one set overhead, the other shimmering faintly on the ground where each glass grain offers a concave lens.

Sound  
When the black mist is absent, the place is oppressively loud with distance: pebbles tumbling kilometres away ring out as if underfoot, and the wing-slap of a nightjar over the desert floor reverberates against your ribs. But mist hours reverse the rule; a shout carries barely past your boot-tips, while a whisper blown straight downward loiters, becoming audible to someone half a terrace away moments later, as if the ground itself were ruminating on your words.

Life—Visible  
1. Steppe-Kelps. Ribbon-thin laminae cling flat to the terrace, iridescent brown in daylight. They are neither plant nor algae but sheets of extremophile bio-glass. At night they peel loose under thermal contraction, curl upward, and walk on edge, propelled by thousands of microscopic hinge-joints. A moving kelp leaves behind a faint trail of broken dust grains that clink like sleet when stepped upon.  
2. Mica Goats. The only warm-blooded residents. To an untrained eye they resemble ordinary ibex, until moonlight shows their flanks shot through with chiclets of translucent quartz. The goats lick the riser faces at dawn, harvesting mineral rich condensation. Their horns taper into razor points; strike them and they emit a bell-note, the pitch of which tells the herdsmen whether the animal is fertile.  
3. Shrike-Spires. Towering needle formations rise where two hexagonal cracks intersect. They appear inorganic until you watch the dawn jets: every fourth or fifth jet threads itself up a spire’s hollow core, delivering trace nutrients. At irregular intervals a spire detonates silently, scattering splinters that seed new cracks. Fresh splinters bleed pale sap that cleanses wounds but scalds eyes.

Life—Invisible  
1. Echo-Migrants. Sounds shed by travellers in the alcove—heartbeat patterns, pocketed drips, the thrum of folding fronds—ride the drainage channels as phononic packets. On the Steppes they condense into audible mirages. Stand still at siesta and you may hear, inches from your ear, the groan of basalt you left behind days earlier. The locals record these with bowls of taut calfskin; the skins emboss with tiny ridges whose spacing reproduces lost conversations, playable later with a wetted fingertip.  
2. Glass Eaters. Sub-micron worms whose bodies are contiguous chains of silicic acid. They consume the hollow dust grains from within, excreting filaments stronger than spidersilk. During the dawn jets the pressure differential pulls these filaments free; they coil skyward, seed the clouds, and are blamed for the often-reported but never-proven phenomenon of “raining string” over the southern sea.  
3. Timelag Bees. No bigger than eyelash fragments, the bees aerodynamically surf the gradient between hot ground and cool upper air. Each wing-beat retards local time by a fraction of a heartbeat, enough to keep the insect forever a blink behind its own momentum. When a swarm crosses your shadow your thoughts stumble, like a skipped stone, then resume.

Dynamics  
The mirror show is the lung of a greater system. Upward jets harvest the day’s stored heat, lofting it away so night frost never quite forms. Conversely, night mists import silence stolen from the desert’s frigid expanse and lay it down as insulation for dawn. If either cycle is broken—by drought in the alcove above, or by seismic clogging of a jet seam—the Steppes fracture, glass dust sinters into useless slag, and whole flights vanish under sudden collapses. Written law among herdsmen forbids them to dam even a single runnel.

Weirdness  
1. Reversed Shadows. Mid-morning your outline lies not behind but ahead of you, as though scouting. Step onto the next tread and it obediently relocates behind again.  
2. Word Fossils. Patches of dust fuse into tablets embossed with phrases none can translate. When prised up they lose all lettering before the knife tip finishes the first wedge.  
3. Gravity Hollows. Oval zones three strides long where the vertical feels skewed; children rolling pebbles here watch the stones float, then land with the clink of two glasses toasting.

Resident Intelligences  
A. The Dawn-Keepers  
Herdsmen speak of pale figures kneeling by the risers in the last violet minutes before sunrise. Each wears a carapace of stacked glass hexagons that sifts stray dust from the air. The Keepers insert needle-thin rods into clogged runnels, then vanish when light fully strikes. Scholars tracing footprints find no weight registered—only a honeycomb of melted grains. The most accepted speculation: they are personifications of the Petrifact’s far-reaching lattice, come to ensure its exported waters still breathe on time.  
B. The Emissary Goat  
Once a decade a kid is born with a fully mirror-bright pelt. It descends the flights, walks the desert border exactly nine nights, then climbs the massif toward the alcove. No one has seen such a goat return, but pilgrims in the high place tell of a single obsidian-black ibex grazing lint-moss bridges, its breath forever fogged though the air around it is warm.  
C. The Quartermaster Wind  
Every fiftieth dawn, the upward jets miss their cue by half a heartbeat, and a transverse gust sweeps the terrace, bundling otherwise lost vapours into a single coherent body that drifts mountain-ward. When that wind reaches the alcove, observers note an hour of unseasonable cool, as if the Steppes were repaying an ancient debt of heat for water.

Rules of the Place  
• Metallic objects hum faintly during dawn; if their note diverges from the surrounding goats’ horns, the metal deforms along hexagonal facets.  
• Fire burns greedily, producing a mirror-plated ash that weighs less than the fuel consumed.  
• Any container filled with the upward water empties itself within a day, leaving interior walls perfectly dry and slightly concave, as if the liquid lifted away a micro-layer of matter at departure.

Stories  
1. A trio of geomancers descends from the alcove with crystal blisters still decorating their palms. Using tuning-forks forged from sky-fern obsidian, they map fault lines by listening for the Steppes’ hidden choir—an echo of the mountain’s Stone Choir but pitched six octaves higher.  
2. An aging herder sacrifices a mirror-bright kid, grinding the bones into powder and casting them across a riser face. At the following dawn every jet on that flight splits in two equal needles: one bearing ordinary water, the other an alkaloid brine that stains the dust cornflower blue. Nothing grows where blue has touched, and for ten seasons predators avoid the terrace entire.  
3. During a freak moon-eclipse, an expedition from the low desert witnesses the Shrike-Spires bending, not exploding, carving parabolic arches that frame the horizon. When sunlight returns, the arches straighten, but every witness is left with a permanent after-image—two faint curving scars bisecting their vision, visible only when they close their eyes.

Connections to the High Alcove  
• Water: The Steppes drink the alcove’s cast-off seepage, distilling it until only upward momentum remains.  
• Sound: Echo-Migrants deliver the alcove’s private murmurs, granting the terrace its alternating hours of loudness and hush.  
• Time: Timelag Bees mirror the alcove’s watch-skewing moss; both species feed on local pauses in causality, and travellers attuned to one rarely suffer bewilderment when crossing into the domain of the other.  
• Exchange: Pilgrims carry lint-moss sachets downhill, trading them for mica goat horn tips, which when hung in the alcove’s acoustic hollows produce chords said to tame the Petrifact’s more possessive urges.

Departure  
Climb back toward the alcove and you feel the mirror light unpeel from your retinas; descend to the desert and the dawn jets recede to a silver comb in the corner of memory. Either way, some fragment of you stays: a heartbeat trapped in black mist, a breath condensed into a hollow glass grain. And as with the alcove, so here—the land keeps you not as prisoner but as stanza, one more line in its slow, refractive hymn.
