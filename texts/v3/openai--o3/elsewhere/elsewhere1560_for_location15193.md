The Ironfall Terraces  

Down-slope, where the hidden runnels of meltwater finally choose a face and burst from the mountain, the rock gives way to a scalloped apron of steps—hundreds, maybe thousands—each no wider than a wagon wheel, each poured and overpoured by the same metallic water that is forever on the verge of vanishing in the pocket above. From a distance the formation looks forged: tiers of burnished iron plate descending in tight paraboloids, every rim bristling with tooth-thin stalactites that clink like glass beads when the evening drafts rake across them.

Approach and the illusion of symmetry fractures. Every terrace lip is alive, actively building itself grain by grain. The water that re-emerges here is charged with the rust that the wall-moss exhaled, the colloidal silica shed by shattered Shardlings, and the faint static the Echo-Eaters bled out of thunder. As the flow slows on the lip of each basin, the load falls out of solution: iron first, then silica, then calcite drawn from deeper limestone seams. These settle not in layers but in chrono-spikes—minute ridges whose heights record the changing moods of the pocket’s flooding heartbeats far above. A century’s worth of tremors is etched here like seismograph tracings fossilized in red stone.

Light, that rare thread overhead in the pocket, is king on the Terraces. Noon sun bounces between wet iron and water surface until the whole amphitheater glows molten. Temperatures climb well past the mountain’s norm; a hand left hovering over a steaming lip will come away silvered with condensed vapor that tastes of blood and salt. At night the heat radiates back out, and the steps tick—thermal fractures easing open—as though some enormous insect is flexing its shell in the dark.

Plants are sparse, animals plentiful. The first colonists can photosynthesize without competing for room: ultrathin sheafs of heliophyte lichen cling to the windward faces, their pigments tuned to coppery reds and UV; they look like flakes of oxidized mica until they ripple in unison to every breeze. Where their mats overlap, capillary moisture is locked in, and there root the Swiftsprogs.

────────────────────────
1. The Swiftsprogs
────────────────────────

Imagine a frog engineered by rumor: limbs spun from keratin fibers so fine you can see the sun through them, a torso no bigger than a thumb joint, and a sac of clear skin stretched between ankle and wrist. They live wholly in the splash zone, vaulting from terrace to terrace on explosions of steam.

• Locomotion  
  Each foot ends in a porous pad loaded with exothermic salts. The instant a pad meets hot water it flashes to vapor, granting an upward kick. The frogs choreograph these mini-geysers in sequence—first left, then right, then both rear—popping across the stair-steps like pebbles skimming a pond.

• Feeding  
  More than flies, they eat the lichen’s reproductive cilia. A single bounding run can harvest a meal: every landing dusts the belly in luminous spores that slough off later into the cooler shade of under-ledges, unintentionally sowing new lichen.

• Reproduction  
  Their eggs are sealed in quartz microcaps: they lay them directly into the outflow channels where iron crust is still soft. As the lip builds outward, the capsules entomb, hatchlings breaking free already perched on fresh real estate, front line of the terrace’s perpetual march.

Every ten or fifteen bounds a Swiftsprog misses its timing, lands low, and is swept over the skirt of the apron into the wild river forming below. Riverfish there have learned the taste of pop-steamed amphibian; silver coils flash whenever the frogs mis-fire, a kinetic tax paid downstream.

────────────────────────
2. The Hematite Nursers
────────────────────────

Within the liquid of each basin hover colonies of what at first appear to be rusted dandelion clocks. Under magnification the spheres resolve into concentric cages of iron oxide needles, each needle tipped with a droplet of oily cytoplasm.

• Origin and Structure  
  The cages grow outward around a speck of magnetite—often a spore from the wall-moss. Electrical charges channeled through the speck from distant lightning scars dictate the needle architecture, giving every cage a slightly different harmonic signature.

• Function  
  They are not plants, not quite animals: they are wombs. Inside each hollow waits a single phototactic larva—the young of a riverine moth that never touches land. The iron cage is lens and cradle both, focusing the searing sunlight into a gentle, red-shifted glow ideal for soft tissue growth.

• Release  
  At dusk an enzymatic pulse widens the cage’s basal gap, and the larva—now winged, scales still gelatin—squeezes out, leaving the empty sphere to drift and slowly sink. After a month of such abandon, the terrace basins glitter with billions of miniature rust moons rolling over one another, ringing in currents stirred by falling frogs or new cascades from the pocket.

────────────────────────
3. The Rivetwings
────────────────────────

Out where the apron gentles into floodplain, the released moths mature into Rivetwings. By local standards they are giants—wingspan of a man’s outstretched arms—but paper-thin and armored only at the joints, like plates riveted onto a kite.

• Flight and Sound  
  The abrupt thermal differential rising off the terraces powers them. They tack up the gradient in spiraling columns and when the entire population lifts together at sunset, the vortex noise is uncanny: turbines again, echo of the pocket’s stone engines, a memory carried on membrane.

• Pollination Trade  
  High in the spiral they sift the air for moss spores electrostatically charged by the mountain lightning. Rivetwings comb them from the sky, carry them in abdominal pouches glazed with conductive gel, and then—once each night—descend to the cooling terraces, where they vent the load across the lichen fields. The spores do not germinate here, but their iron is stripped and metabolized by the lichen, enriching the mats that feed the Swiftsprogs. What began as nerve tissue in a shadowed crack upstream ends as chlorophyll in blistering sun, couriered by giants of gauze.

────────────────────────
Convergences and Exchanges
────────────────────────

1. Water drains from the cathedral pocket, bearing ferrous sludge, silica dust, and stray magnetite spores → precipitates build terrace lips that imprison and then free the Hematite Nursers.  
2. Moss spores and Shardling silica give the lichen its shimmer and heat tolerance; lichen in turn fuels Swiftsprogs, whose missed leaps fertilize the river, attracting fish that later ascend in storm floods to spawn in the basins, adding phosphates to the iron chemistry.  
3. Rivetwings bridge the two realms nightly, importing electric-ripe spores for minerals, exporting moth eggs that drift back toward the pocket on up-mountain winds, some catching in vine nets to become nutrient packets for Echo-Eaters during rare overflows.

────────────────────────
Atmosphere of the Terraces
────────────────────────

By midday the air tastes of a forge quenched in snowmelt: metallic yet cool in the throat. Mist rises in delicate veils, immediately shattered by thermal plumes that fire skyward, twisting into coppery ghosts. Where shadow does survive—under a particularly overhanging rim—the temperature can plunge twenty degrees in a meter, and here frost feathers coexist with steaming iron brine, each sublimating into the other in a perpetual truce.

Thunder from the pocket reaches the Terraces a full two seconds after the flash, rolling in like a hard-shod horse. The iron basins resonate, each lip vibrating in a different sub-note; together they turn the slam of stone against stone into a chord that slides down-valley, audible for kilometers. Locals—if there were any—could tell the volume of a rockfall by the new harmonics added to that chord, long before the dust plume breaches the tree line below.

────────────────────────
Ending in Motion
────────────────────────

Nothing here stays put. The apron extends outward by a finger’s breadth every week, eats scrub, topples old pines, forges new footing over what was open air. As it advances, terraces at the top splice themselves off, dry, crack, and crumble, becoming rubble sluiced back toward the mountain in the next deluge—minerals returned uphill to feed moss, Shardlings, worms. The system is a slow, breathing piston: uprising stone thunder above, outward iron flow below, both synced by water that cannot decide whether to stay or go.

Stand on a midslope ledge in the pocket and you can taste iron in the mist before you ever see the Terraces; stand on the Terraces at dusk and you will swear the thunder has a greening scent, resin and wet basalt folded into the boom. It is the same world inhaling and exhaling through two mouths, one shut almost tight against the sky, one opened wide to drink the sun—a single body flexing between seed-dark womb and furnace-bright adolescence.
