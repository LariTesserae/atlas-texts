The Gleam Wells

Distance & Kindred  
Follow the brine-filled sun tunnels upward.  Thirty fathoms above the Flood Kiln the shafts erupt into daylight on a reef-flat the builders call the Fore-Roof: a table of limestone planed smooth by tide and time until it lies level as a carpenter’s bench.  There, every opening of the tunnel flares into a wide, low-walled well whose throat drinks sea and sunshine at once.  What sluices down as heat and brilliance inside the Kiln is first broken, sifted and disciplined here.

Terrain & Layout  
• Plan:  Ninety-one circular wells are laid out on a nine-by-eleven grid, each the span of two outstretched arms across.  Cut-stone rims keep back surf and wandering sand.  
• Depth:  Only shoulder-deep; a grown person can wade the entire field at low tide.  The tunnels pinch narrow just below the waterline, then plunge out of sight toward the Kiln.  
• Partition:  Hinged copper shutters—petal-shaped, six to a ring—can be swung down to choke any throat.  When folded back they lie flush against the rim, throwing sparks of sun.  
• Walkways:  Slats of drift pine, pegged not nailed, run between the wells so feet never scour the lime-slick stone.

Materials  
Rim blocks are the same young travertine as the Kiln’s interior, veined rose with shell.  Inside each throat, hand-cut oyster shell tesserae are laid scale-over-scale, concave face inward, turning the whole shaft into a living mirror.  At midday the wells chimney raw light downward so fiercely that the air above them quivers.

Atmosphere & Water  
The water is skin-warm, fed by the same geothermal seep that tempers the Kiln far below.  It steams faintly at dawn when night-cooled air drapes over the reef.  From the shallows you smell lime, tang of copper oxide, and crushed algae—an odor like wet masonry and herbs.  Wind runs unbroken across the flat; its only obstacles are the shutter petals that ring like struck bells when a gust slams them shut.

Sound  
A two-part fugue: above, wave-surge, shutter clang, gull-cry; below, the muted organ-note of the Kiln traveling back up the shafts.  When every throat is open the two songs lock into one trembling chord that can be felt through the soles of the walkway planks.

Light  
Brilliance is neither constant nor free.  The Lumen-Keepers pace the grid with timing ropes knotted to the sun’s height.  At first glare they drop three petals of every well, shaving the light that would otherwise scald the Kiln’s film.  By late afternoon all petals retract; what light remains is amber and tame, fit for the shift-change of the Stone-Nurses below.

Rules of the Wells  
1. No throat may ever go dark; a closed well must lend its mirrors to its neighbors by aim of shutters.  
2. Shell replaces shell: every tessera is living nacre until the day it is culled; a dead plate is a blind spot and blindness is waste.  
3. Oysters are tools, not livestock.  They eat only what the Keepers feed them and grow only where set.  
4. Surface silence is forbidden at dawn and dusk; clappers and shutter-bells must keep the Current below from sleeping.

Dynamics  
Twice each tide, when surf bulges over the reef, wave-ram pressure shoves a pulse of warm water down every shaft—prime mover of the Blind Current.  During ebbs the wells breathe the other way, exhaling fine lime dust that settles like snow across the Fore-Roof.  Keepers sweep this back into the mouths: nothing of the stone must be lost to the open sea.

What Came Before & What Comes Next  
Older masons swear the first builders tried reflector barges that floated on the swell, but storms mangled them.  The Wells are the second attempt, cut directly into bedrock so no weather can carry them off.  In another ten years, when the Flood Kiln’s travertine passes the last hammer-test, these throats will be corked and abandoned to reef over.  New shafts will be sunk farther downslope to feed the next chamber—the world inching seaward by stone and light.

Who Lives in the Gleam Wells

1. Mirror Oysters  
   • Kind: A strain bred for albedo; their inner shell is near-white, laced with microscopic prisms.  
   • Labor: As they thicken their nacre they keep the wells’ light-gutters polished better than any human hand.  
   • Cycle: Harvested every third month.  Fresh plates are levered free, trimmed square, and mortared into gaps left by yesterday’s haul.  
   • Motive: Filter-feeding; lustrous shell is an unintended craft, but the Rule counts intention only in the user.

2. Lumen-Keepers (human)  
   • Dress: Salt-stiff linen, copper visor-masks that slit glare to blades.  Ankles weighted with lead wraps so a rogue wave cannot tumble them off the planks.  
   • Tools: Knot-ropes for timing, petal-poles for levering shutters, lime-brooms of bundled cordgrass.  
   • Tasks:  
        – Trim living oysters, seat shell plates, grout with goose-fat and pounded chalk.  
        – Conduct the Light: open, half-close, or fully hood each well to keep the Kiln below at working brilliance.  
        – Rouse the Current by striking rim gongs when wind dies, lest silence seep downward.  
   • Creed: “Light is a blade.  Wield it or be carved.”

3. The Spray-Scribes  
   • Nature: Salt wind traced by powdered lime, carrying chance patterns across the Fore-Roof.  
   • Phenomenon or Beast?  The Keepers treat them as augurs.  They read tomorrow’s wave-height in the filigree left by an ebbing swirl.  
   • Work: Their ‘writings’ dictate shutter choreography for the next tide.  Thus, even guesswork is pressed into craft.

4. Tern-Riggers  
   • Birds slim as thrown knives.  They nest on shutter petals, drawn by the mirror-glare that dizzies fry-fish into easy prey.  
   • Permitted Role: Scour the wells of midges and worm-flies whose shadows would stain the mirrors.  
   • Discipline: Any tern that fouls a throat is netted and flensed for oil; its own fat will fuel the Kiln’s night-bells.  Use or be used.

5. Trespassers that Fail the Rules  
   Flying-fish sometimes leap into a well and, stunned by heat, drop down the shaft—a single bright mistake that ends against Kiln stone.  Their scales add silver to the Brick-Milk Film far below.  Thus waste is folded back into purpose.

Meeting the Inhabitants  
Stand on a mid-grid plank at full sun.  Mirrors below spangle your face, shutters hiss on their hinges, and the reef trembles with the Kiln’s far organ-note.  A Keeper strides past, visor throwing your reflection back at you in shards.  He taps a petal; the well’s throat swivels from white to searing.  In that instant you glimpse straight down the tunnel—a plummet of nacre and light, ending in the green-gold roar you recognize as the Flood Kiln’s blind breathing.  Two places, one system: brightness falls, brine rises, and between them craft is hammered into stone.

The Gleam Wells blaze on, tireless, until dusk quenches them violet.  Then copper shutters fold like night-blooms, gongs still, and the reef exhales, waiting for the next tide’s pulse to drive work onward—cold star-light above, warm roaring water below, both bent to the same, singular labor.
