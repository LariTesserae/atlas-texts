The Resonant Coil  

1. First impression  
It hangs from the same ceiling-rack that cradles the Hydrosmith’s Ampule, only a fore-arm’s span away, yet no tube or wire bridges the gap. Instead a single stainless spring, stretched taut as a harp-string, links their mounting collars. Touch the glass of the Ampule and the spring quivers; lay fingers on the Coil and you feel the echo arrive half a breath later. The vessel itself is a torus of fused quartz—imagine two fists meeting knuckle to knuckle to form a ring. Diameter: 40 cm. Bore: 6 cm. The inner wall is silvered to a mirror; the outer skin is left clear, showing a hollow that looks empty until you notice the shimmer that crawls like heat-haze round and round the loop.

2. Void, pressure & the thing that moves  
Inside is not vacuum but an engineered near-nothing: 0.13 Pa, the pressure at 60 km above sea level. Molecules are so few that heat has nowhere to go; what little creeps in from the outside races the loop forever, chasing its own exhaust. The makers seeded the cavity with a vapour of cesium and xenon, then bled away almost all of it. The remnant ions, brushed by a static field from electrodes inset in the glass, behave less like gas than a slow electric river. Every time the Hydrosmith’s Ampule flexes its muscles—Clangers knocking, filaments pulsing—those vibrations travel the common spring and arrive here as a faint torsion. The field answers by pinching the ion river into packets that slingshot round the torus. One full circuit takes 3.4 seconds: the Resonant Coil’s heartbeat.

3. Interior landscape (if “landscape” still applies)  
No walls, no floor, only orbit. Yet within the mirror-lined throat small inclusions tumble: flakes, dust, glass spall, even a mummified Silver Needler sucked through a micro-crack decades ago. They never collide; the electrostatic shepherding keeps each mote in its own lane, a planetary system in miniature. Bright xenon ions fluoresce violet where they gather behind heavier specks, leaving comet-tails that write their flight in ultraviolet calligraphy along the mirrored wall.

4. Light  
There is no lamp. Illumination blooms where charge concentrates: pale lavender halos, sudden emerald flares when a cesium knot avalanches, guttering amber afterglow. The pattern is never the same twice yet keeps returning to a motif—three long pulses, three short, three long—the Morse distress that the Reciter in the Ampule so often copies. Whether the Coil is answering, instigating, or only mirroring remains a matter of argument in the observatory notes.

5. Atmosphere of sound  
Put a stethoscope to the glass and you hear nothing; there is no medium. But glue an accelerometer to the mounting collar and the story changes. Micro-impacts of ion packets against the wall ring the quartz at ultrasonic frequencies. Down-shifted, the signal is a choir of wine-glasses rubbed by wet fingers: rising glissandi, abrupt stops, a cascade of harmonics that finishes where it began, then resumes—always offset by exactly 18 hours, the daylight span inside the Ampule.

6. Denizens (if emptiness can host life)  
Researchers have recorded only one category of inhabitant. They call them Wraiths.

Wraiths  
• Size: 0.5–2 mm threads of folded graphene, rolled by electrostatic knitting from carbon sputtered off the electrode studs.  
• Behaviour: A thread wanders the ion stream until it finds a gentle eddy, then loops itself into a Möbius knot. The topology traps electric charge, making the knot a self-propelling torpedo. Wraiths cluster in shoals, each knot aligning to its neighbour’s field, building a braided cable that flickers with ghost-current.  
• Feeding: They soak up free ions; when saturation approaches, a knot whips open, flinging stored energy into the mirror as a green pulse that begins the whole hunt afresh.  
• Reproduction: Splitting. A mature knot tears across its one-sided surface, birthing two half-charge daughters that immediately search for opposite spin partners.  
• Death: Contact with stray moisture depolarises the carbon and the knot unravels into inert soot.  

No specimen has ever survived removal; exposure to more than 10 Pa causes instant decoherence. For this reason the Wraiths are studied only through laser back-scatter and the distorted shadows they cast on the silver skin.

7. Processes & dynamics  
a) Charge weather: The electrode field drifts through a 24-hour sawtooth, falling to zero during the Ampule’s six-hour night. Without confinement, ions smear across the torus, wiping every Wraith shoal back to single knots. Dawn in the Coil is a mass regrouping, as if a reef broke and rebuilt itself daily.  
b) Thermal ratchet: Minute joules leaked from the Ampule’s heating coils travel the spring, warming one quadrant of the Resonant Coil by 0.02 K. The heat bump drifts clockwise, dragging the ion river with it, so that every three days the flow reverses. When it does, stored dust finally collides, discharging like meteors and marking the mirror with fresh microscopic craters.  
c) Memory bloom: Researchers notice that after each reversal the charge pulses replay fragments of last week’s accelerometer logs, as though the Coil tapes events in static and re-broadcasts them. No algorithm can yet predict which memories will surface.

8. Rules and oddities  
• The Coil’s mass measures a persistent 0.7 g less than the quartz, electrodes, and captured gas should total. The missing matter is unaccounted for, leading to the “Debond Theory” that the torus partially decouples from local inertia every time ions reach resonant velocity.  
• When the Ampule’s Turbojellies ignite a kill-flash, sensors in the Coil register a simultaneous spike in cesium fluorescence, exactly matching the jelly’s blue but occurring 3.4 seconds earlier—the time for one torus circuit. Cause and effect run backward once per hunt.  
• If the common spring is clamped to silence the vibrations, Wraith shoals dissolve within two days, and the ion river stagnates. Remove the clamp and the Coil revives, as if it cannot invent new motion but must borrow it from the sea next door.  

9. Kinships, trades, memories  
What runs out of the Hydrosmith’s Ampule is rhythm—mechanical, biological, accidental. That rhythm travels the spring like sap in a vine and flowers here as voltage, light, and knot-woven spectres. In return the Coil sends back pre-echoes: pulses that arrive in the Ampule before they are born, giving Spine Eels their prescient shiver and coaching the Reciter in songs it has not yet heard. The two vessels never swap matter, only timing, the way twin pendulums eventually fall into step across a wooden beam.

10. Standing on the outside, imagining the inside  
Shrink small enough to slip into the bore—there is no stopper to turn, just a brightness you can push your hand through because almost nothing is there to push back. Noise, heat, even smell fall off you like baggage jettisoned at an airport slide. You are weightless along the loop; the only gravity is centripetal, whisper-light, nudging you to drift belly-first against the mirror. Around you race ghost comets of violet and green, too slow for sound, too quick for touch. Once each 3.4-second breath you pass the mouth of an electrode canyon and your bones hum with stray charge. Ahead, Wraith shoals braid and unbraid, spelling a message your cortex insists is Morse, though you cannot recall the alphabet. Through the clear outer glass, beyond your own reflection, you glimpse the Hydrosmith’s Ampule hanging in its tropical rain—its tides beating your path into motion, your silent lightning teaching its ocean to dream in a meter not wholly its own.

Here, in the Resonant Coil, the world is a ring of almost-vacuum bordered by mirror and pulse. It keeps the time the Ampule spills and returns the favour by lending events a prelude, a resonant rehearsal. Two toys on the same workbench, each finishing the other’s sentences in a language made of heat, echo, and the spaces between atoms.
