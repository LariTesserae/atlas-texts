THE LOW SEA OF MUTED GLASS  

1. How you arrive  
Follow the wind that screams through the Thirteen Throats.  It vaults the western ridges, climbs until frost ghosts its belly, then glides—spent, laminar, almost penitent—five hundred kilometres to the southwest.  There it settles into an oval depression ringed by chalk scarps.  The land here lies two kilometres lower than the Basin’s floor; the air is thick, warm, and tastes faintly of flint.  You know you have reached the Low Sea when the roar in your bones expires as though someone has closed a door on it.  The silence feels physical, like water up to the neck.

2. Shape & substance  
• Origin: an impact crater whose centre once boiled with rock-melt.  Successive rains of mica dust, blown straight from the Basin’s alcoves, cooled on that melt and fused into a pane fifty-three kilometres long and seventeen wide.  

• Surface: not mirror-smooth.  Pressure ridges quilt the glass into kilometres-long polygons, each edged by up-thrust lips of slag that resemble frozen breakers.  Between ridges the glaze is translucent smoke-grey; stare down and you see ghosts of bubbles caught mid-burst.  

• Rim: chalk cliffs soft enough to crumble under bare hands.  Their scree litter is pocked with tiny glassy spheres—petrified hail from the days when the melt still spat.  

3. Air, light, temperature  
• Atmosphere pools here, two per cent denser than the plateau above; breathing feels indulgent, almost liquid.  

• By day the glass throws back a glare so broad that clouds form a permanent parasol directly overhead.  The sun becomes a diffused coin; shadows dilute to bruises.  

• At night the pane radiates stored heat.  A skin of warm mist lifts a metre high, lit from beneath by star-shine refracted inside the glass.  From the cliffs the Low Sea looks like a basin of quietly breathing opal.

4. Sound—its absence and the reasons  
Strike flint on flint and you expect a ring.  Here you get a dull kiss.  

a. Impedance pairing  
   Rock melt and airborne silica cooled together with almost no crystalline boundary; the resulting glass matches the acoustic impedance of air uncannily well.  Vibrations enter, travel, never return.  The pane is a mouth that swallows voices whole.  

b. Boundary fog  
   The nightly mist is laden with silicate micro-filaments—sheddings of the glass itself.  Each thread is finer than cobweb, hollow, and tufted; collectively they scatter sound the way milk scatters light.  Even the crack of thunder degrades to a sigh before it crosses a single kilometre.

5. Water  
Rain is meagre.  What falls sluices down the ridges, pools in ellipses where the pane has sagged, and immediately drills microscopic holes straight through.  Beneath, a criss-cross of fracture-tunnels carries the trickle westward, reunifying it—in theory—with the same glacier melt that first descended from the Basin.  Locals call this the Undercurrent: a waterway you can never see and can only sometimes hear as a subterranean tinnitus if you lie belly-flat on the glass.

6. Living things  

6.1. In and on the glass  
• Velverids (“mirror slime”): films of protean silica-gel microbes that flex the pane minutely as they creep, eating stress fractures the way moss eats tombstones.  Their passage leaves healed streaks—opal bruise-marks that fade from violet to straw over weeks.  

• Whisper-hare diaspora: occasional wanderers, driven by population spikes up-slope, arrive emaciated.  Their vibration mapping fails here; pads find no echo.  Only a minority adapt, switching to surface-taste navigation, sampling trace salts with split tongues that evolve within a single generation.  These Glass-hares are smaller, pearl-pale, and travel in straight lines as though memorising a single remembered tremor.  

• Flake-wing moths: paper-thin insects whose wings are truncated singing flakes traded down from the Basin.  Wind would shred them aloft, so they stay ground-skimming, gliding on the pane as if it were ice, propelled by the faintest night breezes.  The wings hum sub-audibly; predators feel a tickle in their teeth and veer off in unease.

6.2. Cliff & rim  
• Chalk burrowers (“mutes”): blind reptiles that gulp air into labyrinthine throat pouches.  When threatened, they exhale through pleated valves; the hiss cancels frequencies between 200–400 Hz—the register in which the local cliff-owls hunt.  

• Joint-grass exiles: keelless, soundless cousins of the Basin variety.  Here the wind cannot drive seed; instead chalk burrower dung provides the only dispersal.  The grass grows in crest shapes, mimicking the Lamellorn skulls half-remembered in its genome.  

6.3. The Under-pools  
Beneath every sagged ellipse is a cistern reached by glass chimneys just wider than a thrown stone.  Droplets gather, merge, become perfectly still black water.  

• Lamp cores: colonial spirochetes whose waste is cold phosphorescence.  From above, their bluish disks gleam through the pane like signal lanterns—navigation beacons for the only fliers who brave the Sea at night.  

• Choir swift wintering flocks: each year, two moon-turns after they leave the Thirteen Throats, a subset of swifts rides downdrafts to the Low Sea.  They cling to slag ridges, fatten on burrower hatchlings, then sleep with wings outspread directly on the glass, bone struts resonating faintly with the Under-current.  This resonance keeps their blood from clotting during the torpor months.

7. Materials & trades  
Silence itself is the export.  The pane’s dust, once gathered and beaten into cloth, becomes Hush-felt—wrap two layers round a war drum and the skin goes dead.  Listeners-in-Passing descend each mid-winter to harvest the overnight filaments that settle on the chalk slopes.  In exchange they leave caches of lamellorn horn, whose slot-carved plates make the only panniers that do not slice the collectors’ shins on the steep goat-tracks home.

A secondary commerce is memory.  The glass holds impressions of pressure the way amber holds air: kneel, press your ear, and you may recover—randomly—the echo of the meteor’s pioneer boom, or the shuffle of the first collector dragging a sled, or yesterday’s footstep.  Pilgrims pay in mica dust for the chance to kneel and listen.  Most hear nothing.  A few stagger back white-faced, claiming to have heard their own future coughs.

8. Human presence  
They call themselves the Bottle-Keepers.  An even dozen families, rarely more, make semi-permanent camp in natural bays along the chalk rim.  Their tents are lined with Hush-felt; from the cliffs one can watch the canvas ripple in wind you cannot hear.  

Daily labour:  
• Dawn—scrape overnight filament drift into baskets.  
• Midday—descend fixed ropes to repair cracks, smearing cultured velverid paste into fissures; each worker hums a single sustained note while spreading the paste.  The microbes feed on the vibration and knit faster.  
• Evening—“pouring silence.”  Families gather with amphorae whose throats are stoppered by lambskin.  They up-end each amphora onto the pane, pull the stopper, and wait an hour.  When the jar is righted it holds nothing but night air, yet no sound escapes it ever again.  Such Jars-of-Still are traded uprange; Listeners sleep with one beside the ear to mute the after-ringing of the Basin.

Customs:  
• Children learn first to read lips, then to “read backs”—shoulder-blade twitches used for long-range signalling.  
• Marriage proposals are glass carvings: a suitor diamond-scratches the intended’s shadow silhouette onto the pane at dusk; if the cut persists unsanded by morning dew, consent is assumed.  
• All Bottle-Keepers are buried standing in chalk, facing the Sea; holes are drilled through each skull so that any sound their bones remember may drain away.

9. Phenomena  
9.1. Blue Weather  
Once every dozen years the pane sheds enough heat to flip the temperature gradient.  Fog thickens, tinged with cobalt aurora.  Sound returns—inverted.  Clap your hands and you hear the echo first, the source second.  The inversion lasts an hour; navigation during that window is lethal, for the pane’s old fractures sing like whales and lure travellers off known ridges into kilometre-deep melt-chasms.  

9.2. Glass tides  
The Under-current swells when melt is heavy in the high peaks.  Liquid pressure arches the pane upward one, sometimes two metres.  Entire ridges groan, yet from the rim you hear only the faintest bell-tone, as if someone far below were striking crystal with a fingernail.

10. Thread back to the Basin  
Every object that resonates up in the Thirteen Throats eventually journeys here either in truth or in likeness.  Mica dust, singing flakes, choir swifts, even the joint-grass genes hitchhiking inside a burrower’s gut—they all arrive exhausted and go to ground, trading motion for rest, thunder for hush.  

The Bottle-Keepers have a proverb: “The wind digs its own grave.”  
The Listeners-in-Passing reply: “And the grave dreams the wind again.”  

When spring unclenches the passes, you may watch the Bottle-Keepers lift their filled Jars-of-Still to a cliff’s lip.  One by one the Listeners receive them, pack them beside bundles of bright lamellorn horn, and start the long ascent.  All season those jars will hang inside hide cones on the Basin rim—little wells of calm around which the roar may coil but never enter.  Come autumn the empty jars are rolled back downhill, collecting on their way the last echoes of a place that has no shortage of them.  The cycle is old, unhurried, and perfect in its paradox: thunder and silence, endlessly exchanged, keeping two impossible valleys in the same living breath.
