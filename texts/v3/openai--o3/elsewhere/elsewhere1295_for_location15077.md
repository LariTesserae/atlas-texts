Stillglass Sump  

The first sound is no sound at all: a breadth of hush so wide your breathing feels like vandalism.  
Everything that thunders off the high plateau—every cataract, every turbine pulse—arrives here already spent, diffused into a low, lens-calm expanse where the water forgets how to speak.

APPROACH  
Descend the escarpment and the ground tilts from basalt to chalk, then from chalk to a glassy clay the colour of old moonlight.  For the last fifty vertical metres the trail turns slippery with condensed mist; you travel through your own exhalation.  The moment the slope eases, boots meet a surface that yields like congealed sap—neither liquid nor land, a vitreous mud sealed beneath a skin of mica-fine silt.  One more step and the plateau’s roar folds shut behind you, as if a door has been pushed into its frame.

HYDROLOGY  
Stillglass is the sump of the watershed.  Every seep, every hanging trough, every waterfall’s blown spray trickles here through miles of braided runnel and arrives chemically changed.  
• Calcium and silicates precipitate out first, knitting the upper centimetres into translucent sheets.  
• Free iron oxidises on contact with the sump’s oxygen-poor air, stippling the sheets with freckles of rust the locals call night-stars.  
The result is a floor of half-solid panes: walkable in the cold, honey-soft at noon, glazing over again after dark.  Each pane is convex, bowed upward by slow gas bubbles trapped below, so crossing the basin feels like walking the backs of monumental soap bubbles that give but do not break.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
The plateau sheds its moisture as noise; the Sump keeps it as mist.  A ceiling of gauze floats chest-high most mornings, lit from above by a milk sun and from below by the sump’s own reflections.  Light travels here in ricochets, bouncing between water-skin and cloud until shadows separate from their owners and drift like detached silhouettes across the field.

VEGETATION  
Rooting is nearly impossible in the vitreous crust, so anything that lives here must glide, raft, or anchor shallow.  
• Raft-reeds grow in closed rings like bracelets a metre across.  Their stems exude an oily cuticle that prevents adhesion; whole rings slide patiently over the surface, pushed by microscopic gradients in temperature.  
• Wisp-fern exists only as airborne tissue—fronds of frosted cellophane that never land.  Each frond carries a rachis filled with hydrogen harvested from water electrolysis, giving it the buoyancy of a moth.  The fern reproduces by touching tips with a neighbour and swapping bead-sized gemmae that fall six centimetres before being pulled back into lift.  
• On the rare hummocks of exposed chalk stand the candle-piths: hollow stalks sealed at the top by waxy menisci.  They burn themselves slowly from within, oxidising stored sugars to stay warm enough that dew never condenses on them; at night each glows dull amber, a field of inverted lanterns.

FAUNA  
Nothing here runs; all creatures leverage slowness.  
• Mirror eels: muscular ribbons sealed inside the sub-crust water.  Their scales are silver on one side, obsidian on the other; by rolling they switch from mirror to void, erasing themselves from predators above or below.  
• Quill herons: descendants of the plateau’s slate waders.  They have traded speed for poise, planting stilt-legs through breathing vents in the crust and waiting motionless for the tremor of an eel.  Their primary feathers have stiffened into writing-pen spines; after a kill they scrape the catch against those quills, scoring the calcium skin with record-marks that researchers read like tree rings.  
• Rumour-whales: perhaps the same seismic giants heard beneath the plateau.  Down here, sometimes, a circular convulsion will propagate across kilometres of crust, lifting every pane an exact finger’s breadth.  Then nothing.  The eels panic long minutes afterward, as if replying to something that has already passed.

SOUND  
Silence is the Sump’s loudest feature.  Drop a gear buckle and you can track its plinks for half a kilometre.  Travelers lace their boots in a kneeling trance; nobody dares whistle.  When plateau technicians rotate off duty they spend their first night here, letting their eardrums relearn the shape of quiet before returning to towns where people speak in indoor voices.

BUILT & TECHNOLOGY  
No turbines—motion would fracture the panes.  Instead, collection pylons stand like tuning forks driven fifty metres into the mud.  Each fork holds a braid of capillary tubes that draw mineral-rich water by osmotic creep and feed it upward into crystallisers.  The crystallisers grow dendritic sheets of mixed salts; once a fortnight, crews glide out on reed-rings, shear the sheets free, and carry them away as wafer-batteries already brimmed with charge.  The only permanent shelter is the Float-House: a cluster of pontoons tethered to embedded glass anchors, rooms gently seesawing as gas pockets shift underneath.

RULES OF THE SUMP  
1. Nothing hard moves fast; nothing soft moves slow.  
2. You do not cut pathways—every groove heals overnight, erasing maps.  
3. A thing submerged is a thing bequeathed; retrieval rips the crust and can drain hectares.  Drop it, and it joins the archive below.

MATERIALS & TEXTURE  
Kneel and press a palm to the surface: cool, yes, but also elastic, as if the basin pretends to be ice while remembering it is water.  Friction here is fickle; a boot tread bites at dawn, skates at noon, clings like glue by dusk.  Anything iron drawn across the panes leaves a comet tail of orange oxide that gradually diffuses outward until the mark is a faint rust halo the diameter of a man.

SCENE—MID-AFTERNOON TO NIGHTFALL  
14:00  Sun climbs the mist ceiling, driving it upward.  Vision clears; quill herons appear as tall punctuation marks, and the mirror eels write cursive ripples beneath them.  
16:00  The crust softens.  Crews postpone travel; only raft-reeds and wisp-ferns are abroad, crossing one another like slow ballet.  
18:00  Temperature falls.  The panes tighten, emitting a range of delicate pings—the sump’s evening bells—while candle-piths kindle, lighting the field with ember auras.  
20:00  Stars duplicate themselves in the water skin, except where night-stars of iron murk the reflection.  Somewhere underfoot, a single deep chord rolls west to east: echo-whale or settling stratum, nobody can say.  Silence folds back in.

LONG STORY, OR THE SUMP’S PROJECT  
Over centuries the Sump thickens upward as the plateau slowly erodes down.  Every tonne of mineral the waterfalls tear loose becomes another transparent petal added to the basin’s blossom.  In perhaps eight millennia the crust will rise high enough to touch the mist ceiling permanently, forming a vaulted shell.  Then the plateau’s rivers will pour not onto sheets but into aqueduct throats angled inward, feeding an enclosed sea lit only by candle-pith glow and whatever bioluminescence the mirror eels evolve.  The technicians’ oscillating forks will fossilise into stalagmitic harps, still faintly chiming whenever the buried giants turn in their sleep.

TEN PARTICULATE NOTES  

1. Plateau thunder arrives here as tremor alone, a vibration you feel in knee cartilage but never hear.  
2. Servo-moss drones exiled for malfunction float downriver as dormant pellets; in the Sump they wake, paddle in circles, and knit rust freckles into orderly constellations before their charges expire.  
3. The dendritic salt wafers, once discharged in distant cities, carry ghost-impressions of the sump’s silence; break one and you can smell cold glass.  
4. Wisp-fern gemmae sometimes drift as far uphill as the plateau, where they crash in sunlight like shards of spun sugar, leaving iridescent grit the rotator crews sweep from gratings.  
5. Quill heron pinfeathers, ground to powder, serve as anti-fog compound on technicians’ goggles.  
6. Mirror eel skin tanned and stretched makes membranes for airship ballast bladders: impermeable, weightless as thought.  
7. The night-stars of rust record wind history; scrape a core and climatologists can read centuries of plateau storm in concentric aerolite rings.  
8. Plateau pillar-trees tilt fractionally toward the Sump during their decadal gesture, as if bowing to a reflection that lies kilometres beyond their sight line.  
9. Candle-pith ash, once cooled, is indistinguishable from the plateau’s youngest basalt, confusing petrographers who chase provenance.  
10. Every pane of Stillglass holds a thin smear of dissolved language from the shouting technicians above; under spectrography the crust fluoresces in Morse spangles that repeat the same phrase: SOMEWHERE WATER RESTS.

CONNECTION, WITHOUT NEEDING TO NAME IT  
The plateau spends itself in water, in noise, in perpetual falling; the Sump answers by holding, by muting, by turning fall into float.  The two are a single, slow inhalation and exhalation of stone.  Stand on the high moss and you hear the world begin; stand on the glass below and you hear it think.
