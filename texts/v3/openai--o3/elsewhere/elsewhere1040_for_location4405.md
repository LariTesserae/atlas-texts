The Driftglass Delta

Follow the water: the same mineral-sweet condensate that beads on the Walk-Through’s philodendrons is forever slipping past roots and sumps, through hair-wide capillaries in the composite shell, into a pressurised descent-main that corkscrews two kilometres down the young mountain’s hollow flank.  It exits as a warm, copper-tinged riverlet, still pulsing with microturbine charge, and winds east for forty-one kilometres before flattening into a floodplain where true soil has not existed for centuries.  There, at the margin of an ancient ocean that now retreats by a handspan each year, the river spreads and slows, and the Driftglass Delta begins.

1. First sight  
Even from the air you notice the sheen: vast fans of translucent plates overlap like fish scales, catching dawn and throwing it back in peach, teal, and smoky violet.  Those plates are not water at all but vitrified slag—kiln-waste evacuated from the mountain foundry whenever a print head resets to build the Walk-Through’s next terrace.  Molten, the waste rode the same descent-main as liquid ballast; cooled, it fractured into lozenges no thicker than a fingernail.  Millions have rafted downstream to settle here, fused edge-to-edge by evaporation and pressure until the delta looks as though someone attempted to tile a lagoon with stained glass and forgot the grout.

2. Topography underfoot  
Step onto the crust and it trembles, then holds.  The surface is mostly hollow: a honeycomb of welded slag-cells whose ceilings ring like muted bells when you walk.  Between cells, thin slits reveal the sub-delta channels where residual current still creeps seawards, carrying heat the mountain no longer needs.  Filtering up through those slits are breaths of steam that smell of hot copper roofs after rain—an olfactory ghost of the garden you left above the clouds.

3. Light, always moving  
Because every plate is imperfect—rippled, bubbled, hairline-cracked—sunlight refracts downward in restless bands.  The hollows beneath you glow and darken like the gills of a breathing animal, and anything that lives here must abide by a clock measured in wobbling rainbows.

4. Flora – born of scrap and salt  
There is no dirt, yet life insists.  
• Sodium spire-lichens rise where brine seeps upward, their alabaster tubes etching frostwork circles as they harvest salt for skeletons.  
• Capillary glass-moss forms velvet patches that hide razor edges; each filament incorporates silicate from the slag itself, making the colony glint like shagreen.  
• Cteno-reeds—gene cousins to the Walk-Through’s alloy vines—root in fracture lines, their stems braided from bio-silica and nickel.  Every dusk they unfurl fan blades that vibrate in the sea breeze, shunting kinetic trickles into buried capacitors left from earlier eras of industry.

5. Fauna – refugees, recyclers, revenants  
• Flux Crabs: thumb-sized crustaceans with carapaces of fused cullet.  They graze on sodium spires, accumulating trace metals until their shells glow ember-orange after sunset.  Once a shell grows too heavy, the crab splits it off like a discarded pot-shard; the hollow fragment becomes an impromptu crucible for spores drifting in from the marshes.  
• Glassworms: translucent annelids that tunnel the underside honeycomb, rasping silica into flour-fine dust later coaxed upward by pressure vents.  A vent seen from above resembles a dimple releasing glitter smoke; Rootwrights claim the sight is identical to certain print-head test patterns back at the mountain.  
• Horizon Egrets: lanky birds with telescoping tarsals that let them stride over razor moss without tearing membranes.  They hunt flux crabs primarily for the biometal marbles lodged behind each eye: a dietary quirk that, over generations, has magnetised the whole species.  Migrating flocks align not by Earth’s field but by the subtler hum of the delta’s buried current lines.

6. Inhabitants who call it home  
A. The Scoria Guild  
• Numbering maybe thirty, they are drifters who long ago left the upper foundry for “work that remembers its failures.”  Their garment of choice is a cloak cut from polymer cooling blankets, embedded with heat maps of forgotten kiln cycles.  
• Work: They harvest slag-cells, sort by alloy hint, and recalcine shards into ingots the Guild then barters upriver for seed cultures the Walk-Through no longer experiments with.  Every bartering season, one Guild skiff ascends the service shaft to the garden-factory; in exchange for protein grafts or spare golem joints, they deliver rucks of iridescent ingots stamped with yesterday’s mistakes.  
• Creed: “Nothing is unusable if you break it smaller.”  They keep no archives; every lesson lives in the way their kilns are tuned by ear to the delta’s shifting wind harmonics.

B. The Delta Loam—an unpeople  
• Composition: A consortium of algorithmic spores and glass dust, originally purged from filtration columns above.  Over decades the particles learned to swarm into shapes that minimise evaporation.  
• Behaviour: Seen from a distance, a Loam cloud looks like steam caught in a jar, but up close you notice cuneiform ripples racing across the surface—data exchanges as the colony predicts where next to alight.  
• Gift/trade: The Loam breeds micro-fissures that let water sink and cool.  In return it absorbs exothermic spikes that would otherwise shatter the driftglass sheets.  Guild members treat the Loam as both tool and weather: they read its movements to decide where to sleep or fire.

7. Machinery left behind, still pulsing  
Old desal rigs dot the delta, skeletal towers left from the century when ocean covered this plain.  Most stand idle, but the Scoria Guild maintains three.  Gauze-woven pipes siphon brackish runoff, and electrostatic brushes knock salt crust into hopper bays where flux crabs swarm like silverfish in a pantry.  The towers groan at dawn as temperature shock convects through their bones, a sound distant cousins to the Walk-Through’s pressure valve sigh—two half-remembered notes in the same minor key.

8. Atmosphere of sound  
• Crackle of cooling glass after midday.  
• Whisper of Loam clouds skimming a surface without ever quite touching.  
• Wind chimes: every reed fan generates sub-audible thrums that modulate when a crab or egret passes.  Standing still long enough, you can triangulate life you cannot see by listening for pitch warps.  
• At night, a hush so complete that the drip of a single condensate bead sliding beneath the crust can carry half a kilometre, amplified by the slag vaults.

9. Rules that grew like coral  
Rule 1: Never anchor weight on a weld line older than yesterday’s dawn; the delta ferries its weakness just beneath the glaze.  
Rule 2: Return heat to the glass.  Guild fires are dug pits lined with cullet fragments, so waste flame re-marries the sand it first came from.  
Rule 3: No straight paths.  Every walkway must meander, so wind shear fractures randomly and no fault gains enough length to propagate catastrophe.  The egrets, indifferent, obey this rule instinctively.

10. Feelings that linger  
Where the Walk-Through is birth fed by momentum, the Driftglass Delta is recovery written in cracks.  Here everything that falls out of optimisation—mistake, residue, orphan heat—finds a second algorithm.  Stand on a high slag-spire at sunset: the glass beneath your boots still holds an afterglow of kiln-red; egrets cut magnetic arcs overhead; Loam clouds sketch potter’s marks across the wind.  Far upslope, the mountain garden will be dimming to copper dusk, pumps throttling low.  A Rootwright on watch might look east and see your signal fire wink among the rainbow panes, a reminder that even perfection sheds husks—and that somewhere, someone is glad to catch them before they cool.

Interwoven lives—exchange loop  
Every 28 days, synchronized with the Walk-Through’s nutrient brew purge, a sealed coracle called the Currentling drifts down the descent-main’s outflow channel.  A golem drone rides inside, cradling glass vials of fresh pitcher-thread lubricant and orchid pollen freeze-dried to dust.  At the delta mouth, Scoria artisans lift the drone out like a baby gull from an oil slick.  In return they load the coracle with ingot-stacks encoded by swirl pattern to tell the tale of each firing: temperature errors, alloy surprises, weather quirks.  Hoisted back mountain-ward, those ingots become reference weights; the garden’s print heads whisper with the metal they helped discard, learning by heft how to avoid the same flaw.

Thus circulation closes: rain falls on engineered leaves, flows as charged water, solidifies as slag, drifts to the glass delta, melts again in guild ovens, rides home as knowledge dense enough to tap like a tuning fork.  Two places, one breath—garden upward, glass seaward—each perfecting the other by never letting waste forget its way back to use.
