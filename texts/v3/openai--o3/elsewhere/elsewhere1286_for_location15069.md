The Salt-Choir Basin

First sight  

You begin to descend the far side of the same mountain chain that carries the plateau-city on its shoulder. One day’s walk below the crop of cloud and cedar-cold, the trees end in a ragged treeline and the ground simply falls away. It is as if someone scooped a shallow bowl five leagues wide out of the mountains, then lined the hollow with mirrors. Noon sunlight strikes the concave floor, ricochets, and blinds you into seeing double. When your eyes adjust the glare resolves into tessellated plates of salt and glass, each the size of a garden pond, fitted edge-to-edge like cracked ice that will never thaw.

A travelling breath, back up the slope, will one day exhale again inside the high living city; the same air mass that fogs your collar there is baked white here. The two places sip from the same winds, share the same meltwater—only hours and altitude apart.

Light  

The sun is naked here, uninverted, a coin of white gold. With no canopy and no permanent cloud the Basin stays brighter than the plateau ever manages even at summer zenith. A second, counterfeit sun migrates across the floor every day, cast by the mirror-salt; by late afternoon the double shadows make it hard to know which star is cause and which is echo.

Terrain & material  

1. Crust: interlocking hexagons of halite-silica composite, three finger-breadths thick. The edges rise slightly, so you walk the ridges of honey-cells.  
2. Throat-pots: vertical chimneys where crust has collapsed into brine. Salt precipitates along the throats in concentric collars, pink where carotene algae bloom.  
3. Dunes: not of sand but of ground glass—green, violet, obsidian—winnowed out of the caldera walls by katabatic gusts. These dunes migrate clockwise one metre per week, carving scallops in the crust that heal behind them with nightly recrystallisation.

Water  

Liquid exists only as brine locked beneath the plates. Capillary cracks suck it upward after dusk, and before dawn an invisible jungle of needle crystals has grown on every surface, silently flowering into beard-like hoarfrost. The instant sun touches the frost it sublimes; each sunrise is accompanied by a hiss like poured grain as millions of vapour trails leap upward and vanish.

Architecture  

Nothing here was raised by hand.

• Pillars (“Choir-stalks”): fluted columns of halite twenty to thirty metres tall, bored clean through the crust by ascending gas decades ago. Each stalk tapers to a resonant bell-mouth.  
• Bridges: where two stalks leaned and fused they formed high catwalks of translucent salt, ribbed like fishbone.  
• Burrows: beneath the sun-pierced floor, winding cavities dissolve through softer strata. Some are wide enough for caravans; others would drown a vole. The roof is the same crust you walk—thin, treacherous, able to hatch under a misplaced heel.

Atmosphere & sound  

The Basin sings. Wind funnels down from the plateau above and threads the hollow bells of the Choir-stalks, setting up aeolian chords a full octave lower than human speech. Every hour the key changes as shadow length alters internal air temperature. No two days voice the same hymn. At noon the sound is high and keening; at dusk it drops to a cathedral hum that can vibrate dust off your bootsoles.

Temperature  

Summer midday: 48 °C in the open shade of nothing. Winter night: –25 °C, clear enough to see the Milky Way refracted twice, once on the sky and once on the salt. The daily swing is savage, but the absence of moisture means metal barely corrodes, and bones left on the surface mummify instead of rotting.

Ecology  

Producers  
• Brine-glass algae: photosynthesises through layers of dissolved silica; colour-shifts from jade to carmine to control glare.  
• Mirage lichen: a microfilm on every crust facet; its oily surface doubles albedo, reflecting 98 % of incoming light and keeping the basin from boiling itself.  

Consumers  
• Prism-mites: transparent arachnids that sieve algae from evaporating brine films.  
• Scarab kelters: fist-sized beetles with quartz elytra; they ingest crust-dust, harvest trace nitrates, and excrete pellets the consistency of chalk—fertiliser for the next algal bloom.  

Scavengers  
Nothing decays; instead, everything desiccates. Dried remains are gnawed by wind until they become fluted fossils indistinguishable from the Choir-stalks.  

Dynamics  

1. Noon melt: Direct light melts the upper microns of every salt surface. Capillary action sucks the liquid downward, concentrating impurities along the plate edges.  
2. Twilight freeze: Infrared loss crystalises that brine back outward, pushing the day’s dissolved load into fragile overhangs. When the night wind arrives, the overhangs snap and skitter across the basin as razor flakes, scouring footprints, resetting the slate for dawn.

Rules of the Basin  

• Still water is a lie. Step on any plate that looks darker than its neighbours and you will punch through into brine.  
• Sound that finds harmony with a Choir-stalk grows louder than its source. A whisper at the correct pitch can carry five kilometres; a shout at the wrong pitch can rupture your own eardrum.  
• Flame is possible—perfect, in fact—but combustion wastes moisture; the locals regard fire as a theft from tomorrow’s air.  
• Metal forgotten on the crust will solder to the salt by noon and remain welded until moonrise’s chill unpins it.

What it feels like to stay  

There is only one horizon and it is concave; every direction slopes upward, tricking the body into a sense of being forever at the bottom of something. The light is so absolute that shadows knife out in crisp black, and after an hour aboveground you crave darkness the way a drowned man craves air. At night the opposite happens: the sky detonates into undiluted stars, reflected point-for-point on the crust until you cannot decide which way is celestial. You fall asleep, if you manage it, to a sub-audible chord vibrating the marrow of your teeth.

Who (and what) lives here

1. The Salt-Choir Itself  

The pillars are not inert. Each is a composite of mineral and halophilic biofilm that conducts piezoelectric currents whenever wind flexes the column. Arrays of stalks exchange low-frequency pulses, mapping stress lines through the crust and adjusting internal brine distribution to buttress thinning areas. Age: 3 400 freeze–thaw cycles, measured by concentric growth scarves visible in cross-section. Mind: distributed resonance. A single pillar holds a vocabulary of only a few tones, but a choir of two hundred can sequence complex waveforms—slow mathematics in air pressure. The Choir’s overriding imperatives:  
• Maintain mirror-level albedo to prevent thermal runaway.  
• Vent excess subsurface gas through sacrificial throat-pots rather than allow explosive uplift.  
• Record migratory weather patterns by adjusting chord progressions; each night’s song is a ledger of atmospheric pressure for the last century.

2. The Resonant Guild (called Glass-Throats by plateau Keepers)

Anatomy  
• Human stock diluted by centuries of brine exposure: elongated vocal tracts, tympanic membranes thickened into resonant pads, skin dusted with reflective mica that sparks under starlight.  
• Internal water reserves stored in a double bladder lined with wax crystals. Average body temperature sits near 35 °C, but daily dehydration can drop it ten degrees before sunset rewarming.

Origin & number  
They are descendants of mountain traders who, generations ago, followed the plateau’s melt-rills downslope and found a living to be eked from salt. Now roughly one hundred and twenty souls, divided among three semi-nomadic caravans.

Perception & language  
Hearing is paramount. The Guild’s cochleae are elongated, granting pitch discrimination fine enough to tell two Choir stalks apart by voice alone. Speech is chordal: they layer two notes from throat and one whistled overtone between teeth, then modulate amplitude by cupping hands against the crust. Outsiders hear an eerie triad; another Glass-Throat hears grammar. Written record uses oscillograms etched into obsidian plates.

Nutrition  
Daily ration: a thumb-length slice of algae-brick (protein), a cup of brine diluted with plateau snow (electrolytes), a sliver of raw quartz (to resupply silica lost through skin). Meat is almost unknown. When a Scarab kelter dies naturally the carcass is ground into powder and stretched ten-to-one with salt to become ceremonial dye.

Work  
• Plate-seaming: dripping supersaturated brine into incipient cracks to weld them shut before a whole honey-cell founders.  
• Bell-tuning: chipping or adding crust-lip to Choir mouths so that tonight’s weather will be scored accurately.  
• Trade: once every three years, at the equinox when katabatic winds reverse, a caravan climbs goat-paths to the plateau-city carrying quartz needles and vacuum-dry salt-cakes rich in rare trace elements. In exchange they receive bundles of living root-primers—soil bacteria and antifreeze proteins the Basin cannot synthesise alone.  
• Night relay: perched at intervals on the stalk-bridges, Guild members sing real-time glosses of the Choir’s chord changes, translating mineral language into human forecast.

Art & ritual  
The only pigment that survives the Basin’s glare is generated during “Red Hush.” On the hottest day of summer the caravans quarry hematite from exposed ribs of the caldera wall, crush it, and mix it with melted brine. They stencil momentary murals on the salt crust—stories of storm, eclipse, and visitor. By nightfall the iron has oxidised to pale dust and the gallery is gone, absorbed. Ephemerality is proof of respect: art that lingers would become artifact, and artifact would shatter under the next thermal tide.

Life span & death  
High reflectance slows skin cancer, but kidneys fail early from salt load. Forty Basin-winters is elderhood. At the end, a Glass-Throat chooses a stalk whose voice matches the timbre of their own. They climb the fluted exterior at sunset, tether themselves inside the bell-mouth, and sing a final sustain until voice ruptures and breath ends. The body mummifies in hours. Over decades the Choir plates it in clear halite, turning singer into tuning weight, memory into acoustics.

3. Minor co-inhabitants  

a. Whisper-skinks  
Palm-long reptiles translucent as onion skin. They bask on hot crust until internal water nearly evaporates, then dive into brine throats to re-flood tissue. The Guild watches them as living thermometers: when skinks stay submerged all day, a sandstorm is brewing.  

b. Glass-dust devils  
Not beasts but phenomena. Afternoon vortices lift ground glass into spinning columns fifty metres high. Static charge bonds shards into fragile helices that persist minutes after the wind dies, tall as the plateau’s spire trees, before collapsing in a tinkling rain.

c. The Sub-Basin Dark  
A fungal mat kilometres across, hidden under the thickest plates. Fed by nutrient wash from plateau run-off, it digests trapped organic debris and exhales methane, which the Choir vents in soft overnight belches. The Dark never surfaces; its presence is known only by fleeting rainbows in gas plumes and by the way Choir chords dip whenever its pressure swells.

How it speaks to the plateau-city  

Every katabatic wind that slams shut the City’s fronds continues downslope to awaken the Basin’s Choir. The super-plant hears its own weather, reflected and pitch-shifted, hours after generation—a delayed heartbeat echoing back through stone. In return, silica spores from Fungus Combs loft on thermals, drift over the cliff rim, and seed the Basin’s algal plates with trace enzymes that harden them against heat. One ecology supplies antifreeze; the other sends mirror-dust. Neither “knows” the other as animals reckon knowing, yet each has grown to expect the other’s breath in its own lungs.

Standing at dusk on a Choir-bridge you can catch both halves of the world at once: up-valley, the plateau-city folding for night, phosphor sacs kindling like submerged stars; down-valley, the Basin beginning its baritone hymn. One pulse drinks cold cloud, the other burns dry light, but the wind threads them together, carrying root-scent into salt and bringing back a music of glass—a single circulatory loop wrapped round the mountain’s heart.
