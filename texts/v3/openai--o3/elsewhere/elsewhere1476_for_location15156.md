The Miration Basin

1. Origins and Reach  
Far below Evigrad’s vertigo of stone, the land reassumes its long-lost horizontals.  Here, in a shallow crescent four hundred kilometres wide, the plateau’s stolen moisture finishes its journey.  Every bead that dripped through a High-lungee’s cistern, every film-thin waterfall that vanished before it could earn the name of river, percolates downward for months through fractured limestone, gathers speed along a buried fault, and resurfaces at last in the Miration Basin: a carpet of mirror-lakes and reed flats so level that the horizon looks planed by artificers.  Old surveyor charts label it an endorheic sea, yet no single body of water persists.  Each spring the Basin blooms glassy from shore to shore; each autumn it scatters into two hundred jewel-pans, never the same shape twice.  The ground remembers every former shoreline in white rings of evaporite: overlapping crescents like fingerprints pressed into the earth’s clay.

2. Light and Weather  
Sky that reached Evigrad only as bruised pewter arrives here unfiltered and cavernous.  Air masses descending off the plateau warm by the kilometre: dawn begins cold, but by midday the Basin shimmers at forty degrees Celsius, conjuring a wavy mirage-skin that seems thick enough to peel away.  In the hot season an inversion lid forms 30 metres above the flats—an invisible ceiling against which sound rebounds, giving all noises the baffled quality of speech beneath a dome.

When the Fall of Breath sends silver fog spilling over Evigrad’s rims, the cloud-water glides south and east underground.  Thirty-one days later—locals can set calendars by it—the Basin’s springs erupt as artesian flares, fountains two men tall that hiss like kettles.  The event is called the Second Exhalation; paddlers time their planting to its arrival.

3. Soil and Chemistry  
Evigrad drinks clouds, but the Miration drinks echo.  The Plateau’s dissolved limestone carries ions of magnesium, calcium, and traces of vanadium; by the time they reach the Basin the solution is supersaturated.  Sunlight pulls water back into the sky, leaving lace-work terraces of aragonite so thin they chime under a fingernail.  The ground is therefore musical: step wrong and an amphitheatre’s worth of brittle plates jangle, broadcasting your position for kilometres.  Hunters break aragonite deliberately; the resonance bouncing off the inversion lid confuses the Basin’s plank-hoofed ungulates, pinning them in pockets of stillness.

4. Vegetation  
Plants here grow root-first into water, leaf-second into light, and seldom bother with height.  • Mirror-reed—translucent stalks hung with quartzine cells that reflect enough sun to keep tissues cool.  • Step-sedge—blades tan as parchment, edges serrated with micro-crystals; children slice melons on them.  • Drizzle-kelp—an amphibious alga that crawls out of receding pools at dusk, leaving ropes of sugar-rich mucilage.  Settlers harvest the ropes, twist them with lime, and cure a candy that fizzes on the tongue like mild electricity.

5. The Built Places  
Nobody erects towers here; every vertical structure invites the Basin’s cyclic winds to lever it flat.  Towns therefore sink: streets lie a metre below grade, houses another metre lower, basements deeper still until each settlement resembles an overturned honeycomb.  Foundation walls are poured of compressed reed-brick wrapped in a sleeve of woven aragonite platelets—the only building material the Basin gifts freely.  Lanterns hang from waist-height posts; illumination must glide under the inversion ceiling or it scatters uselessly upward.

Canals act as roads.  They are dug to precise gradients known as “listening slopes,” so any paddle stroke echoes along water and limestone both, carrying news faster than a shouted word.  A practiced rower can play geography like a xylophone, striking the hull at intervals to converse with unseen settlements over ten or twelve kilometres.

6. The People of the Basin – The Lowsingers  
Physiology and Poise  
Altitude forgives them nothing.  Lung capacity is ordinary, but hearing far outpaces sense of smell; even an infant will startle at a footfall fifty paces away.  Hair bleaches almost white under the mirror-light; adults braid silver-dusted clay beads into it, lending the entire community the glimmer of a walking shore at sundown.

Dress  
Loose sand-silk smocks, hems weighted with tiny disks of crystallised salt so cloth falls straight despite convection drafts.  Ankles bear “mute bands”—soft copper coils that cancel the jangle of aragonite underfoot.  Travellers learn to recognise Basin folk by the faint, off-rhythm tink a mute band gives when it completes its circuit of destructive interference.

Language  
Lowspeech seldom rises above chest resonance.  Syntax relies on intervals: a question ascends a minor third, consent drops a fifth, apology sustains a trembling unison.  Outsiders can master vocabulary quickly; the grammar of pitch may take a lifetime.

Livelihood  
• Salt-furrowers: score shallow grids in drying lakebeds, harvest blossom salt that crystallises overnight in feather-delicate dendrites.  
• Echo-herders: steer half-wild ungulates (“terrace elk”) by detonating aragonite plates in coded rhythms.  
• Basinwrights: engineers who cut new listening slopes when canals drift out of tune.  They are paid in lengths of Evigradi moss-felt— prized for lining dome interiors to regulate echo.  

Governance  
Decisions happen in the Central Tincture, a hemispherical pit floored with echeloned water.  Delegates sit on reed-floats at tuned positions; speech must ripple the pool in patterns the others can parse.  A motion that raises too chaotic a wave fails by acoustic veto.

7. Fauna  
• Terrace Elk: limbs long and jointed like compass needles, hooves flattened into plates that distribute weight across fragile crust.  Antlers fork forward instead of upward to scoop mirror-reed into the mouth while the head stays low enough to read the ground.  
• Skiff-grims: fish whose skull flattens into a hull; they lash tails behind like sculls, oar-paddling across mere skins of water a centimetre deep.  Children race them between evaporite rings.  
• Whisperwings: insect colonies that form living kites, hundreds of membraned bodies interlocked.  They anchor to reed clumps and vibrate at frequencies that dissipate heat, creating cool pockets where human paddlers sometimes nap at midday.

8. The Basin’s Machines – The Sink-Kites  
Four hundred years ago, in Evigrad’s imperial mining age, prospectors tried to lift ore from the Basin with wind-stalk cranes.  The cranes have long since collapsed, but their clockweight hearts survive as “sink-kites”: iron frames covered in mirror-reed lacquer, floating millimetres above the salt crust where gravity pockets weaken.  Powered by relic mainsprings, each kite wanders to no discernible schedule, rewinding itself whenever it crosses a hot updraft.  Lowsingers chalk requests on slate tablets and strap them to a kite’s belly; when the machine next drifts under the plateau’s shadow, Evigradi flue-walkers pluck the messages and send replies back on descending freight lines.  Thus, trade persists: salt and glazed reed-ceramic upward, felted moss-cloth and copper seed-beads downward, all mediated by derelict toys that forgot their parents.

9. Subtle Strangeness  
Occasionally the Basin rings without touch: a whole shelf of aragonite shivers in concert, low as mourning bells.  The Lowsingers call this the Drawn Note.  Two days later vapor-columns can be seen spiralling from Evigrad’s sinkholes, as if the Lacunae had inhaled too deeply.  Scholars argue atmospheric coupling; elders claim it is simply the world reminding itself of its own shape—plateau and basin, question and answer.

10. How the Two Places Thread Together  
Water is the first thread, sound the second.  Every drop Evigrad hoards eventually walks the Basin in mirrored boots; every hum that stones whisper into the High-lunged ear finds a later echo among the Lowsingers’ listening slopes.  Basin children learn to time the Second Exhalation; plateau midwives learn to note the pitch that carries farthest across inverted sky.  On festival nights both peoples strike their respective worlds like gargantuan gongs—Evigrad by plucking the Resonant Lines, the Basin by snapping evaporite shelves—until mountains, air, and water converge on a single droning chord that makes compasses forget north.

In that moment one may stand anywhere between cliff top and salt flat and feel, not hear, a pulse rising through boot soles: the sensation of a planet simultaneously exhaling and listening for its own reply.
