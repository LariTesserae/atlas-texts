Mouth-of-Sighs  

Follow the water: every tear the Body weeps between its stone teeth begins a descent no eye will ever track entire.  Droplets vanish through hairline cracks, gather in capillary threads, merge, and slip downward for three days and a night, pressured by the weight of two vertical kilometres.  At the mountain’s root they meet a slow breath of geothermal warmth, shiver once, and expand into steam.  What results is not a spring but a perpetual exhalation, a tide of mist that issues from a slot canyon so narrow you cannot walk it shoulder-forward.  The locals call that slot the Wind-eye.  What leaks from the Wind-eye spreads across a hidden basin half a league wide, mantling everything it touches in a beaded glaze and feeding the wetland they name Mouth-of-Sighs.

Look first with your ears.  The place is never silent; it talks in layers.

1.  The ground note is a bassoon hum—steam booming against rock inside the Wind-eye.  Each pulse travels the canyon length, strikes the open air, and collapses in a sigh that gives the place its title.  The Kennar in the high basin insist they can feel that same rhythm underfoot on still nights, as if the lower chamber were the echo of a heartbeat they share.

2.  Over the hum rides a second register: the metronome clack of reed-arms knocking together.  A single plant makes no more sound than a fingernail on glass; a hundred thousand in motion turn the air into a dry rain of wood.

3.  The treble is woven by insects—two distinct chords.  One rises at dawn as diaphanous flyers chase the first shafts of light; another swells at dusk when the land-crabs leave their burrows to scale the reed lattice and shear off tender tips.

Sight arrives next.  The wetland floor is a chessboard of mirror pools, each only ankle-deep but framed by raised lips of mineral as sharp as snapped porcelain.  Steam drifts over them, curls, condenses, and falls back as a warm drizzle.  Where the crust breaks it oozes rust-orange mud shot through with pale feathery crystals: cooled salts left behind by a million prior evaporations.

The plants—the famous sigh-reeds—root nowhere near water.  Instead they anchor in the mineral ridges, drinking through filaments so fine they double as musical strings.  A mature reed stands twice the height of a person, jointed like bamboo, but every internode flares into a petal-wide vane, turning the stalk into a living wind instrument.  When evening drafts push through, whole groves resonate; walkers must lean into the song as if against a physical weight.

Fauna of the Mouth arrange themselves by temperature gradient rather than by height or speed.

•  Rim wanderers  
On the high, cool lip of the basin, lavender-skinned karnock graze dust that sweats salt when licked.  Their shells are flat spirals, the geometry of ammonites reinvented in keratin.  Each dusk they file downslope to drink scalding water, their armor sizzling; minerals plate the growing edge of every whorl, so an adult karnock carries a three-coloured record of years: ice-blue birthband, brown adolescence, white maturity.

•  Mid-swath opportunists  
Between hot vents and cool rim roam tumble-hares, fist-sized bundles of fur that corkscrew across crust so brittle a human foot would break through.  The hares’ claws secrete an antifreeze alkaloid that temporarily softens crystal, letting them dig burrows that reseal hours later into airtight safety domes.  They eat the reed leaf litter, concentrating trace metals that would kill most life; once a season they hop to the rim and shed their fur in ragged wads the Kennar prize as mellowing agents for their vinegars.

•  Core domestics  
Closest to the vents live the lantern crabs, distant cousins to the vynn above.  They walk on four legs, brandish two for cutting, and carry under each eye a gland that exudes cold-light mucus.  A startled lantern will flick head ridges, splattering glowing beads that hang mid-air like stars before hardening to tiny pearls.  The basin folk harvest them to inlay snow-flutes; the Mouth folk weave them into prayer curtains that map constellations never visible through the upper lung’s glare.

People of the Mouth are called the Sahnt.  They claim no kinship to the Kennar, yet their dialect shares bone-core grammatical twists—paired verbs that mark intake and outflow separately, as if every action needed to show what leaves when something arrives.

Stature and bearing  
•  The average Sahnt stands a head taller than a Kennar but stoops habitually, shoulders pressed in by generations navigating reed forests.  
•  Hair is worn in two braids only, laid forward over ears to dampen the reed choir so one may think.  Silver rings crimp the braids; each ring records a year a person refrained from speech during the hot season, listening instead for pattern in the vent-breath.

Clothing and heatcraft  
Where the Kennar master cold, the Sahnt manipulate gradient.  Their jackets are double-faced: one side quilted with karnock shell dust to reflect heat, the other lined with hollow reed pulp that wicks vapour from skin and vents it upward in visible streamers.  Sahnt guides can walk barefoot across crust hot enough to cook fish; the trick is never to linger on a thought, only on movement.

Shelter  
Homes are inverted pagodas grown rather than built.  A family selects a living clump of sigh-reed, girdles it with sinew, and weights the crown so it bends, tips, and arcs until the top re-roots in adjacent crust.  Over four years the bending continues, section by section, until the plant has described a complete ring—a reed torus five paces across.  Crevices between stems are caulked with mud.  Each house throbs faintly with the reed’s surviving heartbeat, ventilating itself without chimney or window.

Economy and exchange  
Trade with the high basin is not constant—distance is three mountain ridges and a fault-scar desert—but it is rhythmic.  Whenever a Kennar resonance well produces an echo garbled beyond local decoding, a messenger is dispatched along the water-paths.  In return for fresh vynn shell (dense enough to sink in hot springs) and fermented Kennar beer (which refuses to freeze even in upland twilight), the Sahnt send canisters of reed air, compressed by reed pistons until each leather bladder holds a music impossible to play up in the Body’s thin atmosphere.  Released at elevation, a single bladder can sustain a Kennar festival fluting for a night and a day.

Belief  
If the Kennar call their basin a Body, the Sahnt speak of a Mouth and a Memory.  Steam rises, they say, because stories ferment in darkness and must escape.  Every vent cough is a parable poorly contained.  Once each heat-peak—the twelfth sigh after solstice—the Sahnt lower carved bone spoons into the hottest pool, trap bubbles, then freeze them instantly in shell-dust.  Held to the ear, a bubble rings with the low hum of the Wind-eye; elders assert the tone carries news from ancestors who drowned upstream when the upper lung first learned to breathe.

Rules of the Mouth

1.  Boundaries breathe.  A crust solid at dawn may dissolve to paste by noon; stake no claim the steam can’t rewrite.  
2.  Every sound has a twin.  Speak only after hearing your own words returned warped through reed or vent; the distortion shows the future shape of what you intend.  
3.  Heat seeks story.  Objects with complicated history—heirloom bone, long-used tools—warm faster at the pool edge.  Wise artisans quench new work there, letting narrative temper the blade.  
4.  Minerals are time made visible.  If white lace creeps across black mud, step lightly; that is an hourglass turned horizontal.  
5.  Nothing descends without rising elsewhere.  The Kennar teach this upward; the Sahnt teach it downward.  Between them flows a completeness no map can lay flat.

Visitors’ primer  
•  Arrive at dusk; the vent’s voice is kindest when day and night share the horizon.  
•  Tie back hair—loose strands whistle against reed vanes, announcing you as outsider.  
•  Accept the silence cup: a vessel of cooled steam.  Drink in one breath; any leftovers scald skin on contact but leave reed untouched.  
•  Do not attempt to enter the Wind-eye.  Those who insist come back altered—eyes filmed, speech reduced to chord progressions only reed and vent can parse.  The Sahnt make them custodians of the hush-pools, loved yet no longer entirely present.

Interdependencies  

Body above exhales water → water becomes steam here → steam condenses, feeding sigh-reeds → reeds trap minerals, building crust that scaffolds karnock and crabs → animal cast-offs enrich mud for more reeds.  

When drought threatens the Mouth, the Sahnt send hollow reed trumpets upslope with the next Kennar caravan.  Played inside the upper basin, the trumpets amplify quiet enough to coax a premature sky-breath storm.  Meltwater increases, the Body wets its own throat, and three days later the Mouth answers with thicker mist.  Both peoples deny ownership of this loop; they speak of it as one might of lungs, throat and lip cooperating to form a single note.

Stay a month and you will learn to read humidity the way others read smoke—to predict the vent’s tempo by the texture of your cuticles, to taste copper on your tongue when a new mineral vein migrates underfoot.  Stay a year and your speech will halve in speed, making room for the reed echo; you may dream in twin sentences, one ascending toward cold stone teeth, one descending into warm steam.

And should you return to the Kennar after such a year, you will find their Body listening a little differently, as though the echo you carry has taught the upper lung a cadence it once forgot.  The world will feel rounder, like two chambers of the same heart: one that freezes to polish its own silence, and one that boils to sing what silence cannot hold.
