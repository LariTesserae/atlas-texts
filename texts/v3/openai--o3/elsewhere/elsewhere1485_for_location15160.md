The Upwell Shoals

1. Where the water arrives  
Follow the drowned caverns that sip the dead of Sieve-Hollow; three tide-shifts later the same water heaves upward again, forty river-miles east, through a maze of basalt chimneys just off the coast. There it births the Upwell Shoals—a half-moon of reefs so perforated that the sea seems to breathe through bone flute–holes. Each surge is cold, mineral-bright, and heavy with the same salts that coat the Hollow’s cisterns. By night the rising plumes glitter green, then blue, then clear, as if recalling every sky they mirrored on their long underground travel.

The shoals themselves are terraces of black glass worn dull by centuries of tidal sanding. Between terraces lie pans of white silt, source of the Frog-Light dust prized far inland. The water leaves again by surface current, skimming north in a ribbon locals call the Spilt Thread; marsh traders pole their reed barges right on that seam and let it carry them straight toward the bowl they can never quite believe exists.

2. Weather and horizons  
Out here there is always a wind—salt-hot at noon, knife-cold at night. Mist never lasts; the ocean eats it too quickly. Instead the air is full of blown brine that crystallises on eyelashes and the edges of nets. Twice a year the sky behaves strangely: clouds lodge on the upwelling column and ride it upward in spinning stacks until they shear apart and drift inland as perfect, paper-thin sheets. When those clouds reach Sieve-Hollow they are the fluttering fog-cranes the gutter-wards read for omens.

3. Structures and moorings  
Nothing stands still on the Shoals; everything is tied, weighted, or growing. Homes—called hawsers—are plank rafts anchored to iron-rich boulders by rope the width of a man’s thigh. Each hawser carries three things: a boom-tent of kelp-cloth, a drying mast for fish, and a cluster of bell-jars that condense the luminous salt the marsh traders come to buy. Walkways are simply extra rafts lashed alongside; when a storm threatens, families unbuckle the walks, reef their tents, and let the whole quarter of the village swing around its anchor like petals on a loosened wreath.

Smelting happens on floating hearth-rafts. The Shoal-folk rake magnetic sand off the basalt shelves, melt it with driftwood charcoal, and tap out ingots small enough to palm—black iron pitted like pumice. Most of that iron drifts inland on the traders’ barges; a tithe returns months later as knife-thin studs set proudly in Hollow collars.

4. Living strands of sea and stone  
Kelp is king. A species they call rope-marrow grows trunks taller than masts and tough as rawhide. Families graft their anchor-lines into living kelp so the village renews itself each spring. Between the trunks bloom cress-anemones whose petals hold indigo dye; this is the blue that stains Hollow tunics and shelf-bark wafers. Farther out lie the Spine Orchards—forests of razor-coral that only sting on the ebb tide, when their polyps wake to hunt.

5. Creatures that claim the surf  
• Mirror-eels: blind, silver, docile unless separated from the deep upwelling; the Shoal-folk herd them with hollow-reed crooks and sell the least fertile adults to Hollow eel-keepers.  
• Tide-rakers: birds with shovel beaks and bronze claws said to echo the god-beast of the First Rake legend; they scrape algae from glass rock, leaving the long parallel scratches that make Hollow exiles shiver.  
• Cowl-turtles: broad as doors, shells veined with the same glowing salts that drift into Hollow mist. Pilgrims from the bowl believe they ferry the dead downstream, though no one in the Shoals ever finds bones inside.

6. Voices on water  
A village on chains hums: anchor-lines thrumming, kelp trunks knocking, hearth-rafts roaring when the bellows pull air. Conversation rides the resonance. The Shoal-folk speak in low whistles shaped against the tongue; a trader sitting amidships can follow a dozen overlapping talks without turning his head. Night belongs to the tide-rakers’ bronze squall and the hollow boom of blowholes clearing.

7. The Shoal-folk  
They name themselves the Drift, for what does not drown must float. Skin: sun-salted bronze on exposed limbs, under-hue of river-pearl still visible where clothes cling. Hair is shaved at the temples so wind can pass; the remaining strip is braided with dried kelp vein that hardens into a natural splint—useful when the wind tries to whip a head clean around.  

Every wrist carries a spinner-ring cut from mirror-eel spine; spinning the ring keeps fingers warm and minds attentive to wave rhythm. Children earn their first ring the day they can dive through a kelp arch, touch bottom, and bring back a handful of salt silt without inhaling panic.

8. Work, trade, reckoning of worth  
Dayshift:  
• Skim-raking luminous salt before sun spoils its glow.  
• Weeding rope-marrow orchards so anchor-lines stay supple.  
• Hammering bloom-iron into sheets thin enough for marsh canoes to carry.  

Nightshift:  
• Netting mirror-eels drawn to the lights.  
• Pouring indigo into bladder-skins; dye must ferment in darkness.  
• Reading the starboard sky for cloud-stacks that foretell inland storms—the signal to pack extra salt bricks for the Hollow against the coming surge.  

Value is weighed not by mass but by drift-time: how long an item can be trusted to float if a chain snaps. Indigo-skins score highest (they bob for seasons), iron sheets lowest (hours at best), so one skin buys five sheets without haggling.

9. Customs held fast with rope  
Courtship begins with a test of balance called the Twin-Walk—two suitors stand foot-to-foot on a narrow spar and hoist a drying rack between them. Whoever keeps the rack level through an entire breakers’ set becomes the “stead,” the other the “sway;” both parts are honored.  

Death is given to the Under-Pull. The body, weighted with its own broken spinner-rings, is lowered directly into the central vent. The Drift time the release with a Hollow tide-belch heard faintly up the shaft; they believe the still heart of the bowl needs a fresh beat now and then.

10. Odd water-laws and living myths  
• A spilled cup of upwell water will crawl back to its source if left un-mopped, leaving a dry snail trail behind.  
• Bell-jars filled during the greenest glow will explode exactly three months later unless emptied under new moon; children dare one another to keep them un-tapped to the last safe night.  
• Some nights the Shoals fall silent and every anchor-rope slackens at once. Old helms say that is the moment the sea bed settles a finger-width lower and the world has to follow.

11. The long splice to Sieve-Hollow  
Traders speak of it as a single breath: water pulled down through the Hollow’s gulping throats, carried below shale roots, steeped in iron sand, then exhaled beneath the Shoals in a spray so bright it stains the moon. The Drift call the Hollow-Folk their “inland mirrors,” and more than one gutter-ward has apprenticed a season here to learn how rope-marrow knots might steady wobbling stilts back home.  

Likewise, when a Shoal anchor yields, Queen Salt alone cannot mend the wound; they send for nacreous bricks scraped from Hollow cisterns, bricks that remember how to glue themselves to whatever still sweats the deep tide. And always there is the frog-light itself, split between mist in the bowl and surf on the Shoals—a pulse travelling the hidden artery that joins two places which may never see one another’s horizons, yet keep the same impossible heartbeat.
