Veinweft Wetlands

A whole day’s scramble below the hanging ledge, where the blue-grey schist disappears under soil and the gorge widens just enough for the sky to sit comfortably, the mountain’s seepwater resurfaces and spreads itself like loosened embroidery. The locals call this expanse the Veinweft Wetlands, because every streamlet that threads the ledge above eventually unravels here into a thousand reddened channels, staining the mud the colour of old blood. Stand ankle-deep and you can feel the mountain’s pulse—one cool beat for every two of your own—thumping through the water.

TERRAIN AND WATER  
From cliff foot to far bank stretches a labyrinth of raised root-islands, no two larger than a banquet table. The muck between them looks solid but shivers when touched; a single misplaced step can send a traveller hip-deep into silt as fine as sighing. The water is neither wholly fresh nor fully mineral. Charged days on the ledge send excess ions sluicing downward; by the time they reach the basin they fizz the surface with microscopic bubbles that glitter turquoise at noon, rusty crimson by dusk. At night those same bubbles wink phosphorescent green—vein-sprites that rode the flow survive here only as afterglows, winking out when touched.

VEGETATION  
Where solid ground refuses to gather, roots do the work. Ribbon-willows braid themselves into living pontoons, their branches plunging instead of rising so that leaves float flat like lily pads. Between these mats spear up copper reeds whose hollow stalks chime in wind: each stem a different length, tuned by beavers who chew them to precise notes for reasons their own. Deeper pockets host lantern-algae—spheres the size of grapes that bob just beneath the surface and glow steady amber. They adhere to whatever brushes them, so wanderers often emerge haloed in light, looking half-divine, half-drowned.

TEMPERATURE AND LIGHT  
The gorge concentrates heat the way a kiln holds flame. Even in winter, river mist smells faintly of warm iron. Daylight arrives all at once, slanting past the cliff rim and ricocheting off water until everything blazes white-gold; no gentle dawn here. At twilight the opposite occurs: mountains steal the sun like a slammed door, plunging the marsh into blue so fast pupils ache. In the hour that follows, lantern-algae glow brightest and the air fills with ghost-midges rising from every pool.

SOUND  
Nothing like the ledge’s hollow roar. The Wetlands speak in murmurs and plucks: water dripping from willow petioles, the harp-note of a reed cut short by a beaver’s teeth, distant gloop of bubbles bursting through mud. Occasionally a timber-camp foghorn rolls down-valley; here its echo lands soft, and every chime-reed answers with the horn’s lowest tone, a greeting passed earthward.

FAUNA  
1. Mire-Beavers (“Chordwrights”)  
   • Fur: Black, shot with bronze where mineral water plates each strand.  
   • Habit: Fell copper reeds, nibbling them to calibrated lengths, then ram the stems upright around burrow entrances. When a wind passes, the reeds produce chords that warn the colony of predators and announce favorable mating currents.  
   • Trade: Stone-rafters prize the cut reeds; bundled, they become pan-pipes that never need tuning. Once a season the rafters descend to swap goat-hide drums for a crate of resonant reed.

2. Bubble-Herons  
   • Legs: Triple-jointed, each step compressing mud enough to puff bubbles. They read those bursts like braille, tracking fish without sight.  
   • Feathering: So pale it mirrors sky; in photographs they register only as disembodied beaks and feet.  
   • Courtship: Two birds walk circles, stamping rhythms that churn bubbles into overlapping rings—marsh mandalas that persist for hours.

3. Hematite Eels  
   • Scales plated with actual iron oxide, rasping when two fish brush. Schools sound like distant sandpaper.  
   • Drawn to electric fizz days; they surface en masse, lips tasting charge. On those nights the water glows as if lit from within by a rolling storm.

4. Mist Leopard?  
   • Wetland folk swear the cat never comes this low, but sometimes, on mornings after charged nights, a single U-shaped trough cuts through reed mats—an indentation the length of a feline body, edges steamed dry. No tracks enter, none leave, and local children are taught a fifth rule: “If the water goes still all at once, lie flat—lightning hunts upright shapes.”

BUILT THINGS  
Half-floating plankways, lashed to willow roots with eel-skin cord, zigzag between research shacks stilted on copper-reed pilings. The shacks belong to Flood-Script Archivists: scholars from the timber camps who record every change in water chemistry on parchment made of pressed conie-fur felt. They believe the Wetlands remember each mountain storm as a different recipe of mineral and light; read enough samples and one might forecast the ledge’s next charged day.

SOCIAL LIFE  
• Market on the Third Still: Once each month the wind dies entirely for a day—no chime-reed music. Sky-goat keepers glide down-valley carrying bundles of beard-hair rope, exchanging them for jars of lantern-algae used to line goat harnesses (the glow spooks eagles during night climbs).  
• Guest Rite: Any visitor must plant a reed where they disembark the plankway, cutting it to a note no other reed around it sings. Over time the Wetlands become an orchestra of footfalls, every human step adding a key.

WEIRDNESS—JUST ENOUGH  
Touch two live lantern-algae to opposite poles of a moss battery taken from the ledge (the Quiet-Foot sometimes bring them preserved in damp cloth) and the spheres flare brilliant white, then split into eight smaller orbs—an exact cellular division unknown in terrestrial biology. Drop the orbs into water and each grows into a new grove of glow, expanding wetland acreage by half a square metre. Scholars argue the wetlands are the mountain’s downstream attempt at photosynthesis, using visitors as accidental pollinators.

RULES OF THE WETLANDS  
1. Never silence a chime-reed; if one stops singing outside a beaver’s gnaw, something larger than you is wading close.  
2. Boat only by night; daylight glare blinds even veteran punters.  
3. Carry no iron blade—hematite eels strike at rust-scent. Bronze or obsidian fare better.  
4. Do not follow bubble-heron mandalas inward; the rings disorient, and people found at the centre speak afterward only in reed-tones for days.  
5. Offer mountain water back to the mud. Travellers from the ledge pour their canteens here; the Wetlands in return send up cooler mist for their climb home.

DYNAMICS  
Tides of air rather than sea govern depth. When katabatic winds scream down evenings on the ledge, they plow the gorge, pressing a wall of cold into the basin and pinning water flat. Pressure forces subsurface gas outward; reeds bend, bubbles frenzy, and eel schools rise glowing like seething constellations. Come midnight the wind withdraws uphill, sucking half the marsh’s water noiselessly into unseen fissures. For an hour root-islands sit marooned on bare mud, showing footprints of everything that walked that day; then the seep springs cough, water returns, and the record is wiped clean.

PATTERNS OF COEXISTENCE  
1. Electrical Communion: Charged water from the ledge keeps lantern-algae fertile; algae glow guides mire-beavers to edible bark; beaver canals aerate mud for hematite eel nurseries; eel waste enriches copper reeds whose chime tells Stone-Rafters when to harvest. A pulse begun in stone ends in music and back again.

2. Memory Trade: Quiet-Foot elders say dreams lost at altitude drift down the gorge and roost in willow mats. Wanderers who sleep on plankways wake speaking fragments of songs only sung on the ledge. In turn, wetland children sometimes mutter slope-finch calls in their sleep, never having climbed so high. No one knows how exchange happens; both communities accept it as atmospheric generosity.

IF YOU MEET THEM  
• Archivists greet with palms dampened in marsh water—dry hands suggest you hoard charge.  
• Mire-beavers ignore bribes of food but will trade reeds for polished mica shards from the ledge; they embed the mirrors above lodge entrances to catch sun, perhaps believing light itself is a visitor worth housing.  
• When a bubble-heron stamps beside your punt, tap the gunwale twice. It will answer with two stamps and depart, marking you as skilled enough not to spook its prey.  
• Leave uphill before first chime at dawn; once the reeds resume their song, uphill drafts begin, and every vapour-kin still alive on the ledge is exhaling. Their mist rides the wind back home, and anything breathing valley air becomes briefly, painfully aware of every lung between here and the sky.

CONNECTION, IN A SINGLE BREATH  
The ledge exhales cold, charged water; the Veinweft Wetlands inhale it, warm it, sing with it, and return it skyward as mist that climbs to re-bead on the stone above—one place’s heartbeat completing itself in the body of another. Between the two hangs a cycle of music, minerals, and memory so constant that neither altitude nor distance feels like separation, only the long measure of a shared song.
