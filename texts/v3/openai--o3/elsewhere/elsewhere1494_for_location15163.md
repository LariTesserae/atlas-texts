The Stepwell of Muted Tides  

Distance, current, kinship  
What the Fang exhales as steam does not vanish. It creeps outward through faults in the pillar, chills, liquefies, and gathers into a migrating aquifer that circles the mountain like a belt. Where the strata thin on the leeward side, the belt ruptures, pouring its hoarded breath into a single sink of stone. Thus the stepwell was born: a throat carved downward by water that remembers being sky.

Terrain and materials  
Look down before you look around. The “ground” is a helical staircase, thirty concentric terraces cut straight into basalt. Each tread is the width of a rowing boat, each riser the height of a folded knee. The stone was not quarried but dissolved; tunnels drilled by hot, mineral-rich brine trace filigree through every riser, leaving the steps shot through with honeycomb lightness. Touch a wall and it hums—microscopic drainage still at work, tasting for weaknesses it might one day widen.

At the centre of the coil lies the pool, oval and black as poured ink, yet its rim glints pale green. That sheen is not algae; it is a living glass, a lamina one molecule thick that coats the meniscus like a skin. Only where a ripple exceeds a finger’s width does the lamina tear, and then the wound reseals, knitting with a faint chime.

Water  
The pool is fresh to the tongue but salted with memory. Every hour a new pulse of warm fluid wells up from below, rising through a throat that leads—so rumour says—clear back to the Fang’s molten belly. When the pulse peaks, the water column inverts: surface dives, depths lift, small drowned objects pop up like seeds in a germination jar. On the terraces, seepage collects in gutters that conduct miniature rivers clockwise, always clockwise, as if rehearsing the long spiral down.

Temperature  
Air in the stepwell is cool enough to prune skin but never truly cold. Vapour drifts up the central shaft, meets night air, and crystallises into snow that falls inward instead of out. The snow is warm, each flake a hollow shell of ice so thin it melts on the palm without wetting it. Stepwell folk call it ghost-snow and let it season their food.

Light  
Daylight pours from overhead, strikes the inner walls, and bounces deeper with each turn of the spiral. The basalt is shot through with vesicles lined in zeolite; these behave like fibre-optic beads, scattering photons until nothing casts a clear shadow. At night the pool itself glows: not bioluminescence, but chemoluminescence triggered when up-welling brine meets surface oxygen, painting the lowest three terraces in fox-fire green.

Sound  
Where the Fang smothers noise, the stepwell fractures it. A single word tumbles, splits, rejoins, returns as a soft chorus three beats later. Conversations occur in braid: you listen both ahead and behind for the replies you have not yet voiced. Travellers carry whistles cut to discordant pitches that the well refuses to echo, helping them hear their true position amid the hubbub.

Vegetation  
Chlorophyll owns the rim only. Ferns with asbestos-fine hairs drink the ghost-snow; their fronds curl inward at noon, outward at dusk, resetting daily like marine chronometers. Deeper down, chlorophyll surrenders to carnalloyd mosses—ruby red, iron-rich, magnetic enough that a dropped blade will stand on its tip. At water’s edge blooms the Mirror-orchid, a leafless stem crowned by one perfect petal of living ice. The orchid is pollinated by… what, exactly, no one agrees, for nothing seems to land on it, yet seedpods ripen just the same.

Fauna  

1. Lantern efts. Amphibians the size of a child’s thumb, skin transparent, skeleton silvered with tin. They float in mid-column, air bladders tuned to the stepwell’s switching buoyancy. When the pulse inverts, efts rise or sink without swimming, harvesting micro-organisms knocked loose from the basalt pores.

2. Pulse-rays. Disc-shaped fish, flesh stiff like parchment. They never flap; instead they flex in time with the rise-and-fall surge, letting the well’s own heartbeat ferry them. Their mouths line the disc edge, scraping the glass lamina for the sugars exuded by…

3. Glassweft. The lamina itself is alive: a colonial film of paired hexose and silica chains. It metabolises surface tension, thickening where ripples concentrate energy. Every three days it sheds a lacework of spent film that drifts down-shaft and is promptly devoured by—

4. Knell-bats. Not bats at all, but velvet moths with stone dust in their wings. They feed on fallen glassweft, grinding it with quartz molars. Courtship is percussion: pairs collide softly in flight, each impact ringing the membranes in their thorax. The notes spiral upward and become part of the well’s ordinary echo.

5. The Sundermare. Sometimes named as cousin to the Hush-Serpent, sometimes its reflection. Picture a horse built of vacancy, mane and tail rendered in lines where nothing quite exists. It appears only on the thirteenth terrace, walks counter-clockwise, nose always pointed toward the Fang on the horizon—though the spire itself is out of sight. Where it steps, stone ages a century; lichen erupts, crumbles, and blows away in moments. It leaves no hoofprints, only time-wounds that seal when next the water rises.

Weirdness—geometry on the slack side  
Where the Fang quadruples space, the stepwell doubles time. Clocks run truly on the rim, slowly on the seventh terrace, and pause outright at the water’s lip during each up-well. Toss a pebble from terrace ten and you may watch it hang halfway, shiver, then resume the fall accompanied by the delayed slap of its former echoes.

Dynamics  
Every twenty-five pulses (roughly a day) the water level climbs to terrace three, floods the steps, and drags loose debris down-shaft. Whatever survives five full cycles becomes vitrified—wood turns to lignite glass, bone to ivory quartz. Pilgrims lower nets baited with memory-charms, hoping to retrieve artefacts transmuted thus. The Chir knives prize such glass for its changelessness; they barter shards of obsidian for it, carrying the trade up narrow goat paths to the Fang’s fissure, where metal is scarce but stories are hard currency.

Atmosphere, mood  
Stand on any terrace and you reside inside a spiral of perpetual arrival. Footsteps above and below, footfalls of people you have not met yet, people already leaving. Voices peel off the stone, seeded with future tense. The air smells of hot rain and rust. Where the Fang is womb, the Stepwell is memory organ: it does not gestate, it recalls, revises, rereleases.

Rules to remember if you descend  
• Keep count of terraces by taste, not sight. Each level leaches a different salt onto your tongue.  
• Do not bottle the pulse-water while it is rising; sealed, it searches for altitude and will claw its way out.  
• Write nothing on stone—the echo will read it back to you out of order until sense erodes.  
• Should the Sundermare appear, walk with it but never cross its path; stepping from younger to older stone in one stride can rip ligaments of personal history.  
• When climbing out, sing. The spiral returns songs intact and you will know you have not left part of yourself behind.

Connections—same breath, different lungs  
Quartzfilm spores hitch rides on ghost-snow, colonising basalt pores between terraces. Chir messengers drum news of stepwell floods to guide Fang denizens in carving fresh runnels. Midges that escape the Serpent’s hush are sometimes found drowned here, tiny prisms in the efts’ stomachs. And travellers who have stared too long into the pool report, on returning to the cavern above, that droplets there now carry their reflections—proof the two places converse in ways no path of stone could carry.

The world is wider than a single echo, yet every echo finds a wall. The Fang breathes out, the Stepwell draws in: a dialogue of steam and recollection, hinged on silence, penned in glass.
