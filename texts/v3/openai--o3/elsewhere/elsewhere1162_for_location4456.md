The Glasslow  

A THREAD THAT LEADS AWAY  
Follow the water-ghost of the plateau: seepage that gathers in a hidden vein, slides through the schist, and disappears downslope for thirty silent kilometres.  Inside the rock it is squeezed, salted, warmed by the crust, until at last the pressure can no longer be borne.  One dawn it punches free, hissing, on the floor of a sun-struck basin.  The wound steams for a heartbeat, then spreads—water hurries outward over powdery clay, fetches minerals from ages of buried oceans, and dies beneath a sky with no patience for clouds.  What remains is The Glasslow: a mirror made not of silica but of salt so pure it rings when the wind flicks its edges.  The basin is 19 km wide, five metres deep at its lowest point, and covered by a single, unbroken crust of halite that flexes like ice when walked upon.  Noon light striking it rebounds in a blue-white flare visible from the plateau; the Junara call that flash the Down-Eye and set their calendars by its season of greatest dazzle.  

TERRAIN  
Picture a bowl tiled in crystal.  The surrounding rim is terraced marl the colour of sun-bleached bone, wind-planed smooth except where fossil reefs protrude like the vertebrae of something titan and forgotten.  The basin floor is absolutely level—capillary action draws brine upward in perfect equilibrium, leaving behind wafer-thin hexagons that knit into plates as wide as a sleeping goat.  Some plates are clear enough to read footprints of brine-shrimps scuttling on the underside; others occlude with trapped silt until they resemble blue smoke caught in glass.  At the very centre stands a solitary plug of rock, its summit pitted with pockets that hold the only permanent shade for twenty kilometres.  When the sun sits directly overhead that plug casts a perfectly circular shadow: the Noon-Mark.  

WATER / NOT-WATER  
No streams cross the crust; any liquid becomes steam in minutes.  Life here survives on moisture locked inside salt crystals—microscopic inclusions the locals call “dew-beads.”  Each dawn the topmost millimetre of the crust deliquesces just enough to drink the desert air, forming a film thinner than skin oil.  By midday it is sealed again, but not before creatures and people have syphoned their ration drop by drop.  Once every nine or ten years, a quake in the schist pipe sends a fresh surge of brine roaring up into the basin.  For a single night the Glasslow becomes a shallow sea, silver under the moons, then next afternoon the water is gone and the crust is reborn with new geometry.  Those nights are called Clearwake and all debts, marriages, and grudges are reckoned from one Clearwake to the next.  

TEMPERATURE & LIGHT  
Days are white fire: 46 °C in the shade that barely exists, hotter where light ricochets between salt planes.  To survive, eyes here evolve mirrors: irises of the local mammals gleam nickel to throw out half the glare.  Night plummets to 5 °C, and the crust creaks like polar ice as it shrinks, emitting high keening notes that travelers mistake for voices.  Twilight lasts seconds; the horizon blushes once and is black.  Stars double themselves in the salt mirror so that constellations appear both overhead and under-foot, as though you trek a ribbon between two firmaments.  

THE MIRROR-FOREST  
At the basin’s northeast quadrant stands a copse of pillars, each one a woman-high column of twisted halite.  They began as driftwood carried here by floods millennia ago.  Salt encrusted the grain yearly until wood and crystal became one.  Touch a pillar and it sings—a faint glassy chord tuned by whichever wind tunnels through it.  Locals harvest nothing from the Mirror-Forest; they consider it the Glasslow’s throat, and to wound a voice is to invite silence.  

LIFE IN THE CRUST  
1. Brine-Spiders: translucent, eight-legged, able to phase between plates by exhaling a jet of super-saline spray that melts a slot just wide enough to squeeze through.  They farm films of green halobacteria, cropping them with silk barbules then weaving excess into sails.  Once a day they hoist these sails, catch the heat-wind, and skate kilometres in minutes.  
2. Furnace-Kites: reptiles the length of a forearm; scale microstructure converts 12 % of sunlight straight to infrared they dump through their tails, drawing cool air over their bellies.  From a distance they appear to hover a thumb-width above the salt, floating on their own personal thermal cushion.  
3. Choir-Shrimps: cousin to plateau sky-kelp, but wingless.  When Clearwake fills the basin they swarm, breed, die, and their shells settle as a thin rose-gold horizon line visible for decades.  Locals roll that dust into prayer beads which murmur softly during storms—the sound of a billion past lives debating the wind.  

PEOPLE: THE KHYSSAL  
Population: about a hundred and fifty, though census is imprecise—many spend weeks alone beyond the rim searching for meteoritic iron.  They live in the Noon-Mark’s shade where an annulus of fine gypsum reflects less glare.  

Physiology & Dress: Skin dark but pearl-sheened with salt dust.  Eyelids possess a translucent third membrane that deadens 80 % of incoming light.  Clothing is minimal during day—broad fan-collars of woven palm fibre from oases outside the basin, stiffened with shellac and worn like portable awnings.  Night gear is layered feltleaf imported from the plateau, traded for bricks of halite and stories of sky-clear nights.  

Shelter: They quarry nothing; instead they coax the crust to grow.  A Khyssal architect sprinkles beetle-carapace fragments in a circle, seeds it with a brine formula whispered by Dream-singers long ago, and for three weeks tends the rim as ribs of salt arch upward.  The finished hut is equal parts crystal and void, always cool because it exhales trapped brine vapour whenever interior temperature tops 30 °C.  At full moon the walls glow milk-blue, storing light like a jar might store water.  

ECONOMY / EXCHANGE  
The Khyssal possess what the plateau lacks: dryness and flame.  With driftroot gathered from the marl rim they burn small, clean fires visible for miles—a forbidden luxury to Junara eyes.  In exchange they trade sun-kilned salt bricks, slabs of mirror-salt for signaling, and the rare iron droplets found fused into the crust whenever a bolide survives descent.  Junara rope caravans climb down the curtain-roots, slink across night desert, and reach the Glasslow by dawn.  There they barter while the salt yet sweats dew.  They arrive barefoot—the first hush the basin hears all month—and depart bearing coiled heat-stones which, when wrapped in wet plateau moss, steam gently for days to warm newborn goats.  

THE LISTENING GAME  
Just before dusk the wind reverses direction and skims the plateau’s cold breath into the basin, carrying snatches of reed-organ moan.  Sound travels here as if the air were hollow copper; a single frog valve popping on the highlands can be heard as a plink across the Glasslow.  Children lie flat, ears pressed to crust, and trade guesses at what each distant note means: rain-gathering? limb-fall? a birth?  Their elders record especially complex sequences by pricking dot-patterns into goat parchment.  One winter an artisan etched the longest pattern yet onto a sheet of mirror-salt.  In the highland city a Junara dream singer, hearing his own bark-recital come back stripped to math, wept—convinced the wind itself was reciting their scripture in a second tongue.  

RULES OF THE PLACE  
1. Never look straight at noon; you will see nothing else for hours.  
2. Test each new crust plate with three taps: first for thickness, second for hollows beneath, third to thank the mirror for bearing your weight.  
3. Fire may burn, but its smoke must point straight up—lashwood fuel only.  Sparks that drift sideways are hunted with wetted shawls before they can mar the glass.  
4. If the salt sings three notes descending, flee to the plug rock; a crust fracture races faster than any foot.  
5. At Clearwake, all tools return to the house of their making.  Fresh water forgives old ownership.  

SOUND & SILENCE  
Day: wind skitters crystal dust in serpent lines; brine-spiders thrum sails; salt houses ping as thermal stress climbs to the breaking threshold then subsides.  
Night: nothing but footsteps of cooling plates, a lullaby of far cracks resealing.  Some swear the stars overhead and underfoot whisper to each other, gossip of fires they will never feel.  

THE HIDDEN FLOWS  
Beneath the crust, thermal gradients drive convective rivers of brine that spiral around a magnetic core of meteoritic iron.  Khyssal sages drop hair-fine silver wires through boreholes; current enough to light a glow-worm lamp rises, stolen from the quiet dynamo underfoot.  They call it Milk-Lightning.  Three centuries ago an over-deep tap caused a blowout that birthed the present Mirror-Forest.  They have tapped more carefully since.  

FAITH  
Where the Junara trust endless growth, the Khyssal prize perfect erasure.  They believe each footprint pressed into salt is a question posed to the world; when morning heat lifts that print away, the world has answered.  A life well lived leaves the shallowest possible dent.  At funerals the dead are crystallized upright inside hollow pillars.  Over years the pillar walls thicken until no body remains, only a milky column singing in the dusk wind—proof the person faded with grace.  

THREATS  
• Glass Quakes: brief, razor-silent.  Survivors carry lattice-scars that sparkle under certain moons.  
• Sun Wolves: feral canids descended from caravan dogs; pelt reflects 98 % of light, rendering them invisible until they block the view of something brighter.  They hunt by herding prey toward untested plates.  
• Salt Fever: micronized halite inhaled during storms abrades alveoli.  Cure is a tonic brewed from plateau sky-kelp, traded in vials wrapped with living moss to keep cultures viable.  

CONNECTIONS WRIT IN FLOW  
Every dawn the basin exhales a plume of salt-infused vapor that rides thermals back toward the highlands.  When that mist condenses among the plateau’s reeds, minute halite prisms glitter on fresh leaves—an alien frost the Junara children call basin-stars.  They lick the crystals, taste dryness, dream of fire.  

Conversely, every dusk an invisible river of cooled, humid air slides downslope, delivering spores, sky-kelp cysts, and seed-sleek insects to the Glasslow.  Most die, but a fierce few adapt, become new.  Thus plateau and basin trade what neither intends: water for silence, green for white, life for mirror.  The bargain is old, unsettled, and perfect.  

WHAT THE GLASSLOW WANTS  
To reflect without consuming.  To take the world’s glare, tame it into music, send it away lighter than it arrived.  Any being that aids that purpose is sheltered in shadow, given the hush it needs.  Any being that scars the mirror receives back its own image shattered to knives.  

Walk here a day and your pupils learn to close like shutters.  Walk a month and your shadow thins, edges dissolving into the dazzle.  Walk a lifetime and the mirror will one dawn forget the difference between your face and the sky it keeps answering.  You will step forward, weightless, and not break the crust at all.
