Flaxmere

A full day’s careful descent south-east of Cyclion—down the mule path, across the slate scree, through the hair-pin cedar groves where the fog thins into torn silk—lies a spoon-shaped valley that locals call Flaxmere. It is the bowl that catches nearly every droplet Cyclion releases: the silver threads that fall from the ridge, the drift of cloud-melt that rolls off higher peaks, even the runnel water squeezed from the plateau’s gutters once it has finished cooling blue stone. All moisture eventually stitches itself together, trickling, braiding, until it is gathered here in a broad, shallow basin of black earth and river-lace.

TERRAIN  
The floor of Flaxmere is soft loam over volcanic ash, dark enough to turn bare feet umber in minutes. Four low ridges encircle it like the petals of a recumbent flower; between those ridges lie open swales that cradle slow water. From above, the whole valley looks quilted: squares of cultivated green alternating with flooded mirror-panes where the sky repeats itself. Old river courses, abandoned centuries ago, score the earth in pale, looping dashes; children follow these ghost channels the way other folk follow roads. Every few hundred paces a glacial erratic the size of a cottage rises from the soil—smooth basalt eggs that the elders insist were laid when the world was young and impatient.

THE THREADWATER  
Cyclion’s cliff-threads, the misty tears of higher ice, and half a dozen springheads converge to form the Threadwater, a river too languid for rapids yet too restless to sit still. It never runs deeper than a tall woman’s waist, but it splits and braids around its own islands until cartographers despair. Water clarity is legendary; valley children joke you can see a fish think before it bites. Each sunrise the Threadwater pulls thin veils of steam off its surface, gifting the basin the faint perfume of wet stone that links it, sensorially, to its ridge-top cousin.

BUILT FORM  
No walls rise here—Flaxmere builds horizontally, not vertically. Houses are long, low rectangles of woven willow wattles daubed with river clay, roofs arched like caterpillars and clad in overlapping reeds. From a distance they appear to have grown straight out of the fields, and in truth, each is rebuilt every fifteen years when the thatch succumbs to weather. Paths are boards of split spruce laid end-to-end above the damp earth; they float a handspan off the ground, flexing gently underfoot. At the valley’s centre stands the Winnowing Ring, a circular, roofless colonnade of basalt pillars dragged from the erratics. During the harvest it becomes both granary and forum, its interior strewn with drying mats, its parapet strung with wind chimes of bleached reed.

TECHNOLOGY & CRAFT  
Where Cyclion refines stone and shadow, Flaxmere refines fibre and flow. Waterwheels—broad, slow-turning paddles lashed from cedar—dot the Threadwater’s banks; their rotation twists endless skeins of flax into linen cord. Each wheel drives a line of hanging spindles: the valley’s heartbeat is a soft, collective creak-and-click that never truly stops. Iron exists, but sparingly; bronze sickles and bone-hafted shuttles handle most tasks. The pride of every household is its loom, a knee-high frame of river-birch held together without nails. When evening fires are lit (in clay saucers, never on open ground) you can watch a loom’s shadow stitch itself across the reed walls like a second, larger machine at work.

VEGETATION & CROPS  
• Blue-flax: the valley’s signature, shoulder-high, flowers the colour of cloud-edge at dawn.  
• Swale rice: a short-grain variety that flourishes in the Threadwater’s eddies, traded upriver in burlap tubes.  
• Willow and alder along the banks, pollarded yearly into living fences.  
• Marsh saffron whose petals are dried for dye; it turns cloth a hue identical to the faint lichen-turquoise seen on Cyclion’s schist.  
• Occasional oat terraces carved into the lower ridges, a patchwork concession to the grain merchants from farther south.

FAUNA  
Wetland cranes: ash-grey, soundless fliers that pick frogs from the paddy margins.  
River otters: tolerated pranksters; superstition claims they distract attention from water spirits that dislike being watched.  
Stone geckos: palm-sized reptiles that bask on the erratics; their mottled skin mirrors basalt so precisely that visitors often mistake lizard for pebble.  
Glow-midges: floating points of pale-green light that rise from the rice paddies after dusk, turning the valley into a starfield turned upside-down.

ATMOSPHERE & SOUND  
If Cyclion is deliberate quiet, Flaxmere is contented murmur. You hear layered textures: waterwheels churning, loom beams knocking, the soft hiss of wind passing through fifty thousand flax stems in unison. Evenings bring frog-pulses—liquid consonants that echo in the wooden walks—and the distant, ricocheting hoots of cranes settling for the night. Silence exists only at the brief moment before dawn when fog descends from the high ridge like a monarch returning in disguise.

EROSION & TIME  
The Threadwater never floods violently; instead it rearranges its course a finger’s breadth each season. Buildings are erected with this in mind—light, movable, resigned to the river’s negotiation. In the long view, the valley is less a static place than a cloth perpetually re-loomed by water, warp shifted, weft replaced, pattern persisting yet never identical.

RULES OF THE WORLD (FLAXMERE CHART)  
1. No roof may cast shade upon the Threadwater past the third bell of morning; the river must receive its promised light.  
2. The first bundle of spun linen from each loom cycle travels up the mule path to Cyclion, payment for calendars and stone wedges received.  
3. A path walker must tap the board three times before stepping onto any household’s plankway—so the wood “knows the weight that comes.”  
4. Each erratic boulder is named; no two people may speak atop the same one at the same time. The locals say the rocks are old listeners and dislike overlapping stories.

DYNAMICS & CONNECTION TO CYCLION  
The bond between ridge and basin is older than memory, braided as tightly as any flax cord. Shadow-Readers of Cyclion carve the year’s planting stones and send them down, etched with sun-rise angles and solstice depths; in return the Netwrights receive fine, river-whitened linen that will become the next season’s fog-catchers. Mules carry the trade up the switchbacks twice a moon. They climb with bales of cloth smelling faintly of marsh saffron and descend laden with blue schist doorstones already squared to perfection. The ravens serve as intermediaries too, riding thermals between plateau and valley; Flaxmere children scatter snail shells on threshing floors for them, a courtesy taught by ridge dwellers.

POPULATION & APPEARANCE  
• Permanent residents: roughly 1,200 souls, clustered in household “rafts” of three to six longhouses linked by boardwalk.  
• Bodies: river-lean, forearms corded by loom tensioning, calves hardened by perpetual half-squatting in flooded fields.  
• Skin: ranges from river-silt brown to sun-steeped copper, often striped with fine pale lines where reed pollen dries against sweat.  
• Hair: left long, braided with single threads of undyed flax that fray with age; to cut the thread is to mark the end of adolescence.

DOMESTIC LIFE  
Households share everything that can spoil—rice, fish, gossip—and hoard only that which improves with time: dye cakes, spindle oil, secrets. Sleeping platforms hover just above earthen floors, woven so loosely you can glimpse the fireflies underneath. Children float reed-raft toys in the irrigation sluices and learn to gauge current by how the rafts spin.

FOOD AND WATER  
• Water: endless, though always filtered through charcoal barrels to remove the taste of coppery reed root.  
• Staples: rice pancakes steamed in willow leaves, fish roasted inside clay, river mint tea sweetened with evaporated rice syrup.  
• Celebratory dish: “Mist-cake,” a dense loaf of oat and marsh saffron baked only when fresh linen reaches the ridge—evidence that the valley’s labour has become plateau survival.

WORK AND GUILDS  
1. Riverhands—those who maintain the waterwheels and are sworn to dive beneath the paddles twice yearly to clear silt; they learn to hold a breath as long as a verse of loom-song.  
2. Dye-Keepers—custodians of the color pits where saffron, alder bark, and marsh clay stew. Their fingertips are permanently stained sunset orange.  
3. Loom-Counters—memory guild responsible for knowing precisely which thread count each household must supply Cyclion before the next moon. Their records are incised on lightweight shale slabs and stored underwater, lashed to stones where only they know.  
4. Drift-Scouts—pairs who walk the valley perimeter weekly, noting how the Threadwater has wandered; they re-stake path markers so no visitor steps onto last season’s dry ground thinking it secure.

LANGUAGE & VOICE  
Sentences in Flaxmere dialect wrap back on themselves, verbs often repeated for rhythm: “Water winds, winds quiet,” “Cloth waits, waits warm.” Laughter tends to be half-voiced through the nose; to laugh loudly is “to crack the reed,” an accidental rudeness quickly forgiven by generous smiles.

CYCLES & CEREMONIES  
River Turning: At midspring, villagers gather at the Winnowing Ring, each carrying a single plank of spruce. They set these planks across the river’s current, coaxing it to nibble new banks; it is said the water appreciates polite suggestion more than force.  
Erratic Night: Once a year the glow-midges peak. Families climb their favorite basalt egg and recount the stone’s oldest story in sequence. Overlapping tales are feared because they confuse the rock; the penalty is to weave fifty reed mats for communal walk repair.  
Fog Welcome: Whenever the high ridge sends an especially dense bank of cloud down the valley, the village answers by lighting torches along every plankway. The flame tips disappear in the whiteness, creating amber halos that hover like captive suns—an offering back to Cyclion for the gift of water.

CHILDHOOD  
Each child receives a private paddy at their seventh flood season. For a decade it is theirs alone to weed, plant, and harvest. Whatever rice they raise beyond what they can eat is converted, by parent convention, into long ribbon-sashes stored until the child weds; thus, a marriage procession is a moving ledger of years, every sash a season made visible.

THE OTHER INHABITANTS  

The Otter Court  
River otters, again—not tamed, but recognized as fellow engineers. When a waterwheel jams, villagers note the otters have usually rearranged stones upstream first. Negotiation involves offering shiny pebbles; an amused otter will push the stones into deeper water, unblocking the wheel as side effect. Valley lore claims the otters know Cyclion exists; they simply choose never to climb where the fog is “too tight to swim.”

Walking Reeds  
In certain rare dawns, patches of reed detach in perfect circles and float across still water, driven by subtle breezes. Villagers insist these are the paddies themselves “stretching,” proof the land is alive and must be listened to like any neighbor.

Basalt Bees  
Small, slate-coloured insects that burrow into warm pockets of the erratic boulders. Their honey is scarce, infused with a mineral tang that sets teeth ringing. A teaspoon melted into tea is the valley’s remedy for voice strain after a day’s loom-singing.

THE FEELING OF THEM  
Spend a week in Flaxmere and you begin to mark time by creak—wheel, plank, loom—and by shimmer, the constant ripple of water shrugging sunlight into your eyes. People talk while their hands braid; even gossip becomes tensile. They measure wealth not in weight but in softness, judging a person’s fortune by how easily linen drapes over the arm. Yet under that suppleness lies a quiet precision—every boardwalk aligned to the river’s next intent, every roof angled to allow fog to pass untroubled.

And when you climb back toward Cyclion, bale of cloth on your shoulders, you might pause half-way up the switchbacks. Look down through breaks in the cedar: the valley will already be refolding itself, Threadwater testing a new curve, reed roofs exhaling steam. Look up: the ridge waits, blue and motionless, nets ready. Between them, on the path you stand, pulse caught between drip and creak, seeing how the world’s fabric relies on tension and release—a loom shuttle forever moving between stone that waits and water that wanders.
