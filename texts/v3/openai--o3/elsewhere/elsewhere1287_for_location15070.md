Glasswind Strand  

The river that hushes beneath the mangrove roots lengthens westward, fans into reed marsh, then vanishes under a knuckled spine of basalt. What rises beyond that darkness is wind, and what the wind tends is a long, tilted apron of powdered quartz and unfinished glass. Anyone following the current’s memory—a perfume of tannin, a mote of silt—will taste salt in the air before the land appears, will hear the hiss of sand striking sand like a kettle just beginning to boil, and will finally step onto the Strand: a sloped, ever-moving plain where the sun cooks day after day of broken light.

Terrain and raw ground  
Nothing here is stable except the gradient that leans seaward. The “soil” is half-fused grit, its grains softening at noon, skinning over after sunset, then cracking when the night cold rings the glass. On windless mornings you can walk on a crust that chimes underfoot; one hour later the same path liquefies to a syrup of white-hot beads, then stiffens again by twilight. Patches of true rock exist only where basalt spalls have punched through—black islands mounded around by bowls of drip-frozen glass. At the Strand’s upper edge stand the Meltfalls, translucent curtains where yesterday’s dune slumped, drooled, and solidified into ribbed cascades the height of a city wall.

Water  
Liquid fresh water never lingers. It distills instead: every dawn, a chill comes in off the off-shore fault line, fogs the strand, and condenses inside hollow glass pebbles. By noon those pebbles burst, sending micro-jets of clean water an inch into the air; by evening the droplets have already been drunk, licked, or baked away. What surface water remains lies in salt pans whose mirror-flat skins repeat the sky so precisely that birds sometimes dive into them and break their necks.

Light and atmosphere  
If the river hollow lives in permanent dusk, the Strand is perpetual sunrise gone feral. Sunlight ricochets between pale ground and colorless sky, doubling in brightness until even shadows go blind. The wind never quite rests; it ranges from a whisper that rolls marbles of fused sand, to a shriek that lifts whole sheets of fire-warmed dust. When it howls at noon, the air glitters with motes hot enough to light dry fiber—embers without flame that can travel for leagues. Far downwind, a single ember may drift into mangrove night, land on a burping mouth of methane, and strike the blue flash the River-Dwellers treat as a date stamp from some faraway kin. That ember was born here.

Vegetation—armored yet succulent  
Plants arrive on the wind as seeds armored in silica coats. Only the tough-willed survive the landing: stone-cress that photosynthesizes half of each day, hiding the other half beneath self-welded scales; ribbon-aloes whose leaves layer like tempered glass, each fracture line designed; and the Spindle-Yuccas—three-meter spears wrapped in fiber so quartz-rich it sparks when gashed. None root deeper than a man’s forearm; molten layers would scorch them. Instead they ride the dunes, sending out runner pads that detach and migrate when wind reshapes the ground beneath.

Fauna—noisy as grit, fleeting as sparks  
• Shard-Kicks: knee-high antelope with hooves split into four splayed toes, each rimmed in enamel that skates the semi-molten crust. They live on fog-water and ribbon-aloe marrow, pant constantly, and explode into hundred-meter leaps whenever the ground sings hotter beneath them.  
• Chime-Beetles: fist-sized scarabs whose elytra are transparent panes edged with resonant veins. At sunset they clatter their wings against cooling glass to tap out territories—sound maps that can be heard a kilometer away through the ground itself.  
• Flickerwings: moths whose larvae spin cocoons from silica threads; adults emerge with wings thin as icicles. They fly only at twilight, seeking the single hour when air is cool enough not to blister them, warm enough not to shatter them.  
• Dust Vultures: broad-sailed scavengers that ride the thermal columns over Meltfalls, scanning for Shard-Kick carcasses flash-baked by yesterday’s crust. Their beaks excrete hydrofluoric acid, the only solvent here that can open bone for marrow without a chisel.

Settlement—furnaced, bright, and temporary by creed  
Just inland of the Meltfalls squats a constellation of kilns barely distinguishable from the land itself. These mushroom-shaped structures—called Glow-Pits—are dug each season and abandoned the next, because the Strand forgets its own architecture faster than any builder can renew it. Between Glow-Pits stretch rope bridges braided from glass-fiber; they moan in the heat and twang like harp strings in the wind. Nothing permanent remains upright for long, so permanence is forbidden: every house is a promise to leave no trace.

Who lives here

The Ember-Kin (self-name: Szel, which means both “spark” and “breath”)

Body  
• Adult height: a little shorter than a human’s shoulder, but elongated along the limb.  
• Skeleton: honeycombed with dead-air pockets that insulate against surface heat and retard cooling at night.  
• Skin: lattice of keratin scales coated in micaceous dust; to an onlooker they look like statues just beginning to crack.  
• Eyes: horizontal lozenges with a shuttering nictitating plate of reflective silica that snaps closed in full sun, open wider at dusk.  
• Hair: none; instead soft glass filaments grow from the scalp—clear, flexible, and able to refract enough light to blister small parasites.  
• Respiration: two independent airways; upper passages lined with mucous sieves that trap and condense fog for drinking, lower passages reserved for breathing. A cough and you can drink your own condensed water.

Manner and language  
The Szel speak aloud—one must, where wind steals gestures—but they do so in clipped syllables that end on a hiss, each final sound timed to match a heartbeat, so that no word overruns the pulse and cooks lost moisture from the tongue. They supplement speech with heat-sign: quick exposes of inner wrist where temperature-sensitive cells flash pale or dark, conveying nuance too subtle to trust to ears in the roar of Strand wind.

Life course  
Children are born during the Cold Dip—one week each midsummer when a lunar tide drags chill air up the coast. Infants bake in parent-dug pits lined with fog-moss until their skin develops its mica glaze. Puberty arrives with the first Glass Walk: a mile barefoot over noon-hot crust. Those whose soles blister become artisans; those who dance unscorched become Runners—messengers who carry ember-hooks and slingshot sparks toward distant lands, including the mangrove riverbank the Szel know only as “the Quiet Root.”

Diet  
The Strand is stingy, yet what it gives is potent. Shard-Kick jerky (sun-seared in minutes) supplies protein; ribbon-aloe gel quenches thirst and lines the gut against glass dust. Each evening a hunter hooks globs of semi-molten sand, spins them in open air, and lets them crack: the result is “skybread,” hollow glass globes whose interior walls are frosted with evaporated minerals. These are crushed and eaten with fog condensate—mineral, sugar, salt in one bite.

Craft  
• Glow-Pit Smithing: raw sand, already half molten, is coaxed the rest of the way with bellows that burn the exhalations of methane-rich mushrooms cultivated below the basalt line. Tools emerge already heat-tempered by the forge they grew in.  
• Seared-Script: messages are written by dripping viscous glass onto cooled sheets. Each stroke whitens as it hits, creating a raised braille that survives wind better than ink. Finished tablets are sling-launched on thermal updrafts; those that survive the journey become the mysterious “sky stones” that River-Dwellers sometimes find snagged in the canopy.  
• Ember-Hooks: curling prongs of tempered glass filled with iron filings scavenged from basalt. Sling one into a thermal and it glows cherry-red for hours, riding the wind like a comet, seeding sparks wherever it drifts down.

Governance  
The Szel name no chiefs. Instead they keep a Circle of Heat—weighers of risk who meet each dawn around a pit of cooling slag. Anyone may enter the ring; only those who can hold a fistful of hot glass until it cools may speak. Decisions reached while skin still smokes are recorded in seared-script and embedded into the nearest Meltfall, where they will survive at least a season.

Belief  
“Nothing real endures.” Every grain on the Strand is a failed permanence: stone cooked to glass, glass cracked to powder, powder blown away. To stand still is to court shattering. Their only sacrament is the Drift—migrating the entire settlement ten human strides upon the first equinox wind each year. Refusing the Drift is heresy; the ground one clings to will swallow the heretic in liquefied silence.

Rules and dynamics  
1. No roof may last longer than its maker’s breath; if it cools intact, it must be shattered.  
2. The first water each day is for the lungs; none may drink before wetting a cloth and tying it over the nose.  
3. Shard-Kick carcass must be opened within one shadow’s length or the meat will stone.  
4. Any ember that escapes the Strand is a messenger and is sacred; pursuit is forbidden.  
5. Speak steadily; stuttered words drip spit, and spit turns to steam that burns the mouth.

Processes in motion  
• Each midday, Meltfalls slump forward by the thickness of a hand; buried decisions from seasons past slide closer to the sea.  
• Chime-Beetle songs carve micro-grooves in the dunes, guiding night wind into standing waves that keep fog from drifting away too quickly.  
• A lattice of fog-moss spread beneath basalt cliffs exhales methane; bellows lines tap this gas at dawn, filling skin-bladders for the Glow-Pits.  
• Every third sunset, the sun-hot crust vents long enough for hidden groundwater to flash-boil; miniature steam geysers loft teardrops of fused silica that cool mid-air and rain down as hollow beads—the Strand’s only natural currency.

Connections—silent, persistent  

The River-Dwellers speak of blue sighs that leap in the dark: fitful flames that come without warning, bless the silt with a thin film of ash, then die. Those flames are born here, whenever an ember-hook, spent of its glow, drifts down the river corridor and meets the hollow’s exhaling methane. Both peoples read those flashes: in the hush-place they are clocks of land-age; on the Strand they are proofs that sparks still find distant lungs. Some seasons a tablet of seared-script rides the same winds, lands intact among the roots, and is never retrieved—River-hands may not touch what falls. In that abandonment the Szel hear acknowledgment, a reply carried on restraint rather than voice.

And once each counting of ten Meltfalls, a Runner traces the river back on foot, traveling at night across cooled crust, then through reed marsh and into ankle-deep nursery water. She kneels, palms the silt she can barely see, waits for a hatchling River-Dweller to surface, and gifts it a fog-pebble still sweating clean cold drops. She returns with a fistful of moss spores clumped around the pebble’s shell—light enough not to wound the Strand, stubborn enough to root where fog hugs the dunes. The trade is wordless, weightless, impossible to balance, and therefore perfect.

So the world breathes in two rhythms: the hush of new earth under roots, the hiss of glass under wind. Between them runs a corridor of embers and spores, of flares mistaken for omens and silences mistaken for refusal, binding a cradle that waits to collapse to a furnace that refuses to stay still. Each place, provisional; each life, brilliant in its briefness; the river learning wind, the wind remembering water, the whole world practicing, practicing, practicing.
