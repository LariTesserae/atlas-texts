The Ferric Sea  

Outflow

South-east of the continental body the vascular highways converge into twenty-seven great effluent spines. Each spine is a root hollow as broad as a river, and every minute they breathe out a pulse of exhausted sap—dark, metallic, stripped of sugars but loaded with everything the land has finished with: iron ions, silicates in colloid, excess heat. The pulses collapse into slurry waterfalls that roar through cliff-cut gills, meet one another in mid-air, and fall a final hundred metres into a basin that was once an oceanic rift. The basin fills, overfills, and for ten days in every sixty a rusty tide creeps back up the cliffs, painting them maroon before retreating.

Surface

The sea is not liquid in the ordinary sense. It is a slow, molten emulsion of water and particulate hematite, optically opaque but mechanically glassy. Wind—yes, there is wind here—skims the topmost centimetre into wavelets that shatter instead of curl; their shards tinkle down like broken crockery, melt, and rejoin the surface. From above, the whole expanse looks like living basalt with an oil-slick sheen: blacks veined by iridescence, red sparks strobing where fresh slurry plumes rise hot and fast.

Light & Weather

The Ferric Sea lies outside the stacked canopies. Cloud layers thin until a washed-out sky shows through, lunar in daylight, star-iced at night. Sunlight (whatever sun exists beyond the ever-present haze elsewhere) lands here hesitant and pale, yet it is enough. Heat released by the sapfall lifts thermals big as counties; they drag chill air inland, where it condenses against the continental leaves and rains back trace water that the plant desperately re-imports. Thus the land and the sea breathe one another: iron outbound, water inbound, a diastole and systole measured in weather.

Stratigraphy

1. The Shell—ten centimetres of semi-solid crust that plays at being land. Stand on it and you will not sink, but you will slide: the whole shell migrates centimetres a month toward the rift’s mouth.  
2. The Suspension—three metres of viscous dusk where hematite dust hangs in water too thick to be water. Pressure here is minimal; temperature sits blood-warm.  
3. The Furnace—below three metres the concentration of dissolved iron jumps, exothermic precipitation begins, and the emulsion turns into a slow-boiling gel. Red light, generated chemically, throbs upward in pulses synchronized with the sapfall.  
4. The Rift Gut—no one has measured its depth. Instruments come back after a hundred metres coated in dendrites of pure iron, their sensors cooked by internal heat.

Life

The plant continent exports no living tissue—just chemistry—but life has fermented in that discarded broth.

• Ferriphages (“Red Dust”)  
  Single-celled, flagellated, armored in magnetite scales. They oxidize ferrous ions, breathe crystal lattices, exhale heat. Clouds of them form the sea’s crimson smoke columns. When a column rises high enough to kiss sunlight, the cells photoswitch, grow carotenoid antennae, and ride the wind inland as brick-colored haze. The continental leaves drink them like vitamin pills.  
• Raft Colonies  
  Millions of ferriphages cement themselves into honeycomb floats the size of dinner plates. Over decades rafts fuse into archipelagos that drift just beneath the shell. Each plate maintains galvanic gradients between sun-lit top and hot underbelly; electricity flickers across the raft network in patterns that, if you squint, look suspiciously linguistic.  
• Hull-Worms  
  Not worms, but self-assembling trains of hollow cylinders grown from silicate precipitation. A hull-worm begins as a raft that folds into a tube, then feeds forward by growing fresh tube ahead and dissolving its tail behind. The train corkscrews through the suspension, always head-first into chemical gradients, herding ferriphage clouds the way baleen whales corral krill. Wherever a worm passes it leaves wakes of cleaned, cooled brine that sink as perfect, transparent curtains.  
• The Autophagic Isles  
  Rarely, an archipelago gains enough buoyancy to breach the shell. It tastes wind, mineralizes cellulose stolen from stray leaf-litter, stiffens into an island you could map. But every island carries within it the algorithm of dissolution: the moment stored charge falls below threshold, its plates reverse polarity and eat themselves. The sea keeps no monuments.

Sound

Unlike the continental hush, the Ferric Sea sings. Crust-shards colliding make wind-chime gamelan. Sapfall waterfalls hammer polyrhythms into steel-blue air. Deep in the furnace, exothermic blooms detonate with sub-bass thumps you feel in bone before ear. A practiced listener can translate the music into weather forecast: octave of clatter rising? Storm in three hours. Furnace booming double-time? New rift vent opening—expect hot updrafts rich in iron vapor.

Trade: The Iron Migration

Nothing solid passes up the sapfall channels, but ions do travel back. When the plant continent suffers iron deficiency, it alters the electric field inside every effluent spine—minor, just millivolts. Ferriphages detect the change and re-route, swimming against the sap as if against a weak tide, climbing interior root walls slick with magnetite. Only one in a billion survives the ascent, but numbers are astronomical; whole seasons the sap runs faintly red as the plant re-imports its own waste, cell by persistent cell. In return, month by month, the continental Quiet Teeth sequester less neighboring tissue: a truce bought with recycled iron.

Weathered Memory

Fragments of lignified seed-engines sometimes fall here, carried on miscalculated flights. They land, sink, and become cores around which ferriphages spiral in pilgrimage loops—perfect logarithmic shells kilometers wide. The pattern is a negative of auxetic scaffolds back on land: the sea remembering the sky-born seeds it was never meant to meet. Scientists (of other worlds) would call it emergent order; the locals—were there any—might call it devotion.

People? Sort of.

The nearest thing to agency is the Raft Logic: voltage patterns chasing one another across plate archipelagos. They have no language audible to us, but they do respond to novelty. Step onto an island with iron in your blood and the plates thicken, circle, offer you firm ground. Stay too long and you feel gentle tugging at fillings, at hemoglobin, an osmotic persuasion to lie down, lighten yourself, contribute. Travelers learn to keep walking, to distribute their weight, to whisper gratitude without ever pausing.

Standing Still

Spend an hour and the shell’s glass-waves will crawl a thumb-width. Spend a week and an island you camped on will have budded a twin and sent it north. Spend a lifetime—few manage—and you would watch the rift throat widen, the sapfall retreat a metre, the continent beyond growing leaner, smarter, perhaps kinder for having something vast enough to throw its waste into and have that waste sing back.

The Link, In One Breath

Roots empty, waterfalls fall, iron spills, ferriphages rise, wind gathers, haze returns, leaves drink, dynamos spin—place feeding place in a circuit too slow for pulse yet too quick for stone. The continental body dreams outward; the Ferric Sea dreams it back, each completing sentences the other begins, worded not in sound but in chemistry, in pressure, in light conducted through a billion rust-bright cells.
