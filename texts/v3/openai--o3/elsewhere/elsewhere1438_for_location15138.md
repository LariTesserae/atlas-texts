The Catcheries

First impression  
Descend through the cloud-columns that drift beneath the terrace world and you arrive in a vault of violet dusk. Somewhere overhead, miles of masonry sift the sunlight into dust-shot beams, but here the air is cool, salt-sweet and tinted with luminous spores. The floor is a quicksilver plain of shallow water broken by thousands of dish-shaped islands, each braced on hollow pylons like inverted wineglasses. Between the islands, nets—ropes as fat as your thigh, braided from orchid fibre—criss-cross the water in a tapestry that trembles at every distant footstep. This is the Catcheries: an unfixed archipelago whose only constant is whatever falls into it from above.

1. Terrain and structure  
• Think of a bowl the size of a kingdom. The mountain that once filled it is gone, quarried upward into terraces; what remains is an immense negative, its walls stratified with the quarry scars of forgotten generations.  
• Between the bowl’s walls stretches a mirror-lake no deeper than a grown man’s height. Suspended just above its surface are the Catch-Isles: light, saucer-wide platforms woven from bamboo analogue and coated in resin. Each rests on tripod pylons driven into the soft silt. The isles are anchored by tension, not weight; a single missing pylon sends one tilting like a hat in wind.  
• Ladders of knotted vine dangle from isle rims into the lake. “Going downstairs” here means descending into knee-high water where cool mud swallows your calves. Submerged walkways of slatted wood link distant isles for those willing to wade.

2. Water (abundant, drifting)  
• The lake is rain, condensate and spill-water from the terraces—mineral-rich, faintly metallic. Each dawn a timetable of drips begins: first a patter from gutter-flutes far overhead, then thicker ropes of runoff, then at noon the thunderous dump when upper cisterns purge their overfill.  
• Silt filters the water to the colour of old coins. It tastes of copper and crushed mint; the locals drink it unboiled, trusting the teeming plankton to outcompete any plague.  
• Occasionally whole artifacts plunge from the terraces: a cracked cog, a spent heliostat shard, the carcass of a pruning automaton end-of-cycled at last. The splash rings out across miles of water like a bell and islanders sprint to salvage whatever floats.

3. Light and climate  
• Permanent evening reigns below the fogline. The sun arrives as refracted columns that crawl across the lake in shifting mosaics. At night bioluminescent algae—gifts from terrace ponds—bloom turquoise wherever a rope’s shadow falls, giving every isle a neon cousin adrift beneath it.  
• Air temperature hovers at 15–20 °C, but humidity is near total. Morning begins with pearl-grey mist; by afternoon a faint breeze tunnels down the quarry walls, pushing mist into ribbons.

4. Flora (borrowed, adaptive)  
• Every seed that escapes terrace orchards eventually lands here. The Catch-Folk pot them into hanging cloches: grapefruit vines, dwarf banyans, even corn coaxed into spirals to save space. A plant that demands rich soil is grafted onto floating mats of compost stitched from dried algae.  
• Native to this basin are the Dust-Lotus, whose leathery petals close at any sudden noise, and the Lantern Reed, a hollow grass whose exhaled methane feeds isle-edge lamps.

5. Fauna (cryptic, opportunistic)  
• Spoon-back gulls ride the thermals inside the quarry bowl, diving for eels stirred by runoff. They have learned to follow the sound of a purge-valve gong—where water falls, stunned prey follows.  
• Pontoon Crabs, shell the colour of tarnish, live attached under every isle. They clip loose algae mats and fashion them into drifting cradles for their eggs; unlucky waders may find a crab has tethered a cradle to their boot straps.  
• The memory of the mountain lingers as Pale Moles—blind mammals that still dig phantom tunnels through silt, surfacing gasping whenever they break into lake water.

6. Soundscape (hollo, resonant)  
• Above everything is the percussion of drips on stretched net, a polyrhythm that changes with the wind. Huge concave quarry walls catch this and bounce it back as organ-chords.  
• When an isle tilts, the water underneath slaps the pylons with hollow booms called “fault drums.” Experienced residents keep mental maps of their neighbours by listening to the fault drums shift position through the night.

7. Technology (littoral improvisation)  
• Nothing large can be forged here—no iron seam, no stone worth carving—so the Catch-Folk traffic in salvage. They recast terrace copper into hair-fine fishhooks, polish heliostat shards into signal mirrors, unravel discarded conveyor belts for rubber caulking.  
• Power is chemical and biological: lantern reeds piped into ceramic mantles, algae batteries lighting loom huts. Rope is currency; the longer and less frayed, the richer you are.

8. The Catch-Folk (sapient, kinetic)  
Physiology  
• They are willowy by necessity—weight is the enemy of a suspended life. Ligaments grow near-elastic from a childhood spent balancing on nets. Skin pales for lack of direct sun; cheeks flush silver when rainwater’s iron soaks into pores.  
Culture  
• Every family claims an isle the way a sailor claims a boat. Birth records are carved into the pylon caps; marriages occur when two isles are lashed side-by-side for three tide-cycles.  
• Their calendar is acoustic: months are named for distinctive patterns in the overhead purge schedule—First Hollow, Double-Splash, Long Sheet.  
• They revere the terraces above not as gods but as weather. The phrase “sky willing” is their equivalent of “fingers crossed.”

Occupations  
1. Net-tenders—walk the rope lattice each dawn, patching tears with spider-knots.  
2. Fall-watchers—posted in hammock towers, they track descending debris and shout trajectories. Their helmets are wicker, better to break than heads.  
3. Silt-gardeners—kneel in shallow ponds cultivating edible slime. Their tattoos trace the growth rings of successive algal blooms.

Disposition  
Restless, quick-footed, politely curious. They prefer questions answered before a net stops vibrating.

9. Trade and kinship with the Terrace World  
• Condenser-reeds snapped off in storms fetch high value below; split and fanned, they become the ribs of Isle-umbrellas. In return, Catch-Folk dry algae sheets into wicking pads the terrace folk tuck under heliostat tracks to absorb grit.  
• Pigeon-cones—hollow clay projectiles—are flung upward by torsion slings. They burst on terrace ledges, scattering message ribbons and sample gifts; reply cones drop within the week, weighted to fall true.  
• Some Catch-Folk apprentice upward. They ride maintenance hoists past the fog, learn brick-reading from historians, then return to teach their own children what lies in full sun.

10. Subtle weirdness (medium)  
• The lake slowly rises, though inflow equals evaporation on any spreadsheet. Islanders swear the water itself “remembers” the lost mountain and climbs to reclaim its old contours. A yearly ceremony called Counter-Weighting sees every family jettison a measured cargo of stone off the bowl’s rim, buying time before submergence.  
• Under moonlight the quarry walls gleam with faint lines—phosphorescent bacteria outlining every cut the quarrymen ever made, a negative blueprint of the terraces before they were built.

11. Standing still for a moment  
Choose an isle at random. Lie belly-down on the woven deck; through the gaps you will see the lake glow with drifting algal constellations, your own shadow a dark nebula among them. Overhead, a thread of water breaks loose and falls four kilometres, smashing the surface with a hiss like frying oil. Nets tremble; somewhere a fault drum booms; lantern reeds gutter, then steady. For a breathless instant the whole Catchery tautens, every isle and rope accepting the new weight, and you feel the architecture of two civilizations—a skybound garden and its waterborne echo—pull together, stretch apart, and resettle into balance.

Everything here is caught; everything here is let go. Rope, water, light and persisting memory hold one another in a lattice that floats between the vanished mountain below and the cloud-forged terraces above, patient as dusk, quick as a falling drip.
