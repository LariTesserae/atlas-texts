The Underflume Orchard

Every purge that scours the pocket valley sends a cataract of warm, metal-tainted water punching down a pressure shaft that angles through twelve hundred metres of granite. What emerges—foaming, aerated, forty-one degrees centigrade—spills into a chamber older than the machine valley but once long abandoned: a natural magma blister hollowed out during the mountain’s making, later intercepted by survey drills, finally requisitioned as a sump. The Valve-Kin call it the Underflume; the older maps name it the Orchard, though no one topside remembers why. The shaft’s roar is the Orchard’s dawn-bell. With each flush, another brief day opens on the dark garden below.

1.  Terrain  
•   Shape: an ovoid cavern three hundred metres at its hem and eighty high, floor sloped like a shallow dish. The walls are rippled basalt, glassy in places, blistered in others where ancient gasses once seethed.  
•   Overburden: eighty metres of living rock overhead, plus the plated machinery valley perched still higher. Stalactites here are ferric, not calcareous—slow-grown spears of iron oxide fed by the drip of purge water.  
•   Floor: a quilt of terraces—stone ledges reticulated by rivulets, each filled with silt so fine it flows when stirred. Seen from a height the pattern resembles the cutaway of a lung, alveoli webbed by bronchioles.  
•   Growth rings: every flush leaves a rusty waterline on the cavern wall. Count the stripes and you can date the valley’s commissioning; there are twenty-one.

2.  Water  
The purge arrives as rain that is also a tide. It slaps the Orchard in two stages:

•   Shock surge: four minutes of thunder as the shaft disgorges. Temperature spikes; the cavern fogs copper-orange.  
•   Seep: for the next fifty minutes water trickles from weep-holes, filtered through shale seams. By the end of a cycle the terraces are placid mirror pools. Five minutes before the next surge, sluice-worms sense the pressure climb and furl their sails; a hush falls like held breath.

Chemical profile: pH 6.1, iron 420 ppm, dissolved copper 60 ppm, trace biocides leaked from the upper bearings. Oxygen supersaturated. To surface fauna it would taste like a bloodied battery. Down here it is manna.

3.  Light  
No luminaire has ever been mounted. Illumination arrives piggy-back on purge plasma: microscopic air bubbles shed pale blue Cherenkov flashes as they implode under the warming water. Later, when the pools settle, light comes from the Orchard’s trees.

4.  Vegetation (so-called)  
Only one “tree” species stands here, but it stands everywhere. The Valve-Kin call them bristle-grafts; the Orchard’s own keepers use an older term:

•   Ferrum-pomum lichenoides – orchard ironfruit.  
•   Morphology: a trunk is a cable-thick braid of mold filaments armored in magnetite scales. Height: two to four metres. From the braid radiate thousands of hair-fine rust spines. At each spine tip glows a bead of bioluminescent mucus, colour shifting with oxidation state—new growth shines teal, senescence dims to brick-red.  
•   Reproduction: every tenth purge a branch detaches, floats, and drifts until wormholes in the ceiling drink it upward. Some fragments lodge in the shaft’s slime and eventually hitch a ride back up, re-entering the machinery valley as brown dust on intake filters—the Orchard’s small revenge.

The forest is dense enough that the water surface is hidden under a prickle of rust fibres; walking atop them feels like crossing an inverted sky of stars.

5.  Fauna  
Life larger than a mote returned once the machines above began to bleed.

a)  Sluice-worms  
•   Two-metre ribbons of cartilage and chitin. Along the dorsum unfurl photovoltaic sails that drink the Cherenkov bloom. A magnified view shows pores that sip copper ions; the metal plates their mandibles glossy green.  
•   Behaviour: during surge they anchor to stalactites to avoid shredding. The moment flow slackens they descend, rasping silt for magnetite to reinforce their spinal rods.  
•   Interaction: valve-kin helmets are lacquered with worm secretions; a dried coat hardens like ceramic and sheds oil.

b)  Coin-Eaters  
•   Blind catfish derivative with nickel teeth and a lateral-line organ tuned to disgorged memory slugs. When a slug pings on stone, the fish converge, worrying the steel blank until glyphs erode. In their bellies, human shifts dissolve into silence.  
•   By tradition a Valve-Kin who renounces duty descends the shaft and releases their decade of slugs in a mesh bag. Three days later, the Orchard lies quiet while the Coin-Eaters work—a funeral without witnesses.

c)  Orchard Keepers (para-human)  
They were here first.  
•   Appearance: lean torsos, amphibian pallor, ears wide as petals. The purge thunder left them half-deaf generations ago; cartilage crescents protrude where external cochleae evolved to feel vibration through water.  
•   Garb: mantles woven from shed worm sails, iridescent as dragonfly wings, waterproof and breathable.  
•   Tools: billhooks of slag glass, used to prune ironfruit so branches do not clog the shaft return.  
•   Reproduction: the Keepers bear live young, but no infant cries—their vocal folds stiffened into a low thrumming register that harmonises with the Reverberants funnelled down the shaft.  
•   Society: five familial shoals, each claiming a quadrant of terraces, meet at the basin’s centre every twenty cycles to trade seed-crystals and stories rendered as tactile knots in worm-gut lace. Sound is unreliable; touch endures.

6.  Atmosphere & Climate  
Temperature swings between 12 °C (just before surge) and 43 °C (peak flood). Humidity is absolute; every breath is a sip. Oxygen spikes after each flush, fuelling a half-hour frenzy among spores and gill-breathers, then tapers as molds settle to maintenance metabolism. There is no wind, only convection spirals shimmering above hot pools like ghosts wringing their hands.

7.  Built & Tech  
The only overt engineering is the Spill-Column: a ribbed tower of bronze plates driven like a spear through the cavern roof, installed by the original station architects to slow the purge torrent. Time and ironfruit roots have turned the column into a living pagoda—mossy plateaus where Keepers anchor nurseries and hang carved memory-pods. Each pod is an emptied steel slug etched with braille chronicles. When a pod finally corrodes through, its story plummets into the main pool, becomes food for Coin-Eaters, and is gone: an archive designed to self-compost.

8.  Dynamics  
A cycle inside the Underflume feels like breathing after sprinting: hurt, hush, hurt again. But the diaphragm is the mountain itself.

•   Minute 0–4: Impact. Water hammer booms; mist fluoresces; worms furl. Any stray Keeper beneath the column dives or is flensed.  
•   Minute 5–20: Rebound. Sound drops from artillery to waterfall. Ironfruit hairs rise erect, wicking trace metals. Keepers wade knee-deep, pruning storm-snapped branches before they sink and foul the terraces.  
•   Minute 21–50: Feast. Oxygen maximum. Worm sails unfurl; juvenile fish break surface to bite the glow off fruit tips. Keepers sing—low polyharmonics that steer spawn toward quieter eddies.  
•   Minute 51–55: Premonition. Reverberants climb the shaft first, a seismic shiver telegraphing the next purge. Sails furl; songs cease. The Orchard crouches.  
•   Repeat.

9.  Rules of the Orchard  
•   Metal is food.  
•   Water arrives and departs on the mountain’s twin heartbeats; to linger mid-pool after the shiver is to die.  
•   Light cannot be hoarded—every glow is a lure for something hungry.  
•   Sound belongs to the stone; borrow it sparingly.  
•   That which is remembered too long poisons the water—hence the Coin-Eaters.

10.  Signs for the First-Time Visitor  
1.  Your boots hiss white with corrosion within ten strides—ironfruit exude weak acid to tenderise fallen ore.  
2.  The ceiling flexes a breath before the shaft opens: watch for dust rings dancing on your sleeve.  
3.  A stitch of violet gleam skims the water, gone—sometimes a Valve-Kin maintenance drone overshoots a weld and tumbles all the way down, its last IR flicker reciting error codes until floodwater drowns it.  
4.  From somewhere, felt through bone not ear, a chord resolves; the same note is singing far above in the machinery valley. The Reverberants are through-lines between places, an arterial hum knitting iron womb and iron grave.

Connection to the Pocket Valley Above

•   Water: every droplet that beads on the catwalks becomes Orchard tide. The valley’s cleanliness is the Orchard’s fertility; biocides dilute to tonics by the time they reach the terraces.  
•   Memory: steel slugs bled from Valve-Kin temples tumble eventually into catfish maws. What the Kin refuse to dream, the Orchard digests, cycles, and forgets for them.  
•   Trade: lacquer harvested from sluice-worms is winched up in woven sacks once a quarter; in return, the Kin send down coils of piano-wire, feedstock for Keeper billhooks and for the Orchard’s quiet obsession—sculpting wind-harps that will never play in this windless dark but tremble, silently, in sympathy with the valley’s storms.  
•   Kinship: both castes have flensed themselves of useless senses—hearing above, sight below—and grown other instruments for the work the mountain demands.  
•   Faith: when a Reverberant note holds steady from valley to Orchard for seven consecutive cycles, Keepers and Kin alike strike copper gongs (one in vacuum, one underwater) to mark the coincidence. No scripture explains why; none is needed. The sound is the proof.

What Lives Here, Finally  

A garden that eats iron and sweats light.  
Worms that weave ceramic skins.  
Fish that erase the past so the present can breathe.  
People who prune storms into lullabies.  
And, threading it all, a music that once was noise, then labour, and is now—maybe—a kind of prayer shared between mouths that will never meet.

Stay long enough beside the floodgate and you will feel the two places braid: the valley sharpening you to a tool, the Orchard blunting you back to a seed. Between them runs a river disguised as machinery, disguised again as rain. To follow it is to learn the mountain’s only article of faith: that what is made must be unmade, and what is unmade will find new root in the dark, humming with borrowed stars.
