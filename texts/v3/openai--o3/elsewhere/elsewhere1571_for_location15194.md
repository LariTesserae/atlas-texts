The Alkaline Step

The river that swallows the Enge dead does not end where they think it does.  
It sinks, yes—slipping beneath the basalt in a maze of fissures too tight for colossi to navigate—  
but ten days later and three thousand vertical feet higher it resurfaces, scalding, in the broken heart of a plateau no basin-dweller has ever seen.

1. Where the water reappears

Picture a cracked dinner plate forty miles across, set askew atop a stack of older plates.  
The glaze is chalk, the fractures are gorges, and from a distance the whole expanse looks bleached and crazed, as if it once spent a century in an oven slightly too hot for comfort.  
Steam stencils every fissure, but the vapor makes no sound; it only trembles on the verge of visibility, blurring the horizon the way heat blurs desert asphalt.

At the plate’s deepest crack the river arrives reborn as brine,  rain-warm and caustic enough to score glass.  
Each surge deposits another film of salt on the canyon walls, so the gorge gleams like a mouthful of polished teeth.  
Water never lingers.  It hisses, flashes to mist, and drifts upward into a sky as flawlessly clear as the basin’s is sealed.

2. Light, sound, and weather

• Light: direct, pitiless.  There is no lid of cloud, only one blue tier after another until the air thins into violet.  
• Sound: almost none.  Wind slides through the canyons in laminar sheets, too even to whistle.  A dropped pebble can be tracked by ear for twelve, thirteen, fourteen quiet seconds before the eventual, tiny click.  
• Weather: a single season—arid noon—relieved only by brief, surgical eclipses when a kite-shoal, translucent even here, swings over the plateau on its long slow circuit between water sources.

Someone who has just climbed out of the basin thinks the Step is trying to kill sense after sense in sequence: first the ears (nothing to hear), then the nose (salt scorches it numb), then the skin (cracked in minutes), finally the eyes (too much ultraviolet).  Most turn back before the first evening.

3. Stone, soil, and the thing that pretends to be soil

Basalt never reached this height; what lies underfoot is a froth of carbonate rock seeded with gypsum prisms the length of femurs.  
Between the crystals flows a granular powder that looks like flour until you touch it—then it pulls away in long elastic bands that suddenly snap, clouding the air with static sparks.  The locals call the stuff flay-dust.  A cuff at the wrist keeps it out of sleeves; a mistake leaves the skin beneath welted and weeping brine for days.

No growth breaks the surface.  Even the microbial reeds of the valley cannot root in the Step’s dryness.  But the land moves.  Every hour or so an area the size of a cottage subsides by the thickness of a thumbnail, the silent, steady settling of a landscape exhausting what little air still clings inside its pores.

4. Inhabitants—The Sodality of White Mark

If the Enge are compact and damp, the White Mark are lengthened, desiccated, dangerously still.

• Height and build: heads brushing seven feet, torsos narrow as fence railing.  Knees hyperextend backward; a person at rest looks half-folded, crane-like, ready to spring but never bothering.  
• Skin: paper-matte.  A lattice of pale fissures opens whenever muscles shift, closes again when they go slack.  Salt crystallizes in the cracks, so everyone perpetually looks dusted with frost.  
• Ears: broad and faun-shaped, mobile enough to furl entirely flat.  Total silence unnerves them; they manufacture faint noise by rubbing thumb against ring-finger in steady syncopation, a private metronome just at the edge of perception.  
• Breath: nasal only.  Mouths stay sealed behind vellum masks soaked in a vinegar infusion that trades moisture for dust.

5. Speech and record

Nothing airborne can safely carry words across the Step; too dry, too thin.  So the Sodality talks in two other currencies—shadow and salt.

a) Shadow-script.  Each person’s mask is stitched with narrow vertical slits.  At noon the slits project a barcode of light beams down the wearer’s torso.  By rolling shoulders and neck the pattern can be modulated into lexemes recognizable to any literate onlooker.  Conversations look like men and women performing very slow, spare dances with the sun as orchestra.

b) Salt-lattice.  On sandstone tablets they trickle a thin stream of brine that flash-dries into raised white lines.  Complex narratives emerge like frost-flowers, read by fingertip even on moonless nights.  When a tale is no longer useful, the author simply brushes the salt back into a pouch for reuse.

6. Infrastructure

• Resonance pits.  The plateau’s constant settling exudes infrasonic pings.  Where two fault lines cross, the Sodality sinks a narrow shaft.  Walls hum.  Pitch drift maps movement beneath.  A city’s safety is just a matter of listening.  
• Vapor snare.  Kite-shoals, thirsting, exhale minute fog when they coast low.  The Sodality stakes arrays of needle-fine glass spines angled to condense that exhalation.  Each good pass yields a thimbleful of water—just enough.  
• Bone-bridge.  There are no trees, no leather beasts, so long constructions rely on the only straight members readily available: the hollow tibiae of step-raptors that hunt the heat-vents at dusk.  Over years a bridge whitens with sun until it is indistinguishable from the salt flat it spans.

7. Economy and debt

Trade with the Enge exists, though neither side has met the other in living memory.

What leaves the basin:

• Jet knuckles—carbon pure enough to buffer acids  
• Reed tiles—tough, alkaline calories valued here as both food and mild antiseptic  
• Leather rope—still oily, still smelling of wet life

What returns:

• Dry silence pressed into salt-cakes.  A cake the size of a fist, broken open in the basin, absorbs noise within arm’s length for an hour: priceless for surgeons and midwives who need to hear a heartbeat unshadowed by thunder.  
• Sunstone lenses—thin gypsums polished ice-clear.  When fixed to an obsidian shard and inverted over a fungal lamp, they turn the lamp’s glow into a tight plane capable of etching basalt.  The Enge etch maps now, spirals nested within spirals, their first durable history.  
• Dust masks woven from flay-dust threads: the only cloth that stays dry in the valley, issued to newborns destined for the kite-aloft branch so their lungs never taste the roar.

No caravan crosses the vertical miles.  Goods move entirely by kite-shoal.  Each evening on the Step, traders lace parcels into a kite’s tassels and slice a thin line across their own palm—the iron smell draws the creature downward, heavier now than air, eager for the bloody humidity of the world below.

8. Cosmology in two mirrors

The Enge say: The world is full; add, never replace.  
The White Mark answer: The world is hollow; pour, never keep.

Where a basin child sees music everywhere and water forever returning, a plateau novice sees nothing but loss—brine hissing into vacancy, rock collapsing grain by grain, voices erased by width of sky.  Both peoples are right, and together they keep the planet’s ledgers balanced: surplus wet transferred upward as vapor, surplus quiet returned downward pressed into cakes.

9. Ritual crossings

Once in a generation, when the overcast thins to gauze, a single Enge adolescent in leather mask climbs a vent-throat staircase hewn for that purpose.  A White Mark peer meets her at the lip of the crack.  They exchange two things:

• A basin resonance shard: obsidian, still ringing faintly with the last drum message recorded during ascent.  
• A step shadow-sigil: a tablet of salt etched with the current map of subsidence, a future ghost of where canyons will be.

They retreat unchanged in number but not in knowledge.  Each returns carrying a silence the basin lacks and a note the Step can suddenly hear if it tries hard enough.

10. Death and incorporation

Sodality corpses are posed standing, feet buried ankle-deep in flay-dust.  Over months the dust wicks every fluid until only a pillar of articulated bone remains, light enough that a mild wind might topple it.  Families do not mourn the fall; they gather each piece, grind it fine, and seed it into the vapor snares.  Bone remembers water; the next kite pass condenses twice as heavy.

11. Lexicon for the traveler

Bitter-blink: the involuntary eye-flutter caused by horizon glare.  
Echo-star: a mote of drifting salt that catches sunlight just long enough to mimic a satellite.  
Cup-hour: the single heartbeat at dawn when river-steam first fogs the canyons; locals stop whatever they are doing, raise both hands, and pretend to drink.

12. Last meeting of the places

Some night—scholars differ on when—the kites will err, diving too low, too heavy with freight.  They will never rise again.  The basin will inherit sudden quiet; the Step, sudden rain.  For a time each land will taste the other’s law in the flesh.

The Enge say the sky then will drop enough silence to hear a single tear strike basalt.  
The White Mark say the ground then will lift enough steam to give every shadow a voice.  
No one knows which prophecy is the truer; perhaps fullness and hollowness will annul one another, or perhaps they will at last speak the same sentence, carried alternately by roar and hush all around a planet that, like any vessel, needs both weight and room to keep from shattering.
