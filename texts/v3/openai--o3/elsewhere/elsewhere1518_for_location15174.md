The Fumeward Stair  

The water never truly leaves the basin.  It rises with every hiss of flash-steam, threads the hanging cloud, escapes through hairline vents under the rafters, and drifts east as a band of bruised-orange vapour the monks call the Pilgrim Plume.  By dawn it has climbed seven hundred metres and a dozen kilometres, thinned to a veiled contrail, and come to ground against the oldest side of the caldera wall.  There, clinging to a volcanic escarpment too steep for soil, the plume cools and becomes a different place—a place that exists only because the engine below insists on breathing.

1. First View  

Imagine a staircase the width of a road, hammered sheet-iron steps cantilevered straight out of cliff-face, switch-backing upward until the mountain edge files itself into cloud.  There is no hand-rail, only tension cables that sing in the wind.  From a distance the whole contraption could be mistaken for an abandoned funicular track, but stand upon a tread and you feel the faint pulse of pumps hidden in the risers.  Warm condensate climbs the hollow stringers, loses heat to the air, and bleeds from pinholes the builders drilled on purpose.  Drop by drop the staircase exudes a permanent mist—enough to cloak the iron in a furry rind of frost despite the mild season.

2. Terrain & Structure  

• The Cliff—columnar andesite, jointed like a cathedral organ.  Basalt slabs quarried from the basin’s own floor were once hauled up here as counter-weights; now they lie mortared into terraces where the Stair pauses on ledges just wide enough for a hut or two.  

• The Treads—five centimetres thick, punched with chevron drainage slots.  Every fifth tread is a sump; every hundredth is a hatch that swings up to reveal a man-sized pipe filled with tepid water forever climbing.  These are the “arteries” that relieve the basin below—one more route for excess heat and mass to flee before the cloud collapses.  

• The Brackets—welded gussets studded directly into the cliff.  Over decades they have sprouted rime stalactites that grow downward in the exact inverse of the basin’s salt wafers.  What salt forgets, ice remembers.  The Archivists come here twice a year to read the season’s story in melt-layer stratigraphy, though the trip costs them a week of aching joints.  

3. Weather  

Sun rarely reaches the Stair; instead gusts roar out of the eastern flats, ram the escarpment, and recoil upward in abrupt updrafts that locals call “fumewards.”  Caught in these, the basin’s condensate accelerates like smoke up an inverted chimney.  Temperature swings twenty degrees in as many minutes.  The only constant is the sound: wind churning through the open stringers, moaning three octaves below the engine’s heartbeat—a drone so deep it feels less like noise than a forceps holding the cliff in place.

4. Illumination  

Day: blue milk.  Night: star scatter, plus an auroral ribbon of green where bioluminescent plankton (yes, plankton—invisible in vapour, incandescent when chilled) crash land on the upper flights.  The monks’ cousins here have affixed mirrors angled to catch that eerie ribbon and send it flickering into work-shelters like a slow Morse.

5. Inhabitants  

A. The Step-Singers  
   They are steeplejacks by trade, altitudinists by calling.  Voices trained to throw pitch across gale roar, they tune themselves against the basin engine they cannot hear directly.  Instead they listen for the Echo’s echo—minute pressure ripples that ride the plume and arrive half a measure late.  If the beat drifts, they hammer the brackets with mallets the size of dinner bells.  The resulting clang travels back down the water columns, translating into turbulence the monks read as warning.  Communication by percussion: code carried in cavitation.

   • Dress—yak-wool cloaks lacquered with shellac to shed ice, goggles cut from stained gauge-glass (a gift-trade with the pump-hall vitriers).  
   • Taboo—no singing while ascending; breath is for climbing.  Descent songs, however, can last the whole vertical kilometre and are said to untangle muscle knots better than any salve.  

B. The Freeze-Tenders  
   Three extended families living in bolted timber pods sunk into the cliff like swallow nests.  They maintain the “rime galleries”—ice-stone hybrids sculpted wherever runoff collects and freezes, then overgrown by mineral spray from the Pilgrim Plume.  Each gallery is a book of accidental bas-relief: sheet-music for those who know how to read subsidence lines and bubble-count porosity.  Tenders chip samples, melt them, and test for trace lubricants.  Too much fern-seed oil in the water means the monks below are over-greasing; too little foretells seizure.  Thus, by flavour of meltwater alone, the Freeze-Tenders audit maintenance they will never see.

C. The Rungfish  
   Not fish, not alive, but persistent.  Once a week the Tenders pour sterile brine down inspection hatches to flush biofilms.  Where salt meets iron at the right temperature, nodules detach and slide—a centimetre per hour—seeking colder zones.  Wind flips them, gravity rights them.  They leave iron-oxide scales on the treads, markings so similar to footprints that more than one green Step-Singer has panicked, thinking the Stair walked in the night.

6. Daily Cycle  

05:00  Plume strikes cliff at its warmest.  Mist envelops the first four hundred steps.  
07:30  Mandatory de-ice round: chisels ring like morning bells.  
12:00  Sun-penetration window (47 minutes on a clear day).  Solar slits cut across the flights; anything organic in the beams fluoresces faint chartreuse.  Tenders spread drying nets for their cloaks.  
17:00  Descent Chorus.  Forty throats, one chord, harmonics falling into the basin’s range.  The Echo often answers with a reversed fragment that flutters snow from the upper bracing.  
23:00  Stillpoint.  Wind drops, temperature plummets.  Condensate freezes in motion, forming banners of glassy fringe that crack at first daylight.

7. Materials Economy  

Upward cargo:  
• Burlap tubes of activated charcoal (for the Stair’s breath-filters)  
• Copper-sulfate beads (vine inoculant from below, repurposed here as anti-lichen shot)  
• Knotweed canes soaked in fern-seed oil (torch-kindling that ignites even in sleet)  

Downward cargo:  
• Icicled bundles of cliff moss, distilled into antiseptic wash for Sling Children’s foot-cuts  
• Calved rime slabs whose bubble counts predict boiler efficiency two days hence  
• Song-scrolls: not paper, but rolls of thin ice etched with phonograph grooves.  Projectionists in the basin spin them before they melt, releasing the Step-Singers’ notes back into the engine hall so the Echo hears its own future.

8. Beliefs & Superstitions  

The Stair folk tell a mirror myth: should the engine ever fall silent, the cliff will fold outward like a ruptured lung and the Stair will peel free, rising on a column of runaway steam until it ladders the moon.  Therefore they, too, cannot abide a missed beat.  Their devotion is not to water’s motion but to water’s escape; they are the engine’s relief valve made human.

They keep no shrines—nothing stands long enough in the blast-frozen wind—but each first tread of a shift receives a fingertip of warm condensate, smeared sun-wise, so the iron “remembers the basin’s temperature.”

9. Encounters  

Arrive from below and you will be sweating; the air will claw the heat out of you like a jealous sibling.  A Freeze-Tender will press a hoarfrost cup to your lips: drink or your teeth will crack on the next breath.  Expect three questions, uncannily similar yet inverted from the basin’s catechism:

1. Have you brought fire? (If yes, it must be banked; open flame shards the wind.)  
2. Can you hold your heart-beat? (They time how long you manage; breath control matters up here.)  
3. Do you understand that stillness is worship? (Move unnecessarily and you waste warmth the Stair cannot spare.)

10. Night Phenomena: The Reverse Rain  

Once every twenty-six hours—exactly when the basin throttles its boilers—the plume thins, pressure eases, and the iron arteries seize.  Water inside the Stair pauses, supercools, and then, impossibly, climbs back downward as vapor drawn home by the engine’s inhalation.  Frost sublimates, mist becomes clear air, and for twelve heartbeats the cliff is dry enough to creak.  In that hush the Step-Singers hear the Echo not as backwards thunder, but as a child’s voice humming a lullaby no one has taught it.  They do not mention this to the monks; some truths are gifts that evaporate if traded.

11. What Connects  

• Heat: stolen from the engine, given to the sky, returned as potential energy in every supercooled drop.  
• Sound: hammered in iron, travelled in water, echoed in cloud, answered in ice.  
• Faith: two sects, one of motion, one of release, both convinced the world stays whole only because they keep doing their small, precise violences to water.  

Stand on the 700th step.  Look down-slope: the basin’s roof-cloud flashes with sheet lightning reflected off oily canals.  Look up-slope: the staircase dwindles into nothing, but above that nothing hangs a faint exhalation—the next plume already forming, already on its way.  Between those two unseeable machines of circulation you are a bead in a rosary, passed from thumb to thumb in an endless prayer that water remain restless.

That is the Fumeward Stair:  
A ladder of frozen breath, an organ pipe for wind, a congregation that sings so the engine may sigh, and a place where every droplet leaving the basin is interrogated—Are you cool yet?  Are you light enough to rise?—before being released to climb again.
