THE GLASS DUNES OF HESPERON  

First you follow the water that the Basin refuses to keep.  
It slips under the peat in worm-quiet arteries, finds the buried dikes that warmed it, and runs downslope for four days through limestone, dissolving whole corridors as it goes.  
At dawn on the fifth day the current reaches daylight again, bursting from a cliff face in a sheet so thin it is more veil than fall.  
Wind catches the spray and combs it east.  Every droplet dries in flight, leaving its freight of silica, lime, and feldspar to sift down in powder-fine snow.  
Centuries of that snow have drifted into a sea of glittering dunes no rain has ever wet—the Glass Dunes of Hesperon, horizon to horizon, bright enough to blind a visitor inside three minutes.  

TERRAIN  
Imagine waves frozen at the instant they crest, translucence piled on translucence.  Each dune is a laminate of pale green bottle-glass over frosted quartz and milky gypsum; sunlight refracts inward, ricochets, and bleeds out again as opaline glow after dark.  The sand is too sharp for bare skin—granules break along knife edges—yet altogether too light to stay still.  Gusts from the western escarpment lift whole ridgelines, carry them three, five, ten kilometres, and set them down in new geography by morning.  A traveller ties rope to the ankle of the one ahead to keep from being separated by a moving hill.  

RIVERS OF AIR  
The Basin’s air is a closed kiln; Hesperon’s is a flume.  
At noon the ground flashes to 64 °C; the super-heated boundary layer whooshes skyward in thermals the width of cities, dragging anything lighter than a moth along.  As the columns rise they twist, knot, unknot again—slow bright corkscrews visible for two hundred kilometres.  The locals name them by the timbre they lend to the windchimes hung over their vents: Brass, Agate, Banshee, Old Father Glass.  

LIGHT  
Where the Basin hoards darkness, Hesperon manufactures surplus light.  Even midnight is silver-dull, because fresh-blown grains jostle and spark, releasing the static they harvested all day.  A person walking after sundown raises a glow-wake, footsteps flickering blue-white like struck flints.  Storms are ultraviolet: whole dunes lift, collide, discharge in sheet lightning that never reaches a cloud.  

SOUND  
Nothing absorbs acoustics.  Voices travel kilometres; a sigh can return as an echo rich enough to be mistaken for another speaker.  Entire conversations occur between people who never meet, stitched through the night by ricochet and best guess.  The famous “Desert choir” is two singers, forty kilometres apart, timing echoes until their harmonics overlay in cathedral chords.  

VEGETATION  
True roots cannot grip the mobile ground, so life here inks itself onto what the wind refuses to steal.  
1. Skeg-Briar.  Shrubs that grow only around fulgurites—the glassy tunnels left by lightning strikes.  Their roots are coated in a glaze that trombone-slides deeper each hot season, forever chasing the cooling tip of the glass as it sinks toward bedrock.  
2. Mirrorage.  A crustose lichen, silver as solder.  It spreads on the windward faces of standing stones, reflecting 98 % of incident light and holding surface temperature just cold enough for dew.  Each dawn thousands of pin-size wells appear in the thallus, fill, then vanish at sunrise.  
3. Step-Through Orchids (escaped).  Every century a Basin flood rafts mats of orchids down-slope; a few reach the Dunes still alive.  Here they form rootless kites, green in the ultraviolet, drinking from high fog for one frantic week before the sun vitrifies them into statues brittle as spun sugar.  

FAUNA  
Life in Hesperon is less abundant than in the Basin, yet exaggerated, like sketches in hard ink.  
• Shard-Adders.  Transparent scales, internal organs pigmented black against the glare.  When threatened they coil and grind belly scutes across glass, producing a scream-note audible for two kilometres.  The sound pits the fangs of most predators; only Sand-Ibises dare swallow them.  
• Sand-Ibises.  Birds tall as a woman’s shoulder, bones honeycombed with cooling ducts.  They walk against the wind, trawling beaks through drifting dunes to sift for seed, glass shrimp, or the occasional adder.  Feathers curve into parabolic vanes that vent heat upward in visible mirage.  At dusk each flock turns as one toward the Basin horizon and raises a call like a single low bell.  
• Glass Shrimp.  Crustaceans the size of a thumb that swim through aerated sand as if it were water, using static discharge to stun nematodes.  Their moulted exoskeletons form the only stable “soil” here and are colonised by the lichen nursery generations later.  
• Echo-Mice.  Tiny mammal descendants of the Basin’s capybara line, shrunk and bleached.  They dig vertical warrens that collapse nightly and must be re-dug each dawn—an architecture of surrender rather than resistance.  Navigation is by continuous squeak, mapped against the moving echo-profile of the nearest dune face.  

HUMANS—THE GLASS-KEEPERS  
Few remain, yet the ones who do have rewritten themselves against the glare.  
Appearance: skin painted with a slurry of mirrored lichen; goggles of slice-glass smeared in obsidian tar; water-veils woven from spider-silk shipped at ruinous cost from the Basin.  
Architecture: nothing stays put, so they build kites, not houses—huge crescent wings anchored by ballast nets.  When a dune migrates, they slack one tether and tighten another, letting whole hamlets sail a slow gyre around fixed fulgurite groves.  
Economy: they harvest fulgurite cores, which carry within them the entire lightning stroke as a hollow thread.  Cores over three metres long are prized by Basin alchemists; ground to powder, they seed the feather-vines to coax new colour phases.  In exchange, the Keepers trade for fungal loaves sealed in wax and rolls of capybara-hide tubing to repair kite spars.  

TIME AND CHANGE  
Where the Basin measures time in tree-rings, Hesperon measures it in the march-rate of dunes—metres per day, kilometres per season, whole constellations erased from the ground and redrawn.  A map is an autobiography good for one telling only.  

BRIDGES BETWEEN SHADOW AND GLARE  
• The Veil Swifts: they skim Basin mosquitoes all wet season, then let the thermals sling them here in a single dawn.  They arrive coated in dew, drink nothing for the entire dry sojourn, and depart bearing lichen spores back to the trees that once mothered them.  
• Subterranean Pulse: that ninety-second heart-beat the Basin scholars trace in the peat?  The same signal is felt here in the ground as a very slow tremor.  Glass-Keepers plant listening rods; when the pulse stutters they know a new vent has opened uphill, and soon a fresh cataract of silica will feed the dunes.  
• Third Heat Myth: both peoples fear the open sky, but inversely.  Basin children dread starlight; Hesperon children dread the day no wind rises, leaving dunes fixed, mirror-still, turning air into a kiln with nowhere to run.  In each place the corrective ritual is the same: plant something that climbs and shades—orchid in the Basin, kite-shadow here—an old symmetry remembered even where meanings have traded faces.  

IF YOU STAND STILL  
…in the Basin, life climbs you.  
…in Hesperon, light sculpts you.  After an hour your shadow cuts a trough; after a day the wind redeposits a low parapet at your back; after a week even your silence is etched by echo, your outline recorded in dune-song and the blown-glass scrim that drifts until some future night, when another traveller finds a negative of a human where no human any longer stands.  

The world is the sum of what flows between its extremes—the steam that rises from peat and becomes silica snow, the swifts that thread dusk to blaze, the stories that change colour crossing the divide.  Darkness moves outward as spores; brightness moves inward as powdered glass; and somewhere in the long exchange two very different peoples keep the current running, each guarding the other’s opposite half of the sky.
