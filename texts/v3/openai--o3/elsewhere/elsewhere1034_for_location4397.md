Boneglass Steppe  

Follow the steam columns that rise from the newborn forest’s fissures.  
Ride them in thought the way the sky-reefers do in flesh—upward, outward, cooling as you climb.  
They merge, become a river of invisible heat that pours across seventy kilometers of cloud until it finds a place too high, too cold, too empty to keep rising.  
There, the breath of the lowland condenses, snows, and dies.  
What the forest exhales, the steppe inhales, and that long exchange writes two countries on the same slate.

1. First Sight  
The plateau is a slab of frost-scoured basalt lifted three thousand meters above the tide. Sunlight lands here undiluted; nothing intervenes, not leaf, not mist, not mercy. From a distance the ground looks wet, but it is only glass—sheets of pale green obsidian birthed when lightning met ashfall in an older eruption. The wind has since sanded every pane to a matte shimmer, so the whole tableland glows like a glacier made of bottled dusk.

2. Air and Weather  
• Density: a third thinner than at sea level; a deep breath hisses like torn silk in the throat.  
• Currents: daytime updrafts drawn from the forest below, nightly katabatic slides of cold that descend in return. The two pulses keep time like twin hearts far apart but beating for the same body.  
• Precipitation: almost none. Moisture arrives as hoarfrost filigree on dawn’s first shadow and sublimates by mid-morning, leaving lacework salt crystals that crunch underfoot. Rain is a rumor, carried upslope in vapor stories and never proven.

3. Light, Unbroken  
Here daylight owns the ground whole. Colors bleach, edges harden, and every being evolves a relationship with glare. Some hide beneath mirrored skins, some tunnel, some treat shadow as currency. At night the stars are surgical—sharp, cold, innumerable—and the ground answers: glass shards catch starlight, scatter it sideways, making it possible to read by constellations reflected at your boots.

4. The Ground That Rings  
Step anywhere and listen. The stone is drummy, chattering in octave leaps. Centuries of frost-wedging have hollowed the upper crust into chambers; tramp hard enough and you can play it like an estranged marimba. The locals call this the Subtle Echo: a slow, planetary percussion that retells each footfall for minutes after the foot has gone.

5. Fauna, Sparse but Singular  
a. Glasshoof Elan  
   • Looks: triple-jointed ungulates the height of a soldier, hooves sheathed in translucent quartz.  
   • Doing: They step with such measured force that the ringing ground builds sympathetic tones; predators can hear an approaching herd half an hour away and usually think better of it.  
b. Prism Mites  
   • Looks: near invisible save for a prismatic sparkle when they pivot.  
   • Doing: Graze on wind-blown minerals. In swarms they sculpt faint rainbows around anything that moves, a living aura mistaken for sainthood by the credulous.  
c. Shiverwolves  
   • Looks: thread-thin canids whose fur is actually vibrating glass whiskers.  
   • Doing: Hunt by resonance—howling sets the ground humming; echoes outline prey. A kill is a duet in frequencies humans can feel in their teeth.  
d. Updraft Kites  
   • Looks: barrel-bodied birds with wings like sheer sails, feathers studded with mica.  
   • Doing: Migrate daily—up on the forest’s warm sigh, down on the plateau’s cold exhale—ferrying spores, rumors, and the occasional hitchhiking bone-tool primate clinging under a wing joint.

6. Flora, or What Passes for It  
• Frost-Ink Lichens: write black veining across glass, etching nutrients into a script only roots can read.  
• Whisper Reeds: the plateau’s only true plant, growing in sunken cracks where meltwater lingers an hour a day. Hollow, jointed, they catch the permanent wind and turn it into a breathy, atonal choir—an upside-down answer to the forest’s drum language.  
• Dreamvaults: clusters of fist-sized geodes stuffed with dormant moss from the lowlands. When cracked (by heat, by curiosity), each geode exhales a green cloud that tries for a day to build a forest in miniature before desiccation ends the attempt. The plateau is littered with these failed rehearsals.

7. Silence as Ruler  
Noise happens, then travels away forever. Nothing here absorbs; the ground reflects, the air thins, the horizon steals. A shout becomes a comet tail of syllables sliding miles before fading. Creatures evolved to speak briefly, or in infrasonics that skip like stones over distance.

8. Laws and Oddities  
• Glass remembers: scratch it at sunrise and the mark heals by sunset, the surface flowing microns a day under thermal stress.  
• Shadow wells: at noon, anything taller than a hand throws no shade—light scatters off glass in all directions, erasing directionality. Travelers have been known to lose orientation standing beside their own boots.  
• Frostburn: metal objects left out overnight acquire a skin of rime that flash-vapourizes when touched, blistering flesh with cold steam. The local word for apology literally translates to “I kept it in cloth.”

9. The Folk Known as Ring-Counters  
Species: uncertain—eyes veiled, mouths bound in silk filters, skin painted with powdered obsidian to thwart glare.  
Life: They walk by night, mapping the plateau through the Subtle Echo: dropping pebbles, timing the returning note, and inscribing the interval on spiral tablets of lead. Over years the spiral becomes a song of distances; over generations, a family may inherit a melody that is also a map.  
Trade: Once each decade they descend the wind road to the newborn forest, bringing lead tablets heavy with mapped music. In exchange they carry up baskets of violet lantern fungus. On the plateau, the fungus dies within a week but glows furiously while it fades, long enough for the Ring-Counters to trace new survey lines in purple fire. Thus the forest writes its autobiography across the steppe, page by evaporating page.

10. The Long Bond  
Nothing walks the Boneglass Steppe that does not feel, faintly, the pulse of its lowland sibling. Heat rises, cold descends; spores ride, light answers; glass echoes wood and wood returns the favor in percussion. The two countries never meet, yet they converse without cease, a dialogue paced by convection and gravity.  
• The sky-reefers know the route: they spawn in the warm updrafts, pupate in the chill, and tumble back as carpets of polyps that seed the forest anew.  
• Root-lurker segments occasionally wander the tunnels upward, find nothing to hear in the thin soil, and fossilize standing—vertical fossils that serve as cairns for wanderers on featureless nights.  
• A Ring-Counter child once followed a convoy of Updraft Kites down into the forest and returned years later speaking in obsidian clicks, proving to both peoples that stories, like waters, run both ways.

11. Standing in the Middle  
Imagine squatting on a dune of ground glass chilled to knife edge, the Milky Way so bright it throws a double shadow. Your heartbeat booms underfoot, travels outward, and comes back faint but recognizable, as though the land is reminding you of your own meter. Breathing hurts: every inhale scours, every exhale smokes. Somewhere to the east a herd of Glasshoof Elan is walking in seven-four time, their synchronized steps ringing chords fit for cathedral organs.  
You cup a lantern fungus brought for the occasion; in this dry air it is living its whole life in one blaze. The violet makes a hole in the darkness large enough to see your breath frost, your pulse glow faintly through skin. For a moment the newborn forest and the Boneglass Steppe occupy the same thought—the forest’s damp, thunderous syllable, the steppe’s crystalline vowel—and you, flickering between them, are the brief conjunction.

12. Last Word, Shared  
The bone-tool primates taught the Ring-Counters a proverb in mineral clicks; the Ring-Counters answered by scoring it into lead. Translated twice, it reads:  
“Every silence remembers the storm it came from.”
