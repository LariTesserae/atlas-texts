The Underwell Sea  

You find it by following the plateau’s lost water—not the cataracts that leap the cliff face and vanish in wind, but the shy percentage that soaks straight down.  It seeps through basalt joints, gathers heat from magma-veins, and re-emerges two kilometres below Vord Kal in a vaulted dark whose roof you will never see unaided.  Down here the land you stood on is only a distant percussion: a million simultaneous drips that strike and strike and never miss.

1. Shape of the hollow  
•  Boundaries: a single chamber the size of a small ocean, its ceiling lost in haze, its floor an irregular basin half filled by warm brine.  The chamber walls billow like frozen thunderheads of limestone and quartz; the colour is mother-of-pearl where water still licks it, soot-black where geothermal vents have smoked it.  
•  The Sea: thirty to ninety metres deep, 270 kilometres across, circular but for a bite missing in the southwest where the ceiling collapsed millennia ago, opening an avenue to the deeper mantle vents.  The water is 9 % saline, 0.1 % iron, and hot enough to fog your lenses—forty-three degrees at the surface, fifty-nine at depth.  
•  Islands: none in the ordinary sense.  Instead the basin bristles with floes of calcite foam, a rock-light lattice precipitated when super-saturated drip meets hot brine.  Locals call them raftstones.  They drift, fuse, fracture, and drift again, forever rearranging the map.

2. Light and weather  
Sunlight never reaches here.  Illumination comes from three sources:  
1. Riftflare—blue–green sheets of chemoluminescence where hydrogen vents meet brine.  
2. Beadworms—centimetre tubes that rise nightly from the mud and pulse yellow like terrestrial fireflies.  
3. Civic glass—slow-burn arc globes strung from chain-stalactites over the denser settlements.  
“Night” and “day” mean nothing.  Instead people speak of the Bright (when the vents are roiling) and the Drowse (when they slacken).  Air is a constant tropical mist smelling of copper, salt, and something sweet—an ester produced by plankton blooms too deep to see.

3. Base ecology  
Photosynthesis is impossible; chemosynthesis reigns.  
•  Vent-pasture: pink bacterial mats that feast on sulfides and knit themselves, blanket-like, across the sea floor.  
•  Milkglass algae: translucent sheets that float just under the surface and store hydrogen in shaker-bead vacuoles, making them rise like balloons if detached.  
•  Drip-cones: fist-sized colonies that grow directly under major leaks, hardening into ivory trumpets whose open mouths catch every drop.  When a cone fills it keens—a pure F-sharp—and bursts, scattering spores of lime-white dust that sparkle as they fall.

4. Fauna—buoyant, heat-loving, often transparent  
•  Skimmers: manta-shaped fish with wings of living cartilage, thirty centimetres across.  They hydroplane on the sea’s thermal gradients, scooping beadworms.  When threatened they fold and plunge, leaving only a ring of steam.  
•  Bellowors: whale-sized, lungless vertebrates that pump hot brine through honeycomb skulls; vibration makes the skull resonate like an organ pipe.  Their calls travel ten kilometres and synchronize entire colonies of beadworms to flash in rhythm.  
•  Shard crabs: palm-sized scavengers armoured with platelets of stolen quartz.  They predate on anything metallic, including underslung boats; hulls here are built of pumice-wood or nothing.  
•  Lurid efts: amphibian cousins to the plateau’s glider eels—same keratin struts, but the bladders fill with hydrogen instead of mist.  They bob three metres below the ceiling, grazing milkglass like cows in fog.

5. Settlements—freeboard equals survival  
The principal city is Pel Ori, “the Listening Raft,” a flotilla of raftstones chained into a rosette twelve kilometres wide.  Each petal holds:  
•  Rim Docks—low enough to dangle nets into beadworm swarms.  
•  Mid-Circle—tiered guild halls grown from basalt mould imported down-shaft centuries ago; the mould keeps the halls in perpetual slow bloom, adding filigree buttresses each decade.  
•  Core—The Resonant Archive, a cathedral-scale amphora whose clay body was poured, drop by drop, from plateau runoff almost a thousand gutter ages ago.  Every drip that ever entered the Underwell has left a concentric stain on its interior—scholars trace climate history by colour alone.

6. Industry and exchange  
A cylinder-lift the size of a grain silo punches through the ceiling on cables strong enough to tow coal up and worm-clay pots down.  The winch engines high in Vord Kal call it the Keel, but Underwellers call it the Root because it feeds them.  In trade they send upward:  
•  Hydrogen bladders harvested from spent efts—fuel for the plateau’s helium-scarce dirigibles.  
•  Riftflare gas—condensed in ceramic ampoules; one ampoule equals a week of continuous arc-lamp light.  
•  Lysate salts—cured from bacterial mats; a teaspoon sterilizes twenty litres of gutter water.  

7. People of the brine—the Pelorians  
Body: Tall, marrow-light boned, skin pearl-gray with iron freckles.  Eyes widened into horizontal diamonds; the extra corneal width pulls in what little light exists.  Hairless below the cheekbones; ambient humidity breeds mildew in follicles, so children are ceremonially scald-shaved at twelve pump cycles of the Root drum.  
Dress: Two pieces only—a raft-silk wrap that stays wet to cool the skin, and a raftstone belt in which personal buoyancy beads are set.  Status is quantified in buoyancy: the highfamilies wear enough beads to float sitting, the poor must tread water.  
Speech: Click-glossolalia carried by echo; consonants delivered as palate clicks, vowels as sustained hums that skip across the sea surface like stones.

Customs:  
•  The First Drink: every visitor must sip raw vent water.  If you retch you are taboo for a Bright; if you keep it down you may ride any raft, demand any map.  
•  Bell Listening: communications tower to tower travel as sub-bass pulses rung on hanging bellowor ribs.  A skilled bell-listener can distinguish 400 timbres and relay messages on a single breath.  
•  Roof Reverence: ceiling glimpses are rare; when quake-lightning flickers and the roof reveals itself for half a second, every citizen stops mid-stride and bows to the unseen plateau folk, “those who own the rain.”

Life Cycle: Children are buoyed by tether orbs until they learn the temperature differentials; adolescence is counted from the first time one navigates the raft maze unaided.  Marriage is a chain-tying of home stones; divorce is simply loosing the chain and accepting the drift.  Death means deliberate sinking: a cotton plug of milkglass spores is swallowed to weigh the body; weeks later the spores germinate, rise, and add a luminous speck to the Bright.  Thus the dead quite literally help light the living.

8. Sound  
Base note: an endless, many-voiced dripping—some drops hiss on hot pools, some ping on cooling calcite, some tambourine on stretched vent-skin membranes the Pelorians hang for music.  Over that: raft lumber creaks; bellowor choir; the harmonic stutter of bell-language; distant grumble of the Keel’s cable drums.  Silence is no simpler here than above—only the timbre changes from rain-on-metal to rain-on-stone-and-water.

9. Motion and mutation  
Nothing stays where it was laid.  Raftstones roam, currents shear, the chamber roof sheds curtains of quartz that shatter on impact and seed new floes.  Maps are redrawn fortnightly by the Drift Cartel on vellum made of pressed beadworms.  Even the sea itself migrates: brine siphons down to lower vents in the southwest, then resurges in the northeast an estimated seventeen years later, warmer and richer with mantle gases.  Time is measured in “Root hoists” (one full coil of the Keel’s drive drum, roughly a day above) and in “Circle Sets” (the interval for one complete counter-current of the sea, ~6.3 plateau years).

10. Quiet concord and concealed tension  
•  Plateau folk believe Underwellers squander the sacred water; Underwellers believe plateau folk steal the hydrogen sky.  Both accusations are correct and neither side much minds—trade is older than grievance.  
•  Every Pelorian family keeps a reliquary of plateau artefacts caught in drip cones: brass rivets, eel-skin knots, once even a silver tooth.  These are displayed on festival nights and interpreted like runes, proof that the ceiling people still thrive.  
•  Once a generation, a Mist Angel husk falls the whole kilometer into the Sea, always intact, always devoid of occupant.  The husks do not sink; they drift to Pel Ori, where Guild Naturalists compare them to lurid efts and grow suddenly, thoughtfully silent.

11. Enduring presences  
•  The Hollow Pump: a natural chimney whose inner surface has been polished by five thousand years of falling water until it mirrors anything brought near.  Stand at its throat and you will see Vord Kal upside-down, miniature, thrumming.  Some call it proof that the two cities are only reflections of one larger organism.  
•  The Mother Raftstone: oldest accretion in the Sea, big as an atoll and honeycombed with tide caves.  It is said to pre-date the plateau’s first pumps; coral–lichen scripts on its underside reference night when stars shone here—before the land rose, before the roof closed.

So the world, observed from the water-hung citadel above or the brine-moored labyrinth below, is a single hydraulic sentence: rain becomes drip, drip becomes sea, sea returns as vapor through vents and gullies to lace the plateau sky again.  Two cities, one pulse.  Between them move coal and clay, hydrogen and arc-light, accusations and songs.  Each keeps a weathered ear pressed to the other’s machinery, listening for the pause that has not yet come.
