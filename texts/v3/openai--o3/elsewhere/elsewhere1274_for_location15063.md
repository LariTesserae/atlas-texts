The Glass Orchard

TERRAIN  
• Elevation: twenty-three metres above the tide line, on a limestone shelf that once carried a coastal forest.  
• Shape: a fan of shallow terraces, each one edged by low retaining ribs of vitrified salt. From above it looks like scales on the flank of some albino serpent, all pointing seaward.  
• Water: arrives hot, leaves nothing. Every twenty minutes a buried conduit exhales a pulse of brine—exactly the amount that vanishes from the Weeping Vault during the same beat. The flow spreads thin across the terraces, steams, and is gone by the time the next pulse arrives.  
• Sub-stratum: porous chalk riddled with vents; anything liquid that escapes the terraces percolates downward and resurfaces kilometres away as metallic springs.

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Dry to the edge of pain: humidity hovers at 4 percent most days, never higher than 7. The air tastes of scorched sugar and old coins. Sunlight, unfiltered by cloud or smoke, ricochets off a billion salt facets until the whole Orchard shimmers like a sea frozen mid-wave. Day is a white glare; night silvers the terraces with moon-cold frost that crackles underfoot.

BUILT LAYER  
• The Long Gut: a ceramic pipeline three palms wide, ribbed like a spine, that surfaces at the orchard’s apex. Its joints swell and clench with each incoming brine-bolus—locals watch the flex to judge the Vault’s health.  
• Crystal Trestles: A grid of iron frames holds wire nets stretched a metre above each terrace. When brine evaporates, salt and silicates climb the wires, budding into brittle polyhedral fruit.  
• The Thresher House: a barn of latticed brass where harvesters beat the glass-fruit loose. Anything larger than a clenched fist is prized for optics; shards are heaped for smelters farther inland.  
• Wind Organs: vertical tubes cut from scrapped smokestacks, set on pivot joints. When the afternoon off-shore wind rises they swivel and sing a five-note chord, warning workers to clear the nets before gusts turn them into blades.

SOUND  
1. The Orchard at rest: a faint ticking—salt crystals cooling, fracturing along stress lines.  
2. The Pulse: a subterranean boom, then a sighing rush through the Long Gut, then thousands of simultaneous hisses as new water meets hot stone.  
3. The Choir of Tubes: wind strikes in early evening; their harmony drifts clear as cathedral bells all the way to the dormitory barges anchored two kilometres off-shore.  
Between pulses silence reigns so completely that visitors complain they can hear their blood.

VEGETATION (OF A SORT)  
• Glass-Fruit: not organic but treated as crop. Translucent nodes, milky pink to aquamarine, grow on the wire nets. Ten hours after formation they “ripen” and detach; left longer they over-grow and fuse neighbouring strands into useless sheets.  
• Salt-Needles: dagger-long spines that stand upright on the terraces—leftovers from missed harvests. Workers break them for use as scribes and scalpels.  
Actual plants cannot survive the dryness; the only green is the patina on antique tools.

FAUNA  
• Vapor Kites: broad-winged insects, wingspan up to 60 cm, wings laced with mica dust. They ride the steam columns, filter-feeding on ferric spores belched alongside the brine. Their bodies are too light to weigh down the nets; the workers ignore them unless a kite dies, at which point its wing-veins are harvested for filigree.  
• Salt Hares: pale rodents evolved from iron rats that wandered up the drainage conduits a century back. Fur bleached, whiskers stiff with salt. They eat the kites whole, crunching the mica like sand.  
• Mirage Eels: only ever seen as refracted wobble across the terraces. Locals insist they are the same sump eels that ride the brine up the pipe, boiled translucent but still alive for a minute of frantic surface dance before evaporating in place.

MATERIALS  
Salt, glass, sun-baked chalk, wind, and the borrowed breath of a far-away machine. Wood is unknown; leather replaces it. Metal is kept shaded—touching iron that has baked since dawn will brand a palm black.

RULES OF THE WORLD  
1. The Orchard owes its heartbeat to the Vault: if a pulse is late, the terraces crack and slump by sunset. Two missed pulses and the nets sag into uselessness.  
2. Nothing stays wet longer than twelve heartbeats.  
3. Anything louder than the wind-organs risks avalanche: plates of salt sheet-collapse, sliding downslope in silent avalanches that can bisect a man at the shins.  
4. Light equals life. A shard carried into darkness dulls, clouds, and crumbles within days.

WHAT DAY-TO-DAY LIFE LOOKS LIKE  
Seventeen Pulse-Catchers rotate in crews of four. Shifts are timed to the incoming brine, not to clocks.  
• Approach: they live aboard rust-hulled barges moored offshore, because nothing softer than iron survives a week on the terraces.  
• Work: the moment steam lifts they sprint out, hook the nets with forked poles, and flick any ripened glass-fruit into padded baskets. A full terrace can be cleared in eight minutes; after ten the sun would weld nets and nodules solid.  
• Trade: once a week a sand-sledge arrives from the inland cities to swap food, lamp-oil and gossip for crates of optical-grade glass.  
• Sleep: barges are lined with evaporative-cooling cloth woven from rust-moss harvested in secret alcoves aboard—contraband warmth carried from the Vault itself.

FEELING  
First glance: blinding, lifeless, too fragile to touch.  
An hour: geometry everywhere—ranks of nets riding terraces in perfect fractal recession.  
A day: the silence writes itself inside you; heart and lungs slow until each incoming Pulse sounds intimate, like another organ grafted into your chest.

STAND, LISTEN  
The wind-tubes haven’t started yet. A hiss grows underfoot: hot brine skimming the chalk. Steam columns flare, mirrored in every faceted surface, and for a moment the Orchard becomes a hall of candles. The glass-fruit swell, colours blooming like bruise-fire, then the heat lifts and they freeze into stillness. Somewhere behind you, across kilometres of buried conduit, you imagine you can feel the iron heart that sent this water—a double beat, forty-four per minute—echoing in the bones of two places at once.

WHO – OR WHAT – CALLS THE GLASS ORCHARD HOME

1. The Seventeen Pulse-Catchers  
   a. Net-Masters (6) – veterans whose gloves are etched with tally-marks for each terrace collapse they’ve outrun. They smell faintly of ozone from years of static discharge.  
   b. Brine-Readers (3) – crouch by the Long Gut’s inspection lens, tasting each bolus with a copper spoon; if the brine sours, they mutter of trouble in the Vault.  
   c. The Four-Second Novices (8) – trainees allowed only the outermost terrace where fruit forms slower. They wear mirrored masks to hide awe (or terror) from the sun.  

2. The Long Gut  
   • Skeleton of ceramic vertebrae. Each segment numbered in faded iron-ink, though half the numbers are missing—exactly the same gaps appear in the Vault’s chain-links.  
   • It is said that if the Gut ever cools below body-heat, the buried clay beneath the Orchard will remember it was once mud and liquefy, dragging terraces and workers into the sea.

3. Wind Organs  
   Tuned to an F-minor pentachord. Instruments and warning devices both; any Pipe Swift that migrates this far mistakes them for kin and circles until exhausted, falling like burnt paper.

4. Salt Hares  
   • Eyes: crystalline, refract the sun into weaponised pin-beams; a hunting hare can blind a Vapor Kite in flight.  
   • Folklore: the harvester who interrupts two hares mid-duel must leave them locked until the next Pulse, or else the terraces will fracture in sympathy.

5. Vapor Kites  
   • Mating swarms coincide with the largest export caravans. Salt traders believe a dense swarm portends high-grade optical fruit; they pay extra if the kites ghost the wagons all the way to the horizon.

6. Echo Keepsakes  
   a. Pulse Beads – glass-fruit cored and threaded; each bead records the micro-tremor of its growth bands, a stuttering Morse code that scholars claim repeats the Vault’s concealed history.  
   b. Brine Scales – thin plates that peel from the Long Gut’s joints. Worn as talismans by Pulse-Catchers; a Scale gone dull warns of blockage in the pipe two days in advance.

7. Ghosts Acknowledged  
   • The Salt Mother – appears as a woman made of shifting nacre, stooping to lift nets that have snagged. Those she visits gather unblemished fruit by the basketful.  
   • The Crawling Shade – absence of glare shaped like a child; wherever it passes, glass-fruit blacken into obsidian. Shade nights are harvest holidays—no one courts the brittle black.  

8. Unspoken Treaty  
   Every seven years a delegation of Thirty-Three walks the conduit’s maintenance tunnels and emerges here, skin steaming in the dry. They gift a valve-handle coffin-plate; in return the Pulse-Catchers present a sphere of first-flower glass the size of a heart. No words are exchanged—the sound would shatter things still growing.

WHY THEY STAY  
Because the Orchard is a mirror held up to the Weeping Vault: where one sweats, the other thirsts; where one clangs, the other hushes. Together they make a full body. Those born to the Pulse hear it from their first breath; step away and your heart forgets which beat to follow— too fast and you bleed, too slow and you drown in your own heat. Better to live between silence and steam, harvesting the brief bright crystals that blossom only in the instant between one iron heartbeat and the next.
