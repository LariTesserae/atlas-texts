The Windglass Plateau  

It begins at the mouth of the old access shaft, three kilometres west of the caretakers’ corrugated hut, where the lift cage once ferried ore to daylight. Up through that shaft rises a draft that smells of rust and root-sweat, a slow exhale from the level below. Follow the breeze upward and you arrive in the open air on a tableland the size of a provincial county—a high, cracked expanse of basalt flaked thin as pastry and lacquered in salt. Locals call it the Windglass Plateau because every surface glitters, and because anything that does not glitter here is quickly abraded to nothing.

Geography and Light  
The plateau sits above the cloud line for most of the year, a hard pan tilted gently south-east so that the sun skims low across it from dawn to dusk. Basalt columns rise in clusters like burnt matchsticks, each plated on its windward face with evaporite rime that catches the light and throws white blades of glare across the ground. Between pillars the rock is crazed by polygonal cracks a handspan wide; in each crack lies milky sand ground from the same rock by centuries of knife-edged wind.

There is almost no shade. Noon can drive ground temperature high enough to warp the lenses of an unshaded field glass, but nights fall forty degrees, and every exposed molecule of water flashes to frost before dawn.

Air and Weather  
Wind is permanent. The buried turbines far below draw surface air downward in a slow, deliberate column, and that draft completes its circuit here. The plateau therefore hosts a standing gale that never quite dies: 35 km/h on calm mornings, 90 km/h in ordinary storms, and once each winter a shrieking mistral that lifts surface salt in curtains half a kilometre high. The air is painfully dry; lick your lip and it burns like menthol from evaporative chill.

Rain almost never reaches the rock. Moisture sublimates out of scudding cloud into diamond dust that sifts sideways and settles in wind shadows for minutes before the next gust claims it.

Sound  
Down below, the world is ruled by a single low A. Up here, there is almost no note at all, only broadband hiss. The basalt spires whistle on gusty days, but rarely in a register the ear can frame; instead they broadcast an ultrasonic sough felt as pressure in the teeth. The two soundscapes—the cavern’s bass and the plateau’s hiss—are the end points of the same instrument: a windpipe forty metres wide whose vocal cords are those twelve truck-swallowing turbine fans.

Flora  
Nothing here grows tall. The dominant lifeform is a crustose lichen—bruised-saffron when living, ghost-white when dry—that occupies the lee of every pebble and ridge. Each thallus is a disc six millimetres across, rimmed with micro-curls that trap wind-blown spore dust. From above the colonies resemble a map of archipelagos too numerous to count. For three weeks each spring, when snowmelt from distant peaks briefly moistens the wind, the lichens flush scarlet and release spores lighter than cigarette ash. Drafts carry them downward; half of what germinates in the under-skin hollow below comes from that scarlet weather.

Scattered among the lichens stand things that might be plants but are not. Thirty-odd mirror stalks—constructions of the first mining company—remain bolted to bedrock. Each is a telescoping mast that once held heliostats to feed the furnace array. The silver has long peeled, leaving skeletal frameworks that ratchet as the mast bearings creak. From a distance at low sun they still flash, turning the plateau into a field of erratic heliographs.

Fauna  
1. Glass-Skippers  
Minute arthropods, semitransparent, with hind legs grown into parabolic springs of silica-reinforced chitin. They spend most of their lives frozen under pebbles, thaw for six frenetic hours when humid wind arrives, then launch themselves on wind shear. Colonies orient by polarised light; specimens released indoors lose all sense of up or forward and simply coil into balls.

2. Smoke-Kites  
Not animals but silk membranes extruded by the lichen in its fertile phase. Each sheet is three hand-spans wide, one cell thick, and coated in hydrophobic wax. The morning updraft lofts thousands at a time. Most shred in the spire maelstrom; a lucky few ride the shaft draft clean into the cavern below, where they snag on catwalk grates and decay into nutrient snow the Root-Skin will later absorb. The Priests sweep drifts of this detritus off the walkways: white, weightless, faintly resin-scented.

3. Basalt Ravens  
Largest true animal here, charcoal-blue birds descended from an introduced corvid that escaped a survey team’s larder. Wing feathers are narrow and stiff to survive sand-blast; eyes hooded by bony ridges that give them a permanent scowl. They eat skippers, lichen nodules, and anything sodium-rich enough to replace minerals stripped from their bones by constant cold. Twice a year they mob the Priests for rock salt when a satchel is opened at the shaft mouth.

Human Presence  
There are no permanent settlements: only a relay outpost called Shift-House One, recessed into the southern scarp. Shift-House is a concrete wedge the colour of ash, sealed behind an airlock door that rolls on rails salvaged from the lift cage. Inside are:

• A bunkroom of eight hammocks strung fore-and-aft like berths in a lifeboat, lined with aluminised cloth to reflect body heat.  
• A salt pantry: rough-cut blocks stacked like masonry, each stamped with the hammer-logo of the Priests below. The ravens pick at the outer layer; the Priests accept this tax.  
• A machine-altar: the stripped guts of a diesel compressor now converted to a hand-turned signal siren. Every arrival ritual begins with two slow cranks, audible only as a tremor in the rock and as a modulation in the cavern’s bass far below.

The Priests come here in pairs after every third circuit of the turbines. They carry nothing but empty satchels on the way up, returning them filled with salt and wind-bleached rust scraped from the heliostat frames. The climb is brutal—four hours up a ladder stiff with rime—but they say the plateau’s silence rinses the drone out of their skulls just long enough to fall asleep again when they go back down.

Exchange and Continuity  
What leaves the Windglass Plateau:  
• Spores and silk-kite husks, seized by the downdraft and delivered to the Root-Skin as trace fertilizer.  
• Salt, harvested from nightly frost that sublimes on basalt and wicks brine to the rock surface. Blocks two hands wide are enough to keep a fan bearing honest for a month.  
• Ultrasound, a breath of hiss that threads the shaft and brushes the fans; the Root-Skin flinches from it, guiding its growth away from the breathing passages.

What returns from below:  
• Heat, minute but precious, rising in the ladder wells; the lichens cluster there, greedily green instead of saffron.  
• Humidity, exhaled by the Root-Skin and condensed against chill stone, feeding the spring bloom.  
• Memory in the bones of the Priests: they scratch maintenance tallies on the heliostat masts, so the plateau’s bronze surfaces carry the same witness marks as the gauge panels below.

Rules and Rhythms of the Plateau  
1. Nothing remains shiny for more than a season. Wind sands everything to matte; only fresh fracture glitters. The Priests carry steel rods expressly to strike rock and make brightness that can be seen from the cavern catwalks when the lift lamp is aligned.  
2. To pause in open ground is to begin eroding. Clothes abrade; exposed skin frosts and chips; tent fabric scours to lace. All human activity therefore happens in the wind-shadow of a pillar or beneath the Shift-House lintel.  
3. Every heliostat mast is sacred. Touch one and you must log the touch: date, time, temperature, barometric hiss (measured by a dangling reed whistle). The logs duplicate those on the bearing housings below; together they form a palindrome of maintenance.  
4. If the draft reverses—an unheard-of thing—flightless matter must be secured. The plateau keeps no secrets from the cavern; the cavern will return no mercy if its lung tries to inhale sky.

What It Feels Like to Stand Here  
You crouch behind a basalt fin no wider than your shoulders, yet it blocks a wind strong enough to bend your spine. Sunlight ricochets off every salt facet until the air itself seems full of needles. Between gusts you hear a paradoxical hush—as if the wind is not present but is the absence into which everything else is falling. Pull a glove off and the skin cracks before you can flex your knuckles. On the horizon the mirror stalks semaphore silently, their rotations not random but phased to the unseen breath exchanging with the turbines far below. You realise you are standing inside one half of a single breathing body: inhale above, exhale beneath, a pulse kilometres long.

And when the dusk finally cools the rock enough that frost flowers begin to bloom, you watch them blossom like instantaneous coral, white branching dendrites that sketch the memory of water upon stone. In that moment the hiss softens, the ravens settle, and down the shaft the low A grows imperceptibly warmer, as though the whole machine is sighing in gratitude for one more completed circuit of salt, spore, and human will.

Connection  
The Windglass Plateau is not roof to the cavern below; it is its opposite lung, its echo in mirror image. What condenses under amber lamps originates as ice dust on basalt. What roars in the dark begins as wind that has scraped itself clean against sun-whitened rock. The Priests move between the two the way blood moves through a heart: up oxygenated with salt and silence, down to feed iron and ivy-flesh. Neither place can finish a breath without the other. And so the world, in this small sector of stone and air, keeps living inside a single, tidal sentence: draw in, let go, draw in—sound and hush, shadow and glare—again, again, as long as someone is willing to climb.
