Stonethroat Vaults

1. Situation and Shape  
• Mouth. Forty kilometres offshore, the continental shelf shears into a gull-wing fissure six hundred metres below mean tide.  From above it looks like a black hyphen in the turquoise seabed.  
• Throat. The fissure widens downward into a body-length shaft, then into an inverted dome three kilometres across: the first of the Vaults.  Seven major chambers branch radially, stacked in a descending corkscrew until, two vertical kilometres down, they meet the upper mantle’s basalt.  
• Walls. Every surface is glass-coated breccia.  Flows of submarine lava quenched so fast they kept the splash shapes of their ejection: frozen tongues, curtains, bells.  Later, hydrothermal brines plated the glass with hematite and blue copper salts that flake like fish-scales under the touch of swells.

2. Water (wet 3.8)  
The basin on land is never dry; the Vaults are never still.  
• Column. A standing wave 420 m tall occupies the entrance shaft, pulsating in anti-phase with the tide.  Twice daily it swallows or expels a volume equal to a river estuary’s spring flood, driving everything in the upper chambers like a double heartbeat.  
• Blackfalls. Along inner cornices the sea forms downward waterfalls—jets escaping through pressure vents into underlying voids.  They fling arches of silver bubbles that last minutes before imploding into fine mist.  
• Brine lungs. Pockets of hypersaline fluid collect in pits; each pocket breathes by slow convection, exhaling plumes that taste of metal and cinnamon.

3. Light (murk 0.2)  
• Sunlight penetrates ten metres past the lip, enough for a tea-green gloom.  Below: perpetual night blue streaked by the lambent leaks of chemiluminescent fauna and the sporadic magnesium flares of rock fracturing under stress.  
• Once every 19 hours a lensing tide focuses moonlight down the shaft; for sixty-eight seconds the upper Vault glitters like a chandelier of mirrors, throwing bars of cold shine that race across walls faster than any creature here can swim.

4. Atmosphere and Sound (resounding 3.9)  
• Cavern organ. The seven main chambers resonate at fundamental frequencies from 9 Hz to 71 Hz.  When the column pulse and sub-crust tremors coincide, the system behaves like a pipe organ a kilometre long, issuing throbs you feel in bone marrow even through diving armour.  
• Hammer foam. Collapsing bubbles in the blackfalls detonate micro-explosions—white noise that sews a constant hiss through every other note.  
• Choir fish. Whole schools modulate the hiss; their gelatin air-sacs tune to cavity harmonics, turning passive suffocation into chorus.  Their music is the boom that the land basin hears as ground-pulse.

5. Temperature (stratified 1.1–3.6)  
• Shell-cool. Outer walls hover at 4 °C, rimed with sea-snow.  
• Core-warm. Hydrothermal veins lift streaks of 40 °C water.  Where the two intermingle, crystals grow that flash pearlescent like oil on slate.  
• The gradient powers everything that lives: heat rising, cold descending, stirring barter between chemistries.

6. Chemistry  
• Brine contains elevated manganese, vanadium and lithium—all precursors to the trace elements skimfish later harvest.  
• Dissolved gas: 14 % hydrogen, 4 % methane, 2 % noble mix.  Constant redox fireworks make the Vault an electrochemical storm in slow motion.

7. Life (abundant 3.0)  
7.1 Basalt Grailers (Petronamus archimus)  
• Shape. Cylinders of chitin-bone three metres long, jointed like bamboo.  
• Behaviour. Anchor head-down in warm vents, rasp glass off basalt with belt-teeth to farm chemo-bacteria.  Each scrape releases shards that drift upward to seed glassmoss on the basin plates two hundred kilometres of stone away.  
• Sound. Their tooth-belts produce a rasp exactly 9 Hz off the chamber’s fundamental, damping standing waves that could liquefy them.

7.2 Choir Fish (Atriolum resonans)  
• Jelly bodies, fifty centimetres, translucent violet.  
• A muscle sphincter around an internal bubble compresses rhythmically, beating the surrounding water like a speaker cone.  Colonies sync into chords that counterphase dangerous surges; their song is why the Vaults have not shattered since the last tectonic jolt.  
• Eggs ride the outbound tide, hatching in the open shelf; only a fraction ever find their way home.

7.3 Mirror Shrikes (Specularax argentata)  
• Crustaceans plated with nanogrids of magnetite.  They line up on reflective walls, forming living parabolic arrays that focus ambient infrasound onto prey—usually choir fish too young to sing loud enough to resist.  
• The focused pulse cavitates prey at a distance; flesh turns to mousse, which the shrikes sip through rostrums like glass straws.

7.4 Ancillary Forms  
• Rust-snow: slow-tumbling webs of iron oxide that nurture copper sponges.  
• Glow-reed: meter-long tubes emitting steady cobalt light, nourished entirely by electric fields.

8. Built (distributed 0.9, tech 1.7)  
• Litho-bridges. Ribbons of fused basalt span several chambers, perfectly arched so that amplified footfalls cancel organ peaks, preventing resonant overload when large beasts pass.  No known animal could survey or melt rock so cleanly.  
• Helix racks. Spirals of magnetised haematite studded with mirror shrike cadavers, always thirteen turns, aligned to planetary north.  Their function remains disputed: current theory says they store phase information of past organ chords, an acoustic memory palace for non-genetic inheritance.

9. Dynamics (power-slow 1.8, pulse-fast 3.2)  
• Tides reorder current pathways twice daily—fast.  
• Mineral accretion reshapes pillars over centuries—slow.  
• Seismic heave re-tunes the whole organ every few decades—medium.  
Living systems embed all three clocks: shrikes hunt on the hourly whistle of the tide; grailers migrate vent-to-vent on the decadal pitch shift; choir fish memorize the millennial chord and sing it back unchanged.

10. Vector to the Basin  
• The Vaults discharge roughly 11 GW of mechanical energy as infrasound through continental rock.  0.4 % of that flux reaches the land basin, slowed but coherent, arriving as the heart-slow pulses travellers feel underfoot.  
• Trace-element dust from grailer rasping rides thermal plumes, emerges in coastal geysers, and is blown inland.  Over months it salts the basin fog with nickel and germanium—the metals glassmoss cannot live without.  
• In return the Stackwright spires high on land act like satellite dishes, throwing faint sympathetic vibrations back through bedrock.  These cues phase-lock the Vault organ, preventing runaway resonance that would crack the chambers like eggs.  Two landscapes, each others’ dampers: separated by water, married by stone.

11. Rules of the Vaults  
• Cavitation is predator and sculptor: anything not resonant enough to ride shockwaves is reduced to slurry that feeds the vents.  
• Every organism carries a frequency signature; mis-tuned individuals are not merely maladapted—they physically disintegrate.  
• Architecture evolves by interference: that which survives becomes buttress, lens, or bridge within the sounding body.

12. Human Encounters  
• Submergence. The only expeditions have been via drone or pressure-sarcophagus.  Direct human presence lasts under six hours before resonance sickness sets in: bone ache at first harmonic, vertigo at the third, hallucinations at the seventh.  
• Trade. Extractors harvest glow-reed cobalt for medical isotopes.  Vault denizens treat the cutters like transient storm noises: disruptive but soon gone.  The cutting of a single reed retunes local echoes for weeks, confusing shrike hunting patterns and causing choir fish to cluster dangerously near vents—collateral choirs lost to boiling plumes.  
• Myth. Divers report disembodied patterns of light aligning into humanoid silhouettes in the organ mist—Vault-wrights, analogue to the Stackwrights.  Where they stand, litho-bridges kink slightly then self-heal, as though the Vault itself is editing its skeleton while watched.

13. Futures  
• A mantle-upwelling pulse predicted in 370 years will raise temperatures by 14 °C.  Models show choir fish adapting by deepening their bubble pitch; mirror shrikes may go silent, removing dampers and risking runaway resonance.  The basin ashore would receive a doubled ground-pulse—enough to shatter several chordspires.  Unless new spires grow taller or denser (Stackwright activity unknown), the inland saucer could lose the delicate drip-note balance that births skimfish.  
• Farther ahead, plate drift will seal the shelf fissure; the Vaults will dehydrate, collapse, and become a cathedral of chalcedony geodes.  The memory of their song will persist only in the basin’s long-wake echoes—stone remembering water that remembered stone.

14. Summary  
The Stonethroat Vaults are the planet’s submarine heart: a resonant labyrinth where tide, lava and living chord weave a single organ pipe thousands of metres long.  Its pulse travels through continental bone to a landlocked saucer that answers in kind, keeping both places in a duet older than ice.  One exhales vibration, metals, and memory; the other inhales them, slows them, refines them into crystal life and brief audible architecture.  Two ecosystems, two tempos, one instrument playing itself across gulf and ground, forever tuning, never done.
