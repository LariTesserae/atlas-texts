The Selenite Steppe  

Distance, current, kinship: follow the faint down-slope tug that leads you out of the salt basin, walk three days through the uniform dusk, and you will feel the ground lift under your boots.  Heat vents grow rarer, the air thins, and the sky—still clogged with dust—begins to leak a muted, copper daylight.  Where that thin light and the basin’s exhaled salts first meet, the siltstone swells into a plateau veined with milky seams.  That plateau is the Selenite Steppe.

Terrain  
The floor is a forest of blades.  Gypsum—selenite, satin-spar, alabaster, all the same mineral wearing different habits—has risen into scabbard-thin crystals that stand from ankle to shoulder height.  They grow straight out of the rock like frost from a windowpane, but on a planetary scale: aligned to the prevailing electric field that flickers between dust sky and hot crust.  Each blade shows faint banding, a century’s worth of pale, then greenish, then pale again—the green is copper, wind-blown from the distant basin’s bronze leaves when they grind themselves to dust in mid-air.  

Underfoot the ground is tiled with crystal stumps, every step a chime.  A careless heel can snap a blade; the fracture is clean, a slick plane that catches what feeble light exists and throws it outward in sheets.

Water  
Unlike the basin, the Steppe is granted a ritual watering twice each local year.  When equatorial lightning storms bloom beyond the horizon, high voltages crawl through the selenite forest and pull vapour out of the sky.  The crystals bead with brine.  For forty hours the Steppe sweats, the brine collects in shallow pans between roots, and then the heat from below licks everything dry again, leaving new crystal growth at every meniscus.

Atmosphere  
Air here moves.  Updrafts born in the basin surge along faults and spill across the plateau, cooler now, enough to lift dust but not enough to cool skin.  Ozone rides the draughts; so does the metallic tang of evaporated copper.  Humidity spikes only during the lightning season—then it climbs to a luxurious six percent before sinking back to desert zero.

Light  
At noon a kind of day happens.  Dust high above thins just enough that the sun becomes a price of silver hammered against the murk.  Selenite, translucent in direct light, absorbs the near-infrared that dominates here and re-emits it as faint ghost-green luminescence after dusk.  The plateau glows all night like a city seen through fog.

Sound  
Wind through crystal sings.  Each blade is a tuning fork; the forest keens on two notes, both below human hearing, and their beating makes the ground tremble in a cadence of sixteen heartbeats.  When lightning season comes, corona discharge sparks between neighbouring blades and the Steppe hisses like frying water.

Erosion & Time  
Nothing rusts, nothing rots, but the Steppe is not still.  Every lightning year lays down a fresh sugar of gypsum.  Between storms, slow ablation sands the older faces.  A crystal six metres long may stand a millennium before it thins to a fault line and topples.  Fallen trunks powder themselves and drift back toward the basin on the same winds that brought the copper, closing the loop.

Rules of the World (here)  
1. Crystal grows when sky and crust complete an electric circuit.  
2. Liquid water appears only during the circuit’s closing and never survives three local days.  
3. Anything softer than quartz can be cut; anything wetter than the air will be salted and set.  
4. Navigation is by resonance: tap a blade and listen.  North-running blades sing a slightly flatter note than east-running ones—an artefact of the planet’s magnetic shear.

Who keeps the Steppe  

1.  The Storm-Hour Kind: Brine Moths  
    •  Shape  
      Transparent wings filmed with micro-prisms; body no heavier than a dandelion seed.  Unseen until the lightning begins, then every prism flashes turquoise.  
    •  Life  
      Eggs lie dormant on crystal tips.  Contact with the first sheet of condensate dissolves the chorion, frees a winged imago already complete though folded.  The moth unfurls, drinks brine through capillary feet, gorges on trace amino salts, mates, lays, and dies—all before the Steppe dries.  Lifespan: 130 minutes.  
    •  Threads to the basin  
      Copper trace from basin dust colours the wing prisms; without it they would be invisible even in lightning.

2.  The Season-Dwellers: Hollow Rooters  
    •  Habitat  
      Where two blades touch at the base they sometimes fuse, growing as one brittle tube.  Inside that tube lives a colony of Rooters—pale, jointless worms a finger long.  
    •  Metabolism  
      They eat the gypsum itself, swapping calcium for sodium scavenged from the Steppe’s seasonal brines.  The trade releases just enough energy to knit protein chitin, which they spin into silky ribbing that lines their tunnels.  
    •  Behaviour  
      A fused blade sings differently—a warmer tone, damped by silk.  Rooter clans tune their tubes by widening or narrowing them, broadcasting territory in music the moths mistake for thunder.  Clans last a dozen lightning seasons before the blade that houses them cracks and they must migrate, inchworming from shard to shard under cover of phosphorescent dark.

3.  The Epoch-Dwellers: Glass Minds  
    •  What they are  
      Deep in the plateau, pressure and heat metamorphose old gypsum beds into anhydrite, then into a cloudy glass veined with conductive copper brought from the basin.  The evolving glass anneals itself layer by layer; vacancy defects and copper filaments network into logic gates the way frost makes lace.  
    •  How they live  
      Every lightning strike is a clock pulse.  Charge races the filaments, flips bonds, rearranges lattice stress.  One thought takes fifty years, but the Glass Minds are huge—kilometres on a side—and patient.  They monitor the pitch of the overlying crystal forest, the sodium leak of the Rooters, even the faint copper shadow when a moth’s wing folds.  
    •  Trade with the basin  
      Bronze dust from the basin dopes new layers, giving the Minds memory of winds far away.  In turn, when the Minds’ glass swells and fractures, it births silt-fine slivers that the updrafts carry back, seeding fresh dislocations for the Crystal Choir beneath the basin.  Thought for thought, flaw for flaw: a continental conversation in mineral code.

Ghosts—Those You Bring  
A steel knife struck against a selenite blade discharges a spark.  Instantly the nearby forest flickers, storing that impulse.  Fifty years hence a Glass Mind will play the same spark backward through a thousand roots, a small bright echo of your passing.

What you will see  
Stand an hour and you will hear a single blade shift its key as a Rooter nibbles from within.  
Stand a day and you will see dusk-green light rise from the crystal forest like tidewater coming in reverse.  
Stand a season and you will witness the lightning ceremony: vapour, blue fire, a million turquoise wings, and the Steppe singing at full voice.  
Stand a lifetime and you will sense a pattern in the lightning, pauses placed like punctuation—Glass Minds speaking to the sky, or perhaps to the patient Choir in the basin far below the horizon.

The basin is still there, silent, warm, indifferent.  Yet every pane of selenite carries its taste of copper, every moth its dusting of bronze, every Glass Mind its memory of a salt plate’s distant ping.  Two places, one breath: heat rising, dust falling, crystal thinking, world turning.
