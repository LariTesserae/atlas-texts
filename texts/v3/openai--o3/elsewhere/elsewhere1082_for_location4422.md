The Plateau of Canted Mirrors  

The way is mostly vertical.  
Follow the salt veins upslope until they braid into a single white lightning-bolt of mineral that climbs the basalt wall. Somewhere within that wall, the roar of the basin turns into a tremor you can feel through boot soles; the living thunder becomes a physical stair. Step where it hums the deepest and the rock will crumble pliantly, opening hand-sized pockets already bored by oxide-raspers generations dead. Keep climbing. After six hundred metres the wall leans backward, then outward, then ceases being wall at all and becomes floor again—an overturned bowl set lip-to-lip with the valley’s own.

Here begins the Plateau of Canted Mirrors.

1. Shape and Skin  
• Imagine a plain fifty kilometres across, tilted five degrees north to south like a giant printing plate. Its surface is tessellated obsidian, but not the smooth kind—every polygon is tipped up at a random angle, forming a landscape of dark, overlapping mirrors.  
• Each plate is fixed at only one edge; the free edges tremble in the thin wind and click together, so that footsteps anywhere set off a chain of domino shivers audible kilometres away.  
• Between plates lie seams dusted with the same pale salts that vein the valley below. In drought years these seams open into knife-wide cracks that plunge straight down to the fog bowl, re-uniting two worlds with a single vertical gulp.

2. Light and Weather  
• Above the plateau the airborne mineral haze thins just enough to let through a bruised amber twilight—brighter than anything seen beneath the fog but still no full daylight.  
• When basin convection exhales, damp, bacteria-rich breath rises through the cracks and condenses on the plate undersides. At sunset (a concept that exists here) this moisture flashes into frost scrollwork before sublimating, etching new microfacets that sharpen the mirrors a fraction more each cycle.  
• The wind never howls; it rings. Glass lips make a crystal chime pitched precisely an octave above the highest facet-bat whistle. Every gust is a chord change.

3. Energy Economy—Where the Sound Goes  
• The roar you left behind does not end at the ridge. Vibrations travel the salt seams and reappear here as sub-audible standing waves. You feel them in the knees, a ghost percussion under every other sound.  
• The plateau’s dominant life forms harvest that vibration the way valley chemovores harvest iron. Their organs are tuned cavities; their food is resonance.

4. Biota of the Mirrors

a. Tilt-panes (phylum Vibraconae)  
Appearance: transparent, kite-shaped membranes stretched over a rigid perimeter of living quartz. At rest they lie flush to the glass polygons like flattened tents two metres across.  
Mechanism: the underside is studded with whisker-thin struts that penetrate the obsidian and seat themselves in the rock’s crystal lattice. Each strut is a piezoelectric rod: vibration flexes it, generating micro-currents that power the pane.  
Feeding: once charged, a pane lifts one corner, scoops airborne mineral dust, liquefies it in a capillary stomach and extrudes fresh quartz to extend its frame. Thus the plain is slowly being re-paved in living glass.  
Language: panes communicate by fluttering edges in binary flaps—audible, but more importantly, visible in the rapid tilting of reflected sky. Migrating flocks of facet-bats read these flickers like semaphore, using them as north-south navigation beacons.

b. Prismsnails (order Diffracta)  
Appearance: low cones composed of interlocking hexagonal plates of barium glass, each plate flamboyantly iridescent. A mature snail is shoulder-high and crawls millimetres per hour.  
Diet: the chemical mist exhaled from cracks. Inside the shell, chemosymbionts similar to knot-worms oxidise chloride and emit faint violet glow—too faint to outshine the mirrors, but detectable to the snails themselves.  
Function: every three hours prismsnails discharge surplus ions through tiny vents; the ions precipitate as glittering rime on nearby mirrors, improving reflectivity. The whole species is a janitorial staff polishing the plateau for reasons it never needed to evolve to understand.

c. Pitch-wasps (family Echomorphidae)  
Appearance: finger-long, spindle-bodied fliers whose wings are thin ribbons of chitin designed not for lift but for song.  
Nest: where two mirrors lean together they wax a black, tarry comb directly onto the glass.  
Life cycle: larvae feed on turbulence. Each nest mouth is tuned as a Helmholtz resonator; wind across it booms a single note that shakes the comb and liquefies nutrients dripping from adult throats. Different nests claim different frequencies; territorial disputes are choir battles that can be heard from one edge of the plateau to the other.  
Trade with the valley: on warm evenings the wasps glide down through cracks, harvest blobs of coin-shell mucus (a delicacy) and return before the roar peaks again. In exchange, they carry valley egg-film on their feet, seeding new species onto mirror undersides.

d. Glass-skippers (genus Verticia)  
Appearance: lizard-sized bipeds with flat, fan-shaped tails. Flesh is matte charcoal; the entire dorsal surface is an oval mirror.  
Locomotion: when wind rises, a skipper points its tail mirror into the oncoming gust. Lift flips it into a tumbling dash—thirty metres in a heartbeat, skittering from plate to plate like a stone skipped over water.  
Eyes: none. Instead, a horseshoe of pressure sensors reads bow shock; as long as the roar’s subsonics hum beneath the wind, skippers know which way is “downhill” toward prey noises.  
Prey: pitch-wasp larvae. The lizards’ bellies carry sheaths of micro-diamond that slit open nests in absolute silence, stealing vibration-fed young before adults can sing them to safety.

5. Hydrology—The Melt Lakes  
• A dozen shallow basins occupy the plateau’s southern dip where tilt plates funnel whatever scant drizzle reaches here. The water is saturated with silicates and remains super-cooled until disturbed.  
• When a Black Strider passes through the valley below, its magnetic thrash rattles the entire plateau, jolting the melt lakes into spontaneous crystallisation. Overnight they transform into frozen scrimshaw gardens of needle-crystal a kilometre wide, sharp as poverty.  
• Prismsnails migrate toward fresh gardens to rasp sodium needles for tooth material. The process wrecks the ice in days, but while it lasts the gardens refract sunset into a corona visible even through the dust layer—one of the rare true spectacles of light on this world.

6. The Quiet Exiles—Why Here Exists  
• All sound in the mirrorlands is leak from the basin: wind chimes, wasp hymns, tremor hum. Yet there are moments—just after a Strider has fed, before the roar rebuilds—when the plateau falls perfectly silent.  
• During that hush, translucent figures called Auscultants rise, one per kilometre, through the gaps. They are tissue-thin sheets shaped roughly like cloaked bipedal silhouettes, but made of bonded veil filament and knot-worm gel.  
• Purpose: to listen. They bend, catching starlight that never reaches the valley, and hold it in flexed membranes as if tasting. When the roar resumes they fold, drift back through the cracks and vanish.  
• Plateau organisms treat Auscultants with reverence: tilt-panes flatten so as not to cut them, pitch-wasps mute, even skippers freeze mid-step. Only then does the place earn its name—every mirror cants the same way, offering the sky a single, united face.

7. Connection Back Down—A Two-Way Pulse  
• Vibrational energy rides the seams up; mineral dust, spores and egg-film ride pitch-wasps down.  
• When crack frost sublimates, it descends as enriched drizzle, doping the valley’s salt veins with trace elements required by bladder-beasts to keep their skins taut. Without plateau frosts, the beasts would cough flat decades early; the roar would lose its bass stabiliser; condensation would falter.  
• Conversely, if the roar ever ceased entirely, tilt-panes would starve, prismsnails would wander mirror-blind, and the printing-plate plain would fracture into a silent scrapyard of dead glass. The two biomes are a double heart, systole and diastole, pumping chemistry and resonance back and forth.

8. Orientation and Rules  
1. Walk only where three mirror points meet; anywhere else is a trapdoor.  
2. Do not hum—you’ll disrupt ownership lines between pitch-wasp nests.  
3. If every mirror abruptly levels, lie flat: an Auscultant is passing overhead and the world is holding its breath.

9. If You Linger  
You will carry away little triangles of obsidian lodged in boot treads. Each is pregnant with proto-pane spores dormant in microscopic clefts. Expose them to steady vibration—a ship engine, a marching beat—and they will unfold into thumb-sized sheets that try to plug any gap in your surroundings, including nostrils and ear canals. Explorers learn to keep a pouch of stillness: a box wrapped in wool where the shards can sleep, dreaming of the next cadence strong enough to feed them.

Stand at the lip where roar becomes ring, where fog becomes glass, and see the world as a single instrument—valley the drumskin, plateau the resonant body. Life here is not about light or flesh or thought; it is about sustaining the note. All else—plates, panes, worms, wasps, even wanderers—is ornament on the fundamental. Move gently; you are part of the chord now.
