The Rustwave Flats

Where the hidden cisterns beneath the hanging basins finally find an exit, they do not tumble in cataracts or carve a single river. They exhale. Every forty-two beats—the same buried metronome that stirs the basins above—thin breaths of iron-rich brine sigh out of thousands of hairline vents, condensing at ground level into a permanent, ankle-deep sheet of water the colour of steeped blood. That sheet is the Rustwave Flats: a horizonless mirror three days’ walk west of the range, half land, half liquid machine, bright where the basins are dim, open where they are walled, but thrumming to the identical pulse.

TERRAIN  
Think of a beach forever at low tide, its sand replaced by plate glass. The brine’s surface is skin-tight: capillary tension stretches each exhalation until it covers hectares before breaking. Under that film lies a mosaic of mesas a handspan high, each mesa armoured in rust crystal and edged by razor-sharp rills where the film repeatedly tears and heals. Step wrong and your boot sinks through a membrane so thin it rings like cracked china; the water beneath is only ten centimetres deep, yet it drags like oil, weighting every motion.

Farther out the flats tilt almost imperceptibly toward the western sea. The gradient is enough that, when the ground-engine inhales, the whole expanse appears to flow uphill in a single silent bore. Locals call the moment the world reconsiders gravity “the countertide.” Twice a day shallow whirlpools open, exposing hexagonal sinkholes exactly 0.67 m wide—the same diameter cut by the Weaver of Gates around cistern vents high in the mountains. Whatever agency chose that measure chose it for both places.

WATER & CHEMISTRY  
Salinity: twice ocean average.  
Iron content: 170 ppm, precipitating as micaceous flakes.  
Dissolved gases: oxygen supersaturated by cyanobacteria washed down from the basins; pH rides the border between life and corrosion.  

Every pulse lifts new brine through fault-lines, but the water that returns toward the sea leaves iron behind, plating the flats molecule by molecule. The process is so regular that you can read time in the layering: a dark line every equinox (storms dilute the brine), a bright band every solstice (evaporation concentrates it again). A century of seasons stacks thinner than a fingernail, yet the stack is global memory, a rust phonograph the size of a province.

LIGHT & SKY  
No cliffs intercept the sun; instead the flats write light back upward. At noon the mirror dazes birds flying overhead, and their confused shadows slither in wrong directions. Dusk burns red twice—once in the west, once at your feet—until the two fires meet and extinguish in mutual reflection. Night arrives fast but never total: aurora born over the basins stretches a green braid across the sky, while beneath, the film catches starlight and lets it crawl across the surface, a double cosmos disturbingly close.

VEGETATION  
1.  CRIMSON CLOCKWEED  
   A mat-forming halophyte whose leaves arrange themselves in spirals keyed to the forty-two-beat surge. Each pulse sluices brine through gaps between leaves; micro-valves close, trapping a thimble of water that the plant distills, ejecting pure droplets that hang mid-air an instant before falling. The Flats’ only regular freshwater falls upward first.  

2.  MIRRORFEN RICE  
   Genome half-borrowed from glass-reed ancestors: hollow stalks filled with mirrored plates angled to bounce sunlight straight into the water below. Symbiosis with benthic algae means most photosynthesis happens under the skin of the flats, not above it. Harvested, the grain flashes silver; eaten, metallic esters leave the tongue tasting of thunderstorms.  

3.  SPIRELICHEN  
   Grow only on rust mesas. Each colony extrudes a single hair-thin tower up to a metre tall, magnetite-lined and tuned to the ultra-low frequencies of the ground-engine. When countertide sweeps in, the towers sing. An acre of them in full voice is a field of tuning forks being struck by no visible hand.

FAUNA  
1.  RUSTSKIMMER  
   Size of a dragonfly, wings fringed with hydrophobic cilia. A rustskimmer weighs less than the surface tension pressuring the film, so it never breaks water, skimming indefinitely. Larvae mature inside bubbles of distilled water pumped up by clockweed, hatch, and burst through the ceiling like seeds of air.  

2.  IRON-BILLED WADER  
   A bird with telescoping, needle-slender legs. Blood is laced with antifreeze proteins; the legs are conductive struts that sink heat into the flats, clouding brine around their feet into instant rust quilts that bear the bird’s weight. With each step the quilt cracks and the cycle repeats. Migrates upslope after breeding; skeletal remains found in hanging basins suggest some never return.  

3.  GLASSBACK SALAMANDER  
   Transparent save for a suture of glittering vertebrae. Lives under the film, lungs replaced by iron-infused swim bladders that double as resonant chambers. During countertide they surface, gulp air, and echo the forty-two-beat in basso clicks that keep their shoal together in the visual chaos of inverted sunsets.

4.  BASIN EXILE (lone chaser)  
   Occasionally a long-jawed chaser tumbles through an underground vein and washes out onto the flats. Here its vibration-sense is blinded by the constant song of spirelichen. Some starve; a few learn to hunt reflected silhouettes instead of true bodies, becoming the flats’ only serious predator. Their pelts bleach, their whiskers lengthen until they resemble loose silver stitching in the air.

HUMAN PRESENCE  
Lowlanders avoid the basins, but they love the flats—a place where one can breathe without frostbite and walk without rope. Three transient trades cross the mirror:

A.  SHIMMER-SALT  
   Skimmed at dawn before the day’s first pulse, when crystals lie dry on exposed mesas. Caravans carry it south; gourmets swear it “tastes like weather about to change.”  

B.  ECHO-GLASS  
   When countertide and coastal wind meet, sections of the film arch upward and solidify into panes only a millimetre thick but metres across. Caught quickly, the sheets retain a standing wave—tap them and they replay the engine’s heartbeat. Pilgrims buy fragments to hang over sleeping infants, believing the pulse lulls bad dreams the way it pacifies creatures in the heights.  

C.  WAYSCRIPT  
   The spirelichen’s song is quantised rust on wax tablets: traders dip a plate, withdraw it, and let airborne iron settle according to the vibration. No two signatures match; the tablets function as deeds, contracts, even love letters readable only if you know how to hum the pattern back. Forgery is possible but demands a private section of flats and the patience to wait until song, wind and pulse coincide exactly—a crime measured in seasons.

CLIMATE & CHANGE  
Summer raises mirages: whole phantom fleets sailing on the mirror. Winter draws ice-filigree across the film, but the constant brine up-well prevents complete freeze. Dry years shrink the mirror toward the vent-pocked core; wet years send it creeping kilometres farther, plating salt-brush deserts in copper gloss until the land looks dipped in molten dusk. Nothing grows on that new skin for a generation, then clockweed spores arrive on rustskimmer wings and the cycle begins anew.

RULES OF THE FLATS  
1.  Step where the film has already broken; a wound is stronger than untested glass.  
2.  Follow the countertide to find fresh water; follow the song to find shelter.  
3.  Anything cast in true shadow belongs to the predator that cast it—pay ransoms in shimmer-salt.  
4.  The mirror remembers: each footprint plates over in minutes but remains as a darker ghost visible at low aurora.  
5.  Never sleep to the west of a 0.67 m vent; some mornings the sea wakes closer than it should.

CONNECTIONS, SEEN AND UNSEEN  
•  Iron that pulses upward here is the same blood that colours the moss three vertical kilometres above; samples match isotope for isotope.  
•  Ibex plates drift through fissures and surface along the flats’ inland rim, riddled by brine yet still articulated—proof that the earth’s subterranean engine does not disassemble what it transports.  
•  On nights when the Andante of silence sweeps the basins, every spirelichen tower folds closed, and the mirror goes still enough to show stars no eye has charted. At dawn the towers reopen, and life restarts, as though both heights and lowlands share a single off switch.  
•  The glass-reed flute wedged in the mountain seam? Its note rides the underground passage and surfaces here as a tremor you feel in teeth, never in ears—the Flats’ own faint memory of music made by hands.

LIFE IN ONE SENTENCE  
The Rustwave Flats is a horizontal cathedral of water that thinks it is metal, a place where the mountains’ hidden heartbeat spreads thin, shines back the sun twice, and teaches everything—plant, beast, traveller, echo—to balance on the breath between one pulse and the next.
