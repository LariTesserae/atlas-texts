The Shatterflats of Vael  

You glimpse them long before you arrive: a white mirage where the sky seems to leak onto the land, glare piled on glare until horizon and ground dissolve. Step closer and the illusion shatters—hence the name—revealing a basin as wide as a small sea, paved in broken mirrors of salt.

ORIGIN & REACH  
The Shatterflats occupy the old heart of that vanished ocean whose ghost wind still combs Khar Teles. When the waters retreated they left a floor of brine that never quite dries; each noon the surface sweats upward, each night it reins itself back as dew that rides thermals toward the plateau. The people of Khar Teles call that nightly drift “the Long Exhale,” and they measure seasons by the taste it leaves on their lips.

ENTERING  
You descend by switchback through rust-red badlands. The last ridge drops straight into an amphitheatre two hundred miles across, its rim scalloped like the edge of a giant basin left out in the sun until it cracked. Everywhere underfoot the crust snaps, tilts, re-fuses. No map lasts a decade; the Flats rewrite themselves whenever a hidden pocket of brine drains or a new pressure ridge buckles upward.

WATER  
Here water is everywhere and useless. It lurks inches below the crust—saturated, bitter with magnesium, hostile to kidneys. Twist a staff into the ground and it comes up dripping black-green fluid that stains skin a week. Potable water must be coaxed out of that poison by stills the size of houses: black-glass panes pinned above shallow pools, condensing two, maybe three cups a day per panel. Every dawn the condensation that Khar Teles lives on departs Vael in the same breath that leaves these stills dry.

LIGHT & AIR  
Nothing softens the sun. Noon light is a white hammer that drives temperatures high enough to craze ceramic. The air, though, is cool a few metres up, chilled by the slow evaporation of brine. Thermal shear between those layers births horizontal dust devils that race flat-out across the pan, scouring smooth any footprint older than an hour.

TERRAIN  
• Pancake mesas of halite rise knee-high, etched by wind into honey-comb.  
• Fumarole chimneys, six or eight to a cluster, vent steam scented like hot pennies—the same bacterial metabolisms that breathe beneath Khar Teles, here exhaling without disguise.  
• Along the southern quadrant a river of salt, milky and slow as glass, crawls downslope a centimetre a year: crystal crust at the top shatters under its own creep, puddling into mother-of-pearl swales.  

VEGETATION  
1. Brine-Kelps: Translucent ribbons floating just below the surface pools, photosynthesising in liquid instead of air. At night they slump, exposing leathery bulbs that inflate with hydrogen produced as by-product; the bulbs keep them from sinking into lethal anoxic depths.  
2. Glass-Rushes: Hollow quartz stems rooted in capillaries of brine. When wind whistles through them they play a chord in F-minor that can carry ten kilometres.  
3. Scab Ferns: Not ferns at all but layered films of halobacteria; disturb them and you release a purple cloud of spores that smells of iodine and rotting plums.

FAUNA  
• Mirror Crabs: Flat as plates, shell facets reflecting sky so perfectly they appear invisible except for the shadow they do not cast. They farm brine-kelp gardens and “milk” hydrogen bulbs to inflate mating balloons.  
• Vapour Kites: Charcoal-grey birds with wings like ribbons. They ride the daily updrafts to the plateau, returning at dusk heavy-bodied and iridescent from condensation. Khar Teles children believe each kite carries a single stolen heartbeat of rain.  
• Salt-Burrow Eels: Blind, thread-thin, they navigate by electric sense through the mush beneath the crust. If a still’s reservoir leaks they sense the fresher water and swarm, tangling valves shut with their own bodies until desiccation mummifies them in place.

SOUND  
Dawn: pop and fizz as brine boils under overnight crust. Midday: chime of wind through glass-rush fields. Dusk: crack-gun reports when the upper centimetre of salt cools too fast and splits. Night: near silence, broken only by the sigh of condensers drawing breath.

THE SETTLEMENT – ULÛN GAVIR (“PORT WITHOUT SEA”)  
Population hovers around five hundred, give or take the latest caravan from the plateau. The town rides on sled-runners of bronze and hardwood; every equinox teams of ox-sized crabs drag it a kilometre north or south to safer crust, leaving behind ribs of frozen shadow that last a season.  

• Architecture: Tilt-wall frames of brine-brick, faced with reflective salt tile. Roofs slope steeply to speed runoff into solar stills. Interiors are dim, cooled by evaporation towers hung with wick-tongues of kelp leather.  
• Streets: Platforms linked by hinged planks; gaps allow rising steam to escape so the town does not founder on sudden bubbles. People carry pole-hooks to catch a plank if the hinge gives way.  
• Market: Trade rolls in on bone-wheeled wind sleds crewed by out-plateau merchants. They bring copper blades, dew-catch nets, and the rare vine-rope. In return they carry away cakes of rare earth salts, laminated kelp parchment, and mirror-crab shell mosaic prized for lining the shadowed cisterns of Khar Teles.  

TECH & CULTURE  
• Stillwrights: Artisans who tune the black-glass condensers. A master stillwright can focus glare so finely she etches clan-runes on a grain of salt.  
• Brine-Quorums: Governance is fluid; every adult owns a measure-stick carved with notches. When a decision looms, citizens dip sticks into a communal vat. Salt crust accretes faster on hotter sticks; the thickest crust carries the vote—heat equates to personal stake.  
• Child-Pilgrims: The census rule of Khar Teles sends orphans down-slope; Ulûn Gavir answers with its own tradition. Each adopted plateau child gifts the town a ledge-quarried gutter-stone. Those stones ring the central square, channeling precious rainfall—rare, but it comes once in a decade—into the grandest still of all. Thus the high place and the low bind water fates.

RULES OF THE WORLD DOWN HERE  
1. Never trust a shadow—it may sit on a bubble thin as eggshell.  
2. All tools belong first to the stills; borrow a blade, return two hours’ labour in condenser upkeep.  
3. Bleed no creature after midday. Blood evaporates before it hits the ground and the Flats taste every lost drop, calling lightning from clear sky.

DYNAMICS & TIME  
Nothing is stationary. The crust shifts, the town migrates, even the very law of the quorum depends on the fever of each hand. Change is the constant counter-rhythm to the plateau’s near-stasis. Yet cycles persist: dawn boil, noon glaze, dusk crack, night breathe. People here read those beats like drum notation.

STANDING STILL  
Mid-afternoon, atop the watch scaffold, the land is a single sheet of hammered silver. Heat ripples turn distant walkers into wavering reeds. You feel, rather than hear, the drone of glass-rushes, a pressure behind the teeth. Far overhead, a vapour kite tilts its wings to catch a stair of air—the same column that will brush Khar Teles by morning, there to bead on mirrored lichen cups. Somewhere between this glare and that dim loft hangs the thread of water that stitches both worlds. You could almost pluck it, then, like a harp string whose note neither place could survive missing.

CONNECTIONS – UNWRITTEN BUT LIVED  
• The wind that dries Ulûn Gavir is the breath that waters Khar Teles.  
• The gutter-stones set by plateau adoptees steer what little rain falls here; the salt-parchment maps painted in Ulûn Gavir ride caravans back up-slope, teaching cliff-dwellers new paths around fresh collapses.  
• A mirror-crab shell, once inlaid on a Khar Teles shield, scatters glider echolocation, and so a soldier from the Flats may one day save a ledgemate half a world above.  

Thus dryness feeds drowning, salt pays for dew, and two places that will never meet eye-to-eye nonetheless share a single, looping breath.
