The Glassroot Grove  

Follow the meltwater that escapes the high hollow’s chiselled bowls.  
It drips, gathers, grows bold enough to choose a single fracture, then drops for a vertical kilometre through the mountain’s bones. All the while it keeps the hollow’s signature in solution—silica dust, ghost-manganese, the faint electric memory of the Choir-Stones’ song.  
Where the fault finally widens to daylight, the water reappears as a pale, steaming ribbon, and everything it soaks turns half-mineral, half-alive. The Grove begins exactly at that breakout: a forest that remembers, in every shining root tip, the bright cathedral of stone far above.

1. Geography and substrate  
•  A hanging valley no broader than a river meadow, tilted like a dish toward the morning sun.  
•  Bedrock is the same carbonate as the hollow’s, but here it lies burrowed by springs. Each vent armours itself with chalcedony, building terraces of milk-blue, rimmed pools that step downhill like a frozen waterfall.  
•  Soil is thin, copper-scented, shot through with black manganese grit. Every rain cuts new runnels; every drought fuses them to porcelain.

2. The Glassroot Trees  
   (primary architects of the place)

   Form  
   •  Outwardly they resemble yews: trunk girth seldom more than a pair of human arms, needles short and wax-dark.  
   •  Below ground, roots drink water so charged with silica that the first metre of xylem petrifies during growth. The living cambium keeps pushing, sheathing the stone core in fresh wood. After two centuries a mature tree carries a translucent column from taproot to crown. Locals call this the “spine-glass.”

   Physiology  
   •  The glass core vibrates when struck by sub-aerial tremors—exactly the piezo tones the Choir-Stones leak into the fault each thaw. The resonance travels tree to tree through root grafts, turning the whole stand into a slow, arboreal seismograph.  
   •  At each pulse the trees shunt a breath of sugars toward their twig tips, a timing aid for leaf flush and cone drop. Thus the mountain above dictates the forest’s calendar without a single beam of direct sight.

   Visible effects  
   •  In full night the trunks faintly glow blue where the living tissues overlay vibrating stone—phosphorescent lichens (unique to the Grove) feed on compounds the cambium exudes when flexed. From afar the valley looks strung with beads of low fire, echoing the hollow’s ember-traced pillars.  
   •  Fell a tree carelessly and the shattered core sings like a gong, a warning the rest of the stand will feel and remember in sugar flux for seasons.

3. Micro-life: The Dowsing Mycelium  
   (symbiont of root and stone)

   •  Filaments thinner than frost wire lace the upper soil, etching patterns identical to the Threaded Ones’ networks in quartz.  
   •  They harvest the same tiny swings in redox potential but specialise in manganese rather than iron.  
   •  When a spring shifts course, the first hint is always a blush of violet mycelium crawling upslope, flagging new mineral water for young seedlings. Foresters watch these stains the way shepherds watch sky for weather.

4. Fauna in the glassshadow  

   Mirror Stags  
   •  Mountain elk that descend each rutting season to lick the mineral pools. Silica plates their antlers layer by layer until every tine is mother-of-pearl. In combat they clash like chandeliers—crashes audible kilometres away.  
   •  After the rut, most ascend again. The few that winter over leave cast antlers that poachers prize as tuning forks; struck right, one can wake resonances in bedrock and lure stags as surely as bugling.

   Echo Moths  
   •  Wings powdered with quartz dust gleaned from trunk scars. When disturbed they scatter, scales rattling in ultrasound bands that bats find painful. Predation here relies on acoustics more than chase.

5. Light and weather  
•  Mist rises whenever warm valley air meets the terraces’ snowmelt. Sunlight shards through droplets, refracting off the trees’ stone hearts; every dawn the Grove fills with white rainbows that shift as the Choir above finishes its morning thaw.  
•  Rain seldom falls hard—mountain shadow wrings storms dry before they cross the ridge—but drizzle may linger for days, beads sliding down trunks as though the wood itself weeps glass.

6. Soundscape  
•  Midday: a hush like a held breath; only water trickle and the occasional antler chime.  
•  Twilight: as temperature gradient steepens, the entire forest enters whisper mode—roots pick up the hollow’s evening note, trunks answer in sub-aural thrum you feel in cartilage more than ear.  
•  Once a generation the Choir-Stones above suffer a slabfall that retunes half the labyrinth. The Grove responds hours later with a sustained, wavering moan that sends birds into frantic sky-spirals and drives elk to their knees. Valley chronicles date years by these “Tree-Laments.”

7. Human presence  
   The Resin-Readers  
   •  A matriarchal guild from the same villages that dispatch the Bonefire Pilgrims. While their silent kin climb, the Readers descend, carrying hollow reeds and small pots of beeswax.  
   •  Each seeks a bleeding seam on an elder tree where living wood meets stone core. They press wax into the slit, capturing exuded sap that stiffens around glass grains.  
   •  Once cast, the plug is withdrawn and shaved into wafers. Under lamplight the silica needles inside align like grooves on a record. Readers stroke them with horsehair bows, translating resonance patterns into weather-verses that guide planting and flood-ditches down-valley.  
   •  A tithe of every harvest therefore traces back—by sap, by song, by trust—to the hollow’s inaudible choir.

   Taboos  
   •  No metal axes; only obsidian or bone may prune. Iron’s magnetic hiss muddles the trees’ listening.  
   •  Any antler taken must be sung to silence: strike until it no longer rings, or the stag that grew it will wander the next year deaf to rut calls and die icy, alone.

8. Exchanges between high hollow and Grove  
•  Water ferries silica downward, but information travels both ways. Vibrations the Grove’s roots send back along the fault finetune the Choir-Stones, a feedback loop tens of kilometres long yet only milliseconds slow in crystal terms.  
•  Pilgrims break slate for hearths; fragments wash into the springs and seed rare clay that clogs vents. When a vent chokes, a Glassroot sapling above it grows woodier, less glassy—nature’s note that too many fires have been lit aloft.  
•  Every few centuries, a Mirror Stag falls while climbing and its antlers tumble all the way to the hollow. Lodged among pillars, they add fresh overtones to the Choir, the forest’s gift repaid with interest.

Thus mountain, hollow, Grove and the villages cupped below read and write one another in water, in vowel-less resonance, in the slow penmanship of stone turned living and back again. Stand where spring meets root, touch glass that once was melt that once was frost that once was starlight, and you can feel the whole arrangement breathing—one long organism, gated by altitude, counting out eras in pulses we mistake for weather and call, for lack of any finer word, the world.
