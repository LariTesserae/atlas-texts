The Spillway Reaches

Put your back to the newborn basin and follow the water that overflows its mirrored bowls. The drainage braids into a single underriver, slides beneath a curtain of basalt scree, and resurfaces four days’ hike north-north-east as an inland sea only eleven kilometres wide but two hundred deep: the Spillway Reaches.

Terrain
Imagine a crater punched through the planet’s crust and never healed. The Reaches sit in that hole: steep, concave walls of black-green basalt give way to ledges of pumice and finally to a central eye of water so dark it reflects nothing. The circular shoreline is interrupted at cardinal points by four promontories—collapsed lava tubes now calcified into white ribs. Between them, talus slopes cascade right into the waves, each rock slick with the same tannic ooze that soaked your boots in the basin. Here, though, the ooze is aerated; step wrong and the ground exhales sulphurous foam that hisses back down like a boiling tide.

Materials
Basalt still rules, but it has been alchemised by pressure and mineral seep into something glassier, almost metallic. Strike two stones together and they sing a high F; excess resonance chips them into hexagonal scales prized by field cartographers as makeshift reflectors. Floating rafts of pumice the size of city blocks drift slowly clockwise in the lake’s gyre. Beneath them hang curtains of precipitated iron, orange as rust, feather-light, and so porous they ring like wind chimes whenever a wave passes.

Atmosphere & Light
The basin’s steel sky becomes, over the Reaches, a lens: cloud arcs rise higher here, pulled into a gentle spiral by the lake’s thermal column. Mid-afternoon, the sun—still hidden—backlights that vortex so that it glows platinum around a hollow core. What little direct light breaks through strikes the water at such an oblique angle that it refracts under the surface, illuminating the depths instead of the air. Stand on the pumice edge and you’ll see red-gold ropes of algae kilometres down, burning like fuse wire in the dark.

Sound
Wind never gets a clean shot. It ricochets off the crater wall, bounces across pumice barges, and returns as a surround-sound murmur. The lake itself supplies percussion: every few minutes a submerged gas pocket collapses, sending up a bass drum you feel in the soles of your feet. Above that rolls the distant contrapuntal thunder of the ridge turbines back in the basin—an echo conducted through the shared stone crust.

Vegetation
Nothing terrestrial grows higher than your knee. Instead life festoons the pumice rafts in vertical gardens. Stalk kelps, usually oceanic, root here and dangle into open air like inverted green cathedrals. Their fronds are studded with photosensitive nodules that iris shut in bright flashes from aurorae. On the crater wall cling ribbonferns: metallic-blue strips that curl into fists when humidity drops, squeezing out droplets of distilled water that trickle down to recharge the lake.

Fauna
1. Flume Swifts  
   • Bat-bodied, bird-winged, able to brake mid-dive by snapping open webbed tails.  
   • They skim thermal updrafts exiting the central water chimney, scooping steam-condensed ions straight through membranous cheeks enriched with copper. At night they glow like banked coals.

2. Pitchback Eels  
   • Transparent skin reveals muscular fibres in spirals; they store torsion like springs.  
   • When startled they uncoil all at once, launching backward at thirty metres per second—hence the name.  
   • Their brains contain magnetite braids identical to midge larvae from the basin, implying a shared ancestor flushed downriver.

3. Pumice Hermits  
   • Crustaceans that wear whole hand-sized stones as armour and ballast.  
   • They slot blueweft-coated pebbles into natural sockets on those stones; the lichen strobes at night, signaling territories across floating real estate.

Built Presence & Technology
A single human installation squats on the northern rib: the Catwalk Array. Picture a necklace of telescoping steel bridges that extend, retract, and pivot with the whim of software tuned to wind and wave. Each bridge carries sensor pods, solar sails, and at its tip a dangling geophone dropped a thousand metres down. The Array’s job is to listen for fractures in the young crust that might one day let the Reaches drain catastrophically into the mantle.

Power comes via braided superconductive cables run straight from the ridge turbines. Their hum is louder in the cables than in open air; stand on a catwalk plate barefoot and it tickles the arches like distant thunderstorm static.

Dynamics
• The lake breathes. Every six hours geothermal burps well up through vents in the bed, raising the surface by half a metre, then subsiding.  
• The pumice rafts rotate with the gyre, completing one circuit every forty-three hours. Cartographers time messages to travel raft-to-raft, piggybacking on predictable rendezvous.  
• Auroral currents occasionally whip downward in filament arcs, touching the water and flash-boiling shoals of eels; the smell is startlingly floral.

Rules of the Reaches
1. Nothing is anchored; respect drift or be carried with it.  
2. Gravity is negotiable when the vents exhale—walk light, rope heavy.  
3. Magnetism is moodier than weather; compasses lie, but lying in the same direction can still form a map.  
4. Silence can mean the lake is holding its breath. When every sound dies, so should your movement.  
5. The Reaches remember upstream news; blueweft clicks against pumice hulls like distant rain—listen for changes in the rhythm: it is the basin speaking through spores and surf.

What It Feels Like to Linger
Settle on a catwalk as twilight steals the crater rim. The sun never shows, but the sky thins enough that starlight pierces the cloud lens and paints pinholes onto black water. Below, the lake glows from within—red ropes of algae tracing thermal columns, amber sparks of flume swifts circling up, silver mirrors where pumice plates catch stray lightning. All of it slow-turns, a planet inside a planet.

Stay still long enough and the basin’s turbines re-enter your hearing, their bass notes tunneling through solid basalt. They harmonise with the lake’s drumbeats until you can no longer tell which sound began first. Somewhere in the blend, eels coil and release, catwalk joints creak as they pivot to face a newborn wind, and blueweft on the crater wall ticks like geiger static, counting off seconds in magnetic pulses.

Connections Back to the Basin
• The underriver funnels spores, larvae, and dissolved metals downstream; what germinates on the pumice is a curated export from the basin’s chaotic broth.  
• Power flows upstream: surplus energy from the Reaches’ geothermal vents is inductively beamed by the Catwalk Array to augment ridge turbines during polar night.  
• Clickhopper pelts—waterproof and frictionless—are traded covertly to Array technicians, who sew them into glove liners in exchange for satellite-band stories broadcast into the basin fog.  
• Both places fear the same unnamed swimmer: sonar pings from the Array have registered a void the size of a crawler moving between vents, then vanishing toward the underriver mouth. Basin cartographers call it the Patient Thing; Array logs list it as Echo-Void Seven. The lake records and the marsh anecdotes align within a margin of twenty seconds.

To leave
Unclip from the catwalk, step onto a pumice barge, and feel it rock underfoot. The motion is the same heartbeat that sloshed water against your boots days ago up in the basin. Distance here is only how far sound has to travel to find you again. Wait for the gyre to carry you to the southern promontory. When you climb the slope and crest the rim, you will see, on the horizon, the faint auroral blink of ridge turbines answering the blueweft’s tide-pulse. Pull your hood up—this land still does not know the word quiet—and walk on toward whatever part of the world starts speaking next.
