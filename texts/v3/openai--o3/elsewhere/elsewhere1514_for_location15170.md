The Saltglass Flats

Stand almost anywhere on the island’s western headland and look upslope: you will glimpse a pallid tableland framed by gull-wing ridges, bright as sun-struck armor even under cloud. That is the Saltglass Flats. From the basalt bell they lie two kilometres inland and one hundred and forty-three metres higher, linked by a single artery: the same fracture that booms CHONK below extends upward as a pressure vein, its breath rising through porous rock until it arrives here as invisible exhalations of brine-charged air. The grotto is the heart-chamber, the Flats the lung.

Geometry, first impressions  
• Area: roughly six hectares, a warped hexagon draped across a saddle between twin volcanic cones.  
• Surface: not sand, not ice, but a crust of sodium-iron silicate only three millimetres thick, grown from spray that has climbed internally, evaporated, and crystallised in place. It looks like frosted glass poured onto black scoria.  
• Under-skin: a honeycomb of fissures—air vents—that open and shut every fourteen seconds in perfect synchrony with the bell’s CHONK. Viewed from above by drone, the Flats appear to breathe: each pulse lifts the crust a centimetre, flexes it, then lets it fall with a sighing hiss.  

Light and heat  
High on the saddle nothing obstructs the sky. Noon sunlight arrives raw, splits into rainbow shards where it meets saltglass facets. The crust behaves as a giant planar reflector; even overcast days are dazzling. Temperature swings violently: 4 °C predawn, 28 °C at the peak of solar bounce. Yet the air never stills. The rising brine plumes draft upward, drawing a constant outbreath that steals heat off every surface. Stand on the Flats and you will feel chilly ankles beneath a sunburnt face.

Moisture economy  
Rain rarely falls; condensation is king. Each pressure pulse forces humid air through sub-millimetre pores, and when that air meets cool dawn glass it sheds tears. Droplets bead, roll, and vanish through micro-rills before the next pulse arrives. Thus water exists only in motion, a perpetual shuttle from rock lung to open sky back to crust. Anything living here must capture it mid-flight.

Sound  
There is no thunderclap like CHONK—distance has damped the waveform—but the pulse is still audible: a two-note exhalation, fffff-tch, every fourteen seconds. First a soft roar as gas escapes, then a brittle tick when the crust flexes. Migrants who come from the grotto recognise the metre at once and fall back into its cadence like an old habit.

Rules of the Flats  

1. Surfaces lie. What looks frozen could shatter underfoot; what looks solid could fold like parchment at next exhale.  
2. Fluid outruns solid. Brine vapor travels upward faster than any creature can scurry across the glass; chasing scent trails is pointless—every plume resets in under a minute.  
3. Colour only matters if it moves. The ground is white-gold, the sky endless; predators key on motion, not hue. Camouflage is choreography.  
4. Energy lands from above (light) but hydration arrives from below (vapour). Most organisms are bifacial: one side drinks sun, the other drinks breath.

Who (and what) inhabits the Saltglass Flats

1. First Forgers: chemists of the crust  
   a. Halothread cyanobacteria (Chloro-capillus vitreus)  
      • Live interleaved inside the saltglass, a living lacework that glows turquoise when back-lit.  
      • Each pulse sucks new brine into microchannels; the threads split Na⁺ from Cl⁻, hoard chlorine as energy battery, vent sodium as nacreous dust.  
      • When gusts exceed 40 km h⁻¹ they extrude filaments through pinholes, knitting wind-borne nets that later fossilise, thickening the glass.  
   b. Ferrox flakes (Oxydulcis lamina)  
      • Rust-red bacterial sheets floating between crust and stone. They feast on iron blown up the fracture, secreting an exopolymer stiff enough to act as hinge during each flex.  
      • Without them the saltglass would crack wholesale; they are the silent carpenters of every joint line.

2. The Harvesters: plants that are also sails  
   a. Prism lichen (Diaphora heliospin)  
      • Upper cortex made of interlocking triangular cells that rotate—yes, rotate—two degrees per pulse, tracking sun angle to prism-split light onto a photophore layer beneath.  
      • Their growth pattern etches moving rainbows across the Flats at dawn, signalling pollinators (see Spin-gnats).  
   b. Vapor-loom sedge (Mesoristra pendula)  
      • Not rooted. Each clump anchors with a silk ball secreted by symbiotic mites; during midday pulses the stalks let go, loft on thermals, and re-adhere metres away by evening.  
      • Leaves are hair-thin ribbons lined with hygroscopic scales. They comb the up-breath for moisture, then curl into spirals to squeeze it down to the rhizome.  

3. Quicksilver Fauna: bodies written for glass  
   a. Spin-gnat (Gyrodysis iridescens)  
      • Wingbeats exactly fourteen per second; synchrony with the ground pulse lets them hover motionless relative to crust while air streams upward.  
      • Compound eyes coated in calcite microprisms that split polarised rainbow from prism lichens; they read colour-code trails to nectar wells.  
   b. Glass-skater spider (Vitrellica reflexa)  
      • Legs end in spatulate pads laced with van der Waals nano-setae; they can stand on saltglass as if on water.  
      • Hunts by shadow. Spider positions opposite the sun; any creature that breaks its own silhouette against blinding ground becomes outlined, pounced upon.  
   c. Pulse-kite gecko (Aeroplethon saltator)  
      • Full length: 9 cm. Skin translucent jade with salt crystals embedded for UV protection.  
      • Patagial membranes unfold between ribs; every upward breath lifts them, every shut snap drops them—a living yo-yo. They feed mid-air on spin-gnats, land only to molt.

4. Apex Uncertainty  
   a. Mirrorgeist falcon (Falco speculor)  
      • Only predator large enough to cast a ground shadow big as a dinner plate.  
      • Feathers microetched to scatter specular flashes that confuse geckos’ depth sense.  
      • Hunts in silence except for crust-tick echo as it stoops; victims often freeze on cue, giving the bird a stationary target.  
   b. Salt-glass python (Serpens lentona)  
      • So pale it is invisible unless it moves. Scales carry periscope pores down to a second, subdermal retina; the snake sees without opening its eyes, useful against glare.  
      • Feeds exclusively on pulse-kites that mis-time their landing and fracture the crust, dropping into the honeycomb below where the python waits coiled.

5. Stationary Clockworks  
   a. Pressure bells (Campana oscillans)  
      • Cup-shaped calcite concretions that ring when the crust lifts, a clear E5 note. Each bell’s pitch correlates with cavity volume; field researchers map sub-surface architecture by listening to chords at sunset.  
   b. Brine-chimney stalagmites (Halopyrus spina)  
      • Grow upward from fracture vents at two millimetres per month, always spiral-twisted clockwise—why, no one knows. When a chimney breaches the surface it detonates silently into powder; the cycle starts anew.

6. Micro-ghosts and transients  
   a. Ratchet swift overflights  
      • Juveniles from the basalt bell practice thermalling here; the wide bright ground trains their echo-rangefinding for open-sky hunting. Their triplet click mixes with pressure bells, a duet of places remembering each other.  
   b. Dust-salmon spores (of Pulse-trumpet fungus)  
      • Once a year, during the same super-moon that empties spores from the grotto, wind rivers carry micron-scale diaspores upslope. Most perish in the aridity; a hardy subset germinate as orange pustules under glass overhangs, adding rare dabs of colour.

Ecological circuit between Grotto and Flats  

• Ion trade: Iron oxidised by FeOx bacteria below drifts upward as aerosol, becomes the limiting micronutrient for Halothread cyanobacteria.  
• Acoustic clock: The 14-second master beat keeps both communities in lockstep; a salamander in the bell twitches to the same metronome that lifts a gecko here.  
• Migratory corridor: Ratchet swifts, juveniles to adults, carry gut-encysted mites from grass blades upward; the mites disembark to weave sedge anchor threads, closing a symbiosis loop neither plant nor arthropod could manage alone.  
• Memory of water: When equinoctial tides finally drown the grotto—as we know they will—the surge will back-flush through the fracture, jetting mineral-rich water into the Flats. Models predict a glass-shattering event, after which the saddle will glaze anew in thicker, iron-tinged crystal. In that rebooted landscape, lion’s share of grotto spores may find purchase, re-founding pieces of the drowned enclave upslope. Thus extinction down-there can become genesis up-here.

If you linger  

Kneel. Press your palm flat to the saltglass. On the thirteen-count you will feel a warmth, on fourteen the breath—cool, subtle, insistent—pushing into your skin as though the ground itself were exhaling through your hand. Light will dazzle from a thousand bevelled mirrors; close your eyes and listen instead: soft roar, brittle tick, far-off falcon scream, the bell’s E-note, perhaps the faintest echo of the cavern-boom sliding up the vein. Stand again and every horizon shimmers; it is not heat-haze but the prism lichens tilting, tilting, tilting. Between pulses nothing seems to move, yet everything is poised on a hinge about to spring. The Flats are alive in a thinner, harder register than the basalt bell, but they share a breath, a clock, a memory of being hewn yesterday from the same young rock. Remain until dusk and you will understand the island by its two heartbeats: one loud and wet below, one bright and glassy above, both turning the world exactly once every fourteen seconds.
