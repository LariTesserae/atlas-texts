The Plateau of Falling Glass

1. Arrival  
From the western rim of the Basin a traveller climbs a stair of frozen lava ropes. Each step thins the breathable hush until, without noticing the instant, you emerge onto a tableland of blinding glitter. At first it looks like snow—heaps and drifts stretching five hundred kilometres to the next horizon. Then sunlight shifts, and every drift flashes green-white flame. You are ankle-deep not in water ice but in the same ammonium-chloride needles the Basin exhales each dusk. Ninety percent of the nightly dust-aurora settles here, trapped by a counter-clockwise gyre that rings the plateau like a moat of standing air. The place is an end-station for the Basin’s breath; where the Basin inhales and exhales, the Plateau simply receives.

2. Surface and structure  
Beneath the glassfall lies the original crust: a buckled shield of impact shatter rock uplifted when the Basin’s asteroid rebounded. Fractures run radial and clean, making natural avenues six metres wide and kilometres long. Between them, the drifts mound into dunes that creep no faster than human fingernails grow, driven by the lightest of afternoon breezes. Each needle packs with piezoelectric charge; walk too quickly and your footsteps spark blue in the corners of your eyes, silent Saint Elmo’s fire that smells of swimming pools and burnt sugar.

3. Light and heat  
Days are harsher here. Without the Lithotrellis canopy to sieve the sun, noon climbs past 50 °C. Yet night drops to –20 °C, and every thermocline cracks the needle crust with rifle-shot reports. These bangs are the Plateau’s heartbeat, as regular as the Basin’s tidal sigh, audible ten kilometres in the still air. At dawn the first beam touches a slope and half a million crystals realign at once, refracting a spear of emerald that can be seen from orbit for the three heartbeats it lasts.

4. The Saltwater Sky  
Humidity is almost nil at ground level, but five kilometres overhead a permanent nacreous cloud hovers, born of sublimating glass. The locals call it the Saltwater Sky. Tiny brine droplets polymerise around chlorine nuclei and fall as invisible drizzle that never reaches the floor—caught instead by a living mesh that hovers in the middle air.

5. The Levitat – architects of the middle layer  
Morphology: each organism resembles a floating kite twenty metres across, spun from a collagen-glass composite only nanometres thick. The body is a flattened torus; the central void traps warm air rising from the heated drifts. Veins of doped quartz generate electrostatic lift when flexed by sunlight.  
Colony life: Levitats hook their margins together edge-to-edge until they form continent-sized quilts, an aerial reef that graze the Saltwater Sky for trace water. Every droplet caught is shunted to nodules where endosymbiotic halobacteria split it for hydrogen and oxygen, venting the latter in faint halos that curtain the clouds purple at dusk.  
Reproduction: When a Levitat patch has hoarded enough hydrogen in its gas bladders, it spools out an anchor of protein wire. The first night-wind strong enough snaps the patch free; it drifts beyond the plateau, seeding distant deserts with pearl seeds that hatch only if they find the right sky chemistry—most never do.

6. The Ground-Dwellers  
a. Glass Voles (Family: Vitreomyidae)  
Size: rabbit-small, mass only 300 g. Fur replaced by overlapping mica scales that lens sunlight onto solar-enzymatic pads along the spine.  
Diet: pure crystal. Specialized molars grind needles into powder; gut symbionts extract trace nitrates and excrete perfectly faceted sub-micron prisms that roll like ball bearings, lubricating vole burrows.  
Water economy: zero. Metabolism routes liberated hydrogen straight to fuel cells; metabolic exhaust is dry hydrochloric gas that vents through cranial chimneys, producing a faint whistle that warns rivals away.  
Relation to the Basin: voles migrate down fracture avenues once a decade when population peaks, where Cloak Mantises harvest them as seasonal delicacies unheard of by the Striders.

b. Echo Owls (Genus: Sonuvia)  
Appearance: disk-bodied raptors one metre across, feathered in velvet to kill reflection.  
Hearing: facial horn arrays convert the Plateau’s crystal pops into a three-dimensional map of every dune’s creep. An owl can pinpoint a vole through two metres of glass by subtracting expected crackle from actual.  
Flight: they do not beat wings; instead they shunt stored electrostatic charge through expandable foil feathers, generating silent ion-winds. A hunting dive glows lavender and smells of ozone.  
Exchange with the Basin: during the Basin’s storm-bright auroras, owls perch along its rim to glean Threadfinches confused by the unfamiliar openness. In return they drop pellets rich in vole prisms that Lithotrellis roots covet as signal amplifiers.

7. The Deep Fracture Lakes  
Every hundred kilometres a radial crack widens into a slot canyon two kilometres deep. At the floors pressure and geothermal gradient intersect perfectly; here, for the first time since the asteroid struck, liquid water pools. The lakes are tar-black brines supersaturated with chloride salts and glistening with floating scum mats of metallic bacteria.

a. Ferric Lanterns  
Bacteria that build iron foil bubbles a millimetre thick. Each bubble traps the lake’s own chemoluminescent gases; at night the canyons glow iron red, strobing as bubbles collapse under their weight. Seen from the Basin’s highest domes, the canyons sketch red necklaces across the plateau.

b. The Rift-Whales  
Colonial aggregates of bio-film and discarded iron bubbles that have achieved both shape and motive power. Each whale is a raft fifty metres long that inches along the brine by electrostatic paddling, scooping Levitat fall-detritus that drifts down like snow. Their slow procession aerates the lake, feeding the Lanterns that in turn fabricate the bubbles the whales need. A ninety-year recursion of life, metal and light.

8. Weather – the Mirrorstorms  
Once every two plateau years (≈930 Basin days) the gyre slackens. Accumulated surface charge discharges skyward and a Mirrorstorm is born: sheets of glass needles lift in static updrafts, form a mid-level haze, then collapse in a glitter avalanche that races the entire circumference in thirty hours. The sound is a planetary rainstick; the Levitat quilts reef upward to ride the surge, and every vole that can digs a metre down.         If the wind catches you in the open your only hope is to huddle in a fracture, drum four quick raps—the Plateau code for “seeking shelter”—and trust that the resident voles will allow you to share their tunnel. Fail the code and you will be rasped to ribbons by a billion airborne spears.

9. Trade paths: the Woven Ascents  
Where Basin thoroughfares reach the rim, Lithotrellis trunks have grown into tight helical staircases that carry the patient walker onto the Plateau. These are the Woven Ascents. At their top each staircase flowers into a thicket of sail-balconies that harvest charge from approaching Mirrorstorms and funnel it down coppery sap-veins to ground roots. In exchange, Plateau organisms treat the Ascents as neutral ground: no owl hunts here, and voles leave untapped drifts that Striders can safely mine for crystal supplements rare in the Basin.

10. Two places, one breath  
• Every dusk the Basin exhales its green snow; every dawn that snow lands here.  
• Every noon the Plateau’s drifts heat, lofting deodorised chlorine back toward the Basin, where Lithotrellis lungs sip it for carbon exchange.  
• Signals, too, cross the height: when a Mirrorstorm kindles, crystals high in the Saltwater Sky polarise into a vibration identical to the Basin’s warning drum. Lithotrellis trunks five hundred kilometres away stiffen their sails, herds shift, mantises anchor deeper. The Plateau and the Basin speak in weather and listen in structure, each a half-diaphragm of the same planetary lung.

11. How to walk the Falling Glass  
1. Bind your boots with woven sail-cloth; bare rubber squeals against the needles and attracts Echo Owls.  
2. Store water as fat, not liquid. Any canteen will flash-rust open when the chlorine fog sneaks in.  
3. Learn the plateau code: one rap means “I climb,” two raps “I harvest,” three slow raps “I bleed.” Voles will approach a bleeding creature; they value salt more than life and will trade guidance for a lick of it.  
4. When night bangs like cannon fire, mimic the cadence with heel-taps. The plateau will count you as echo, not intruder, and let you keep your dreams intact.

Stay long enough and you will feel the symmetry: an endless forest that never sees the sun, an endless plain that reflects nothing else. One grows by inhaling stone and shadow, the other by drinking light straight and spitting it back as glass. Between them the wind commutes, carrying gossip in crystals and returning with whispers of shade. Two chambers, one chest. The planet breathes through both, and so might you—if you learn which silence belongs to which sky.
