The Sunless Tarn  

1. Approach – down the Twelve-Chord Cascades  
Follow any spillway at the High Flood’s western rim, keep the cataract on your right, and let gravity choose. The sheet of lilac water that veils the precipice plunges three vertical leagues before it disperses into vapor and rain; the spray threads itself through a spiral of open shafts—sound-pipes cut so precisely that a falling droplet strikes them in a sequence of tempered notes. You descend among music, ice-cold and unbroken, until all light from above fades to graphite. Your last clear footing is a ring-wall of basalt glazed glassy by ages of back-splash. Beyond it yawns the Tarn. You will not see its water until you have touched it: the lake swallows sight whole.  

2. Basin and boundary  
Imagine the continent-sized mesa of the High Flood inverted into the planet’s crust, a caldera fit for a sleeping moon. The Tarn fills that hollow to within a kilometre of the lip. Its surface is perfect black not because it is opaque but because it is mirror; even a torch held to the water shows only the torch, tier on tier, thinning into a starless recursion. Around the lake’s shore stand hundreds of cyclopean buttresses—roots of the terrace above—each crusted with mineral plates the width of ships. They arc overhead like ribs, holding the weight of a drowned sky.  

Between the buttresses run coves and grottoes crusted with salt lace. Inward of the salt comes the slag zone where the lake has chewed stone into slag-spires and slag-arches, each feathered by capillaries of crystallised manganese. The ground underfoot crunches like charcoal; kick it and you uncover embers that have forgotten heat but not colour.  

3. Water, light, horizon  
The Tarn is not merely dark; it is photoscopic—swallowing most wavelengths, reflecting only the spectral edge of violet. As a result the perpetual dusk inside the basin is stained midnight blue, and distance behaves oddly: a torch on the far shore looks a handspan away until you begin the hour’s paddle to reach it.  

Every ninth sunset—timed to the ten-day storm that wrings the High Flood—columns of tepid vapor climb the cavity walls and strike the colder ceiling rock. A false aurora blooms there: sheets of ionised mist that paint the vault with liveries of indigo and sick gold. Their reflections in the Tarn spin slow spirals, two heavens rotating in contrary silence.  

4. Weather, tide, and mechanics  
No wind moves below the lip; nothing stirs but convection. Yet the Tarn breathes. At intervals too irregular to chart, its surface humps up in concentric swells a metre high, though no shore feels the wave. Those bulges coincide with fresh deluges from the Flood above, suggesting a pressure valve deep under the water that opens, exhales, and seals. In the slack between breaths, the mirror lies iron-still: throw a pebble and the rings reach the far bank undiminished.  

5. Sound  
Absence is the keynote. The only constant tone is a subsonic pulse like a giant heartbeat—one beat every thirty seconds, tested with seismic pendulums and fixed in no cartographer’s grid. Travellers learn to set camp by it: boil water on the first thump, drink on the second, sleep before the third. Any louder noise—rockfall, shouted name—seems stolen, flattened into hush the instant it begins.  

6. Chemistry  
Water from the High Flood arrives oxygen-rich and mineral-poor. In the Tarn it gathers iron, lithium, rare selenium salts eroded from mantle rock. The result is a liquid more conductive than any sea above. Dip copper into it and blue sparks jitter around the metal for minutes. Strike flint and the spark dies an arm’s length from the stone, quenched not by wetness but by charge.  

7. Inhabitants – visible  
a. Mirror skates  
Bodies wafer-thin, edges serrated into micro-fins. They travel the underside of the surface, bellies brushing the meniscus. When a skate feeds it flicks a wing, shattering its own reflection into a lattice of prisms; stunned plankton drift down in glittering ropes, each immediately vacuumed by the mouth-slit. A frightened shoal can turn the lake to splintered silver, then fuse it smooth again in seconds.  

b. Lantern crèche  
Colonies of translucent bulbs a child’s height, rooted to the slag-spires just beneath the low-water mark. The bulbs house colonies of chemoluminescent algae that burn the Tarn’s metals into violet light. At hatching tide—one night in twenty—the bulbs rupture, releasing clouds of motile spores that spell lilac glyphs across the black, language too slow for the eye, too quick for the mind. By dawn the bulbs have regrown and the message is gone.  

c. Prism herds  
Nematic ribbons a metre long, jointed like centipedes. Each segment bears an eye-spot tuned to polarised light; the ribbons travel always at right angles to one another until they knot by the thousand into shimmering prisms that hang motionless in mid-water, catching the stray photons spilled by the Crèche. When hunger grows, the prism unknots and the ribbons slice whatever casting of shadow they can find, including careless swimmers.  

8. Inhabitants – invisible or nearly so  
a. Ghost divers  
Humans gone too long beneath. The Tarn’s salts chelate iron from blood, leaving skin colourless, bruise-pale. Their eyes silver over; pupils shrink to pinholes. They hunt heat and speech both, drawn by the taste of anxiety in breath. A diver’s knife is slate-grey bone, edge unchippable, taken from the jaw of something they will never name. They trade for nothing except silence: offer them a minute of it and they will fetch you through reefs impossible to chart awake. Break the minute and you will not feel the first cut.  

b. The Archive  
Not life, perhaps: rather a habit the water has learned. Any object submerged for more than a lunar month acquires a plating of Tlön-crystal, each molecule aligned to the object’s magnetic ghost. Lift the relic free and the plating shears away in hexagonal leaves that drift back down, murmuring with static. Collect enough leaves and you can play them like wax cylinders on a copper stylus: they repeat every sound, every tremor the lake has known since the last storm. What listens when no-one records is an unsettled question among basin scholars.  

9. The breathing vault – speculation  
Salvagers once lowered a chain of floats carrying microphones, thermistors, accelerometers. At ninety-five fathoms the line went slack though the float remained neutrally buoyant. Minutes later the subsonic heartbeat ceased; seconds after, the line shattered—no tension, just a crystalline snap. The Tarn stayed silent for three days, the longest pause on record. Then it breathed again, and the surface jumped high enough to wash salt off the buttresses. Theories range from magma chambers venting to a titanic hydraulic clock left by the same vanished builders who raised the High Flood. None explain why the pulse modulates, ever so slightly, in response to moonrise.  

10. What the Tarn gives the Flood, and what it takes  
Mist that rises from the water condenses against the caldera roof and escapes through vents into the upper atmosphere. Those plumes seed the lilac overcast that roofs the High Flood itself: the lake is the cloud’s deep lung. Minerals carried upward in that vapor fall again as faint metallic snow, the ferric sleet that feeds the plateau’s first bacterial plains. In exchange the Tarn drinks every drop the mesa sheds, scouring it clean. The two are mirror and negative, each eroding the other in a quiet, planetary breathing that nothing born on stone can arrest.  

11. Rules that hold here  
• Light refracts twice: once off the mirror, once off what it reflects. Distance is a liar.  
• Magnetism carries voice. Whisper with a lodestone in your hand and any floating iron filings will arrange themselves into cursive echoes.  
• Fire is a rumor. Flame shows grey, heat sinks vertical, embers glow only while watched.  
• Silence is coin. Break it near a ghost diver and you owe them a sound of equal weight.  

12. Leaving  
Climb before the ninth sunset or remain until the eleventh; between those times the vapor columns are too dense, the caldera walls slick as oiled glass. Guides knot rope into braided prisms whose angles cancel the Tarn’s optical tricks—follow their geometry upward. As you crest the lip, look back: you will see the whole vast mirror wearing the sky you stand beneath, a perfect counterfeit of day. Some travelers swear the reflection shows clouds not present, moons in wrong phase, strangers walking shores that should be empty. Blink and the image reverts, innocent as oil. The Tarn keeps its dreams where light cannot follow, breathing them out in temperatures and tides until the High Flood hears, and answers in water.
