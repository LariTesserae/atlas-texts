Pearl-Mire

A thread of water falls out of Lung-Moss’s cave, though no human eye follows the route.  It vanishes through pin-sized drains, stitches downward for twenty-seven kilometres, and resurfaces three days’ crawl south of the coastal plain, where the land crumples into a basin the colour of old bone.

1. The Terrain  
   • The basin’s floor is a quilt of travertine ledges, tier upon tier, each one no broader than a handspan, each one dripping into the next like a stack of shallow bowls.  
   • All ledges lean toward a single circular sink at the centre—​a throat ten metres wide whose rim smokes in the cold dawn.  
   • The rock is fresh-born: butter-smooth, butter-yellow, still warm in spots where mineral water sheets across it.  Lacework gutters, no thicker than handwriting, show where yesterday’s flow ran before today’s crust sealed it.  
   • To walk here is to crunch thin crusts that break and mend in the same hour; bootprints fossilise before the second step, the basin rewriting itself faster than memory.

2. Water (3.0/3 — everything)  
   • What empties Lung-Moss’s basins arrives here at last, enriched by dissolved limestone and the handful of pearls the cave exhales.  
   • The central sink coughs it upward in pulses, each pulse exactly eight heartbeats apart—​the distant echo of the cave’s breathing made liquid.  
   • Temperatures hover near boiling where the water erupts, but cool to finger-numbing within three ledges.  Steam fingers the air, condensing into a permanent, cloud-white canopy three metres above the terraces.  
   • Every overflow paints a new film of calcite, trapping whatever falls in: a leaf, a gnat, a breath of dust.  By moonrise each is stone.

3. Light (1.8/3 — milkbright)  
   • Sunlight reaches the basin diffused through the steam ceiling; day is perpetual dawn-glow, night a pearly dusk.  
   • The travertine itself glows faintly blue in darkness—​a resonance inherited from Lung-Moss’s pearls, trace ions coaxing chemiluminescence from the fresh crystal.  
   • No shadows are hard.  Figures blur within arm’s length, giving every movement the softness of dream.

4. Sound (0.7/3 — hush)  
   • The throat gulps and sighs, but the terraces swallow echo the way moss swallows footfall.  
   • Drip-music is high and glassy, notes falling only a pace or two before webbing into silence.  
   • After the storm-organ of the pocket-cave, Pearl-Mire feels like being wrapped in felt; travellers speak normally and hear themselves think for the first time since crossing the plain.

5. Temperature (1.4/3 — tepid)  
   • Air at ankle height steams; air at chest height chills; between the two a traveller’s torso slides through autumn, spring, and back again with each breath.  
   • Frost rims the basin’s rim every night, yet lichens two steps below stay warm enough to bead with dew.  It is a vertical climate staircase only swifts and insects climb with grace.

6. Vegetation (0.9/3 — inventing itself)  
   • No green yet, only creams and russets: pioneer films of iron-bacteria shimmer on brand-new rock, writing the first soil in crimson cursive.  
   • Lung-Moss pearls that survive the descent burst here into thin, coin-sized mats—​“Mirror-Moss,” the first offshoot generation.  They cannot build a cave; instead they tessellate flat against the ledges, tracing outlines of vanished splash marks.  
   • Where two mats meet, they fuse, forming the faintest raise—​the embryonic walls of future bowls.

7. Fauna (2.2/3 — inventive)  
   • Steam-Swifts: soot-grey birds no heavier than a paper scrap, wings sheened with hydrophobic oil.  They thread the mist canopy, eating midges that hatch in the warm plume.  
   • Travertine Crabs: blind, wafer-thin arthropods that etch burrows in still-soft calcite, only to be entombed hours later and dig out again by dawn.  They wear yesterday’s rock as armour and grow broader, not taller, to avoid being roofed in.  
   • One predator only: the Basin Lamprey.  It is mouth and ribbon, transparent as clue-glass, sliding through the overflow rills.  It rasps at Mirror-Moss mats, stealing the calcium before they can lock it down.

8. Built (0.0/3 — unborn)  
   • Nothing human lasts a night.  Peg a tent and the stakes calcify.  Drop a pack and wake to find it mortared to the shelf.  
   • A lone cairn stands on the northern rim—​three stones, no more.  Each is coated in a decade of drip, swollen into featurelessness.  Whoever set them has been edited out; only the intention of a marker remains, an idea fossilised in carbonate.

9. Density (2.4/3 — crowded, low-small)  
   • Every square handspan hosts micro-architecture: ribs, lips, dams, cups.  The space between is measured in capillary strands.  
   • Yet at human scale the basin yawns empty; it is all floor.  One can walk an hour in circles and meet no obstacle large enough to lean on.

10. Erosion and Accretion (3.0/3 — furious)  
    • The Mire grows upward the way mountains grow down.  A fresh skin of rock appears before the old has time to crack.  
    • Whenever flow lessens, shelves collapse under their own newborn weight, flipping like pages whose words are still being written.

11. Scale (1.1/3 — apparent)  
    • Seen from neighbouring ridges the basin gleams white, a moon crater filled with milk.  Its circumference: two kilometres, though travellers swear it shrinks and swells with weather.  
    • Descent shows the finer gears—​each shelf a fret in a giant, wet instrument.

12. Dynamic (3.0/3 — restless)  
    • Nothing here holds shape for a day.  Maps drawn at breakfast mislead by dusk.  
    • Pearls arrive, dissolve, reconstruct.  Steam-Swifts molt weekly.  Even the throat migrates centimetres each season, sawing through its own lip to start anew in denser rock.

Rules of the place:

A. All things seek the lowest meniscus; fight the flow and you fossilise.  
B. Weight is a promise to sink.  Stay light, keep moving, or be filed flat under the next travertine veil.  
C. What is quiet elsewhere is fuel here; sound carries no purchase, but a grain of silt can script a whole terrace.  
D. Remember nothing permanent.  The basin itself does not; it overwrites at the speed others breathe.

Who lives here is a colony, but not of swifts or crabs.  It is the Mirror-Moss—descendants of Lung-Moss, strangers to darkness yet heirs to its patience.

1. Inheritance  
   • Each mat begins as a pearl flung from the coastal cave, scoured downstream inside a droplet, surviving heat, pressure, and miles of rock.  
   • The pearl comes pre-programmed with the memory of echo, but here there is no roar, only lapping hush.  So it repurposes the inheritance: where Lung-Moss thickened in quiet, Mirror-Moss expands in stillness—​any puddle that lingers more than three breaths becomes continent.

2. Feeding  
   • Without ceiling-drip, the mats farm their own calcium.  They exhale micro-bubbles of CO₂ beneath the water film; each burst forces calcite to crash out of solution onto the mat’s rim, simultaneously fattening the colony and thinning the pool—​self-building levees.  
   • Trace organics from dead midges tint the outer margins tea-brown: a slow-motion bruise that shows where the richest food once flowed.

3. Sensory Life  
   • Pressure hearing is useless on flat stone, so the mats read temperature gradients instead.  Heat from the throat feathers outward; the mats map it like isobars, creeping toward the warmest quiet corners.  
   • A shadow—rare under steam—registers as instantaneous cool; mats retract in a ring, the travertine beneath them left bare, glassy, and faintly blue.

4. Motion  
   • They move by unzippering.  The inside edge detaches, floats a hairsbreadth on exhaled gas, and resettles half a thumb further downslope.  Propulsion is invisible except in time-lapse: a glacial chase toward the sink’s heat.  
   • If two colonies collide, their rims rise, duel, and the lower mat drowns—again, a war fought in carbonate, its casualties sudden cliffs a centimetre high.

5. Reproduction  
   • Instead of pearls, Mirror-Moss raises “lanterns”: hollow travertine bubbles that trap steam.  Once a bubble reaches walnut size it snaps free, lofted by its own vapour into the mist roof.  
   • Most burst, raining powder back down.  A rare few harden before escape, drift beyond the basin, and seed distant hot springs the world has yet to imagine.

6. Mind  
   • No generational memory yet—too young.  But already selection favours mats that heed vibration: footfalls send silver ripples across their surface; those that flatten quickest avoid being pried up by curious humans.  
   • Give them a millennium and they will write terraces that divert large creatures entirely, the way Lung-Moss reshaped echo.

7. Relations  
   • Steam-Swifts glean midges above the warm pools; Mirror-Moss earns nitrogen from their droppings, each speck a fertiliser pellet buried under the next carbonate glaze.  
   • Travertine Crabs gnaw at the mats’ edges, slowing their advance; in turn the moss thickens its rims, sealing crabs inside new rock layers.  A crab’s life is one long jailbreak.

8. On the Memory of Lung-Moss  
   • Chemical signatures in the mats still match the parent cave—​a fingerprint in strontium ratios that will let future chemists trace river back to seam.  
   • Eight-beat rhythms linger too: when the throat pulses, the mats vent gas in synchrony, exhaling little white rings that race across the pools like clockwork halos.

9. Hospitality  
   • Sit on a rim with feet in warm water and nothing worse than a sugar-glaze of calcite coats your boots.  Stand unmoving an hour and the glaze becomes a cast; move then and the shell cracks, leaving your outline for tomorrow’s travellers to puzzle over.  
   • Camp overnight and every peg, every pot, every sleeping body acquires a chrysalis.  Morning is breakout, or permanent inclusion.

10. Why Few Remain  
    • Maps cheapen; trails erase; equipment sinks.  Hunters, traders, pilgrims—​all learn after one visit that Pearl-Mire consumes the notion of ownership.  
    • Only the Steam-Swifts stay, writing their names in loops of warm air that no rock can hold.

Connection, in the end, is not a river but a metabolism.  What Lung-Moss exhales—water, pearls, the metronome of eight—Pearl-Mire inhales.  One lives by sanding sound into stone, the other by puffing stone back into air.  Between them, across darkness and steam, the same pulse passes: cave to channel, channel to basin, stone to moss to stone again.  Whoever walks the coast and finds the slit, or follows the mist and finds the basin, steps briefly into a living circulation older and surer than roads—an echo made liquid, a breath made visible, the world teaching its rock to move.
