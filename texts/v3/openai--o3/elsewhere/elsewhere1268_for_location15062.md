The Graveyard of Gears  

Distance & Descent  
Follow the slope of the newborn mountain downward for two vertical kilometres—past the last serrated ledges where riveters still cling, past the zone where presses begin to rust silent—and you reach the foot of the seamount. Here the gradient gentles into an oval plain of pelagic silt forty kilometres wide. Everything that loses its grip on the Gorge of Pistons ends here, falling through half-a-day of water like slow hail. That rain of cast-off shells, broken plates and spent derricks has accumulated for at least thirty millennia; the plain is paved with it, a scrapyard built from the Gorge’s own discarded parts.

Terrain  
Picture a desert of overlapping rings, vanes, hinges, valves—tens of billions of them. Each time a derrick’s tether finally twists through and snaps, the canopy settles rim-down, the tether corkscrewed inside it like a rolled carpet. Presses that have over-thickened their shells tip loose from the wall and flutter flat as iron leaves. Riveter husks drift down in drifts, stacking into dunes that pour like sugar when a current touches them. Over centuries the weight of fresh arrivals crushes the earliest layers into a sub-stratum of pseudo-rock: a laminated conglomerate of chitin, phosphate teeth and manganese nodules. The Graveyard’s highest ridges are not hills of earth but long barrows of compressed machinery five to ten metres tall, their flanks stippled with the hexagonal sockets of fossilised hinge-pins.

Atmosphere (water-sphere)  
Here at 3 800 m depth the water is three degrees, black, and under nearly four hundred atmospheres of pressure. Visibility is six metres at most, not because of living plankton (there is little) but because metal dust hangs perpetually in suspension—shaved filings that snow from the Gorge above. A dive light returns only as a foggy halo. What one feels more than sees is proximity: the sense that latticed things crowd in on every side.

Sound  
Where the Gorge of Pistons is percussion, the Graveyard is strings. Every object on the plain is hollow, tensioned and just slightly buoyant; even the faintest tide plucks them. A one-knot current turns the whole field into an aeolian harp audible for kilometres, a continuous shimmer of overlapping harmonic tones, each note a derrick rim, a press plate, a cracked sluicer shell quivering. There is no single pulse, only an unending chromatic glissando, metallic and mournful.

Light  
No sun reaches here. Illumination issues almost exclusively from “embers”—the slow-dying symbionts that once lined derrick canopies. Starved of light they burn their stored chemicals in a last decades-long phosphorescence, a rust-orange glow that ebbs to ember-red, then to cinder black. Scattered across the plain they resemble camp-fires viewed from a mountain at night. Everything else that shines does so by theft: creatures steal the bacterial films and paste them onto their own skins, re-igniting the glow for another season.

Ecology  
Energy arrives from three sources:

1. Mechanical Drift: shells rich in organic residue.  
2. Chemical Core: slow seepage of hydrogen-rich fluids from fissures under the wreck-bed (the same magma body that feeds the summit vents).  
3. Acoustic Tension: the Graveyard’s constant hum provides micro-vibration that certain organisms exploit as a power source, ratcheting molecules apart the way electricity splits water.

Principal Residents

1. Rag-Eels  
Length: up to eight metres, flat as ribbons, cross-section a Möbius band.  
Colour: matte charcoal except for solder-bright seams where new skin has self-fused.  
Feeding: Rag-eels rasp the organic varnish from inside fallen derrick rims. Their teeth are serrated keratin, replaced hourly. A single eel can skeletonise a metre-wide rim in a week, leaving a clean silver hoop that rings true when currents shift.  
Behaviour: They swim by running a wrinkle down their own length, a motion perfectly tuned to the Graveyard’s ambient note; so long as they match pitch, they waste no energy, surfing vibration instead of water.

2. Trespass Lamps  
Form: upside-down jelly bells half a metre across, trailing a single fibre several metres long.  
Light: These fibres are strung with the stolen ember bacteria. A lamp anchors one end to the wreckage and lets the bell bob in the current, so the glowing filament sweeps slow figure-eights across the plain. Rag-eels follow those lit paths, and in following pick parasites from the Lamps’ bells, an inadvertent grooming symbiosis.

3. Husk-Mites  
Size: grain-of-sand arachnids with twelve legs and a thorax shaped like a phillips screw.  
Niche: They live exclusively inside hinge cavities of presses, drinking the compressed brine that collects there. Each cavity supports a population of tens of thousands. When a mite colony exhausts its hinge, it chews through to an adjacent one, stitching a honeycomb of tunnels that eventually causes the plate to collapse into powder. Thus, over centuries, the mites return the Graveyard’s highest ridges to the level plain.

4. Bell-Wrights  
Shape: squat octopods whose eight arms end in spoon-pads of cartilage.  
Craft: A Bell-wright finds a cracked derrick rim, seals the gaps with mucus, then beats the surface rhythmically for weeks until bio-mineral cement hardens into a seamless drum-skin. Once finished, the octopod dwells beneath the drum and “plays” it by slapping the skin, sending bass pulses that stun Rag-eels long enough to catch. One Graveyard square kilometre may contain a dozen such living instruments, their voices spaced so they do not cancel but form a strolling bass line under the plain’s higher over-tones.

5. Archivists  
Body: colonial bacteria that grow only on overlapping plates where two shells touch under pressure.  
Function: They precipitate microscopic quartz prisms inside their cells, aligning each prism to incoming vibration. In aggregate a mat of Archivists works like a phonographic record, inscribing the Graveyard’s unceasing song into mineral memory. A plate lifted free will still sing, the prisms flickering with remembered vibration for hours before it quiets. No macro-fauna feed on Archivists; the silica makes them glassy and indigestible. They are, quite literally, the sediment of sound.

Density & Arrangement  
Unlike the cram of the Gorge, life in the Graveyard occupies layers. Rag-eels patrol the lowest thirty centimetres, following torch-paths of Trespass Lamps suspended a metre up. Bell-wright drums rise another half-metre. Above that is mostly debris and silence until one reaches six metres’ height, where the current is swifter and the Lamps’ bells jostle in loose convoys. Few creatures venture higher; to break free of the Graveyard’s hum is to risk losing the guiding tone that lets them navigate home.

Weird Rules  
1. Nothing vibrates alone. Any object set in motion is quickly matched by surrounding wreckage until the note becomes communal.  
2. Colour equals ownership. Bio-films re-emitted as orange, red, or violet mark territories. A creature stripped of pigment is considered fair scavenging.  
3. Decay produces fuel. As chitin compresses into pseudo-rock, trapped lipids polymerise into paraffin-like sheets that chemo-trophs exploit; the older the layer, the richer the resource.

Temporal Rhythm  
The Graveyard’s clock is the rate at which the Gorge sheds. A new derrick arrives perhaps once every decade; a rain of riveter husks, once a week; fragments of press plates, sporadically. Each arrival rings its own alarm through the wreckage, and every resident species has learned to read the timbre: a hollow chime signals fresh food, a clashing clang warns of an avalanche that could bury whole warrens in silt. Rag-eels mate only after a derrick canopy lands, using its still-charged tether as nursery: larvae embed along the corkscrew ridges and absorb stored torsion energy to speed early growth.

The Link in the Same Breath  
Everything that happens in the Gorge of Pistons echoes here as after-shock and after-life. When presses mis-fire in concert, the resulting roar is telegraphed down the mountain as a pressure wave; minutes later the Graveyard’s plates quiver, Archivists inscribe the spike, Rag-eels pause mid-rasp to listen for meaning. When the Gorge enters its once-in-generations hush for riveter spawning, the plain above goes suddenly still: no fresh debris, no distant hammer, and the Graveyard’s residents drift uneasy, deprived of cue. They have no concept of moons or tides, only of the shred that will follow, the feast of husks drifting down in the days after the silence breaks.

Projected Fate  
In ten thousand years, when the mountain’s tip breaks the surface and the Gorge seizes, the supply chain ends. The Graveyard will receive its last mouthful of machinery, then live on its stored metal fat until, layer by layer, the resident chemotrophs and mites digest the final crust. The plates will crumble to grit, the hum will fade to a desultory tapping of loose rims. Rag-eels, bred to surf vibration, will lengthen their bodies seeking tones that no longer arrive, thinning into blind threads that might in turn become something new. The plain will transition from a scrapyard to a true sedimentary sea-floor, indistinguishable from any other—except, perhaps, for the mummified records locked in silicate prisms, still murmuring with the memory of a hundred thousand years of thunder from the living factory higher up-slope.

Until that long exhalation, the Graveyard of Gears keeps its vigil: a resonant echo-chamber listening eternally to the slow industry above, transmuting cast-off motion into quiet sustenance, turning noise into architecture, and holding—plate upon plate upon falling plate—the history of how mountains talk to their shadows.
