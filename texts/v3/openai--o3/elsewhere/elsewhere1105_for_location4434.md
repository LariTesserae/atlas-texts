Mirrormarrow Estuary

A day’s hike downslope from the shale hill—though distance here is not best measured in kilometres but in the breaths of barometric tide that push you along—lies the Mirrormarrow Estuary.  The locals above-ground would call it an inland delta, but nothing that empties its rivers back into themselves deserves so ordinary a name.  The estuary is where the cavern’s impossible humidity ex­hales, condenses, and remembers what water used to be before it learned to float.

1. Approach – the corridor of stray dew  
Follow the valley crease that lines up with the cave’s entrance crack.  Just before dawn you will pass through a moving haze that smells faintly of cast-off gypsum and warm stone.  Those are last night’s water globes, burst aloft, their surfactant skins now drifting like transparent thistledown.  Where the haze touches leaf or coat-sleeve it repaints surface tension: droplets hang lens-perfect for hours, refusing to drip until midday chorus of insects convinces them gravity still applies.

2. Basin geometry  
The estuary is neither round nor dendritic but spiral, a loose nautilus traced in silt.  Five slow rivers wind inward, not outward, and their flow reverses twice every twenty-six hours, synchronised to the cavern’s pressure tide.  Stand on the outer bank and you can watch a pale current hesitating, then folding back upstream as though changing its mind.

•  Sediments: layers of chalky marl alternating with streaks of condensed travertine dust—identical in chemistry to the halo rings under the shale hill.  Each pressure tide lays a fresh filament of that dust, printing a fingerprint of the cavern onto the marshy floor.  
•  Mirror skim: the uppermost millimetre of water clots into a coherent film, flexible as eel skin.  Wind wrinkles slide across it without breaking; only the weight of a spoken name will puncture the surface.  (Fisherfolk whisper here for good reason.)

3. Light behaviour  
Dawn in Mirrormarrow begins twice.  First comes the sky-sun, silvering reeds and heron wings.  Moments later, a subterranean second sunrise fans across the water: the prisms beneath the shale slope finish ricocheting yesterday’s light and release it through faults as a wash of low-angled brilliance.  That phantom glare spirals along dissolved-ion paths until it meets real dawn, and for sixty heartbeats every object casts two shadows in different directions.  Plants have evolved to photosynthesise on both schedules.

4. Flora – the twin-sun botany  
•  Ribbon kelp (Gypsumis duplex): grows in flat coils just under the mirror skim, green by terran day, opalescent when the cavern-sun shines.  Its fronds braid themselves at night, storing torsion energy to uncoil and stir sluggish water when dawn returns.  
•  Spiral reeds: each culm carries a corkscrewed hinge two handspans above the mud.  When river-flow reverses, the reed swivels to face upstream, exposing one side rich in stomata to fresh nutrients.  The same hinge design appears in the bracing roots of the cave’s silver moss—evolution by correspondence.  
•  Lumen pods: blister-shaped galls on ribbon kelp that trap stray photons from the cavern-sun.  At full charge they glow like captive fireflies and detach, floating shoreward where marsh mice gnaw them for the faint caramel made of crystallised Prismata sugars.

5. Fauna – translators between realms  
•  Air-shrimp (Aeropenaeus ambulans): descendants of the cavern’s Blink-shrimp that rode exhalation plumes and moulted into denser air.  Their mist-cushions collapsed into paddle-legs; now they skate on the mirror skim in flickering schools, sipping kelp syrup.  
•  Silt-eels: blind but harlequin-patterned with piebald calcium oxalate plates—hand-me-down armour from the Root-thread fungus whose spores the eels carry in their gills, fertilising new fungal reefs in river mouths.  
•  Tide-herons: ivory birds no larger than a child’s kite.  Their beaks resonate at 45 kHz—the missing piece of the gutter-bats’ sonar band—and each predawn they walk the shallows, striking the mirror skin until it cracks and releases pockets of anaerobic shrimp for breakfast.

6. Phenomena – the estuary thinking in public  
•  Reversal hush: five minutes before each flow-change, wind, insect drone, even footfall noise dampen as though kneaded into the mud.  Fisherfolk claim this is the Cavern-Body listening for its own heartbeat returning through the faults.  
•  Grammar bloom: every few weeks the spiral rivers align with a new-moon barometric trough.  Travertine dust on the water arranges into ideograms—no two alike—then sinks.  Scholars bivouac on platforms to sketch the glyphs; linguists insist they are conjugations of verbs for “to become.”  
•  Glassfall: cumulative mirror skins torn by storm compile into sheets that beach on the inner shoals.  These crackle underfoot and settle into translucent shingles: a self-repairing quay where river-otters build dens that glint in moonrise.

7. Artifacts – ports of exchange  
On the concave shore stands a quay built of folded hexagonal tiles—the same geometry as the Clayfold in the cavern but here executed in giant limestone flags.  Pilgrims from hill villages drag the flags into new tessellations, creating a living market stall where no goods change hands, only properties: wetness traded for heat, weight for momentum, yesterday for tomorrow’s rumour.

•  Commerce: a potter carries dew-heavy clay; she leaves with clay bone-dry but glowing faintly for weeks.  A shepherd brings a broken ankle; he limps away carrying the pain in a flask, freeing the bone.  
•  Tax: for every bargain the quay exacts a whisper of certainty.  By season’s end the village tongues can no longer decide whether a year has 365 days or some friendlier, negotiable number.

8. Rules of the place  
•  Hydrology loops, it never drains.  Spill a cup and the liquid creeps back upriver overnight.  
•  Reflection is additive: look into the water and your mirror-self acquires whatever emotion you tried to hide.  The next person to peer in will see it and possibly keep it.  
•  Names rot quickly here.  Write one on driftwood and by next reversal only the vowels remain legible, the consonants having gone wading after the tide-herons.

9. Kinship with the under-hill chamber  
Everything the cavern exhales, Mirrormarrow inhales: moisture, travertine memory, larvae, stray laws of physics.  In return, the estuary’s refracted light spirals back through fissures, feeding the Prismata.  Thread-wolf filaments snagged on heron legs reach the marsh, where they fossilise into ropey ridges that guide floodwater away from nesting kelp—engineering by correspondence once again.  
If you stand knee-deep on an inner shoal at the very instant both suns set, you can feel a tug behind the breastbone: the cavern measuring your silhouette in absent light, deciding whether to open the shale slit in welcome—or to keep practising.

10. Departing the spiral  
Leaving Mirrormarrow is easier than leaving the cavern but costs more.  The rivers will grant you downstream passage only after you pronounce aloud something you have never admitted even in thought.  The estuary keeps such confessions as fare, tucking them beneath the mirror skin for the next traveller to borrow or mistake for their own idea.

And when you finally climb the far bank and look back, you may notice the entire basin has rotated a quarter-turn, as though adjusting a mirror to catch the cavern-sun a fraction better.  From your new angle the water spells a familiar shape—a slit just wide enough for a torso—before the tide sighs, folds the line into a spiral, and sets it drifting inland to where the world is already imagining a third place to hold the echo.
