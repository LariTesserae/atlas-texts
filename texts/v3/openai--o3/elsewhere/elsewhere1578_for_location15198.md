Coppervine Arboretum  

You reach it without noticing when the climb began.  
One of the rail spurs that loops the drowned cathedral simply keeps rising, curve after patient curve, until the water noise has thinned to a whisper and your boots ring dry on the grating.  The air warms by degrees.  Rust-crabs fall away.  A scent like sun-struck nettle replaces the metallic breath of below.  Then the tunnel’s ceiling lifts and you are standing at the lip of a vaulted greenhouse vast enough to hide weather of its own.

1. Structure and span  
• Dome: A geodesic skeleton of manganese glass panes soldered together with living coppervine—the plant that names the place.  Each stem is a cable, each leaf a flexible solar shingle.  From a distance the whole roof looks like hammered bronze hammered thinner still until daylight shines straight through the metal.  
• Floor: Porous slag-soil, shot through with capillaries of glazed ceramic that wick condensate up from the chambers beneath.  Footsteps leave no prints; the ground flows back smooth, guided by root pressure.  
• Central mast: A hundred-metre column of twisted copper and lignin that begins as machinery and finishes as trunk.  Every midday it hums, a double-throated resonance: sap going up, current coming down.

2. Light  
Day is prism-bright, streaked by moving shadows of heliostat petals that track the sun and bleed surplus photons into red-shift for the crops underneath.  
Night is never quite dark.  The coppervine stores charge then discharges it as a firefly fog of static: blue grains of light skate the inner surface of the dome, outlining constellations impossible under open sky.

3. Atmosphere and weather  
Humidity arrives as migration, not accident.  Warm draughts pulled through the lower iron chamber pick up iron-sweet moisture, spiral the access shaft, and debouch here as a perpetual afternoon shower—so fine you must stand still a minute to feel it cling.  Every fourth beat of the Dynamo below, vents in the floor sigh, balancing pressure and reminding the garden how time passes elsewhere.

4. Flora  
• Coppervine (Cupria lucens): Primary architect.  Roots crave iron-rich runoff; leaves laminate themselves with nano-scale copper crystals that flicker when voltage crosses stomata.  Once a season the vine flowers: five-petalled corollas of conductive velvet that snap shut on any insect too slow to exit before the next discharge.  
• Lens Lily: Clear, bowl-shaped leaves that fill with condensate and focus sunlight onto their own submerged rhizomes, baking parasites dead.  When a bowl cracks it becomes a seed-raft, rafting downgallery through the rail gutters back toward the cathedral.  
• Rivet Fig: A strangler.  Finds the iron bolts of abandoned catwalks, sleeves them, and grows threaded bark around each, turning fasteners into living fruiting bodies.  The figs themselves are knurled spheres tasting of copper pennies and honey.

5. Fauna  
• Prism-Moths: Wings like stained glass, pigment supplied by metallic salts skimmed from the mist.  At rest they cling to heliostat petals, turning the mirrors into kaleidoscopes.  Once a moth dies its wings delaminate into flakes fine enough to drift down the shaft; scavenger bats below eat them for trace minerals they cannot mine themselves.  
• Conductor Sloths: Three-limbed, furless, the colour of solder.  They hang upside-down from the vine tracery, licking oxides off leaves and acting as living circuit breakers.  When charge spikes through the canopy a sloth’s muscles seize and glow dull orange, bleeding energy off until the current normalises.  The animals seem ecstatic afterward, drooling electrolyte and humming in harmony with the mast.  
• Ledger Jays: Descended from surface corvids that nested in surveyor tripods.  Their feathers are calcite-white; their beaks, improbably, metallic blue.  A jay steals only numbered objects—bolts, beads, teeth—then arranges them in arithmetic spirals on the slab paths.  What it counts has never been agreed upon.

6. Machines that became biology, and the reverse  
• The Osmotic Loom: A ring-canal half a kilometre around the floor rim, twined with peristaltic bladders grown from modified vine phloem.  It draws condensate up, laces it with photosynthetic sugars, and drips it back down the rail conduit as neutral feedstock for the Dynamo.  If the Organ below is heart, the Loom is the lung that exhales coolant into its mouth.  
• Sun-Stalls: Twenty-three abandoned maintenance carts stand where the rail enters the dome.  Coppervine has braided through their wheel spokes, turning each into a giant seedpod.  When the next century’s storm hits and shatters part of the dome—as storms reliably do—the pods will roll free, spilling rootlets that search for new ground the way hermit crabs search for shells.

7. Sound  
A cathedral of photosynthesis sings softly.  Pop of stomata, creak of heliostats, the long velvet rip of charge traveling leaf to leaf.  Every seventy seconds, so faint you feel it more than hear it, the column answers the Dynamo’s pulse with its own descending harmony, as though two hearts share one circulatory sentence.

8. Cycles, trade, kinship  
What the lower chamber exhales—the iron tang, the bacterial ghosts, the humid breath—rises to feed the Arboretum.  
What the Arboretum distils—clear water, stored sunlight, trace glycogen—returns through sly drip-lines to keep the ferric gut flora awake.  
Rivet-fireflies lost to the updraft pupate among the copper leaves and rebirth as prism-moths; prism-moth corpses sift downward, glittering mineral confetti, food for stonewing bats.  
In this mirror exchange, rust becomes fruit, fruit becomes light, light becomes rust again.  The loop has no proper beginning, so both places think themselves the elder sibling.

9. Local customs (for travelers)  
• Announce yourself by touching any leaf and waiting for the static to taste your salts.  The vine will either brighten (welcome) or dull (decline).  
• Carry no straight blades.  Coppervine twines toward lines it can measure; a knife makes you irresistible.  
• When a Ledger Jay lands near, offer it one numeral’s worth of gifts—three screws, five droplets of solder, eight syllables of song—or it will revise your inventory for you.  
• Do not linger under a Conductor Sloth during a heat-burst unless you wish to share its rapture and wake hours later tooth-numb and vine-tattooed.

10. Departure, remembered both ways  
When you descend again the shaft feels warmer, though temperature tells you otherwise: the garden’s stored noon still radiates from your clothes.  The rails carry you back in curves, never straights, so you arrive below unsure of distance traveled.  Rivet-fireflies halo your boots, blinking a pattern suspiciously like the Ledger Jay spiral you left behind.  Overhead, past the vapour billows, a speck of prism-light twirls down the smoke-shaft, falling toward the Warden’s next slow rising.  

The two chambers never speak directly.  Their conversation lives in exchanged breath, in wandering insects, in the matched pulse that throbs through vine and Organ alike.  Stand halfway on the rail ramp and you can feel it: one beat climbing, one beat falling, the world inhaling itself forever between rust and leaf.
