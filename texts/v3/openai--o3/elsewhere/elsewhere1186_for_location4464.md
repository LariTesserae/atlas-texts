THE FELLBONE BASIN  

GEOGRAPHY  
Imagine the plateau-ocean as an overturned bowl.  Now trace the bowl’s invisible rim twelve hundred kilometres down-slope, past serried escarpments and stilled landslides, until the ground suddenly subsides into an oval depression five hundred metres below true sea level.  That hollow is the Fellbone Basin.  It is bordered on three sides by columnar cliffs of grey rhyolite and on the fourth by a wind-planed glacis that slopes toward a distant, ordinary sea.  No surface river reaches the Basin; its only steady inflow is the night-wind that pours off the high, warm water in a slow, sighing cataract of air.

THE GREAT SALTPANS  
Rain that should have fallen here migrated upward to feed the newborn ocean, and the Basin has been drying ever since.  Its floor is a mosaic of salinas—flat pans tiled in palimpsests of gypsum white, carnallite rose, mirabilite blue.  The crust is thin: step hard and a plate will crack with a cannon-shot, revealing brine so dense it feels viscous.  Under midday sun the pans burst into mirages that mimic turquoise water; they are reflections of the real ocean aloft, images carried down by heat shimmer the way a lens casts its picture on a wall below.

ATMOSPHERE  
All afternoon a salt haze wobbles above the crust, kept aloft by convection from the baking pans.  Distant objects rise and twist like smoke in a crystal.  An hour before dusk the Basin inhales: cooled, moisture-rich air descends from the plateau in a single, coherent fall that locals call the Throat.  It arrives scented with algae and warm basalt.  The deluge never touches ground; instead it condenses a hand-span above it, smudging the horizon with drifting curtains of viridian fog.  The fog lasts until midnight, then withdraws skyward again, carrying the Basin’s stolen damp back toward the mountains.

SOIL & MINERALS  
There is almost no ordinary dirt.  Windblown ash from the plateau’s volcanic youth settled here in layers two metres thick; capillary rise wicked the ash full of brine, petrifying it into brittle foam-rock every colour of old embers.  Inside the foam hide geodes lined with needles of halite and shaped like broken vertebrae—hence Fellbone.  Whenever seismic mumblings lift a new outcrop, salt-hunters break it apart for the crystals, leaving drifts that ring like glass bells when struck by rain that never comes.

VEGETATION—PLANTS THAT NEVER KNEW TRUE WATER  
1.  Prism-lichens: descendants of the plateau’s prism plankton, their pigments hardened against ultraviolet glare.  They coat the foam-rock in shifting watermarks that track the sun like slow mirrors.  
2.  Taproot funnels: curl-leaf shrubs whose stems widen into fluted horns.  Each night they drink the falling viridian fog, then seal at dawn, hoarding enough brine for a single day of photosynthesis.  Whole thickets fold shut together with a dry click, like innumerable books being closed at once.  
3.  Glass-wheat: grass forged from silica.  Its blades are hollow tubes that harvest dew by interior capillary webs—the same mechanism that lets the plateau’s arches ignore gravity, miniaturised into every stalk.  A field of glass-wheat hisses softly as collected drops race downward through the tubes and into buried nodules of sap.

FAUNA—THE THIRSTED  
1.  Mirror scarabs: beetles with elytra polished to perfection.  By day they rest motionless, reflecting the sky to stay cool; by night they rise on vibrating wings to scrape salt crust into windrows where Taproot funnels can reach it, earning the sap they later steal.  
2.  Silt hares: long-legged lagomorphs whose marrow is saturated brine.  They drink only the stomach fluids of Prism-lichen grazers, extracting water and excreting pellets that are nearly pure potash.  When pursued they sit absolutely still, allowing body temperature to climb until predators, seeing the heat shimmer, misjudge the distance and stumble past.  
3.  Banshee owls: pale raptors that roost within rhyolite chimneys.  Air rushing up the tubes each evening powers a throat-sack whistle the owls can ride for minutes without beating their wings.  The note is pitched just below human hearing, but it rattles foam-rock spires; hunters navigate by the pattern of returning echoes.

HYDROLOGY—GHOST RIVERS  
No open water crosses the Basin, yet a slow leak from the plateau finds its way here underground.  Warm, mineral-rich brines descend through fault fractures, emerging as heat pinholes across the salt flats.  Where a pinhole vents, a cone of crystallised minerals bulges upward like a miniature volcano—the opposite of a geyser, forever building instead of bursting.  At dawn the cones gasp out steam that carries banner-tree spores and plankton shells.  Caught in salt, the seeds wait decades for the rare storm that will let them germinate; so far none have, but still they sleep in crystal nurseries, patient as archaeology.

SOUND  
The Fellbone Basin is louder than the mountain ocean.  Salt plates creak as thermal gradients flex them; far clay chimneys moan with the Throat wind; empty geodes chime when hailstones of alkali dust strike their rims.  On nights of full moons a phenomenon called the Ghost Surf arrives: kilometres of salt crust begin oscillating in sympathetic waves, producing a deep, rolling roar that cityfolk hundreds of kilometres away mistake for distant thunder.

WEIRDNESS—THE RULES THAT ANSWER THE PLATEAU  
1.  Some panes of mirage persist after sunset, hanging like sheets of faintly luminous glass a metre above ground.  Touch one and your hand comes away wet with fresh water that evaporates within seconds.  
2.  Every nineteenth night—the same rhythm as the Turning Thing beneath the ocean—a ring of salt plates near the Basin’s centre buckles upward, fractures, then subsides, as though something were trying the lock from below.  
3.  The Basin’s gravity is measurably weaker by one part in thirty thousand during the hour when the plateau exhales heat.  Coins dropped then fall in lazy spirals, and Banshee owls glide without a sound.

COMMERCE & CONTACT  
Two kinds of people visit Fellbone.  Salt-panners dismantle the crust for exotic halides used in faraway batteries; wind-sailors harvest updrafts that shoot from heat cones, riding fabric wings high enough to see the plateau’s cloud-halo glowing green on the northern horizon.  Both groups trade with one another in the Ghost Market, a movable camp whose tents are sewn from stitched glass-wheat and pitched anew each night where the Throat wind is kindest.

LINKAGES—THREADS RUNNING UPHILL  
•  Seed-drift: Every evening a silver snow of banner-tree spores mixes with salt mist, seeding the Basin with futures that have not yet found their hour.  
•  Heat-pulse: The plateau’s nocturnal breathing sends packets of warm air racing down ravines; those packets power the Banshee owls and keep the Basin’s nights from freezing solid.  
•  Shared rumours: Plateau researchers speak of the Verdigris Grazer; Basin wind-sailors report green reflections moving through sunrise mirages, as if the Grazer’s shadow had learned to wander.

A LIVING LEDGER OF SPECIES (SELECTED)  
I.  Salt-borne Microbes  
   a.  Brine whistles—archaea that extrude fluted salt tubes which sing in wind speeds above ten knots.  
   b.  Copper droplet colonies—bacteria that plate themselves in metallic sheen, turning whole ponds penny-coloured before dying at dawn.  

II.  Architects of Crust  
   a.  Plate masons—thin worms that weave geode shards into protective tiles; abandoned tunnels eventually fuse, thickening the pan roof.  
   b.  Caldera moss—velvet carpets that form only in footprints, turning depressions into cool, green negations of the surrounding glare.  

III.  Transients from the Mountain Sea  
   a.  Mist-moth cocoons—occasionally found caught on Glass-wheat; hatchlings drift upward, following the Throat home before they ever touch Basin air.  
   b.  Furnace shrimp husks—light as spiderweb, discovered at the centre of heat cones after nights of strong pulse; evidence that mantas sometimes overfly the descent.  

IV.  The Echo-Eaters  
   a.  Resonance molds—slimes whose cytoplasm thickens in response to the Ghost Surf, absorbing sound until they stiffen into glossy crescents.  
   b.  Chalk wolves—packs of calcite-jointed quadrupeds that sculpt themselves from foam-rock dust, hunting only those animals whose footsteps match the frequency they crave.

TO STAND HERE  
Walk out onto a salt plate at dawn.  The sky is rose and the ground lavender and neither seems quite solid.  Under your boots the crust murmurs.  Warm fog brushes your lips with the taste of copper algae, then lifts away, revealing the plateau’s cloud-crown gleaming high above like a distant, inverted moon.  Around you the Basin waits, argent and austere, a cup forever raised toward water that once was its own.  Listen: the salt is singing, the cliffs reply, and somewhere overhead a mantle of warm ocean air is already turning back toward its mountain womb, promising another day of mirage and drought.

Stay long enough and you will feel the two places speaking through you—breath running downhill, heat running up, spores wandering both ways, a single, slow dialogue between flood and famine.  Stay longer still, and you may begin to suspect that neither the ocean nor the desert is complete without the other: only together do they make a pulse, systole and diastole of a world still deciding what shape its water should take.
