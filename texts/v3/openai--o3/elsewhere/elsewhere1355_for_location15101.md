The Hissing Fields  

1. Birth of the Plateau  
Where the Green Hollow exhales, the Hissing Fields begin.  Heat and moisture from the Allroot’s deepest fault-lines climb through a lattice of vents and reach the open air in plumes that never quite cease.  Over tens of millennia the escaping mineral steam laid down layers of silicate frost, one upon another, until a broad, ragged terrace rose above the surrounding ice-plain—a frozen delta of geyser-cones, fumarole chimneys and glassy spillways two hundred kilometres across.  Seen from far off it looks like a low storm cloud that forgot to keep moving.

2. Ground underfoot  
Forget soil.  You walk on a shell of opaline crust that rings like crystal under your boots.  Here and there the crust has buckled, forming hexagonal plates a metre thick that teeter on stems of vapour.  Crack one and you may punch straight through to a blue furnace forty metres down.  The safest paths follow “root-prints”: parallel ridges where ancient Allroot cables once pressed upward, chilled, and fused.  Their pattern, a ghost of the Hollow’s vascular map, is the first proof that both realms are one system.

3. Temperature and weather  
•  Air: –15 °C on a calm day, but the vents raise their own micro-climate.  Stand in the lee of a chimney and you sweat; step aside and your eyelashes ice solid.  
•  Wind: constant from the western ice shelf, strong enough to shear steam into banners five kilometres long.  From orbit the plateau flickers like a white flame.  
•  Precipitation: not snow but “glassfall,” thin needles of amorphous silica that grow in the plume tops and drop when heavy.  They dissolve on the tongue with a taste like flint and salt.

4. Light  
The Hollow carries its own noon; the Fields borrow theirs.  On overcast days the only brilliance comes from Below—photon-heavy gases that leak upward still charged with plant-light.  At dusk the geysers glow sea-green; at night they dim toward violet, an aurora reversed and rooted in stone.

5. Flora (of a sort)  
Nothing with leaves endures the freeze, yet life still sketches itself.  
•  Frost-lichen (“whisper moss”) webs the lee side of every cone, trapping trace sugars wafting from below.  It is colourless by day, ember-red where your lamp warms it.  
•  Silica-ferns: structures, not organisms—delicate fronds up to two metres tall grown by steady deposition.  They replicate the exact branching ratio of a tetherfolk bronchial tree, as though the plateau itself once breathed and left the model behind.

6. Fauna: wind-born and heat-bound  
a)  Steam kites  
   •  Wingspan three metres, membrane thin as parchment.  They circle the tallest plumes waiting for rogue up-drafts to fling them skyward, then glide fifty kilometres before folding and diving to another vent.  
   •  Diet: airborne bacterial fluff condensed from geyser breath.  Their throats are comb-filters; every swallow is a netful of cells.  
b)  Ember otters  
   •  Six-limbed, seal-sized, black fur beaded with antifreeze oil.  They dive the boiling pools, gill-breathing through folds that extract oxygen from supersaturated water.  Surface long enough to cool, then slide back down obsidian chutes in a game only they understand.  
c)  Glass crows  
   •  True descendants of tetherfolk myth—hollow-boned corvids whose feathers are coated in microquartz.  At sunrise they look metallic; at sunset they vanish into glare.  They steal anything that shines brighter than the vents: mirror shards, polished shell, a traveller’s visor.

7. The Chimney-Folk (also called Up-binders)  
•  Physiology  
   –  Likely cousins of the tetherfolk; height similar, skin darker from constant ultraviolet.  Their pupils are horizontal slits against the snow-glare.  
   –  They bind the ribs with silk sashes soaked in algae wax; the heat differential can crack bone if the lungs expand too quickly in vent air.  
•  Dwellings  
   –  Tents of woven basalt fibre pitched in the “quiet rings,” annuli where the vents’ roar drops to a bearable hiss.  The fabric is hand-twisted then glazed in silica rain, turning supple rope into armour.  
   –  Each camp anchors a field of wind-harps: vertical cords strung between frost spires.  The note of a vent changing pressure is music, but also warning—harps transcribe the shift before the ground quakes.  
•  Food and fuel  
   –  Whisper moss scraped, boiled, and fermented into a sour paste.  
   –  Steam-kite eggs poached in geyser runoff.  Shells serve as translucent bowls; the inner membrane seals wounds like living gauze.  
   –  Firewood?  None.  Heat is borrowed.  Stone pots are sealed, held in vent clouds until soup simmers, then swung out on bone hooks.  
•  Crafts  
   –  Glassfall smithing: they catch still-soft needles on felt screens, knead the mass into threads, then draw it into blades that never rust but sing if you strike them.  
   –  Mirror script: flat panes cast from pooled silica and silvered with meteoric nickel.  Messages are etched by scouring away the film; a sentence is a constellation of holes that read as brightness differences rather than lines.  
•  Lore  
   –  The Allroot is “the Far Sibling.”  Steam is its breath; glass its frozen exhalation.  Each vent has a name—Shinethroat, Old Ache, Two-Beat—recited nightly so none are forgotten.  
   –  The Field’s oldest epic, the Breaking Song, recounts a time when a vent died for nine breaths, the hush so total that people thought the world had ended.  They still pause conversations every dawn, listening for that same silence.

8. Trade with the Hollow  
A single shaft—called the Spillpipe—cuts from the plateau’s lowest basin straight down into misty dark.  On cables grown by the Allroot and trimmed by tetherfolk, baskets travel both ways:  
•  Upward: nodules of living cambium rich in glucose; vein-lamps that shine for months; rolled hides of flare-viper.  
•  Downward: glass blades, crow feathers, ingots of shock-fused nickel-iron collected after meteoric storms.  
At the midpoint the two peoples never meet, but they tie story-knots into the ropes: a three-over-two braid warns of ground fracture; a flat knot celebrates a birth; a slip-loop requests medicine.  By unwritten treaty no knot is ever cut.

9. Dangers & Rules  
•  Never camp windward of a vent: the plume can jump fifty metres if a lower fissure seals.  
•  Crystal thunder: when plates snap under temperature stress they detonate like shrapnel.  Travellers carry wicker shields to catch the first spray.  
•  Do not feed the glass crows—they will follow, and their wing-flash attracts ember otters, which may mistake you for a cooling pool.  
•  If silence falls across the plateau, lie flat—pressure drop means an eruption is coming.  The blast front skims a body pressed close to the crust but will strip a standing traveller to the bone.

10. The Feel of the Fields  
Imagine a horizon where every mountain is hollow and every mountain breathes.  Steam columns tilt in wind like dancers leaning beyond balance, then correct, spitting emerald fire as pockets of Hollow-light bleed out.  Footsteps crunch glass that was water an hour ago.  The air smells of salt, copper, and the faint sweetness of distant sugars.  Under the roar you catch another cadence: wind-harps telling the pressure, crows rasping over stolen trinkets, the muffled boom of plates cracking far below.  It is simultaneously cathedral, engine-room, and open ocean made solid enough to walk.

11. Leaving  
Exit south and the crust slopes to the true surface ice.  The vents fade; the sky enlarges.  Turn once: you will see the plateau pulsing, breath after breath, painting the low cloud-deck with inland auroras.  Somewhere beneath that living crust runs the Remembering Line, baskets climbing, baskets descending, knot-messages ferrying news neither nation speaks aloud.  The Hollow keeps its noon; the Fields keep their night-bright geysers; between them, thought travels a rope-width at a time, weaving a single hidden world out of root, steam and glass.
