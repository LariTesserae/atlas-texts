The Steppe of Iron Rain

A single saddle-shaped plateau fans out on the lee side of the same volcano whose summit hides the Cupola of Volcanic Bone.  Nine kilometers downslope and half a kilometer lower, it catches everything the upper chamber expels or sheds.  What drifts out of that furnace on the wind—slag pearls, spun-sugar glass, errant pyrospores, the heat-bruised scent of ribbon serpents—ultimately falls here like weather.

1. Ground and structure  
• Surface: a skin of hematite scree sintered smooth by centuries of thermal lightning.  From a distance it looks wet; up close it flakes like mica, each flake tuned to ring at a different pitch.  
• Craters: thousands, none larger than a cooking pot, formed where glass rods jettisoned by ribbon serpents spear the ground, flash-vaporizing a plug of iron shale.  The rods shatter, leaving starbursts of needle glass that turn to sand within a day.  
• Sub-strata: twenty centimeters below is the “cold tide,” a permanently liquid sheet of gallium-indium alloy.  The metal rises through capillary cracks at night, silvering the soil; by noon it retracts and cools, leaving fractal mirrors on the steppe floor.  
• Horizon: everywhere the ground ends in a lip of vitrified basalt that overhangs a cloud-sea of ammonium mist.  Updrafts roar through organ-pipe vents, giving the entire plateau a low, ceaseless drone.

2. Light and sky  
Daylight barely tops the surrounding peaks, arriving as blown-out white for a single hour.  The plateau’s native glow comes instead from two imports:  
• Slag pearls—marbles forged inside slag-lice higher up—retain heat for days after landing.  They dot the flats like embers, each whispering infrared.  
• Shard-sand from serpent glass catches that infrared and fluoresces teal.  A windstorm after nightfall looks like a silent aurora skimming the ground.

3. Climate engine  
Every ninety-third breath—the very pulse that rocks the Cupola—slips down-slope as a pressure wave.  By the time it arrives, the wave is cold, having traded heat for speed.  It hits the plateau as a wall of wind that:  
• Scoops megatonnes of shard-sand into the air, sand-blasting everything exposed.  
• Drives the gallium tide underground, unloading trace metals into fissures where organisms feed.  
• Hums across the tuned hematite plates, making the whole steppe sing a chord in B-flat for three breaths.

4. Biota—thin, but obstinate  
Life here survives on fallout and sound, not on heat.

a) Mirror Lichen (Speculum mycelium)  
   • Not a lichen but a colony of silica quasipolymers seeded by stray pyrospores.  
   • Growth: etches itself into hematite flakes, hardening them into parabolic mirrors that refocus morning sun onto the colony’s heart.  Each sunrise is a micro-solar furnace lasting seconds.  
   • Yield: releases vapor-phase sulfides that drift upward; mantle kites scent these clouds from the Cupola and, once in a lifetime, glide down the ravine to feed.

b) Rust-Rams (Ferricapra sonans)  
   • Size of a large dog, mass of a suitcase.  Skeleton: hollow ferrite struts; skin: anodized iron felt.  
   • Diet: licks condensed gallium at fissure mouths, precipitating indium in a second stomach to build head-horns.  
   • Courtship: two males back into a slag-pearl field at dusk.  They tilt their mirrored horns so the pearls’ glow bounces between them; the brighter reflection wins.  
   • Predators: none terrestrial.  When a ribbon serpent feels its glass rods running low, it may uncoil all the way here on a thermal glide and swallow a whole herd for silica.

c) Shard-Gnats  
   • Minute drones of fused glass, spun from the same rods that make the craters.  They self-replicate by knitting shard-sand in mid-air.  
   • Swarms drift just above the ground, scouring everything for vibration.  They feed on the plateau’s B-flat resonance, vanishing when the singing stops.

d) Bone-Kites  
   • Fugitive mantle kites that failed to ascend back to heat.  Their gas sacs collapsed, so they skim the steppe on cartilage skids, using thermal eddies off slag pearls.  
   • They have turned carnivore: envelop shard-gnat swarms, enclose them, then cook them with residual bioluminescence.

5. Sound economy  
• The plateau’s ringing is not incidental; every organism uses it.  Rust-ram hooflines are serrated to catch the note and channel vibration up the legs, powering slow peristaltic pumps that move liquid metal through the blood.  
• Mirror lichen spores germinate only when hammered by the fundamental frequency; without it, the colony lies dormant for centuries.  
• Shard-gnats ride the same standing waves to stay airborne.  When a vent pulse is late, entire swarms crash and shatter—a hail of glass snow powering the soil with silicates.

6. Memory of the upper chamber  
   The Cupola’s history is strewn across the Steppe like driftwood.  
   • Old slag pearls, cooled and dull, lie in sinuous trails marking where ribbon serpents once crawled as juveniles before learning to fly the thermals back up the mountain.  
   • Glass crab prisms, ejected by up-fault quakes, land point-first; any sunlight that strikes one is bent down to the gallium tide, where it paints slow, wandering scars of heat that guide rust-ram migrations.  
   • A single intact basalt arch rests near the plateau’s north rim, half-melted yet unmistakably sister to those inside the Cupola.  Scholars (human or otherwise) think it was thrown here whole during the collapse that opened the summit.  Mirror lichen armors its underside, turning the arch into the brightest object for sixty kilometers whenever dawn strikes.

7. Risks and passages  
   To walk the Steppe of Iron Rain:  
   • Wear no loose metal; shard-gnats will strip it for broodstock.  
   • Step only while the wind drones—silence means the gallium tide is about to surge, and it will swallow ankles like quicksilver.  
   • Never shelter in a crater; they fill without warning when another serpent’s glass rod spears in from the stratosphere.  
   • Carry a warm slag pearl.  The glow tags you as a moving heat source; ribbon serpents looking for stationary stores of silica will ignore you.

8. The thread between places  
Magma’s breath goes up, slag-lice live on it, serpents feast, the serpents’ glass falls, life down-slope feeds, and the wind carries indigo spores back toward the mountain each dusk.  The world has tied the Cupola and the Steppe into a single, two-chambered lung—one inhaling heat, the other exhaling iron song.  Stand in either and you feel the other moving, like a twin pulse under the skin of stone.
