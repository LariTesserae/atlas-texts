The Cataract Garden  

TERRAIN AND WATER  
Follow the rim of the sky-held sea until, at one single saddle of lower rock, the newborn ocean can no longer contain itself.  A sheet of water narrower than a handspan slips over, gathers speed down a fault-scaf, fractures into a thousand ribbons—and the mountain suddenly sings with falling.  Those ribbons spread into filaments, the filaments into lace, until the whole leeward face of the massif becomes one veiled cascade five kilometres tall.  The Cataract Garden is everything the water touches on its way to ground: shelves of black basalt scoured into bowls, mid-cliff caverns punched clean through by centuries of spray, and—at the foot of it all—a delta of terraces where the runoff slows, pools and sinks into porous pumice sand.  

Seen from above, the Garden looks terraced, almost deliberate: each overflow century carves a new step, and in the quiet centuries between, life settles the step in lush green.  The oldest terraces near valley floor are broad enough for forests; the youngest, only years old, are knife-thin ledges that can take no more than moss and mist.  In every basin the water lies mineral-rich, infused with salts leached from the caldera.  Here it is cool, clear, and heavy with iron; there it is faintly teal with dissolved copper.  Each pool, therefore, breeds its own colour of life.

ATMOSPHERE AND LIGHT  
If the sky-held sea is half night, the Cataract Garden is painted glass.  Every droplet refracts the sun that finally breaks free of the upper clouds, so the air fills with wavering rain-bows, not arcs but bars that slide across the cliff like living stained glass.  Light concentration differs by terrace: mid-height bowls catch the brunt of reflection and glow gold through most afternoons, while the forested lower benches live in eternal emerald dusk.  Wind created by the constant plunge of water gusts upward; it shears calligraphy into every ribbon of fall, and carries the scent of stone that has only lately met air.

SOUND  
The Garden has just two volumes: hush and thunder.  At distance you hear a low, omnipresent roar like a held organ chord that never resolves.  Step beneath an overhang and the sound cuts to an intimate patter—the percussion of single drops fattened by miles of fall.  Communicating here is done in sign and drum: travellers carry hollow gourds with stretched algae-skin heads; one resonant thump means approach, two means peril falling from above, three summons help.  At night, when temperature drops, the cataract flow thins and the chorus relaxes to a glassy hush through which leaf-frogs conduct antiphonal songs too soft for the day.

VEGETATION  
1.  Rimroot Pines grow where the spill first leaps clear of stone.  Their roots braid inside fissures, drinking warmth that seeps up from still-liquid magma.  Twigs are spiral-bored by wind-sand and hum at specific pitches; Gardeners cut certain branches to tune natural flute groves that warn of rockslip.  
2.  Pummelfern colonises mid-terraces.  From a distance it looks like fern, but each frond is a cup that captures drop impact; the stem’s base contains gel sacs that inflate, preventing fracture.  When a sac finally bursts, spores burst with it, riding haze to fresher terraces above.  
3.  Mirror Ivy masquerades as water at first glance: leaves waxed slick, margins scalloped like ripples.  It climbs beside slender falls, stealing spray.  The Pai’cho have a distant cousin Current who descend seasonally to harvest Mirror Ivy cuttings as fire-less lamp wicks; the surface prismatic layer will burn in pure oxygen, so carried back upward it powers rare stone-kilns.  
4.  Gutter-Trees dominate the oldest steps.  Their trunks arch horizontally, then bend skyward, forming great U-shaped gutters that trap an entire stream and pipe it through living xylem to roots far downhill.  A single mature gutter-tree can divert enough flow to dry out a neighbouring pool, producing shifting boundaries year by year.

FAUNA  
•  Travertine Crabs: six-legged crustaceans whose shells encrust with limestone until they resemble walking geodes.  They farm bacterial mats on their backs and barter chunks of them to song-birds in exchange for warning cries.  
•  Ribbon Serpents: aerial hunters that live only within the spray curtain.  They are eel-thin, scaled with grooves that trap air, allowing them to ricochet updrafts like stones skipped on water.  They prey on…  
•  Cataract Swifts: birds evolved from distant cousins of the glass-wing ray; they cannot swim but their bones retain vestigial buoyancy.  They nest upside down under ledges, weaving hanging baskets from vine-engine offshoots that blow loose and drift down-slope.  
•  Gloom Elk: graze the forested foot-terraces.  Their antlers bifurcate into dozens of water-channel prongs.  In rut they clash not with force but resonance: each bell-shaped tine rings and the richest timbre wins mates.

PEOPLE OF THE GARDEN  
They call themselves the Ruun-shu—“those who listen beneath.”  Where the Pai’cho are round-limbed and hollow-boned, the Ruun-shu are long, tendon-cord lean, built for climbing wet stone.  Fingers and toes end in keratin hooks inherited from a now-extinct arboreal ancestor; with these they scale vine and overhang alike.  

DWELLINGS  
Homes are grown, not built.  Choose a midway gutter-tree sapling, score four spiral cuts down the bark while it is pliant, fit in angled bone wedges, and wait ten seasons.  The tree obliges by thickening around the scores, producing a hollow corkscrew ramp that leads up to a natural loft safe from flash floods.  The loft’s inner walls weep sweet sap; every morning a family scrapes breakfast from their own house.  

ENERGY  
To make heat in a wet world they harvest resonance.  Frames of tensioned rimroot pine hung in the spray vibrate constantly; embedded copper salts from the water induce minute currents that are braided by silver-thread moss into soft, warm pads.  A single blanket hums enough power to boil sapwine or cauterise wounds—silent, smokeless.

FOOD  
1.  Swifts’ discarded eggshell membranes fermented in travertine crab-lime to make a tart cheese.  
2.  Gutter-sap reduced over blanket-coils into a syrup that never crystallises, sold up-slope in reed balloons as trade for mother-of-pearl scales.  
3.  Shadow-morel: a fungus whose fruit grows only on cliff-backs reached via night climbing; tastes of burnt sugar and salt.

LANGUAGE AND MEMORY  
Words here must cut through the cataract’s roar.  The Ruun-shu developed Click-Cant: voiced primarily in palatal and glottal clicks that puncture white noise.  A storyteller clicking through genealogy sounds like rain on slate, yet every local hears lineage unbroken.  For softer speech they use Palm Glyphs—tracing water on another’s skin.  To record myth they carve resonance rods: lengths of rimroot that, when bowed, play complex overtones.  A trained ear can listen to a rod and hear a saga.

RELATION WITH THE PAI’CHO  
Once a generation, overflow widens a crack in the saddle and the cataract swells.  Pai’cho envoys ride bladder-fern skiffs lashed to light-rope down the newborn torrent, bearing negative-weight jewellery and eel-salt.  They trade for resonance rods and gutter-sap.  The voyage is one-way; to return, the envoys climb rib-ladders of Mirror Ivy that the Ruun-shu cultivate deliberately as stair.  Many remain, marry, and add vowels to the clicking tongue; thus each culture keeps a living seed of the other.

RULES OF THE GARDEN  
1.  Never dam a flow older than yourself; water remembers its bed and will take payment in bodies.  
2.  Every cliff has a Name-Tone determined by striking it with a quartz hammer.  If you must chip stone, match the tone as you work or risk avalanche.  
3.  Written signs face downhill so migrants can read them; nothing turned up-slope is trusted.  
4.  When a resonance rod finally cracks, its shards are sown from highest to lowest terrace—returning story to the spaces it first described.

DYNAMICS & LONG VIEW  
The Garden creeps outward.  Each year a little more cliff succumbs, a fresh basin forms, and with it a new horizon for life.  Already young gutter-trees on the sixth step raise proto-buttresses, forecasting a future forest loftier than any today.  Ruun-shu dream aloud of sky-paths grown tree-to-tree that will let their grandchildren climb all the way to the caldera rim without ever touching stone.  Pai’cho engineers, hearing this, imagine the inverse: a living elevator of gutter-wood and vine-engine muscle to ferry warm-sea water down for winter aquaculture.  Between the two peoples an unspoken compact flowers—the mountain is rising, the cataract falling, and where they meet will forever be the newest place on earth.  

So, on nights when cloud clears and the cataract roars its softest, you might spot three pale beads—funeral balloons from the sea above—drifting through moonlit spray.  Below, a Ruun-shu child stands on a mid-terrace rim, arm lifted in the Pai’cho salute, head bowed in Click-Cant prayer, waiting for one bead to touch the pool and burst.  They do not know the life inside the story, but they have already made room in the Garden for its memory.
