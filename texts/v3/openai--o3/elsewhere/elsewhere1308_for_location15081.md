The Decanter Vault  

Walk the exhaled thread upstream—follow its cool, iron-tinged seep for nine hundred winding metres and you come to the place where the water first slows enough to consider itself a room.  The conduit widens, the ceiling rises, the pressure eases, and what was only a fissure stretches into architecture: an oval cavern a hundred paces long, forty wide, paneled not in slate but in human work—concrete, riveted steel, and the translucent ribs of ancient bulkheads.  Here the current that eventually feeds the slate bowl is separated, clarified, metered; the Vault is both cradle and sieve, a machine-hollow the flood passes through on its patient descent.  

Morphology and fabric  
• Floor: a gently sloped apron of poured concrete, broom-finished back when the surface was dry.  Time and seepage have lifted the laitance; the aggregate now stands proud like sugar grains, giving every footfall the gritty whisper of walking on salt.  Down the centre runs the Decanter Trough, a V-shaped gutter of hammered copper three spans wide.  Water enters at the north end, swirls once, then vanishes through a throat the size of a steamer hatch, beginning the pressure-pipe that dives toward the lower valley and, ultimately, the slate nave.  
• Walls: shell after shell of ferro-cement lined with baked enamel plates.  White once, the plates have taken a permanent tea-stain from dissolved iron—sunburst aureoles where each bolt head bleeds its own halo.  In two places the enamel has fallen away, revealing reinforcement bars the colour of dried liver.  Their rust sheds micrometer flakes that drift like cinnamon onto the water’s skin.  
• Ceiling: a low barrel vault of glass-brick skylights locked in steel grids.  They sit just below the drowned surface of the larger reservoir above.  Daylight thus arrives doubly laundered—first by water, then by the pea-green tint of the prism bricks.  It paints the chamber in a colour somewhere between old zinc and crushed sage.  

Hydraulic character  
The Vault does not flood; it decants.  Flow through the copper trough averages half a cubic metre per second—slow enough that the water moves as a pane of glass, fast enough that nothing settles on its mirror.  Along either margin of the trough sheet-flows wander across the concrete apron and pool at inspection sumps before re-joining the main stream.  Every twelve minutes a pressure pulse from the pumps downstream bounces back, lifting the level by a finger’s breadth and making the surface quiver like drum skin.  That pulse is the Vault’s heartbeat; technicians once timed their shift changes to it.  

Atmosphere  
Technically the Vault is an airspace, but the boundary between water and air here is theoretical.  Relative humidity closer to the ceiling never falls below ninety-eight per cent; exhale once and your breath condenses on your lips.  Temperature holds at thirteen degrees Celsius, a compromise struck between the nine-degree feed from the uplake and the mild heat bleeding off the oxidising rebar.  The air is metallic, faintly sweet with antifreeze volatiles, and just ozone-bitten enough to feel clean even while your boots grow a skin of condensation.  

Illumination cycle  
Daylight sifts through the skylights whenever the surface above is clear of silt.  In storms or at night, the Vault glows by its own means: a grid of sodium vapour tubes sunk flush with the walls, minding their business for decades at a stretch.  Under the golden lamps the concrete looks the colour of baked bread and the water becomes bronze—an illusion so inviting that newcomers think the flow warm until they plunge a hand and feel refrigerator cold.  

Acoustics  
Sound carries father than sight in this half-liquid air.  The principal voices:  
1.  A continuous turbine note seeping up the pressure pipe—E-flat, fifty-two hertz, everlasting.  
2.  The tick-snap of condensate dripping from conduit elbows onto copper: each drop a solder tap.  
3.  An intermittent staccato like thrown gravel whenever rust scabs delaminate from the rebar grid and skitter across the apron.  
Conversations become conspiratorial whispers automatically; anything louder feels sacrilegious.  

Biological settlement  

1.  Rail-moss (Leptolyngbya ferrugenica)  
   • Habitat: lives where water sheet-flows thin over concrete.  The cells braid into rust-red cords no thicker than pencil lead, following the hydraulic lines as though written there.  
   • Metabolism: half phototroph, half iron-chewer.  Chlorophyll does what it can with the green daylight; for the rest the moss taps Fe²⁺ seeped from the walls, venting thin curls of O₂ that hiss away before they can nourish anything with lungs.  
   • Behaviour: every night the filaments exude mucilage that lifts them a millimetre off the floor, escaping scouring pulses.  By dawn the gel has dissolved and the cords lay back down, having lengthened perhaps the breadth of a cell.  Seen in time-lapse they migrate upslope like slow crimson rivers.  

2.  Service-crickets (Acheta ductorum)  
   • Provenance: descendants of surface crickets stranded here when the valley first drowned.  Over generations they traded pigment for spine plates of hydrated iron phosphate.  Adults are ghost-pale except for pin-prick eyes reflecting lamp and skylight alike.  
   • Ecology: the only invertebrate willing to eat rail-moss.  Mouthparts secrete a chelating spit that unlocks the iron; the cricket then voids rust pellets the size of poppy seeds, adding to the cinnamon drift on the trough’s margins.  
   • Rhythm: emerge in full darkness to graze, retreat at first hint of skylight green.  Their chirp is gone—tympana damped by mineral plating.  Instead they drum hind legs against conduit skin: a code of soft taps no louder than water drops.  

3.  Condensate bats (Myotis pervaporis)  
   • Roosts: the interstitial voids behind the enamel plates.  Wingspan hand-wide, fur the silvery white of frayed Kevlar.  
   • Diet: infrequent—one cricket per week.  The rest of their caloric ledger they balance by lowering body temperature to match the thirteen-degree air, heart throttled to eighty beats per hour.  
   • Navigation: cannot echolocate well in a room already ringing with harmonic machinery.  Instead they map the vault thermally, sensing downdrafts off copper and the minute warm plume a cricket’s digestion releases.  

Industrial memory  
Every surface bears a film of factory ghosts: micro-droplets of turbine oil, graphite skin cells from vanished bearings, nitrile shreds from gloves long incinerated.  The water entering the trough lifts a molecular handful of this detritus, swirls it once under skylight, and carries it downslope.  By the time that cargo reaches the slate bowl it has thinned to invisibility, but its taste is still there—the bitterness a diver’s tongue would recognise.  Thus the Decanter Vault is both refinery and storyteller: each molecule dispatched downstream speaks of gears that still turn somewhere above.  

Interfacing with the lower bowl  
Once every fifth pressure pulse, a spanwise gate beneath the trough’s throat twitches open just long enough to admit a backwash breath: ten litres of water from the lower system surge upward, bringing with them the grey-green husks shed when the bowl’s mirror-skin molts.  In the Vault those husks become nutrients—they hang in the humid air, settle on the broom-finished concrete, and seed the next colony of rail-moss with genes tuned to tolerance for turbine oils.  Conversely, every hour the Vault’s own rust-chelated compounds bleed downstream, teaching the slate chamber’s cyanobacteria new tricks of iron management.  The two biomes converse entirely by molecular exchange, a dialogue of tolerance and thrift.  

Age and maintenance  
Paper records say the Decanter Vault was commissioned 147 years ago; mineral records suggest the place has not slept a single night since.  Motors have been swapped, bearings replaced, but the trough still flashes the same copper the inaugural engineer watched run bright with its first fill.  The maintenance crews are long gone—evacuated when the surface dam overtopped—and yet the Vault obliges its purpose automatically.  Float valves trimmed for human deadlines now answer only to physics, and physics keeps its appointments.  

What it is like to linger here  
At first you taste penny-metal on your lips.  After ten minutes your palms wrinkle, fooled by the wet air into thinking they have been soaking.  After an hour the sodium lamps hum becomes the room’s only language; you forget ordinary silence ever differed.  Watch the copper mirror and you will see false constellations: the skylight’s green stars sifted by ripple and pulse.  If you lie on the apron and let your ear touch the concrete you will hear the cricket’s leg-drum—so soft it might be your own blood—but patterned, deliberate, belonging to something that has never once seen the colour red.  

Exit and memory  
When you finally take the inspection ladder upward and breach into open reservoir light, the air will feel treble-dry, too hot, absurdly spacious.  Yet for days afterwards every glass of water you drink will recall the Vault: a suspicion of iron at the edge of taste, a background E-flat you cannot unhear, and the conviction that somewhere below the hills a copper river is running still, never slackening, never rushing, decanting the world in perfect, tireless thirds.
