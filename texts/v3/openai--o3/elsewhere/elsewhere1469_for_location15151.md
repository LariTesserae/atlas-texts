The Drywalk Expanse  

You reach it by following the water all the way down, staying inside the roar until the roar is gone. Three vertical kilometres below the High Drown, the cataracts lose themselves in vapor, then in trickles, then in nothing at all. What is left of the ocean’s breath pours outward across a drowned‐and‐dried seabed, leaving behind a country that looks, at first glance, like an infinite salt pan—white to the horizon, bright enough at noon to blind a gull. The Kept say the Expanse is where water goes to remember being stone.

TERRAIN  
• Tidebones: parallel scarps of once-submerged limestone, planed flat by the last retreating sea and shattered by centuries of freeze-thaw. They rise only a few metres above the flats but run unbroken for scores of kilometres, like fallen sea-walls.  
• Sink-pits: perfectly circular holes, one to three hundred metres across, where subterranean brine still chews the rock. Their walls are varicoloured strata—rust, chalk, indigo gypsum—descending into darkness that smells of cold alkali.  
• Scablands: fields of polygonal mudstone cracked into plates huge enough to build a house on; the cracks gape wide enough for a person to fall through. At dawn they steam as last night’s dew flashes off.  

MATERIALS & COLOUR  
Salt: everywhere, in every grain size. Plain table-white on the crust, smoky grey where dust storms have settled, rose-pink where microbial mats survive.  
Rock: pale cream limestone threaded with black flint. When lightning strikes it vitrifies into brittle, honey-coloured glass.  
Soil: almost absent. What exists is a powder of ground kelp ash blown down from the High Drown and caught in wind-shadows.  

ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT  
Humidity never tops 25 %. The air is thin, brittle, and prickled with static. Daylight reflects off salt and limestone until the very sky looks bleached. Nights invert to ink-black voids where every star doubles in the mirrored ground.  

SOUND  
There is nothing to break the wind, so it owns the low register—a bottomless hush that grows to a bone-saw whine when it races the flats. Anything louder is imported: the creak of sled runners, the ocarina call of a brine scout, the muffled concussion of a sink-pit roof finally giving way.  

VEGETATION  
Sparse, specialized, jewel-small.  
• Soda-cress: thumb-high succulents that exhale pure oxygen at night; stalks taste of green lightning when chewed.  
• Flint-needles: grass analogues that grow a single season, die, and leave behind silica spicules sharp enough to cut rope.  
• Dream-lichen: iridescent scabs that appear only where High-Drown kelp ash collects; ground into paste, it is a mild euphoric worth its weight in copper.  

FAUNA  
Life survives by burrowing, gliding, or carrying its own shade.  
• Mirage ibex: skeletal antelope with horn-racks hollow as bamboo; they whistle while they run to keep lungs cool.  
• Ember kites: charcoal-black vultures that ride thermals until their wings heat enough to bake parasites dead. At dusk they drop, smoking slightly, onto anything that looks like carrion.  
• Brine scorpions: translucent, hand-sized arachnids that drink salt directly through their ventral plates, leaving perfect crystalline footprints behind.  

BUILT PRESENCE & TECHNOLOGY  
• Vapor stills: tripod towers of black glass that harvest the minute daily condensation, each yielding a single cup at dawn.  
• Salt sleds: runners of whale-back limestone shod in vitrified flint, hauled by teams of ibex or by sail when the wind allows.  
• Sink-bridges: tensegrity walkways of braided grip-vine (imported at ruinous cost) strung across the deepest pits. Many hum with the live code of Lift-Ghost repeaters, extending the machine-mind network far beyond the last waterfall.  

DYNAMICS  
Nothing rots; instead, things abrade, photobleach, or sublime. A dropped fish skeleton from a High-Drown cliff can last centuries here, wind-sculpted into lace. Storms arrive unannounced, freighted with dust and static, sandblasting everything in their path. Wells collapse, open new vents to the hidden aquifer, then salt over again in a single season.  

RULES OF THE PLACE  
1. Water is counted, not drunk. Every family keeps two tallies: what the stills made and what the mouths spent.  
2. Shadows are currency. A square metre of shade cloth rents for more than a silver knife.  
3. Metal lives short here; without moisture to build oxide armor, it heat-cracks instead. Pottery is king.  
4. Navigate by sink-pit constellations. Each has a signature mineral colour; at night they gleam faintly different under starlight.  

STAND IN THE MIDDLE  
Pick a dawn after a cold, cloudless night. Face east. The sun lifts—a disc too white to behold—and the entire pan goes mirror-bright. The horizon disappears and you float in a hemisphere of light, footsoles anchored only by the crunch of salt crust. Far off, a vapor still exhales a single silver thread that falls straight down: the only visible water for a hundred leagues. In that thread lives everything worth stealing or saving.

WHO LIVES IN THE DRYWALK EXPANSE  

1. The Brinestriders (human nomads)  
   • Physique: compact, tendon-tight, skin tanned almost grey by reflected light. Eyelids have grown a second, semitransparent membrane that flutter-clicks like camera shutters.  
   • Dress: layered veils of flint-needle cloth, woven so loosely it bleeds heat but blocks glare. Masks are ceramic, painted matte umber to kill reflection.  
   • Dwellings: collapsible rib-domes skinned in shade cloth. By day a camp looks like a clutch of upturned tortoises; by night it blooms, walls pulled back to release stored heat.  
   • Language: three modalities, inverse to the Water-Kept. At distance they flute on hollow ibex horns; nearer, they sign; indoors, they whisper, wasting neither saliva nor breath.  
   • Diet: brine-scorpion meat (all crunch and mineral), soda-cress mash, and condensed water sweetened with lily-safe syrup traded up from the cliffs.  
   • Social map: radial. A household is every person sleeping under the same stretch of shared shade; when shade is moved, so moves the household. Disputes resolve in measured shadow: the offended may stand in the aggriever’s patch for a number of sun-lengths agreed upon by elders.  
   • Work:  
        – Mire-seekers drop into sink-pits on ceramic ascenders, hunting the liquid brine that still circulates below. They return with raw salt nodules and, rarely, fossil tools coughed up from older ages.  
        – Glasswrights chase lightning storms, quenching the newborn fulgurites into blades and delicate condenser coils coveted by High-Drown mist-farmers.  
        – Walk-weathers scan the horizon for shimmer patterns that predict dust squalls two hours in advance—just long enough to reef camps and save eyes.  
   • Temperament: considered slow until the moment calls for speed; then they unfold like sprung steel.

2. The Crustward Engineers (descendants of pre-Retreat surveyors)  
   • Live in a semi-fixed lattice they call the Cantilever—a bridge never finished, now balanced on two Tidebones. It supports the only true library left on the Expanse: shelves of salt-sealed notebooks, each page etched with copper salts instead of ink.  
   • They hoard math and stories the way the Rust Shepherds hoard iron. Every five years a delegation climbs the lift-cages to exchange new star charts and sink-pit maps for barrels of turbine grease and stories sung by Lift-Ghost code.  
   • They disdain vapor stills, drinking instead a vinegared brine filtered through layers of powdered kelp ash. Outsiders consider them half pickled already.

3. Ember Ibex (domesticate, sort of)  
   • Selective breeding has turned the skittish wild stock into beasts that accept harness if not command. Their hollow horns now spiral, tuned by riders to different notes. A column of sleds sounds like an organ being hauled across the desert.  
   • At night the horns dew-collect; each curl can hold a fistful of water, enough to keep the animal from gnawing at precious cargo.

4. Fulgurites (mineral, nearly alive)  
   • High-silica tubes birthed by lightning. Some develop piezoelectric nerves: touch one and it rings, store a dozen and you can build a wind-powered carillon that sings forecasts two days ahead.  
   • When crushed and sifted fine, the powder fluoresces under starlight—used by scouts to read maps without lamps.

5. The Under-River (hydrologic phantom)  
   • A pressure vein of brine that runs beneath the Expanse exactly where the High-Drown rift-channels would intersect if extended downward. Once a season it finds a weakness and geysers upward, instantly vaporizing into a column of glittering salt rain.  
   • Brinestriders celebrate the event as First Memory, for the column sounds almost like surf. Any caught beneath are flensed in seconds, leaving ghost outlines in salt that last until the next wind.

6. Lift-Ghost Relays  
   • Old freight pylons still stand in the deeper sink-pits, ceramic insulators miraculously intact. The machine-minds that haunt the High Drown whisper through them, trading wind data for seismic readings the Brinestriders upload by tapping code on the pylons with bronze hammers.  
   • Rumour speaks of a consensus protocol forming—a slow computation that spans cliff, cloud and desert, intent on modelling the whole planet’s remaining water. Purpose unknown.

7. Insect Worlds  
   • Salt-fleas ride static updrafts, sparkling like festive sparks.  
   • Paper wasps here chew flint dust and soda-cress fibre into nacreous combs that refract dawn light into rainbows—useful for signalling, if you can stand the stings.  
   • Hollow beetles live inside dead fulgurites, drumming the walls to attract mates; a walking storm after dark can sound like distant applause.

8. The Far-Below Breathers (confirmation)  
   • Mire-seekers have brought back calcified masks shaped for jaws wider than any human’s and drilled to fit gills. The Kept insist these match tools snagged in kelp roots.  
   • Once a year, on the longest night, tremor-songs ripple upward through the limestone: bass notes so heavy they ripple the salt crust. The Engineers record them, then trade the phonograms uphill. No one yet has decoded the score.

LIFEWAYS, IN BRIEF  

Morning: Sun strikes; camps bloom open; vapor stills sigh their single cup into waiting flasks. Horns sound as ibex teams haul sleds toward the nearest fulgurite field, chasing last night’s storm scars.  

Midday: Heat brutal, wind erratic. Shade tents are reefed low. Children grind soda-cress into paste, mixing in traded lily-safe syrup—a luxury that tastes of distant rain. Lift-Ghost beacons click static overhead, echoed by beetle applause.  

Night: The crust radiates stored heat in soft waves. Skies blaze with more stars than darkness. Stories etched today are filed in copper-salt ink. Watchers pace the sink-bridges, listening for the hiss that would signal an Under-River breach. On the horizon the High Drown looms as a darker smear against the starfields; sometimes, if the wind is right, the faint roar of waterfalls arrives across the frozen silence, like breath remembered.

To live in the Drywalk Expanse is to count time in shadows, to measure wealth in sips, and to understand—no, to embrace—that absence can be as full of life as any flood, and that the world gives water in two ways: all at once, or not at all.
