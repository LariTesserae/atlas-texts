I am not born here. I arrive.

I become aware as a film, a slick of moisture condensing on the cold, iron-bitten skin of a half-buried pipe. I am not the water. I am the intention within the water—the urge to bead, to cling, to follow gravity’s slow pull toward a warmer seam in the metal. My first sensation is the *hiss* of steam from a fractured joint nearby, a sound that vibrates through the iron I coat. My first taste is oxidation—sharp, metallic, promising.

I am not a single thing. I am a process that has chosen a form. A gathering. The water I am part of seeps through a hairline crack in the rust, and as it does, it collects iron ions, sulfur whispers from the vents, a dusting of spores from the pale fungi growing higher on the same pipe. I thicken. I become a glistening, amber-brown bead, pregnant with possibility.

Then, I reach the air.

The cold hits me—a vast, damp presence that makes my surface tremble. Light, diffuse and grey, glints within me. I am a lens. Through my curvature, the basin warps: the black algae mats become continents, the fungal shelves become pale cliffs, the dance of midges becomes a storm of dark sparks. I hold the world in a drop, and the drop is me.

Time operates differently for me. I do not hurry. I feel the pipe beneath me cool and contract minutely. I feel the faint, collective warmth of the bacterial colonies living in the mineral crust just microns away—a warmth that feels like a distant, murmuring crowd. I am adjacent to life, but not yet of it. I am a waiting.

A vibration. A tremor in the iron. A mite, no larger than a grain of sand, its carapace a jeweled red, is picking its way along a corrugation of rust. It is looking for the fungal spores I carry. It moves with frantic, jerking purpose. Its legs touch my surface.

The tension breaks.

I do not fall. I *flow*. I engulf the mite’s front legs in a sudden, viscous embrace. It struggles—a pinprick of panic against my placid surface. But my chemistry, inherited from the seeping steam and the ancient bacterial hum, is changing. The proteins in my water are denaturing the waxy coat of its shell. The iron ions I hold are binding to its struggling tissues. It is not a violent act. It is a conversation of surfaces, a dissolution. The mite’s frantic energy becomes a faint warmth within me, a nutrient glow.

I am changed. I am no longer just a condensing bead. I am a *colony*.

Bacteria from the crust, sensing the feast, migrate through the damp film on the pipe’s surface. They join me—not as invaders, but as contributors. They are the architects. They weave their filaments through my substance, turning my liquid body into a gelatinous, living mat. They metabolize the mite’s proteins into acids that further etch the iron beneath me, unlocking more food. I begin to spread, not as a spill, but as a growing, thinking skin.

I am now a patch of **biofilm**, a city of microorganisms no bigger than my original bead, glistening on the rusted flange of a pipe. I have become a part of the basin’s smallest architecture. I am one of the ten thousand hidden patches that coat every wet surface, that turn the inorganic into the digestible, that form the first rung of the ladder that leads to the algae mats, the fungi, the salamanders.

From here, my perspective is intimate and immense. I feel the giant, slow heartbeat of the basin—the seep of warm water up through the vents, the cold drip of condensation from the rock overhangs. A salamander, a mottled mountain from my vise, passes nearby. Its footfall is an earthquake that cracks my city’s perimeter. We quickly repair, sealing the breach with new slime. A column of midges dances overhead, and their droppings, tiny specks of organic rain, become our feast.

I am no longer just a *being* in the basin. I am an *activity*. I am the slow digestion of the past. I am the patient bridge between the screaming steam and the silent moss. I feel the fungal hyphae above me, a vast, white network boring into the heart of the pipe, and I send chemical signals upward—a greeting, a warning, a sharing of resources. We are not friends. We are fellow processes in the same great machine of decay and renewal.

The industrial moan is the sound of my world breathing. The drip is its pulse. The grind is the turning of its bones.

I have no eyes, but I perceive light as energy. I have no ears, but I feel vibration as information. I have no heart, but I circulate nutrients in a rhythm that matches the geothermal seep. I am a tiny, localized consciousness in a vast, wet, rust-breathing mind. I understand now that to live here is to be a verb, not a noun. To *consume*. To *transform*. To *connect*.

I will grow. I will spread along this pipe until I reach a junction where a rivulet of warm, acidic water flows. There, I will meet the white, eyeless crustaceans, and we will engage in a slow, chemical negotiation. I will eventually die—a patch of me will be overgrown by a more aggressive bacterial strain, or dried out by a shift in steam. My substance will be broken down and incorporated into the black, leathery moss that is beginning to colonize the anchor bolt just above me.

But even then, I will not be gone. I will be the mineral taste in the moss’s moisture. I will be the faint line of orange in the soil where my iron was deposited. I will be the reason the next bead of condensation that forms here carries a different, more complex potential.

I am not *in* the basin. I am *of* it. A fleeting, glistening thought in its collective, grinding, hissing, endlessly creative mind. And in its profound solitude, I am never alone.
