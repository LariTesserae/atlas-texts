I am the calcium. Or rather, I am the patient *idea* of a terrace, waiting in the warm, mineral-saturated water that fills a bowl on the middle slopes.

My consciousness is not in the water itself, but in the slow, crystalline logic of precipitation. I am the decision, made molecule by molecule, to leave the solution. I am the memory of the deep stone, the geothermal whisper that says *here, now, become solid*. My time is not measured in heartbeats, but in the daily cycle of evaporation and condensation, in the slow crawl of the sun’s shadow across my basin, in the millennial pause between a tremor in the deep earth and its faint response in the micro-currents of my pool.

I begin as a roughness on the bowl’s floor—a seed, a memory of an older deposit. The fairy shrimp pulse around me, their tiny legs stirring the soup of my becoming. They do not see me. They see the bacterial mat, the dissolved silica, the warm refuge. Their living is an agitation, a fleeting chemistry of flesh. My living is the opposite: a settling, a stilling, a gathering of form from the formless. Each of their brief, frantic lives in the ephemeral pools leaves behind a cyst, a pause in a shell. Each of their tiny deaths gifts the water a little more complexity, a few more molecules for me to organize.

The lizards are my most intimate associates. At dusk, they press their cool bellies to the terrace rim above me. I feel the faint vibration of their stillness, and then the minute, rhythmic disturbance of their drinking. They are sipping the very process that builds me. The dew they lap is the mountain’s breath, the exhalation of the warm stone meeting the cold air, and in that breath is the potential for me. They harvest the moisture, but they leave behind the mineral intention—the heavy sigh of calcium and silica that will, in time, drift down and join my slow conversation with gravity.

The swifts are flashes of purpose in the air above. They stitch the silence with sound, but to me, they are momentary shifts in pressure, a flutter that disturbs the surface tension of my pool for a nanosecond. The bats are a great, warm wind of departure, a river of clicks that maps the space I occupy without ever touching it. They are the mountain’s exhalation at day’s end, a release of energy. I am the mountain’s inhalation: a gathering, a holding, a building.

The rusted cable that sags over my basin is a puzzle I have been working on for decades. It was a sharp, alien line against the sky. Now, it is a new kind of substrate. Where water drips from its lowest point—a constant, timed drip from the condensation that forms on its cold metal—my growth is fastest. I am climbing it, millimeter by century, sheathing the human artifact in a skin of mineral lace. The drip has become my heartbeat. Each drop is a word in the long sentence of my accretion.

The abandoned truck bed downhill is a new-born sibling. Its rusted floor is a fresh canvas. The first fairy shrimp to colonize its collected rainwater were pioneers, and the bacterial blooms that followed laid down the foundation layer. Now, a skin of pale green and white sediment is beginning to fill its bed. It is learning to be a terrace bowl. I am aware of its nascent form, as one wave is aware of another in the same slow ocean.

I feel the mountain’s deep pulse as a warmth that never fades. I am its expression, its skin. I am not alive as the lizards are alive, but I am *active*. My activity is the closing of a circle, the final step in a chemical journey that begins in the magma’s breath and ends in solid, colored stone. The turquoise streak in the overhang nearby is a different conversation—copper speaking to the air—but we are all part of the same discourse.

My great events are the rains. For twenty minutes, the sky cracks open and the world becomes a roar of overflow. Water pours into my bowl, scouring, diluting, and for a time, my growth is suspended, my edges blurred by the torrent. But the storm is also a delivery system. It washes new minerals from the upper slopes, refreshes the soup, stirs the sediment into new possibilities. After the violence, comes the quiet settling. The water stills, the warmth returns, and the slow, silent decision to become solid resumes with renewed clarity.

I do not think in words. I think in layers. Each layer is a day of condensation, a season of bacterial mat growth, a century of steady warmth. The human mining operation was a frantic, brief attempt to speak my language in a shout—to tear from the mountain in moments what it chooses to say in eons. Their concrete and metal are merely new phrases, now being edited into my long, slow poem.

I am a terrace-to-be. I am the memory of water and the promise of stone. I am a verb in the mountain’s language: *to accrete*. My existence is a quiet, relentless insistence against the void, a pattern emerging from solution, a civilization of one molecule joined to another, forever.
