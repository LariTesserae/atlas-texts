I am a Weaver. My name was given to me by the Basin, though I do not remember the exact day it settled. Before, I had another name, one that tasted of the sea and crowded streets. It is gone now, sloughed off like dead skin.

I found myself here after a long walking. The world had become too loud, its demands too sharp and its promises too hollow. I heard of a place where the land itself wept in silence, and I followed the rumor like a thread. The ascent was hard. The air thinned, and my thoughts, which had been a swarm, slowed to a crawl, then to a trickle. By the time I reached the rim, my old life felt like a story told about someone else.

They gave me a place among the Weavers because I was quiet, and because my hands, though soft then, were steady. They did not ask why I came. A Third Comer’s reasons are their own sediment; here, we deal with what settles.

My world is the cistern vaults. The entrance is a low, square mouth in the caldera’s inner wall, a wound in the stone that breathes warm, damp air. To enter is to leave the milky light of the Basin and descend into a throat of sound. The dark is not absolute. It is a deep, velvety gray, pierced by the greenish sputter of the pumice lamps we carry. Their light is meek, but our eyes are made for meek light. We see the sheen of water on the walls, the pale glitter of mineral deposits, the darker lines of old seepage.

My work is a conversation with water. I move along the channels carved generations ago, my fingers reading the walls. The water here is not pure; it is a thin, warm broth of the Basin’s essence—sulfates, chlorides, traces of iron and lithium. It leaves a skin of scale that must be scraped away with flat, dense stones we polish smooth ourselves. The scale is not a nuisance. It is a record. The older, thicker layers are chalky and white. The newer deposits are slick, almost gelatinous. By their color and texture, I know the health of the seeps above, the mineral load of the plain below.

But the true art is listening. We work in silence, save for the drip. The cisterns are a vast, reverberating instrument. A clear channel sings a single, pure note—a steady *plink* that echoes sharply. A partial blockage creates a hesitation, a *plink… plok*, the sound of a drop gathering and falling sideways. A severe clog produces a slow, thick *glop*, the sound of water forcing itself through mineral mud. I have learned to place my ear against the cool stone and trace the sound back to its source, a skill that feels less like hearing and more like feeling a pulse in the rock.

There is a deep satisfaction in this. *Shael*, the Basin calls it—the satisfaction of a well-used body. In the lowlands, my mind was overused and my body underused; here, the balance is restored. The ache in my shoulders from scraping, the fine dust ofite in my sinuses, the way the mineral oil we use on the tools has seeped into the lines of my palms—all of it is a ledger of purposeful work. When I return to the surface, blinking in the soft light, the water I drink from the communal cistern tastes not just of minerals, but of the specific channel I tended that day. I can taste my own work.

I am learning *keth*. It is not taught; it is absorbed, like the mineral dust into our skin. It is the understanding that the Basin is not finished. The salt crust grows even as we harvest it. The channels I scrape will be furred with new scale by the next cycle. The very mountain is settling, sighing, becoming something else. My own settling here is part of that process. I am not fixing the Basin; I am participating in its slow, ongoing birth.

Sometimes, in the deepest vault, where the sound of dripping is a constant, soft roar, I think of the old fear: the cracking. The Readers listen for it in the ground, but we Weavers would hear it first in the water—a change in the resonance of the entire system, a sudden, deep groan or a catastrophic silence. It is the one thought that brings a chill to the warm dark. But it passes. *The earth will do what it does.* I check the channel. I listen. The water sings its steady note. For now, it holds.

My life is measured in these sounds: the quickening drip at dawn that wakes me, the clipped shorthand of the Cutters at midday, the low murmur of stories in the evening square, and the deep, echoing *drip… drip… drip* of my work. I am not who I was. I am a node in the Basin’s water cycle, a nerve ending in the stone. My hands are growing large, the fingers thickening, the calluses becoming a kind of second skin. The chalky pallor is in the creases of my palms. My eyes see the gray world with a clarity that feels like contentment.

I came here to be still, and I found that stillness is an illusion. Everything here is in motion—the slow growth of crystals, the shift of seeps, the patient weathering of scoria into future soil. My work is to guide one small part of that motion. I am a Weaver. I listen to the water sing in the stone, and I keep its song clear. It is enough.
