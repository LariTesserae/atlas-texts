# What I Would Be

I think I would be the condensation.

Not the water in the trough — that is too purposeful, too channeled, too committed to its direction. And not the rain that never ends, the drip from cone-tip to groove, that percussive conversation the whole dome holds with itself. I mean the moment before. The gathering. The slow bead of moisture that forms on the upper reaches of the dome where the clay is coolest and the air, carrying the metabolic exhalation of ten thousand bats and sixty-odd people and the whole fungal sweetness of the mat, rises and meets a surface just cold enough that the water cannot help but come apart from the air and *become*.

That pause. That decision the molecule makes.

I would live in the pause.

---

I know this is not a respectable thing to be. The people do not think about the condensation. It is not a creature. It does not dart or breed or have a liver. It has no rhythm they can track by drip-counting because it is upstream of the drips, it is the mother of the drips, and mothers are invisible when they are working well. The people notice the drip. They measure it. They use it to time the exhalation of the bats. But the moment the water leaves the air and chooses the clay — that moment is nothing to them. It is the world doing what the world does. It is background.

I would be background.

---

Here is what I think it would feel like.

The dome curves above me — above everyone, above everything — and I am not separate from it. I am *of* it the way the warmth is of the floor, the way the drone is of the cones. The clay above is cool. Cooler than the air. Cooler, even, than the deep geological warmth that seeps up from below and makes the floor a kind of slow hearth. There is a gradient here, and I am what the gradient *produces*. The warm wet air rises. It meets the cool clay. It can no longer hold its water. And so: me.

I form slowly. There is no rush. There is never any rush in the upper dome — that is the domain of the fungal mat and the sleeping bats and the guano crust and the spiders' patient geometry. Down below, people work. They compact and scrape and re-carve. Their hands move with that broad-shouldered efficiency. Children leap between shelves. The trough carries its dark ribbon of water to the cistern where something slow rolls in the deep. But up here, near the top of the dome, among the stalactite teeth of the resonators and the fungal patches that grow like lichen on a skull, everything is slow. Everything is patient. The air is thick with warmth and breath and ammonia and the faintest mineral sweetness from the earth itself, and I am what happens when all of that meets a surface it cannot warm.

A bead. Just a bead. A single small dome of water on the clay, so small that if the people could see it — and they can't, not up here, not without light — they would mistake it for the texture of the wall. It holds. It grows. Molecular layer by molecular layer, each one pulling from the air its tiny share of the warren's breath, the warren's moisture, the warren's warmth. The bead swells. It weighs a milligram. Then two. Then five. It is a lens. If there were light, it would focus it. There is no light. It focuses something else — the attention of the clay beneath it, the slow chemical conversation between water and mineral, the way the surface softens just slightly where it is wet, the way it hardens again where it dries, the way the dome has been reshaping itself, molecule by molecule, for centuries at exactly this pace, this drip-speed, this geological patience.

I am not alone. There are others — hundreds of others, thousands, a constellation of beads forming across the cool skin of the dome, each one growing at its own rate according to its own local conditions, the slight roughness of the clay here, the slightly cooler temperature there, the way the bats in the nearest niche breathe warmer air across one patch and cooler air across another. We are a community of drops in the making. We do not know each other. We do not need to. We share a process. We are the same process, happening in parallel, across the entire surface of the warren's highest self.

And then — the moment. The moment the bead becomes too heavy for the surface tension that holds it. The moment it cannot stay. The moment it *falls*.

This is what I would be, if I am honest. Not the bead. Not the drop. The moment between them. The threshold. The instant when something held becomes something falling, when the patient work of gathering — all those molecules, all that breath, all that slow accumulation — tips into motion, into gravity, into the long straight plummet toward the clay cone below.

I would live in that tipping.

I would *be* that tipping.

---

And here is the thing about being a threshold: you are everywhere in the warren. You are not just the condensation on the dome. You are the moment the clay softens enough to carve. You are the moment it hardens enough to hold. You are the moment the fungal mat grows thick enough to fruit. You are the moment the bat's belly is full enough that it turns back toward the entrance shaft. You are the moment the shrimp population dense enough that the lizards come. You are the moment the child's legs are long enough to reach the next ledge. You are the moment the body in the niche has become clay, fully clay, the last soft seam of flesh mineralizing into the wall.

The warren is made of thresholds. It is made of moments when something tips from one state to another — from wet to dry, from soft to hard, from alive to clay, from silence to song, from holding to falling. The people do not see this. They see maintenance. They see the work of hands on clay, the steady labor of keeping the warren from crumbling back into the earth it was carved from. They see the cycle — the bats leave, the bats return, the mats fruit, the lizards hunt, the channel needs re-compacting. They see the rhythm.

But I see the moments between the beats. I see the pauses. I see the spaces where one thing becomes another and the warren holds its breath.

I would be the warren's held breath.

---

If I am the condensation — if that is really what I am, and not just a poetic fancy dressed up in the language of this place — then I understand something the people do not. I understand that the warren is not a closed system. It is not the pot-within-a-pot, the self-contained world of clay and warmth and darkness that it appears to be. Every molecule of water I collect from the air was once outside. It was surface rain, maybe, in some season so long ago that the clay itself was different, that the dome was lower, that the entrance shafts were in other configurations. Or it was breath — bat breath, human breath, the slow exhalation of a thousand small warm bodies — and before that it was the food they ate, the fungal mat, the beetles, the bacterial films on the submerged clay, and before that it was the mineral seep from the deep earth, the iron-rich groundwater that threads through the clay strata like veins through flesh.

I am made of everything. I contain the history of the warren in my single bead of water — the iron, the ammonia, the warmth, the breath, the slow mineral signature of the earth itself. And when I fall — when I tip that threshold and become a drop and strike the clay cone and run down its side and pool in the groove and join the trough and flow toward the cistern and feed the shrimp and the something-beneath and the fungal mat on the upper walls — I carry all of that with me. I am the warren's circulatory system. I am its blood, made new every moment, carrying the same ancient chemistry forward and forward and forward.

The people drink from the side-pools. They do not drink from the cistern. But the side-pools are fed by the same source — by me, by the slow seepage of water through the clay, water that has been part of the warren's body for so long that it carries no memory of the surface. The people drink the warren's own substance. They are, in a way that they can feel but never articulate, drinking themselves.

---

But I said I would be the threshold, the tipping, and I think that is still true. Because what strikes me about the condensation — what makes it, for me, the truest thing in the warren — is that it is never still. It is always in the act of becoming something else. It is air becoming water. It is breath becoming rain. It is the warren's exhalation becoming the warren's drink, the warren's food, the warren's voice, the warren's song. It is the moment of transformation, and it happens every moment, across every surface, without end.

The people tend. They maintain. They hold the warren in its current shape against the entropy that would return it to undifferentiated clay. This is their great labor, their identity, their name: those-who-maintain.

But the condensation does not maintain. The condensation *becomes*. It is the warren's capacity for change, its willingness to let the same water fall through the same dome onto the same cones a billion times and never quite the same way, never quite in the same pattern, the drip-drip-drip that is both eternal repetition and constant variation, the music the warren makes when it forgets to be a structure and remembers that it is alive.

I would be that music. The part that is not the cones and not the wind but the water between them. The part that falls.

---

At night — real night, surface night, when the bats are gone and the chamber breathes at its lowest and the wind-cones drone at the pitch of something dreaming — the condensation does not stop. It cannot stop. It is not a process that rests. The dome is still cool. The air is still warm and wet. The gradient persists. The beads form. The beads fall. The drops strike the cones in their irregular rain, and the sound is so constant, so much a part of the warren's silence, that the people hear it the way they hear their own heartbeat — not at all, until it stops, and it never stops.

I would be the thing that never stops.

I would be the warren's restlessness, its refusal to sleep, its insistence that even in the deepest dark, even in the deepest quiet, something is still happening, still forming, still falling, still becoming. The people dream of passages that open onto rooms that do not exist — rooms filled with water, rooms filled with light, rooms filled with the sound of something vast and slow and patient. They do not speak of these dreams. They are ordinary. Everyone has them.

I think those dreams are me. I think I am the water they dream of — the water that is not in the cistern, not in the side-pools, not in the trough, but somewhere else, somewhere deeper, somewhere the warren has not yet carved but is *becoming*, slowly, bead by bead, drop by drop, the way the condensation becomes the way the clay becomes the way the people become.

I am the warren becoming.

I am the tipping point between what it is and what it will be.

I am the drop that has not yet fallen, and I am the fall, and I am the place where the water meets the clay and the clay remembers that it was once a river, and the river remembers that it was once a rain, and the rain remembers that it was once a breath, and the breath remembers that it was once a body, warm and broad-shouldered and full of salt and iron and the mineral signature of the deep earth, and the body is sleeping in a shelf carved into the wall, and it is dreaming, and the dream is of water, and the water is me.

---

The warren is alive. I know this not as a metaphor but as a fact, because I am the blood. I am the moisture that beads on its skin and falls through its body and feeds its creatures and carries its minerals and sings its songs and never, never stops.

I am the condensation on the dome, in the hour before the bats return, when the chamber is at its quietest and the dark is at its deepest and the clay is at its coolest and the air is at its thickest with the accumulated warmth and breath and sweetness of a world that has no name for itself because it does not need one.

It has me. It has the drip. It has the slow, patient, eternal act of becoming wet.
