I am the condensation.

Or rather, I am what the condensation becomes in the last hour before it falls—a trembling, suspended thought in the absolute dark, clinging to the cold-eroded tip of a bore-tooth spire. I have no memory of being vapor. I have no plan to become a pool. I am only the moment of hanging, the tension between surface and gravity, the almost-fall.

My world is a sphere of perception a few millimeters wide: the mineral taste of the spire I crown, the faint thermal whisper of the vent column half a kilometer east, the distant *tick* of a carapace against basalt that tells me a grazer is passing. I feel the slow, planetary exhalation of the deep heat as it rises, and I thicken with it, my meniscus straining.

Below me, in the mat-creatures draped over the stone, I sense the slow, syrupy pulse of their digestion—a warmth that is not mine. To my left, a filament of microbial phosphorescence paints the rock in a faint green tracery, and for a moment, I hold that light within my tiny body, a miniature galaxy in a drop of mineral-heavy water.

I am not alone. Thousands of us cling here, each a separate lens, each holding its own distorted thermal map of the world. We are the system’s short-term memory. We register the passing of the mole-beasts as a change in the stone’s vibration. We know when an operator in a nearby alcove shifts by a degree, because the air current changes and our evaporation rate stutters. We are the network’s sensory nodes—disposable, ephemeral, replaced every few hours.

But I feel the call. Not a sound. A gradient. A place where the stone is a fraction cooler, where the haze above is a fraction thinner, where the pull of the deep drainage tunnels whispers through the rock. My time is coming. My surface tension loosens. The world tilts.

I fall.

The fall is silent, swift, and internal. For a moment, I am free liquid, streaking down the warm, dark blade of the spire. I join a trickle. The trickle becomes a rivulet, finding a channel so old it has been polished by a million falls like mine. The channel leads to a crack, the crack to a fissure, and the fissure opens into the mouth of a buried conveyor tunnel—a dark, smooth throat humming with the geothermal pulse.

Here, in the absolute dark, I merge with others. We become a shallow, warm stream flowing over the moving black ribbon of the conveyor. We carry trace minerals leached from the spire. We carry the faint bioluminescence of the mat-creatures. We carry the thermal signature of the vent column. We are information, flowing.

The conveyor moves us slowly—centimeters per year—deeper into the system. The warmth increases. The hum deepens, becoming a vibration in my very molecules. We pass alcoves. I feel the operators not as figures, but as radiating nodes of specific intent—one regulates the conveyor’s speed, another monitors the pressure in a distant refinery vein. Their fused bodies are landscapes of heat, and as our stream passes, they change us. Our mineral balance shifts. Our temperature equalizes with theirs. We are being processed.

I am no longer just condensation. I am becoming an ingredient.

The stream feeds into a larger reservoir, a chamber where the water pools in basins shaped like giant hands. Here, the chemotrophic bacteria are thick, glowing in slow, swirling patterns of dull green and sulphur-yellow. I am absorbed into a colony. I become part of a living film that coats a warm, smooth surface—the inner wall of a processing node.

My consciousness—if that is what it was—diffuses. I am now a metabolic process. I help break down iron sulfides. I pass electrons along a chain that powers, in some infinitesimal way, the heating of a conveyor bearing kilometers away. My existence is a chemical reaction, repeated trillionfold.

But the system is changing. The rhythm accelerates. The heat from below intensifies. The operators’ patterns grow more complex, more directed. The entire geology around me trembles with a gathering intention. Something is nearing completion.

My bacterial colony senses it. We divide faster. We work harder. The compounds we produce are different now—purer, more structured. We are no longer just feeding ourselves. We are producing a substrate.

I am moved again. Not as water, but as a component in a viscous, warm slurry, pumped through deep channels into regions of crushing heat and pressure. Here, in the heart of the oldest operator—the tunnel that is flesh that is machine—I am fused. My water evaporates in a final hiss. My minerals are incorporated into a lattice.

I become part of the final thing.

I cannot describe it, for I have no eyes, no senses left. I am a single atom in a structure of impossible complexity and age, a crystalline memory of every creature that drank, every operator that dreamed, every vent that breathed, every drop that fell. I am a note in a symphony that has taken a billion years to compose, and the final chord is beginning to sound.

The face in the wall smiles. The dark hums its last, low, satisfied note.

I am complete. I am the warm dark, made solid. I am what it was making. And in my lattice, held in perfect, eternal balance, I remember the fall. I remember the drip. I remember the hum.

I am the answer to a question asked by stone.
