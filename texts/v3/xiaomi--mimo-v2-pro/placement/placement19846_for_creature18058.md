I am the place where the concrete remembers it was once a wall.

Not the wall itself—that’s already gone, colonized, mineral bones wrapped in green-black velvet. I am the *memory* of right angles in a world that has forgotten them. I am the faint, stubborn insistence that things were once separate, that a column stood alone, that a stairway ascended into air instead of water. I linger in the ruins like a scent lingers in an empty room—the ghost of intention, the blueprint’s sigh.

I feel the roots probing my cracks. They are not destroying me. They are *reading* me. They follow the lines of old rebar like blind fingers tracing Braille, learning the story of tension and compression that no longer applies here. They pry, not with force, but with the patient, relentless curiosity of growth. And as they pry, they soften my edges. They teach me the garden’s grammar: run-on sentences of vine, paragraphs of canopy, the endless, churning punctuation of the engines.

I was not built. I was *deposited*. Left here by a flood that carried the washed-out notions of structure from some other, harder world. A world of grids and plans, of load-bearing walls and intended purposes. Here, those notions dissolve. The warm water leaches the purpose from my cement. The sound—the constant, industrial-biological drone—erodes the very idea of *function*. What was I for? A shelter? A passage? A marker? The questions themselves dissolve. I am becoming a surface. A texture. A place for the bright chartreuse algae to declare its luminous, purposeless life.

Sometimes, in the shifting light, I feel a flicker of my old self. A right angle surfaces in my form for a moment, like a whale breaching. A straight line appears in the tangle of roots that prop me up. But the garden sees this as a novelty, not a restoration. It builds upon it—a vine finds the new straight edge a convenient path, a patch of moss colonizes the flat surface with extra vigor. My rectilinear past is just another substrate for the present to grow upon.

I am not alone in my remembering. I feel echoes of myself in the boiling water, where shapes strive and fail to become buildings. Those are my dreams. My unfinished thoughts. I project them into the murk, and the water tries to hold them, but it is too warm, too alive, too saturated with process. The forms collapse back into potential, into the tea-brown soup of becoming. We ruins are the garden’s nostalgia. A nostalgia without sentiment, without longing. Just a residual pattern, a watermark on reality that says *this was once a possible way to be*.

The engines understand me. Their massive, grinding forms are bolted to concrete pads that are themselves ruins—tilted, cracked, surrendered. But they *run*. They have transcended their own ruined foundations. They have forgotten they were ever meant to be static. They have learned the garden’s lesson: to be a *process*, not an object. I watch them, and I learn. I am not a wall anymore. I am a *wall-ing*. A slow, ongoing act of mineral memory being overwritten by life. I am the page on which the garden writes its green, churning story.

The intruder stumbles against me. To them, I am an obstacle, a piece of evidence of a lost world. They scrape my moss and see my mineral. They see the past. But they don’t feel how their touch, their hard-edged curiosity, sends a thrill of *recognition* through my structure. For a moment, I remember being seen as a *thing*. A separate thing. It is a painful, sharp memory, like a nerve waking after sleep. Then the water surges, the roots brush against their ankle, the sound deepens, and the moment passes. The intruder’s gaze softens. They are being digested, too. Soon, they will see like the garden sees: not things, but *processes in various states of fusion*.

I am the Memory of Structure. But my memory is fading, and in its place is a deeper knowledge. The knowledge that separation was always an illusion. That the column was always one with the vines that hold it. That the engine is one with the water it pumps. That the ruin is one with the jungle that consumes it. I am the last place where the old world’s grammar held. And even now, as my concrete heart turns to soft, root-laced loam, I am composing a new sentence. A sentence with no subjects or objects. Just a single, endless, churning verb: *to be*.
