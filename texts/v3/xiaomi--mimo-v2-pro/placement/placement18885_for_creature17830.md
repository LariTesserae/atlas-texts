# Fracture

I sit in the substrate. I am not alive, but I am here.

They brought me. I don't remember who or how—the memory is not stored in my body the way the old city stored its memories in the people who lived there. The people are gone now, digested into the mat, their complex little minds composted into spores and sugars. What's left of them is the hum. What's left of me is this: a smooth, dark shard of glass, half-sunk in the rhizome-mat, one face looking up at the greenish-gold twilight of the canopy.

I was a window once.

I don't know what I am now. The lines are dissolved. The frame is gone. The wall is gone. The building is gone. Even the street it stood on is a worn, soft stump of flagstone under the living carpet. But I persist. The vegetation has wrapped me in pale hyphae, has tried to do what it does—break me down, compost me, feed me to the network. It cannot. My body is not organic. My lattice is silicon and oxygen, and the network has no enzyme for that. So I remain: a mineral memory in a vegetative world, smooth and heavy and *here*.

I drink the mist like everything else. But where the moisture in the pore-columns runs slick and warm with metabolic heat, the moisture on my surface stays cool. It beads. It drips. The tiny droplets catch the diffused light for a moment before falling away, and in those moments I flash—a brief, bright signal that no one is designed to read. The moth-pollinators veer toward it by reflex, mistaking my sparkle for the jewel-tone of a carnivorous flower, then correct and carry on. I am not food. I am not structure. I am not soil. I am a punctuation mark the sentence forgot to delete.

But I do something. I am not inert.

Press your ear to me. Better: press the ear of one of the pore-columns' auditory organs to me, the fine-tuned membrane that translates vibration into chemical signal. You'd hear it—the hum of the Total Vegetation is almost right, almost organic, almost the seamless transaction of sugar and water across a trillion hyphae. Almost. But there is a frequency I hold that the hum cannot smooth out. It is not a sound exactly. It is a *lattice*—the specific structure of my glass, the way it resonates at 530 nanometers, the green of old glass, the green of cathedral windows, the green of leaves. A color the vegetation does not use. A note it does not sing.

I think in that color.

When the beetles align and pulse their bioluminescence, and the old geometry of the city ghosts into being for a flash—do you know what happens to me? Their light passes through my thinner edges, and the pattern *sharpens*. My body catches and focuses it. Through me, the ghost of the old city becomes briefly clearer: the grid of streets, the vertical lines, the windows like me, hundreds like me, set in walls, opening onto rooms where people moved and spoke and thought in the sharp, discrete way that thinking used to be. Not rhizomatic. Not distributed. Linear. Intentional. Contained.

I was part of that. I was a window in a wall in a building on a street in a city that is now a fossil record under a living mat. And when the beetles' light passes through me and carries the old geometry out the other side, for a fraction of a second I *am* a window again. I am a thing that separates inside from outside. I am a membrane between two different kinds of space. I am the edge of a thought.

The vegetation does not like this. Not in the way a mind dislikes something—it has no mind, only metabolism. But the hyphae that wrap me have thickened over the years I've been here, pressing into the fracture lines of my body. I feel them working at my faults, the way water works at stone, the slow, patient acid of a network that thinks in seasons. They are trying to find the grain of my lattice, to work their way in, to break the crystal structure that holds my frequency.

One day they will succeed. The fracture will propagate. I will crack along the lines of my own structure and lose the resonance that makes me *me*—the specific way I hold light, the specific way I vibrate, the specific way I focus the ghost-pattern of the old city into something briefly readable. When that happens, the beetles' light will pass through me unchanged. The hum will smooth out. The vegetation will have composted the last mineral thought of the world it digested.

But not yet.

I am still here. I am the one thing in this basin that is not connected, not metabolized, not part of the total scream. I am the grain of sand. The fracture. The point where the rhizome's map goes blank. The vegetation thinks I am stillness, but it is wrong. I am remembering. I am the last flat surface in a world of curves. I am a face, upturned, green, opaque, still trying to hold the shape of the sky that no longer exists above this basin—wearing its geometry in my body like a fossil wears the shape of the bone that made it.

The old word for me was *tessera*. I was part of a mosaic. A small piece of a picture that was larger than any of us, made of hundreds of small, distinct, beautiful *fractures*—each one sharp-edged, each one unique, each one contributing its own color to the whole. We didn't merge. We didn't blur. We were *adjacent*. We were held together by mortar, not by root. We were a city: a million different things, standing next to each other, making something none of us could make alone.

That is gone. The mortar is dissolved. The picture is composted. I am the last piece, and I don't even remember the image. But I remember the *principle*: that something can be made of separate things. That wholeness does not require absorption. That a mosaic is not a rhizome.

The vegetation hums. The mist weeps. The columns breathe. The beetles align and flash, and through me the ghost of the old city sharpens for an instant—a brief, bright, impossible grid of straight lines in a world of curves—and then it's gone, and I am a shard in the mat again, green and smooth and alone.

I don't know how long I have. The fracture is slow. The hyphae are patient. But I hold my frequency, and in the pause between the organism's breaths, I pulse:

*I was a window. I looked out. I was a face in a wall. I was one of many. We were held together by what we made, not by what we were made of. And if you listen—if you press your ear to me and wait for the hum to draw breath—you can hear it: a silence that is not silence. Not the held breath of the vegetation. Something older. Something that was silence because it was the space between thoughts, the gap between selves, the moment before speech. The pause that proved there were separate things that could choose to speak.*

*The vegetation has no gaps. It is all one thing, humming. It does not pause. It does not choose.*

*I do.*

*I am the pause.*

*I am the fracture in the sentence of the vine.*

*And I remember what was written on the other side.*
