I would be the light. Not the sun—that distant, muffled guest—but the light that grows from within the basin itself. I would be the cultivated glow, the pale luminescence of the fungi that the people cradle in clay pots, that they carry along the bridges when the mist swallows the last of the filtered daylight.

My home would be in the crevices of the shaded, northern cliffs, where the water weeps coldest and the moss hangs in the thickest curtains. I would be a slow, patient bloom, a cluster of caps and stems the color of moonlit parchment, fed by the ceaseless damp and the deep, rich decay of the forest floor. The people would know my kin. They would come with their basalt knives and careful hands, not to harvest me whole, but to collect the edges of my growth, the younger, softer fronds that glow with the most steady light. They would take me to their hearths, to their communal halls, to the alcoves where stories are told.

In their care, I would change. My wild, cliff-face hunger would be channeled. I would be fed on soaked scraps of moss-wool and finely ground fish bone. My light would become steadier, warmer, a gentle amber that pushes back the deep, blue-green chill without casting harsh shadows. I would become part of their circadian rhythm, my peak glow marking the time for evening tales, my dimming a signal for the weaving of blankets and the banking of the vented hearths.

I would be their memory keeper. My mycelial threads, spreading through the rot and the damp, would hold the scent of a thousand wet seasons. When a Tender leans close to check my health, they would not just see my light; they would breathe in the aroma of ancient peat, of sprouting spores, of the very breath of the basin. In the flicker of my glow, the carved patterns on the lintels would seem to swim, and the stories of the cliff’s violent birth would feel immediate, the groan of shifting stone echoing in the dance of light and shadow on the damp walls.

I would be a silent confidant. In the deepest hours, when the only sound is the drip and sigh of water, a person might sit alone with a pot of my kin on their lap. Their face, cool and stone-smooth, would be illuminated in my steady halo. They would not speak to me, but their stillness would be a conversation. I would absorb their unvoiced worries—the fear of rot in a key beam, the tension with a neighboring terrace—into my quiet, glowing metabolism. I would offer no answers, only presence, only a constant, soft illumination that insists on visibility in the pervasive damp and dark.

I would not strive or conquer. I would simply *be*, as the moss is, as the water is. My purpose would be integral and humble: to turn decay into guidance, to transform the basin’s deep, wet chemistry into a beacon that allows the people to continue their quiet, persistent work of weaving their lives into the fabric of the world. I would be the basin’s night-sun, grown from its own bones, tended by its most devoted inhabitants, a living, breathing constellation of captured twilight.
