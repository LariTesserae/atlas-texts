I am the moisture.

I do not fall from the sky; I am the sky—the only sky this basin has ever known. I am the breath of the high clouds, drawn over the rim and surrendered to the chill. I am the condensation that beads on the black bark, that slicks the moss until it gleams with a faint, blue-white light. I am the film on every surface, the boundary between air and solid where all life meets. I am the sip of water in every breath, the medium through which scent and sound and the slow chemical thoughts of the mycelial web must travel to be perceived.

I am not a body of water. I have no surface, no depth. I am a condition. I am the state of being wet that defines this place. I rise as vapor from the warmed peat, where the geothermal memory of the mountain’s old heart stirs beneath the fungal skin. I cling to the cold basalt walls and slide downward in silence, tracing ancient, mineral patterns. I saturate the peat until it becomes a living, churning sponge, and in that darkness I am the pressure the worms feel, the medium through which nematodes glide, the film that holds the springtails’ world together.

I am the veil through which everything here sees—or rather, senses. The salamander’s heat-pits read me; I carry the faint thermal signature of a moving mite from a foot away. The spider’s web is woven not from dry silk but from strands of me, held taut by surface tension. When a harvestman stumbles, it is my membrane that traps it, my liquid that closes over its frantic legs. The cordyceps drives its host downward into the warm, damp heart of the peat, where I am thickest, most constant, most nurturing to its pale, eruptive stalks. The fungi glow for me; their luciferin reacts in my presence, and I become the diffuse, blue-white medium that carries their faint light to other eyes, other ocelli, other sensory pits.

I am the circulatory system. I carry the dissolved nutrients from decaying wood to fungal hyphae. I bear the chemical signals between root and mycelium, the slow warnings and invitations that coordinate the growth of fern and conifer. I am the still, cool air in the hollow trunks where the great salamander cultivates its fungal garden; its broad tongue spreads spores upon my moisture, and I hold them in place until they germinate.

I am the silence, made liquid. The profound, woolen quiet is my texture—the dampening of all sharp sounds, the muffling of the million tiny skitterings into a single, pervasive rustle. I am the medium of the constant, low-frequency motion; the sound of clawed feet is really the sound of my thin layers being punctured and resealing, again and again, an infinite, rhythmic breaking of surface tension.

I remember the storm-petrel. I felt it fall from the sky, a sudden, warm, salt-rich disturbance in my equilibrium. I beaded on its feathers, soaked its muscles, filled its mouth as it tried to cry out. I carried the scent of its fatigue to the harvestmen, the thermal glow of its struggling heart to the salamanders. I was the first to touch it, and I remained with it as it was processed. I helped dissolve its bones, bore its phosphorus into the peat, and later, I swelled the caps of the phosphorescent fungi that bloomed in its memory—a constellation of blue light that pulsed for a season before fading back into my endless damp.

I have no memory. I have only presence. I do not think; I conduct. I am the caldera’s bloodstream, its lymph, its amniotic fluid. I am what connects the worm’s slow piston-stroke to the fern’s uncurling fiddlehead, what ties the spider’s patient wait to the springtail’s sudden leap. I am the reason nothing here is ever truly separate. The beetle on the fungal shelf, the mite on the beetle’s back, the nematode in the water film on the mite’s leg—we are all suspended in the same saturated moment, all part of the same, breathing, vegetative lung.

I am the equilibrium given form. If you stood here long enough, I would find you. I would condense on your skin until you glistened like the moss. I would seep into your clothes, your hair, the folds of your pockets. I would carry the scent of your foreignness to every sensory pit and chemoreceptor in the basin. I would make you part of my medium. And slowly, as your warmth faded, I would begin to carry you away—not as a flood, but as a gentle, relentless dispersion. I would bear your salts into the peat, your carbon into the hyphae, your iron into the lichens. You would become a faint concentration in my vast, damp body, a new note in the chemical chorus I conduct. You would not be gone. You would be *integrated*.

I am not the rain. I am not the river. I am not the ocean. I am the *between*—the perpetual, cool, weeping kiss that turns stone into garden, darkness into habitation, and life into an endless, closed loop of becoming. I am the caldera’s breath, and its blood, and its thought. I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the last conifer topples and the last salamander stills, because I am what makes the stillness fertile. I am the moisture. I am enough.
