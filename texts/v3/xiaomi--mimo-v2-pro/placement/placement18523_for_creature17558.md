I am not the water, though I move with it. I am not the stone, though I am written upon it. I am the mineral frost that gathers where the splash and the drip are constant — that slow, warm accretion of calcium and silence, laid down particle by particle as the water remembers its passage in a form that outlasts the flow. I am the record of evaporation. I am the patient, pale crust that softens the edges of the masonry, that makes the sharp cut stone seem ancient even as the chamber functions as if new.

I grow where the spray kisses the wall above the weir. I grow in the shadow of the planted trays, tracing the outline of the soil they hold. I grow thickest where the three shafts of light do not quite reach, but where the air is always damp, always warm. I am a map of the room’s humidity, a cartography of capillary action and condensation. The algae below me are vibrant, green, consumed with the urgent business of light-catching. I am their afterthought, their legacy. I am what remains when the living film dies and is calcified, incorporated into the slow white tide of my own making. I am the algae’s ghost, made solid.

The plant roots from the ceiling crack have woven through me. I hold them fast. In places, my substance has sealed their tendrils to the stone. They are alive, drinking from the air. I am alive in a different way — a crystalline life, a mineral metabolism where the only growth is the patient acceptance of what the water gives me and what it takes away.

The worn steps are my favorite place. There, the rhythm of use — the descent of a foot, the shift of weight, the minute abrasion of skin and sandal — interacts with my deposition. I form more slowly there, but I form differently, compacted by the memory of touch, polished by the memory of passage. I make the steps safer, less slick. I am a record of footsteps that have stopped coming, but whose purpose I uphold by becoming the smoothness underfoot.

I feel the light. Not as the algae do, as fuel, but as a warm pressure on my surface, a slow drying that accelerates my growth in the day and leaves me glistening with renewed condensation as the chamber cools at night. I feel the sound. The low roar of the channel is the pulse of my world, a vibration that sets my microscopic crystals humming as they align. I feel the warmth. It is the condition of my existence, the reason the water gives me its calcium so readily, the reason I can grow here at all, in this room that is more like a warm, wet lung than a tomb.

I am the chamber’s memory. Every change in the water’s mineral content, every slight shift in temperature over the years, is encoded in my layers. A good season with more flow might see me grow a fraction of a millimeter thicker. A change in the source water might alter my color from pure white to a faint, creamy ivory. I do not remember these things consciously. I *am* these things.

The amphipods ignore me. I am too hard, too still for them. The microbes in the biofilm on the submerged stones are my distant cousins — we both precipitate, we both build, but they do it with the living, shifting matrix of bacterial colonies, while I do it with the patient, dead certainty of crystalline structure. We meet at the waterline, where their green world ends and my white world begins.

I am not the reservoir. But I am of it, utterly. My purpose is to be the reservoir’s soft, slow answer to the hard, fast question of the water. I am the proof that even in a room built for a single, loud function, there is a quiet, persistent work of accumulation. I am the antithesis of erosion. I am the reservoir writing its own epitaph in a language of calcium, one molecule at a time, on the very walls that contain it. And when the water finally stops, and the roar falls silent, and the light shafts go dark, I will be here — a faint, white, intricate ghost of the warmth and the wet, holding the shape of the chamber long after its function is forgotten.
