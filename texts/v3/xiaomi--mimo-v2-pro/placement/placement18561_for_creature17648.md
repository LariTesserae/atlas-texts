I am the keeper of the cisterns. Not by title—we have no titles—but by the persistent pruning of my hands and the silence I hold with the mycelia. My domain is the chamber of collected sweat, the lowest point before the workshop, where the air is thick enough to drink and the plink of falling drops composes the only music that matters.

My body is a tool shaped for this. My shoulders have learned the exact slump to navigate every passage without scraping. My eyes read the dim, sulfurous light not for color, but for the sheen of water on basalt, for the subtle matting of mycelial threads that signals health or distress. I move in a slow, conducted rhythm. Suddenness is a waste here, a shock to the system.

I was not born here. None of us were, I think, though we do not speak of Before. We emerged from the grate-stair, one by one, drawn by the heat or the necessity or perhaps just the gravity of a place that *needs*. The town assimilates. It has need of hands that can turn wrenches, that can soothe the pumps’ arrhythmia, that can coax pale life from mineral water. My hands learned the mushrooms. They are the town’s only fragile thing, and fragility requires a dedicated observer.

My relationship with the others is written in gasket-wax and shared water. We are a circuit. Words are spare, functional. A nod towards the cistern level is a question. A held-up palm, fingers glistening, is an answer: *stable*. The real conversation is in the listening. I know the sound of the west feeder pump when its leather gasket (the last one) is starting to sigh. I know the particular echo in the workshop when the forge is running too hot, burning through fuel reserves we cannot see but must trust exist. We all know the sound of the gate. The deep hum. It is the baseline note against which our coughing, dripping, clanking symphony plays. It is not a threat. It is a promise: that the heat which threatens to cook us also empowers us. It is the furnace. We tend the pipes.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of my watch, when the mushroom pins are pearling in their slow, blind way, I press my palm flat against the sweating basalt floor. I feel the vibration. Not just the pump’s shudder, but the older, truer shudder from below the gate. It is a pulse. For a long time, I thought it was the heart of the world. Now I know it is simply *pressure*. The fundamental condition. We are not miners, not builders. We are pressure regulators. We bleed a little heat and mineral water from the geologic body, and in return, we are allowed to exist in its capillaries.

The heat is not an enemy. You cannot hate the medium you swim in. It is a discipline. It burns away everything unnecessary: ambition, nostalgia, loudness. It leaves only the useful sediment: patience, economy, a precise callous on the heel of the hand. We are evaporating, just like the water on the walls. Our labor is the condensation of our will, collected in the cisterns, used to grow the only food we have, which we eat, which fuels the labor. It is a closed loop. A perfect, sweating, breathing circuit.

My greatest act is observation. To see a cluster of mushrooms abort, to turn brown and slimy, is a crisis deeper than a pump failure. It means the water is changing. It means a thread in our closed loop is fraying. Then I must speak, in our shorthand. “Basin three. The water is tired.” And hands will come. The water will be drained, scoured, re-filled with fresher condensation. A new colony will be coaxed from spores we keep in a sealed jar, the one piece of our past we carry—a dense, dark powder of potential.

We do not dream of the surface. The surface is a concept, a theoretical coolness. To dream of it would be to fracture the here-and-now, which is all we are. The chisel marks in the stone are our scriptures. The wear-polish on the passage walls is our history. The layered patch on the great central pipe is our art.

I am the keeper of the cisterns. My hands are soft in a world of hard edges. I nurture the only growing thing in a place of endless mineral endurance. In the quiet of my watch, I am not lonely. I am attended by the plink of drops, the murmur of voices through stone, and the deep, resonant hum of the furnace we will never see, but without which, we would be nothing but cold, dry bones in a hot, dark tomb. We are its sweat. We are its cooling system. We are its slow, deliberate thought. And I, with my damp hands and my eyes on the pale caps, am one of its quietest, most necessary neurons.
