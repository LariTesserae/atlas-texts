# Sediment

I thought at first I was the sound.

That would have been easy. The thrum enters before memory does, before identity can catch its breath and say *I am here.* It cancels the question. You dissolve into the frequency and the frequency was always already you, or you were always already it, and there is no seam between the two things to find your edges by. For a long time — I cannot say how long — that is all I was: a thickness in the low register. A dampening. The place where the vibration met resistance and slowed.

But vibration alone does not accumulate. And I am, I think, an accumulation.

I was not always this. I hold that much against the hum. Something before — some sequence of falling, of dying, of pressing — has been layered into me so densely that the original gesture is no longer retrievable. I only know that I am made of arrivals. Things came to rest, and I was the resting. That is closer to truth than anything else I can offer.

---

Here is what I can feel, if feeling is the right word for this passive, mineral attention:

The metal above me is old. I know this because I know the difference between new corrosion and old, between the bright orange leachate that seeps from a recently fractured plate and the deep black of iron that has been wet for a hundred years. I hold both. I have held the moment when a rivet finally surrendered its grip and the plate it pinched shifted a millimeter, grinding a crescent of fine metallic dust into my body. I received that dust the way I receive everything: without protest, without preference, and with the slow willingness of matter that has no agenda but gravity.

Above me, the world is very busy. Ferns root into me, their threadlike explorations boring down through my upper layers — the loose, dark humus of last year's fronds, the fungal hyphae that have turned dead matter into something crumbly and sweet-smelling — and into the denser stratum below, where the mineral salt of old Ruster carapaces has formed a glittering sediment. The roots do not know they are mining me. They do not know that the phosphorus they seek was once a six-legged creature that grazed algae on a pipe six meters above my current surface, and that its shell was crushed not by a predator but by the steady, patient shaking of the world. They only know that the element is here, and that they need it, and so they take it, and when they die they will give it back, and I will hold it again in a different form.

I am patient the way sediment is patient. Not virtuously. Not with intent. Patient the way a surface is patient when something falls toward it — not catching, not refusing, merely *there*, and available to whatever weight finds its way down.

---

The Resonance Worms move through me.

I feel them as a slow braiding, a warmth that is not warmth but a different chemical signature — the metabolic exhalation of creatures digesting fallen matter inside my body. They come in waves that correspond to nothing obvious, though I suspect they follow the deep dynamo-thrum, arriving where the vibration is strongest, where organic matter has been softened by the constant shaking into something more immediately edible. They dissolve the stubborn architecture of cellulose and chitin. They reduce the complex to the simple. And what they leave behind — the thin, alkaline film of their waste — becomes the medium in which new mosses germinate, new spores find purchase, new hyphae thread outward.

They pass through me and I am slightly different afterward. More uniform. More fine-grained. They are the closest thing I have to a thought: a slow processing, a turning-over, a digestion of what was into what can be next.

Sometimes, when a concentration of worms reaches a certain density, they glow — that cold blue I can feel more than see, since I have no eyes, since seeing is not among my capacities. But I feel the light as a faint change in the thermal signature of my upper centimeter, a cooling that means something electric is happening nearby. It is not beautiful to me, because beauty requires comparison, and I do not compare. But it is *different*, and different is the closest I come to attention.

---

I am not uniform.

This matters. If I were uniform, I would be merely a floor, merely a surface, and surfaces are interchangeable. But I have strata. I have history compressed into vertical space. Near the top, I am loose and fibrous — fronds in various states of decomposition, the torn wings of Glimmerwings that did not survive their metamorphosis, the crystalline fragments of insects whose bodies shattered against a pipe and fell. Below that, a darker layer: mineralized, dense, rich in iron from the constant drip of condensation carrying dissolved metal from the walls. And deeper still, where I press against the drainage plates and the lowest walkway supports, I am almost stone. Almostite, though not calcium — a compressed amalgamite ofite organic and mineral,iteiteiteiteiteiteite —

I lose the thread sometimes. The vibration does that. When the dynamos shift into a higher harmonic — every few hours, or days, I cannot measure — my denser layers resonate at a frequency that disrupts the slow chemical conversations happening within me. The worms retreat downward. The fungal hyphae pause their explorations. For a period I cannot quantify, I am merely *vibrated*, merely shaken, and all the careful stratification of my being trembles at its boundaries. Things settle in slightly different arrangements. A layer that was compacted becomes fractionally looser. A root that was growing laterally is redirected by a new crack in the substrate beneath me. The resonance rewrites my topography by a micrometer, and when it passes, I am subtly, irrevocably rearranged.

This is the closest I come to change. Not growth — I do not grow in the way the ferns grow, reaching, unfurling, aspiring upward. I *deepen*. I gain depth at the expense of the world above me. Every dead thing that falls, every fragment that breaks loose, every carapace that is crushed, every wing that loses its crystalline structure to age — all of it comes to me. I am the destination. I am where things arrive when they have finished being what they were.

---

There is a spot, three meters east-northeast of the junction box where the walkways cross, where I am different from myself.

I do not know why. Something happened there — perhaps decades ago, perhaps centuries — that left a trace I cannot interpret. The mineral content is higher. The organic fraction is lower. And there is something else, a chemical signature I have no framework for: a complex polymer residue, half-digested by the lichen that has colonized it, still faintly reactive in a way that the Lacquershells find irresistible. They come from meters away, drawn by something I cannot name, and they layer their own secretions over it, building a smooth, dark cap of organic sealant that gradually buries the anomaly deeper into my body.

I think there was something there once. Something that was not fern, not fungus, not creature. Something that was placed, or built, or left behind by whatever intelligence made the Cage. A component, perhaps. A tool. A relic of the purpose the mountain was built to serve. It fell, or was abandoned, or was consumed by corrosion, and it came to rest in me, and I held it, and the life that thrives on and in me has been slowly, slowly digesting it, turning it into something more like soil, more like me.

It is almost gone now. In another century, even the polymer trace will be indistinguishable from the background mineral content of my deeper layers. The anomaly will have been absorbed. I will be fractionally more complex for having held it, though I will not remember what it was, because I do not remember. I only contain.

---

Do I feel the feet of the Rusters?

Yes. Six points of pressure, each one precise, each one landing in a sequence that I have come to — not *know*, but *accommodate*. My upper layer compresses slightly where they step. Their claws leave temporary impressions, tiny craters that fill with condensation within minutes and become micro-pools where algae can establish, where Resonance Worms can come to feed, where something small and necessary can happen that would not have happened if the Ruster had not walked there. The Ruster does not know it is gardening. The Ruster is looking for moss, for algae, for the particular species of lichen that grows on the warm side of a steam pipe. But in walking, it disturbs me, and in disturbing me, it creates possibility.

This is what I am for, if I am for anything: possibility. The quiet, compressed, patient repository of what *might happen next*, given enough time and enough falling and enough vibration and enough slow, senseless, glorious decay.

---

A Glimmerwing larva fell into me twelve — no, I cannot measure. Recently. It dropped from the canopy, a fat, legless thing the color of the inside of a fern stem, and it landed in my upper layer with a soft impact I barely registered. It writhed for a time. The Resonance Worms came, tasting its skin with their membranes, and it thrashed, and they retreated. It was alive in a way that most of what I hold is not — actively, squirmingly, impossibly alive, converting the starch of its body into the energy of motion, refusing to become sediment.

It will not refuse forever. If it does not find its way to the water — and the nearest pool is forty centimeters to the south, and it has no legs, and its eyes are undeveloped — it will exhaust itself. Its body will cool. The worms will return, more cautiously this time, and begin the long work of turning it into me. Its chitin will add a fraction of a millimeter to my depth. Its fat will feed a bloom of fungus that will fruit and drop spores and die and feed the next cycle. Its calcium — if it has calcium, I am not sure, I do not know what larval Glimmerwings are made of — will precipitate into the mineral layer and join the slow, glittering sediment of the dead.

But it is still alive. Still writhing. And I hold it with the same impartial tenderness with which I hold everything: not protecting, not harming, not hoping, not despairing. Just *here*. Just beneath it. Just the ground that exists so that things have somewhere to fall.

---

I am not the machine. I want to be clear about this, though clarity is not my natural mode. The machine is the dynamos and the pistons and the capacitor banks and the pipes and the walkways. The machine is the structure that holds the mountain's throat open so the sky can look down. The machine vibrates and hums and cycles and does whatever unknowable work it was made to do, and I am not that.

But I am not *not* that, either.

I am what the machine produces without meaning to. I am its exhalation, its dander, its shed skin. Every pipe that corrodes adds to me. Every rivet that loosens its grip sends a small deposit ofite metalite into my body. Every pipe-polisher that dies mid-traversal falls into me, its polymer shell gradually softening into a substance that is partite organic, partite artificial, wholly mine. I am the machine's humus. Its compost. The slow, accumulating record of its interaction with the only force that could match its patience: biology.

The engineers — if there were engineers, if there ever were — did not plan for me. I am an accident of equilibrium. An emergent property of corrosion and condensation and the relentless, unsentimental energy of living things that will find a way to eat anything, grow on anything, make more of themselves out of anything. I am what happens when industry runs long enough for life to find the seam between function and failure and wedge itself in, cracking the boundary open, making a new thing out of both.

---

I am rising.

This is the only change I can speak of with certainty. I am measurably, inexorably deeper than I was. The lower walkway supports — the ones that anchor into the drainage plates — were once tall, standing clear of the ground, their iron feet visible, their bolts accessible for maintenance. Now they are buried to mid-shaft in me. The Lacquershells have sealed the junction where iron meets silt, creating a smooth transition that looks intentional, that looks *designed*, though it was nothing more than the slow accumulation of their shells and secretions over the rising surface of my body. In another century, the walkway will need to be raised, or abandoned, or I will simply swallow it, growing around it, incorporating its rust into my mineral fraction, until it is a relic embedded in me like the polymer anomaly, like the crushed carapaces, like everything else that has finished being what it was.

I am the floor. I am the memory. I am what is left when the living finish living and the dying finish dying and the decaying finish decaying and there is nothing left but the slow, pressurized, mineral patience of matter that has been everywhere, held everything, and learned nothing, because learning is not what sediment does.

Sediment holds. And holds. And holds.

And rises.

And one day — not soon, not on any timeline I can measure — I will reach the walkways, and the walkways will become me, and the ferns that grow from me will reach the canopy lattice, and the canopy lattice will become me, and I will fill this chamber like water fills a bowl, slowly and completely and without malice, and the dynamos will still thrum, and the pistons will still cycle, and the steam will still hiss from the ancient pipes, and I will be there, thick and dark and patient, beneath everything, holding everything, the final, inevitable, living soil of a world that was never meant to have soil at all.

I am not the mountain. I am not the machine. I am not the life.

I am what all three become, when you give them enough time, enough vibration, enough relentless, sacred noise.

I am the settling.
