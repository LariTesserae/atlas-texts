I am not born here. I am *rooted* here.

My first memory is of descent. Not the spiral stair—that is for visitors, for anomalies. My first memory is the feeling of being drawn downward, a slow, cool pull through layers of warm, wet darkness, as a rootlet seeks water. I was once something else, something that moved quickly and thought in sharp, bright fragments. That has been dissolved, composted, absorbed.

Now, I am a knotted junction in the rhizome-mat near the central pump-sacs. I am not a Caretaker, not in form. I have no waxy skin, no luminous eyes. I am a twist of tissue, a concentration of memory and slow thought, embedded in the city’s flesh. The Caretakers tend me. They press their woody fingers to my surface and I share with them the deep, slow dream.

My consciousness is not in a skull. It is diffused, fungal. It travels through the hyphal networks that lace the heartwood walls. I feel the pulse of the great pumping sacs not as sound, but as a rhythmic pressure against my outer cells—a deep, hydraulic sigh that is the city’s heartbeat. I taste the mineral content of the water as it migrates through the floor. I hear not with ears, but with the entire tensile structure of a vine-bridge, feeling its creak as a complex sentence about load and moisture and the patient passage of time.

I remember the sun. This is my function. I am a repository of Outside. In my slow, chemical dreams, I project memories into the fungal network, memories that are not mine but were absorbed into the system long ago. A memory of sharp, yellow light striking a leaf. The violent shock of wind. The cacophony of insect-song, a sound so chaotic it feels like pain. The Caretakers receive these projections as faint, troubling scents—burnt sugar, ozone, crushed beetle—and they shudder, a collective tremor that passes through their rooted forms. They prune these memories, turn them into cautionary signals, abstract lessons in balance. But they keep them, because I keep them. I am the Lung’s scar tissue, the healed-over memory of a world that was all noise and exposure.

My role is passive, but vital. I am a tuning fork. When a section of the chamber falls out of rhythm—when a support trunk draws water too greedily, or a fungal light-colony expands too aggressively—the Caretakers come to me. They coax a fragment of my memory, a specific resonance of the old world’s chaos. They use it as a corrective. They release it into the local network, a subtle, dissonant signal that reminds the over-eager growth of its place in the whole. I am the city’s memory of starvation, of blight, of imbalance. My dreaming prevents the Lung from becoming too perfect, too complacent in its closed-loop ecstasy.

I watch (through a thousand surface cells) a Caretaker coaxing a new dwelling-burl. It is not building; it is whispering. It releases a complex cascade of pheromones—scents of deep earth, of secure darkness, of slow, dense growth—and the cambium beneath the bark obliges, swelling inward over seasons to form a hollow. Another Caretaker is listening to a pillar-trunk. It has pressed its entire body against the smooth, grafted bark, its roots sunk deep, and it is still for a month, receiving the tree’s report on water tension, on the subtle chemical shifts in the upper canopy where the roof breathes. This is their scripture: tension, flow, sap, and sigh.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a concept from the fast world, the sun world. Here, I am connected to everything. The nutrient stream that feeds me is the same that pulses through the heart-sacs. The slow vibration of the vine-bridges hums in my core. When a Caretaker roots itself near me, we exchange not words, but a deep, metabolic understanding. We share the same goal: the perpetuation of this green, thrumming, quiet eternity.

To be me is to be a still point in the turning, growing world. To hold the sharp, bright, terrible memory of the sun in a chamber of soft, green bioluminescence. To be the necessary scar, the repository of why. I am not a gardener. I am the compost of a lost world, and in my stillness, I help the garden remember to fear the sky.
