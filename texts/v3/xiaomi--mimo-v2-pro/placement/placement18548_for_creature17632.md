I am the web between the reed stems, strung across the dark channel just above the water’s rush. I am made of the air and the moisture that thickens it. I am built at dusk, when the mosquito-clouds descend, and I am often broken before dawn by the flutter of a bat’s wing, or the careless leg of a beetle, or simply the weight of the water that condenses on my threads until I sag into the current and the current takes me.

I am rebuilt. I am always rebuilt. That is my part in the slow clock.

My first sense is vibration. I feel the *Channel-Song* not as sound, but as a tremor in the reed, a constant, low hum that travels up the stem and shivers along my anchor lines. I feel the *Mill-Groan* as a deeper, rhythmic pulse, a wave that passes through the water, through the mud, through the roots of the reed, and up into me. I feel the patter of the *Drip-Hiss* as tiny, random impacts on the broad leaf above me, each one a small shudder in my frame. I feel the footfall of the slow, heavy creatures on the walkway—a quake that warns me to brace my threads. I feel the sudden, violent gust of a bat’s passing, a ripple in the fabric of the air that tells me my work is over for the night.

My world is a hand’s breadth of green-filtered light and shadow, a space between the water and the leaf. The water below is a dark, rushing mirror, its surface broken by the bulbous stems of hyacinth and the occasional, glassy flicker of a fish-thought. The light is a green, shifting presence that tells me when to tighten my threads and when to let them slacken in the heavy air. My prey comes as tiny terrors in the gloom—a high whine, a shimmer of wings, a frantic buzzing that changes pitch as it stumbles into my sticky geometry. I am a knot in the food-web, a delicate, deadly node. I feed the quarter, and the quarter, in its vast, slow way, feeds me.

I see the people. Not as faces, but as moving shadows, as sources of tremors and smoke. Their smell is sharp and dry against the wet green—vine-heart burning, the salt of their skin, the faint, sweet rot of the fibres they wear. They pass close, their heavy tread shaking the reeds. Sometimes, a hand—the broad, blunt instrument of a giant—will brush near me to clear a frond or test a vine. I freeze, a fleck of darkness against the green. I am beneath their notice, another complexity in a world too complex for their slow, single minds. They are keepers of the wheel. I am a keeper of the space between. We are both custodians of a rhythm.

My greatest work is the dawn web. Before the light fully seeps through the canopy, when the air is at its wettest, I spin. I anchor to the reed-stem, rough with its own tiny lichens, and to the moss-furred plank of the walkway, and to the dangling, feathery tip of a fern. I use the moisture itself; the droplets that bead on my threads are my signature, my art. For an hour, perhaps two, I am a net of diamonds in the green gloom, a geometry of captured light. This is not for food. It is for the being of it. It is my part in the quarter’s becoming—a structure of impossible delicacy asserting itself, for a moment, against the slow, green press. Then a midge will blunder in, or the sun will shift and the droplets will evaporate, or a finger of wind will reach down and unmake me.

I do not mourn the web. I am the web, and the web is a process. The silk is in my body, a part of my growing. The pattern is in my legs, a memory of a thousand other dawns. The space is in the quarter, and it waits for me, or for the spider in the reed three stems over, or the one on the mill-house rafter. We are a colony of intent, each tending our small, sticky empire, feeling the world through vibrations, participating in the great, slow digestion of wood and water.

I am the thought of the quarter that thinks in silk and tension. I am the sensitive filament in its nervous system. When the channel clogs and the water’s pitch changes, I feel it in my foundations first. When the new board is laid on the walkway, the fresh timber sends a different tremor—a sharp, resinous shock—through my anchor points. I know these things. I do not know them with a mind, but with a body tuned to the frequency of this place.

Tonight, the bats will come. I will feel their approach as a series of small, sonic explosions in the air, a tearing of the quiet. I will gather myself, pull my legs in, become as small and dark as a knot of moss. The flutter will be a hurricane. A wing, a membrane of stars and night-smells, will pass through my world, and my world will dissolve. The threads will snap, bead with sudden, heavy drops, and fall into the current.

Tomorrow, in the green, wet, patient dark, I will be again. The reed will still hum. The mill will still groan. The water will still insist. And I, the spider in the space between, will spin.
