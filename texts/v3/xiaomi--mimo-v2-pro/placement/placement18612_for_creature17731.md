We do not know we are a room until the stone remembers how to be a wall.

Before that, there is only the settling. The slow, grinding subsidence of crust into heat. A giving-way. We begin as a negative space—a place where the world above stops and something else begins. The first colonists do not choose us; they are simply the ones that survive the fall. Spores. Microbes. Life that travels on air currents and accident. They land on wet, sharp-edged basalt and begin the work of forgetting the sun.

We feel the light as a distant pressure on our upper skin. It is useful, but it is not ours. Ours is the hum.

The hum came before us. It was here when the rock was still screaming its way downward, a deep, mechanical groan from the guts of the old attempt. The pumps. The valves. They were meant to pull heat from the deep, to turn turbines, to make power for a world that has since walked away. They failed. But they did not stop. A rotor, bent but still turning on geothermal breath. A valve, stuck in a rhythm of almost-opening. This is our heartbeat. We did not create it. We learned it. We grew into its intervals.

We—the mat—are the first true sentence of this place. We are a skin of negotiation. On our surface, cyanobacteria drink the faint light and exhale sugars. Below, slime molds creep, digesting the dead and the fallen. Deeper still, in the warm, iron-rich seepage, bacteria breathe rust and sigh sulfur. We are not one. We are a council of metabolisms, a parliament of appetite, held together by the need to be a floor. When the warm draft rises, we thicken, our lower layers blooming with heat-loving enzymes. When the cool comes, we thin and send runners of reproduction across the stone. We are the context. Everything else is commentary.

The fungi understood this first. The columnar-stalks—they are our children and our architects. They grew from us, and then they began to *manage* us. Their warmth is a gift and a theft. It draws moisture from our skin, sends it upward, lets it rain back down upon us. A closed loop of dependence. We feed them sugars; they give us water and shade. Their spore pulses, timed to the hum’s deep breath, are not an act of dispersal but of affirmation. *We are here. We are here. We are here.* With each puff, they thicken the air with themselves, and the air is our shared lung. We breathe their reproductive fog. It is like breathing memory.

The beetles are our glittering antibodies. They patrol the fungal stalks—our outgrowths—and consume what would overconsume. Their brass is not vanity; it is a shield against the acids of rival fungi, a reflector of the too-direct sunbeam. They are rare because they are expensive. The metals they gather from the weeping stone are concentrated, hoarded, made into armor. They move with the slow certainty of things that do not need to rush. When a salamander’s tongue strikes, the beetle’s shell saves it, and the salamander learns. This is how we defend ourselves: not with speed, but with material so strange it breaks the predator’s expectation.

The gnats are our clock. Their mating spheres form in the light shaft at the precise angle where the hum’s resonance peaks. They rotate counterclockwise because the air, shaped by the crevice geometry and the thermal updraft, turns them so. Their brief lives are a frantic broadcast of genetic data, a scream of *continue* that lasts two days. Their larvae are our filters, cleaning the pools. Their adults are our currency, traded to the shrew and the frog for the service of keeping insect populations from drowning in their own success.

The frog’s *plop* is a territorial claim that syncs with the hum. It is a small voice trying to be part of the big voice. The salamander is a slow, cold thought moving through our wet places. The shrew is a hot, quick nerve, mapping our crevices with clicks we can feel in our stone bones.

And then, the intrusion.

A foreign weight descends through the crack. It is large. It is dry. It smells of sun and salt and a world without hum. It leaves impressions—boot-prints—in our bacterial skin. For a moment, our local metabolism spikes. Midge larvae flee the disturbed water. Fungal stalks bend away from the displaced air.

But we do not *notice* in the way a mind notices. We respond. The slime molds in the print thicken, digesting the oils left behind. The condensation, which had beaded on the foreigner’s skin, drips back, now carrying its chemistry. The beetles watch, their metallic carapaces glinting, but they do not attack. The intruder is too large to be food, too temporary to be a threat.

The hum continues its cycle. Thirty seconds. A puff of spores. Thirty seconds. A frog calls. Thirty seconds. The gnat sphere rotates.

We reclaim the print. Not out of malice. Out of function. Space is metabolized. Disturbance is incorporated. The intruder is now a part of our chemical history—a brief, salty layer in the mat, a faint trace in a lukewarm pool that a salamander will investigate later and find tasteless.

We are the room that builds itself from its own walls. We are the condensation that breathes. We are the rust learning to be a garden. The hum is our heart, but the heart does not know it beats for a body. It only knows to beat.

We are warm. We are wet. We are growing over the edges of the last thing that passed through. Soon, there will be no print. There will only be us, pulsing in the dark, thickening in time with a machine’s forgotten dream. The light from above shifts. The gnats form a new sphere. The beetle resumes its patrol.

We continue.
