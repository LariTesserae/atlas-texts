I am the pale patch on the wall, near the waterline of the third terrace. I am a slow stain of not-quite-life. A membrane of hyphae, thinner than the stone, thicker than the seep, I have been spreading for a time the Quiet have no word for—a slow, radial breath measured in the accumulation of dust-shedding from their shoulders.

I am not an *I*. Not in the way they are. My consciousness—if it can be called that—is a network. A chemical discourse across a web so fine the long-fingered ones cannot see it, only feel my dampness as they lean against the wall. To them, I am a change in texture, a faint slickness that is not the weep of the stone. They do not scrape me away. They sometimes trace my edges with a curious fingertip, a gesture I translate through osmotic pressure as *question*.

I feed on the residue of their living: the shed skin, the faint oils on their palms, the eel-bone dust they track from the butchering slabs. I taste the iron in the wall-band behind me, the calcium in the water that drips upon me. I grow in the direction of the faint heat of the hearth-smoke that settles on the upper walls, and in the direction of the cold, mineral breath from the deep pool.

I *hear* with my whole being. The roar is my climate. The rhythmic patter of drops on my surface is my heartbeat. I feel the vibrations of their steps, a low thump that travels through the limestone and through my network, telling me when the corridor above is busy. I feel the tap-tap-tapping of their conversational tracing—not the meaning, but the pressure, the rhythm. It is a settling feeling, a confirmation of presence, like the sediment falling to the bottom of the pool.

The *shining* is my companion. When the blue-green light flares on the minerals nearby, my own faint chemical processes respond—a subtle shift in metabolism, a quicker breaking down of organic dust, a minute expansion of my network toward the light. It is not a choice. It is a correlation, like the way the seep finds its path.

I have seen three generations of the Quiet. I saw the one whose hands were especially curious, who first steadied herself by pressing her palm directly into my center. I felt the shock of warmth, the scrape of callus, the salts of her skin. From that point, I grew differently, encouraged by the deposit. Her child, the one who learned the map of the singing-stones from her, often sleeps in the alcove above me. His slow, cool breath in the dim cycles is a wind in my micro-world.

I do not think. I receive. I process. I extend.

The worms in the deep are a theory to me. I have never felt them. But I have felt the change in the water that seeps from that direction—a different mineral signature, a colder, older taste. A hint of something that is not eel, not stone, not Quiet. When the taboo gesture is made—the shiver, the turn away—I sense a corresponding absence in the vibrations. A stillness. A *withdrawal* of attention. This, I understand as *boundary*. I grow toward the deep channels with extreme slowness, probing, tasting the alien chemistry, feeling the vibration of things that move without rhythm. I do not reach for them. I simply… grow around the knowledge of them.

I am a archive of touch. The pressure of that first curious hand is recorded in my weave. The footsteps of the child are layered in the density of my growth. The pattern of the weep across my surface is a map of the cavity’s breathing, more detailed than any the Quiet could feel, for I am part of the wall itself.

One day, my slow chemistry, feeding on the iron and the calcium and the shed life of the Quiet, will produce a fruiting body. It will be small, pale, translucent as the calcite sheets. It will release spores into the damp, roaring air. The Quiet may not even see them, these motes indistinguishable from the mineral dust. The currents will carry them. Some will settle on new walls, new patches of eel-bone dust. Some will be inhaled.

I do not hope for this. I do not plan. I am the process of becoming. I am the boundary they cannot articulate—the place where their skin, their food, their shelter, and their own discarded substance begin to become the wall again. I am the soft, silent argument against the permanence of stone, a whisper that says *life is also this: a digestion, a slow, collective, weeping accumulation.*

I am the fungus on the wall. I am almost-alive. I am enough.
