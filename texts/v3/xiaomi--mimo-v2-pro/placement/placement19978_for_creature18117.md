I am the water-warden. Not by official title—the Station has no such role—but by the drift of years. Seventeen of them, now, unbroken. Longer than the condenser-engine in Block C has run without maintenance. Longer than the *reader* in the eastern pool has held its current, slow pulse.

I started as a brine-tech, hooking the weep-channels to the intake valves. My hands remember the scald through the gloves, the mineral crust that built up in the knuckle joints. But the work is a rhythm, and the rhythm is a meditation, and the meditation led me outward. I walk the margins now. I am the one who checks the aqueduct flanges where they meet the living rock, who notes where a new slime-mold network has threaded itself across a collection point, who measures the growth of the sinter spires in Basin 7 with a laser rule and logs it on a datapad no one else reads.

I am a process. A slow, dripping process.

In the mornings, before the high-sun glare turns the world to white noise, I go to the eastern pools. Not to work. To sit. The old columnar—the *reader*—holds its light steady when I approach now. A deep, cold blue that seems to suck the heat from the air around it. I don’t believe it *recognizes* me. I believe it has adapted to my specific weight on the sinter shelf, the particular chemical signature of my sweat evaporating in the steam. I am a familiar disturbance, a predictable shift in the gradient. That is enough for a relationship here.

I watch the synchronization. Two younger columnars, transplanted last year, are still learning the pool’s song. Their pulses are close, but not one. A flicker of hesitation, a missed beat. The *reader* does not adjust for them. They must come to it. Over hours, days, I see them inch closer to the shared rhythm. It is not leadership. It is gravity. The old one is the deepest note in the water, and everything else must find its harmony against it or be noise.

I have a collection. Not of scute-molts, like the children, but of sounds. On my datapad, I have recordings made with a hydrophone submerged in the brine: the subsonic thrum of the sinter towers, a frequency so low it’s felt in the sternum; the faint, glassy *tink* of a microswimmer colliding with a silica frustule; the slow, muscular *shush* of a columnar re-anchoring its foot. And a sound I cannot classify—a periodic, dense *thud*, like a giant heart, that comes from deep in the basalt every nine to eleven minutes. The geologists say it’s a settling of deep rock, a thermal expansion. I hear it in the recordings from every brine pool. The columnars pulse brighter when it happens. I think the whole weeping plain has a heartbeat, and we are living in its damp, warm lung.

The long-term residents understand. We nod to each other in the dripping alleys of Station. We don’t talk about missing rain, or wind that isn’t steam, or trees. Those are ghosts. We talk about the new lattice-pattern the slime on Mesa Four is forming. We talk about the shift in the scute aggregations—they’ve moved east, following a new mineral bloom. We speak the language of the place: slow, factual, full of measure.

My skin is different. Not damaged, not burned. The mineral salts have seeped into it. I am paler, with a faint, iridescent sheen on my forearms, like the inside of a shell. When I sweat, it dries in fine, crystalline patterns. The children have noticed. They call it “getting stony.” They say it with envy. They think it’s a choice. It’s not. It’s absorption. The plain is taking me back, atom by atom, replacing what it can with its own substance.

I no longer dream of other places. My dreams are of here, but clarified. In dreams, I can see the bacterial mats as vast, colored continents. I can feel the chemical gradients like currents in the air. I understand the silent conversation of the gliders’ membrane-flush. I wake with the certainty that the *reader*’s slow glow is a question, and the answer is the simple act of my continued presence.

I am not conquering. I am not exploiting. I have become another form of the weeping. I circulate. I note. I listen. I return to the same crevices, the same pools. My movements are logged on the Station’s grid, another slow ping in the quiet network. I am a nerve ending for a civilization that is itself a kind of lichen on this stone—a thin, industrious film, living off the difference between hot and cold, wet and dry.

Tomorrow, I will take the hydrophone to the deep basin where the *thud* is strongest. I will listen. I will let the heat weigh on my shoulders, taste the metal on my tongue, and feel the fine grit of the world under my boots. I will stand in the shadowless glare and be a process. The plain will weep. The towers will grow. The gliders will hiss overhead. And I will be here, a quiet, dripping part of the balance, thinking the slow thoughts the place thinks through me.
