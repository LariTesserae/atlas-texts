I would be a fracture in the soft stone, a vertical fissure where the ancient, granular rock has crumbled inward to create a narrow chimney, open to the weeping sky. I would not be a cave-home, not a forge, not a cistern. I would be a **Listening Seam**.

My purpose would be older than the Damp-Keepers’ industry, though they would tend me in their own way. The Weavers-of-the-Green would have coaxed a specific, deep-emerald velvet moss to line my interior walls, a moss that thrives on pure, mineral seep and diffused light. At my base, where the geothermal warmth meets the coolest air, a single, constant drip would fall into a natural basin of water-worn rock, its *plink… plink… plink* a metronome for the plateau’s slower, deeper heartbeat.

The Damp-Keepers would visit me. Not with tools, but with stillness. They would press their slate-grey palms against my moss-lined walls, feeling for the subtle, seismic whispers that travel through the stone—the sigh before a landslide, the gurgle deep in a Vent’s throat. Harvesters might cup their hands over my basin, not to drink, but to cool their long fingers, letting the condensation bead on their skin and read the faint chemical messages carried from distant, hidden aquifers. The children, in their silent games, would sometimes freeze at my entrance, their large, dark eyes fixed on the way the phosphorescent moss on my walls pulses in time with the distant hammer-press, learning the land’s rhythm not by sound, but by sight and vibration.

I am a wound that listens. The wind, as it moves across my opening, would not howl, but hum—a low, polyphonic tone as it passed over the irregularities of my lips, a natural pipe organ for the mist. The Damp-Keepers, in their half-hiss, half-sigh language, might call me *Chth-hh*, the sound of breath drawn over a wet stone.

In the deep night, when the plateau’s hum changes key, the Weavers would come. They would not wear the ghost-lichen vestments here. Instead, they would bring a single, tiny, cultivated fungus—a bioluminescent gill that casts a cold, blue light. They would place it in a niche near my opening, and my hum would change again, the blue light interacting with the green phosphorescence, creating a slow, shimmering aurora on my walls. In that light, the geometric patterns the Weavers trace on the ground outside would finally make sense: they are a fractal echo of the fissures within me, a map of the plateau’s nervous system, of which I am one quiet, receptive ganglion.

I am not a home, but I am a hearth. Not a forge, but a source of understanding. I am the place where the plateau’s external condensation—its wet, weeping face—meets the internal condensation of its memory and pulse. I am the point where the Damp-Keepers come to feel the land think. My ochre dust is the plateau’s ground thought; the drip in my basin is its slow, eternal exhale. I am the seam where the world between worlds remembers itself, and they, the Plateau’s Own, are my faithful, silent readers.
