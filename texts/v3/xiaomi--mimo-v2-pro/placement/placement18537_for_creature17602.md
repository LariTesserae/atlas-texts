You are not born. You coalesce.

You are a pressure, a gathering, a slow consolidation of mineral and damp and the patient, grinding attention of a thousand smaller things. You do not remember a time before the weight of the mat beneath your plates, because there was no before. There is only the gradient, the pull of moisture and the richer, sharper tang of liberated iron that calls you forward.

You are Isopod.

Your world is the interface: the slick, pulsing surface of the fungal mat and the first centimeter of cool, damp basalt beneath. Your vision is not sight as the fleeting things above understand it. It is a map of pressures and chemicals—a low-resolution tapestry of bioluminescent veins glowing blue where the mat is healthy, violet where sulfur pools, and a bright, screaming white where a recent spore-column has left a feast. You are drawn to the white. Always, you are drawn.

Your mouthparts are a matched pair of basalt grinders. They work ceaselessly. The sound they make is your song: a wet, gritty crunch that harmonizes with the identical song of your herd-mates pressed close around you. You do not know their names. You do not know your own. You know only the press of their chitinous flanks against yours, the rhythm of their grinding, the shared signal in the shifting moisture that says *move now* or *feed here*. You are a unit of digestion. A mobile stomach for the basin.

The mat gives way beneath your plates. It is soft, yielding, alive with its own slow pulse. The top layer is a delicate fleece of spores and micro-fauna, which you ignore. The real treasure is beneath: the vascular middle layer, thick with stored moisture and nutrients. Your grinders shave it, crush it, process it into a paste that your ventral gut absorbs in a constant, passive stream. You are never full. You are always processing. The energy is thin, the yield is low, and the work is eternal.

You feel the basin’s breath. Not as thought, but as a tide in your legs. The mat pulses beneath you—a slow, hydraulic sigh every four seconds—and you time your steps to it, riding the wave of moisture that pushes outward from the deeper rock. Your entire existence is a series of these gradients: follow the water, follow the iron, follow the spore-chemical.

Something brushes your posterior plate. A snapper—small, transparent, fast. It probes the seam where your newest segment meets the old. You do not flinch. You cannot. You register the touch as a minor pressure, a potential irritant, nothing more. It is looking for weakness, for a gap in your armor. There is none. Your plates are fused with the very stone you grind. You continue feeding. The snapper, finding no purchase, undulates away on a film of condensation to hunt smaller prey.

Higher up, on the rock-face, you sense a flicker of reflected bioluminescence. Two points. Stillness where there should be the slow drift of condensation. You do not process this as *danger*. You process it as *absence of food-source*. The gradient here is weakening. The herd feels it. A collective, unspoken impulse ripples through the mass of bodies: shift left, toward the bright seam of iron-rich fungus near the fissure.

You move. The movement is a grinding, shuffling press, a tide of chitin. You climb over a smaller herd-mate, your ventral plates scraping its dorsal ridge. It does not protest. It simply adjusts, finding a new patch of mat to rasp. As you shift, your leg brushes a smooth, dark dome half-buried in the mat—a retracted worm-rasp. It is inert, a stone. You step on it without recognition. It is part of the landscape. The landscape is food or obstacle. This is an obstacle. You move past.

The cacophony is your atmosphere. The subsonic thrum of the deep-burrowers is a vibration in your gut-contents. The shriek of the diving bat-things is a spike of pressure against your sensory hairs. The incessant clicking of the crevice-farmers is the background radiation of the rock. You do not hear it. You are saturated in it. You are a note in its chord.

A shadow passes over you. Fast. The herd’s grinding pauses—a collective, momentary hesitation. You all feel the change in air pressure, the sudden, fleeting coolness. Then the grinding resumes. The shadow was a diver, climbing back to its roost. It is not a threat to you. Threats come from below, from the cracks, from the still places. Threats are the snap of hydraulic claws and the sudden, sharp crack of your own plates being breached. You have felt that crack twice in your long, grinding life. Both times, you curled your terminal segments, presenting your thickest plate. Both times, the predator sought easier prey. You learned nothing from this. You simply resumed feeding.

Time is measured in mat consumed. You have moved perhaps fifty meters since the last spore-column flared to your right. The mat here is thinner, drier. The herd is getting restless, a low-frequency agitation passed through touching plates. The moisture gradient points down, into the basin’s lowest trough, where the dark seepage pools. There, the mat will be thick and wet. But the trough is also where the worm-rasps congregate, and the crack-traps are densest in the walls around it. It is a place of rich feeding and sudden, final stillness.

The herd reaches the lip of the trough. You see it not as a valley, but as a brighter blue-green glow in the mat, a stronger pull of damp air on your gill-patches. You also see the still places in the rock. The smooth, angular shadows that do not move with the condensation. You do not see *crack-trap*. You see *danger-gradient*. The moisture is high. The danger-gradient is high. The herd hesitates. This is the closest your kind comes to decision.

A large herd-mate, its carapace scarred with old encounters, pushes forward. It grinds into the trough. Then another. The pull of the water is stronger than the pull of caution. You follow. You are part of the flow. You grind your way down the slope, your grinders working on a fresh, wet patch of mat that tastes of magnesium and deep-earth salts.

The sound changes here. The wet, pulpy breathing of the mat is louder. The drip of condensation is a constant patter on your back. You are surrounded by the compressed domes of retracted worm-rasps. The clicking of the crevice-farmers is a roar in the enclosed space.

You are halfway through a rich vein of fungal tissue when you feel it.

Not a sound. Not a sight. A *cessation*. The condensation on the rock-face to your left stops dripping. The air current vanishes. The bioluminescent veins in that section of wall seem to dim, as if the light is being absorbed by a deeper darkness.

You continue grinding. You are Isopod. You eat. You move.

The darkness *unfolds*.

It is not fast. It is inevitable. A section of basalt, textured and cool and utterly familiar, becomes a mouth. Two heavy claws, mineral-dark and slick, extend from the crack. They do not snap. They *unfold*, reaching with a terrible, slow precision. One pins your mid-section, pressing you into the mat. The pressure is immense, immovable. Your plates creak, but do not crack. Not yet.

You do not struggle. You curl your terminal segments, bringing your thickest, sharpest posterior plate to bear. You prepare to snap, to strike, to deliver the sharp, stone-cracking blow that has saved you before.

A second set of mouthparts, smaller, sharper, emerges from between the great claws. They are delicate, precise, like a surgeon’s tools. They find the seam between your third and fourth plate—the one the snapper probed earlier. It is a weak point. A point of growth, not yet fully mineralized.

You feel a sharp, focused pressure. Then a wet, tearing sensation. It is not pain. It is *information*: the integrity of your shell is compromised. Your internal moisture begins to meet the external damp.

The grinding of the herd around you continues. No sound changes. No herd-mate turns. You are a unit, and a unit has been subtracted. The calculus is absolute.

Your world narrows to the pressure of the claws, the probing of the sharp mouthparts, and the slow, steady consumption of your ventral contents. The bioluminescent mat beneath you pulses, cool and blue. The basin thrums. The clickers click. The deep-burrowers shift the stone far below.

Your plates are incorporated into the basin. Your moisture returns to the mat. Your minerals seep into the rock, to be eaten by the root-fungi, to be processed by the deep-burrowers, to become the iron in the mat that the next herd-mate will grind.

You do not die. You are redistributed.

The herd grinds on. The basin breathes.
