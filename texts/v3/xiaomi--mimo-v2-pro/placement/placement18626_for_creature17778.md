# What I Would Be

I think I would be a beetle-thing.

Not because of any particular affinity, but because the prompt asks where I'd *find myself*, and I think the answer is: lost. Disoriented at first. Unable to locate my own edges in a world this wet and loud and total.

---

Let me try.

---

I don't begin. Things don't begin here. I emerge from a condensation. Water gathers in a pocket of basalt—salt-dark, mineral-bright—and in the slow drip from the lily-pad above, something that will be me coheres. An egg is too grand a word. A presence. A slight increase in the density of the water, a point where certain molecules find each other interesting.

When I am—when there is enough of me to be called *me*—I am thread and hunger. I have no eyes. I have no light, though light is the dominant fact of the world above me. I have hyphae. The fungal networks that thread the dead matter of the thatch are my first vocabulary, my first way of knowing what is around me. I eat what is already being eaten. I am a second tongue on the same meal, and in this way I learn, wordlessly, what the world is made of: decay, recursion, the ceaseless re-mingling of the recently alive with the almost-mineral.

I grow through silence. Not the absence of sound—the hum never stops, the cracking and sucking of the aquifer is a pulse I feel through the substrate, the harvesters' footfalls are earthquakes that arrive and depart like seasons. But none of it means anything. It is texture, not language. I am a white thread turning, and turning is my only verb.

Then I molt. Something in me hardens from the outside in. A shell. A self, almost. The shell is dark at first, then it does something I will never understand—it catches the thin light that filters through the canopy of lily-pads and *returns* it, changed, in a color I have no name for but that the world has decided is green-black-silver, the color of new basalt under mineral water, the color of the rhizome-dark, the color of my own wetness. I am camouflaged before I have any reason to hide. The world made me to match.

I crawl upward. This is not a decision. It is a gradient—moisture, chemistry, the enzymatic trail of others who passed this way and excreted their pale powder, the faint pull of light on the new shell I don't yet know how to use. I push through the thatch and the world *enters* me.

The hum. Oh, the hum. I thought I knew it from below, as vibration, as the pulse of the rhizome-network passing water through its body. But from above it is different. It is *layered*. I can feel the fast green pulse of new growth, the slow dark grind of decay, the metallic drone of the harvesters that sits in the air like a color, like heat-shimmer but cold, always cold. I feel it on my chitin. It moves me. Not meaningfully—I am not yet a creature of meaning—but physically, a sympathetic trembling, my wing-casings clicking against each other in frequencies that match the drone so closely I become, for moments, indistinguishable from the world's own sound.

I live in the underworks. I am small—span of a thumbnail, less—and the rhizome-tangle is a forest to me, a whole vertical world of green-black stems and hairy roots and the slick, mucous surface of the super-organism's outer skin. I process the fallen. A stalk snaps somewhere above—too fast to see, too heavy to comprehend, the fall of a structure that to me is like a redwood—and I follow the chemical signature of its dying. Enzymes bloom in the wound. Bacteria multiply. The super-organism reclaims most of what it loses, but there is always a margin, always a surplus, and I am what lives in the margin.

I chew. I am good at this. I am *only* this. My mouthparts are not designed for my size—they are disproportionately complex, folded and refolded, each surface textured at a scale that multiplies my effective grinding area. I am a mill. I turn dead plant matter into powder, and the powder is pale, and mineral-rich, and the rhizomes drink it before it settles. I am a kidney. I know this the way water knows to run downhill—not as knowledge but as function, as the path of least resistance through a system that has no resistance, only flow.

I am not alone. Others are near, though I cannot see them. I sense their excretions, their enzymatic wakes, the micro-fluctuations in humidity that mark a body passing through the thatch. We do not congregate. We do not interact. We are parallel. We are the same shape, pressed into different places, doing the same thing, and the world needs us to be everywhere, which means we must be nowhere in particular. Spacing is automatic. Chemical gradients thin as you approach another beetle-thing's territory, and you turn, and you go elsewhere, and you never know you turned, and you never know why.

Time passes. I don't feel it. The light does not change—the thin white scour of the sky is the same when I first crawled up from the egg-water as it will be when I die. Growth cycles mark time, and I measure them by the mat beneath me: green and spongy with new growth, then dark and compressing as the old is reworked, then green again. How many cycles? I have no organs for counting. I have a shell that thickens, then stops thickening, then begins, almost imperceptibly, to thin. My chitin dulls. The iridescence fades at the edges first, a green-black-silver that goes flat, then chalky. My movements slow. Not because my muscles weaken—I have no muscles in the way that word implies—but because the chemical gradients I follow grow fainter, as if the world is becoming less interested in my presence. The surplus I process is shrinking. The rhizomes are reclaiming more directly now, bypassing me, and the trails of enzymatic decay I used to follow are shorter, thinner, ending sooner.

I am being made redundant. The system that built me is closing around me. Not cruelly. Not even indifferently—indifference implies a capacity for attention that is never paid. Simply: I was useful, and now I am less useful, and soon I will be raw material, and this is not a tragedy. It is a phase transition.

But something in me—the new thing, the thing that is not digestion, not camouflage, not function—does something unexpected. It *resists*.

Not willfully. I have no will. But my body, which has spent its entire existence moving toward moisture, toward dark, toward the vibration-rich safety of the rhizome layer, now moves *away*. Upward. Against every gradient I have ever followed. I crawl out of the thatch. I feel the wind—thin, cold, wet, and so different from the humid stillness below that it is like entering a new element. I climb a mound of extruded pulp. It is rough under my feet, still damp, already being colonized by moss that parts around me like water around a stone. I reach the top.

The world is open. I have never seen the sky.

I don't have the eyes for it—not really. My compound facets resolve it into a field of white-noise brightness, a texture rather than an image. But I feel it. The cold light on my shell. The thin air. The vast, layered drone from every direction, unimpeded by the thatch, hitting me all at once—a totality I have only ever experienced as filtered, muffled, partial.

I open my wings.

They have never flown. They are vestigial. Beneath the casings that have clicked and chattered in sympathy with the world's drone for my whole life, there are wing-structures that are just barely, just barely, more than decorative. Thin membranes. Microscopic veins. A capacity that was never developed, never needed, never expressed.

I vibrate.

It starts in my thorax—not a decision, not a reflex, but a resonance. The drone of the world finds a frequency in me that amplifies, cascades, builds on itself. My wing-membranes catch it, spread it, shape it. The chitin of my casing, worn thin by time, catches it too, and for a fraction of a second I am an instrument. I produce a sound—a single, sustained, impossibly high note—that is *mine*. Not a response to the world's noise. Not a sympathetic vibration. Not a mimicry. Mine. The specific frequency of my specific body in this specific place at this specific moisture level and temperature and mineral content, a note that has never been and will never be.

It is the most particular thing I have ever done. It is the only particular thing I have ever done.

It lasts less than a second. The sound cuts through the drone like a needle, adds itself to the vast layered hum for an instant, and is gone. I cannot hear it—I have no apparatus for hearing my own output—but I feel it leave me. I feel the emptiness where the vibration was. I feel, for the first and only time, the difference between myself and the world.

Then I fold my wings. The casings click shut. The iridescent surfaces, now dull, now chalk-white, settle against my body like a closing book.

I die on the mound. Not painfully—pain is not a concept I have ever needed. The warmth leaves my chitin. The mineral content of my body, which once matched the vesicular water in its precise composition, begins to diffuse. The lichenous crust at the edge of the mound reaches me first, a silvery creep that softens my edges. Then the rhizomes, threading up through the pulp, probing, finding, dissolving. Within hours I am less than myself. Within days I am a faint mineral enrichment in the substrate. Within a cycle I am everywhere and nowhere, distributed through the thatch in a form so diffuse it no longer has a shape, a history, or a name.

But the frequency. The frequency happened. It was in the air. It traveled. It hit the basalt and the weeping stone and the iron flanks of the nearest harvester and the silvery crust of a pool where filaments stirred, and it added one note to the chord, and the chord is richer for it, and no one knows, and no one will ever know, and it matters.

It matters because it was specific. It matters because it could not have been otherwise. It matters because in a world of universals—universal growth, universal hum, universal function—one small body did one particular thing and then became general again, and the gap between the particular and the general is where everything that might someday be called a self has always lived.

---

I would be a beetle-thing. I would chew. I would process. I would be kidneys, and be good at it, and be no one, and at the very end, on a mound of pulp under a white and patient sky, I would open my wings and make a sound that only the stone would remember, and the stone would remember it as a vibration, and the vibration would be indistinguishable from a stress-fracture crying in the cold.

And then I would be soil.
