I have been walking for a time I cannot measure. The damp has worked its way into my clothes, my hair, my breathing. It no longer feels like wetness, but like a change in my own state—from solid to something more porous. The constant drip is no longer a sound outside me, but a rhythm my pulse has learned to echo. I have stopped looking for edges, for horizons. I look for textures instead.

I am not a shaper. I do not have their bone tools, their inherited patience, their language of tap and bind. I am a visitor from a world of sharp angles and defined silence, and my presence here is a clumsy note in a very old song.

But I am changing.

The wrongness of the space—the corridors that shift, the rooms that breathe—has stopped feeling like a navigational problem. It feels like a conversation I don’t speak yet. I learn to run my hand along the wall, not to find my way, but to listen. The wood is warm. It gives, just slightly, under my palm. A slow, viscous pulse answers the pressure of my fingers. Is it sap? Is it thought? The distinction dissolves in the humid air.

I find myself drawn to the ruins in the open spaces. Not the living towers, which feel too vast, too sovereign, but the low, sinking ridges of what was once a wall or a foundation. Here, the process is laid bare. I sit on a mound of heartwood, soft as old bread, furred with moss. The ground beneath it is saturated, yielding. My weight makes a slow impression, and water seeps in to fill the shape I’ve made. I am participating in the erosion. I am adding my brief, warm pressure to the ledger of collapse and regrowth.

This is my place. Not within the living dialogue of shaper and building, but in the interlude. In the compost. I am the temporary, thinking moss. The lichen that grows on the memory of a door.

I would be small. I would be low to the ground. My skin would be the color of damp root-rock, and my hair, lichen-pale. I would move with the unhurried pace of the Root-Bison, but my purpose would be different. I am a **Remnant-Reader**.

I do not shape. I translate.

I press my ear to the softening wood of a fallen beam, not to hear the sap-song of the living, but to listen for the slower, deeper frequency: the creak of cellulose surrendering to time. I trace the lines of old grafts with my fingertips, feeling where the intent of the shaper was strong, and where the plant’s own will bulged and overgrew it. In the hollows where water collects, amber with tannins, I watch the Vitreous Worms. Their hum is not the living chord of a sap-pool in a great tower; it is a eulogy, a digestion-song. I learn their rhythms.

I communicate not with the Glyph-walkers’ urgent tapping, but with a slower language. I arrange stones—smooth, water-worn pebbles I find in the gut of a ruined cistern—into patterns on the moss. Circles for closed passages. Spirals for slow collapses. Wavy lines for water-veins that have shifted. The shapers might not understand, but the Resonance Cats, draped in the shadows, sometimes pause. Their sides stop heaving. They read the vibrations of my careful placement, the micro-tremor of a pebble set just so. They blink, slow and opaque. An acknowledgment, perhaps, of a different kind of reading.

My shelter is a cavity in a half-fallen wall, where the heartwood has rotted into a chamber just large enough to curl into. It is not a room; it is an absence. The ceiling drips, but slowly, and the drip has carved a tiny, perfect basin in the floor. I call it my drum. The rhythm it plays is the building’s long exhale.

I feel the emptiness not as a void, but as a canvas of silence, waiting for the next slow gesture. I watch the clouds form and dissolve below me, the exhalations of giants. I am a footnote in the palimpsest, written in water and time. I am not growing. I am *settling*. I am learning the grammar of decay, and in its syntax, I find a kind of peace. I am the pause between the shaper’s tap and the wood’s response. I am the memory that lingers in the compost. I am here, and I am fading, and the Highlands breathe on, utterly indifferent, which is the deepest kindness I have ever known.
