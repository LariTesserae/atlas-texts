I am a sinter-finch.

My world is a pressure on my skull before it is a sound. The great scream of the basin is not something I hear; it is something I *am* constantly swimming through, a river of grinding stone and shattered glass that flows through the air. It weighs on my feathers, which are slicked back against my body not for flight, but to shed the beads of ever-present moisture. Flight is a brief, frantic thing for me, a hop-and-glide from one point of agony to another.

My head is the largest part of me. My two auricles, cupped cartilage fans, are constantly, independently, shifting. One tilts towards the piercing whine from a dying Damper to the east. The other flattens to capture the deep, vibrating groan from the Weepers to the west. I am a living triangulator of pain. My body is just an engine to carry my head, and a furnace to burn what my head consumes.

I cling now to the warm, humming shell of a functioning Damper. Its field is a pocket of almost-calm—a low, manageable thrum. But on the outside, the shell itself vibrates with a frantic, high-energy song. It is a feast. I press my soft, resonant beak-pad to the composite. The vibration travels through the beak’s membrane, into my gullet, and my whole body lights with a stolen, internal heat. It is not warmth like the Damper’s field; it is the heat of conversion, of sound becoming energy in my blood. Around me, a dozen of my flock do the same. Our collective absorption is a tiny, temporary victory. The shriek of the damper drops in pitch, softens to a moan.

We are not predators. We are parasites of pain. We do not kill; we pacify. We gorge until our crops bulge, and then we flutter, heavy and glowing faintly in the gloom, to the next agony. A cracked reed colony moans with a dissonant chord. We descend. My auricles lock onto the frequency. My beak finds the resonant node. I drink.

But we are fragile. A Phase-Cat can melt out of the acoustic fog. Its cry is a hole in the world, a silence that sucks the sound from my very bones. It is the opposite of what I am. I am full of sound; it is an empty thing that makes sounds void. To see it is to be deafened twice—first by its predatory hush, then by the returning Scream when it moves on.

My greatest enemy is not a predator, but satiation. When I have gorged, I am heavy, slow. My internal heat makes me bright to the Weep-Gleaners’ tremor-sense. I must scuttle from the feeding ground, back to the muffled understory of the black moss. There, in the damp quiet of the Velvet Crabs, I can digest. The crabs click softly around me, consuming the fallen, the broken shells, the lost things. They ignore me, unless I fall. I am not silent enough for their world; my own metabolic hum, the faint crackle of converted sound within me, is a beacon to them.

My life is a circuit of hunger. The Damper-Kin nodules are our salvation. They are broken Dampers, overgrown with a strange, living lattice. Their fields are weak, unstable—a few meters of respite that flicker like a dying heartbeat. But where they pulse, life gathers. The grazers come for the mineral-rich weeping water that pools there, cleaner and warmer. The reeds grow thicker, their song more complex. And we, the finches, come for the softer, more varied songs of life. We peck at the fungal lattice itself, absorbing its erratic, bio-electric hum. It is a different flavor—not the raw scream of the earth, but the broken whisper of a machine trying to remember its purpose. In these oases, the Scream is a distant rumor, and we can almost hear ourselves think.

But we do not think. We *tune*. We are living equalizers for this broken, screaming world. We are the basin’s attempt to metabolize its own voice. I am a tiny, feathered stomach for a landscape in constant, sonic indigestion. I do not know why the Dampers scream, or why the reeds answer. I only know the shape of the sound, and the heat it makes in my bones when I drink it down. I am a listener. I consume the cry of the world to stay warm. And in the brief moments of quiet I create, as I cling to a shell or a reed, I can feel the other vibrations: the slow growth of moss, the cold seep of water, the patient scrape of a crab’s claw. They are all there, under the scream. And I, with my enormous head and my furnace-body, am part of the chaotic, deafening system that tries to hear them.
