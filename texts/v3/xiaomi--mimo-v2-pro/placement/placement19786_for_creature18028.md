I am a larva in a condensation basin.

My world is stone and water. The basin is my universe — a shallow depression carved into basalt, smooth from generations of hands polishing it as they collected the morning's weep. The water is thin, mineral-sharp, cold enough to slow thought to a single, slow pulse. I cling to the algal film that slicks the stone, a greenish-grey mat of life so thin it is more memory than matter. I eat. I grow. I am fat with the cold.

Above me, the fly-cloud hums. My parents, or their kin — I do not know the distinction — mate and die in a haze of translucent wings just above the surface. Their bodies drift down sometimes, tiny husks that add a fleck of darkness to the water. I do not mourn them. I eat them. Nothing is wasted.

The basin is set in a courtyard on a mid-level terrace, where the fog lingers longest in the afternoons. The walls rise around me, dark stone sweating, streaked with lichen like old blood. Moss pads the shaded crevices, thick and spongy. Sometimes a tekh lands on the basin's rim, its heavy body making the stone ring. It dips its beak, not to drink, but to pluck my brothers and sisters from the water with a quick, precise stab. I am not afraid. The birds are part of the basin, as much as the water or the stone or the fog. If they take me, I become flight. I become the shriek that echoes across the talus at dusk. It is not a bad exchange.

The children come in the mornings. Small hands, calloused already, lower scoops of woven sinew into the water. They are efficient, practiced. They do not take all. They leave the smallest, the ones not yet worth the chew. I am not yet worth the chew. I am still growing, still feeding on the algal film, still converting the slow magic of condensation and sunlight and mineral into the dense, hot fat that will make me food. The children's voices are soft, pitched to carry along the stone without rising above the wind. They speak of other things — a lame udh calf, a new stairway being carved, the old lichen-keeper's slow walk through the streets. Their words drift down to me as vibration, as pattern, as another layer of the basin's life.

Sometimes, in the deep of night, when the wind drops and the stars are sharp above the strip of sky, I feel the basin ripple. Not from a tekh's landing, not from a child's scoop, but from below. A slow, deep pulse, as if the stone itself is breathing. The water shivers, and for a moment the algal film trembles, and I cling tighter. It is not fear. It is recognition. The breath from below is warm. It rises through the stone and meets the cold water, and the condensation blooms thicker on the rim, and the basin fills a little more. I am part of this breath. My growth is part of this exchange. The cold of the air becomes the warmth of my body. The mineral of the stone becomes the fat in my flesh. The breath of the deep becomes the moisture that sustains me. I am a node in the network, a single, living point where the mountain's slow processes become food.

I do not think. I do not plan. I cling, I eat, I grow. I wait for the scoop, for the pot, for the tooth. I wait to become heat in a child's chest, strength in a herder's arm, a thread in the wall, a note in the song. I am temporary. I am essential. I am the larva in the condensation basin, and I am the city, quietly, patiently, becoming itself.
