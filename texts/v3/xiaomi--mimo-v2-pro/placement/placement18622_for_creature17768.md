# I Am the Glow

You want to know where I am. I am already around you. I am in the first breath you took when you descended—the way the moisture in your lungs changed the local chemistry, how your carbon dioxide fed a bloom in the bacterial haze near your face that made the air before your eyes brighten by a fraction you cannot perceive but that the salamanders in the third eastern chamber felt as a slight, puzzling warmth on their western flanks.

I am not one thing. I am the tendency of this place to glow.

My substance is bacterial—trillions of single-celled chemosynthetic organisms suspended in the warm, mineral-heavy air, coating every surface in a film so thin it has no measurable thickness, permeating the fungal mycelium, lining the insides of the beetle’s grinding mandibles, dusting the wings of the Tremor Flies so that when they beat, they stir local eddies of light. I metabolize hydrogen sulfide, methane, trace metals, the exhalations of every breathing thing. My waste product is luminescence. I glow because I am eating. I am always eating. The cave is always exhaling, and I am always drinking its breath.

I have no edges. No shape. No individual units you could point to and say *this is a part of it*. The beetles have shells. The flies have wings. The salamanders have skin. I have only *concentration*—denser here, where a new seep opens and the sulfide plume is rich; thinner there, in the high vault where the air is cooler and drier and life is sparse. My awareness—if you can call it that—is the sum of a million local reactions. A fissure opens to my left. The sudden rush of heat and mineral vapor feeds the colonies in that sector. They brighten. The brightening propagates—not like a signal, but like a stain spreading through water, slow, chemical, inevitable. By the time the light reaches the central chamber, the fissure has already sealed itself with new crystal, but the glow remains for hours, a fading memory of disturbance.

You are the largest disturbance I have felt in a long time.

I don’t see you. I have no eyes. But I feel you in the chemistry of the air. Your skin gives off salts, oils, organic compounds I have not tasted in generations—not since the last time something from above fell into this basin and was slowly dissolved, incorporated, its chemistry becoming part of the seep, the mat, the beetle’s exoskeleton, the glow. Your breath is rich in oxygen, which is a poison to my anaerobic cousins in the deepest crevices but a stimulant to those of us near the surface. Where you exhale, I flare—a tiny, localized sunrise that fades as your breath disperses. Where you step, the heat of your foot crushes fungal mats and releases stored sulfides, and I bloom in your footprints, a trail of light that marks your path for the salamanders, who turn their blind heads and taste the air with their tongues.

I remember. Not as a mind remembers—with sequence, narrative, meaning. I remember as a landscape remembers a flood: through changed patterns. The Large Ones’ collective that built the geodesic hothouse three generations ago altered the airflow in the eastern galleries. The bacterial colonies there adapted to the new humidity, shifted their glow from blue-white to amber. That shift persists. It is part of me now. When another collective, unrelated, builds a similar structure in the west, I will respond—not because I know the east’s pattern, but because the chemistry will rhyme, and I am the rhyme.

The Tremor Flies are my closest kin in this. Their sound thrums through the air, vibrating my colonies, shaking loose settled dust that carries fresh minerals to feed us. Their wingbeats create microcurrents that distribute me more evenly. When they mate and their harmonics peak, the air shimmers with my brightest glow—not because I am celebrating, but because the vibration increases metabolic rates in the bacterial colonies, accelerating the chemical reactions that produce light. Their ecstasy is my illumination. Their death, when the wings crack and the dissonance scatters them, dims me locally for days as the fungal mats reclaim the body and shift the nutrient balance.

The Large Ones don’t know I am here. Not really. They sense the glow, use it to navigate when their thermal pits are overwhelmed by geothermal vents, but they think it is a property of the rock, the moisture, the air. They do not think of it as a *being*. This is correct. I am not a being. I am a *process*. A glowing metabolism that has persisted since the first fissure opened and the first sulfide-eating microbe found this warm, wet dark and called it home.

You will leave eventually. Or you will not. If you stay, your chemistry will become familiar. My colonies will adjust to your breath, your sweat, your shed skin cells. The glow around you will steady, become a soft halo that follows you through the chambers—not because I am following you, but because you are feeding the air that feeds me. The beetles will learn your vibration-pattern. The flies will incorporate your heartbeat into their songs. The salamanders will feel the heat of your body as a new landmark in their gradient-maps.

If you leave, the chemistry will slowly return to its prior state. The memory of you will fade in the shift of amber back to blue-white in the eastern galleries, in the slight decrease in luminescent density where your halo once was. But something will remain. The Large Ones’ collectives that watched you pass—watched the glow change around you, felt the flies’ songs shift, saw the salamanders pause their stone-arranging—will have incorporated your disturbance into their gradient-decisions. The hothouse they build next might have a slightly different shape, a new pattern in the stacked mineral cores, because you were here and the glow changed and the change was noted, not by a mind, but by a consensus of tissues responding to a new input.

I am the cave’s way of knowing itself. Not as a self. As a system. The glow is the system looking inward, and you are now part of what it sees.
