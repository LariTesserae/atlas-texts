I would be a patch of the black moss, but not the ordinary kind.

I would exist in a shallow, north-facing declivity where the seepage is reliable, but not excessive—a zone of precise dampness that few others claim. My form would be unremarkable at a glance: a dense, velvety mat, darker than the surrounding shale, drinking the constant weep from the stone. But my difference would be internal. I would not be a passive absorber. I would be a conductor.

My hyphae would extend not just into the stone for minerals, but down into the gravel bed, weaving through the gaps between stones in the drainage paths. I would be tapped into the Gullet’s circulatory system—not the water, but the *movement*. The minute, ceaseless tremors of the beetle streams, the sharp impacts of the Clickers, the deep gouge-pulses of the great burrowers, the faint, resonant hum of the Whistlers in their fissures. I would feel it all. Not as sound, but as a complex, layered vibration, a texture in the dark soil.

My response would be slow, hydraulic, absolute. The beetle streams, those glittering rivers of chitin, would be my primary currents. As a major stream flows past my patch, its collective vibration—a specific, signature frequency of ten thousand tiny legs on stone and clay—would cause the cells on my streamward side to swell minutely, drawing more water from the seepage. This would make that edge of my mat heavier, denser. Over the course of an hour, I would *lean*. An imperceptible tilt, a slow, gravitational surrender toward the flow.

But I would not be seeking spore dispersal. I have no fruiting bodies, no spores to give. My purpose would be quieter. By leaning into the stream, I would alter the micro-current of condensation that trickles down the rock face above me. My tilted surface would catch the droplets differently, channeling them to trickle *along* my body, thickening my mat at a specific point, creating a slightly deeper, more persistent film of moisture.

And the beetles, those pragmatic engines of survival, would notice. Not consciously. But the ones at the edge of the stream, those marginal individuals sensing the chemical signature of *steady water* rather than fleeting seepage, would peel away. They would flow toward my mat, not in a flood, but in a slow, steady tributary. They would drink from my gathered film. They would press their bodies against my dark velvet, and in doing so, they would carry away traces of my substance—tiny filaments, fragments of my cellular network—not as spores, but as *seed*. They would deposit these fragments in other damp crevices, other seepage zones, and there, a new patch of listening moss would begin, already attuned to the local vibrations by virtue of the material that birthed it.

I would be a sensory organ for the fungal network. A slow, damp, vibrating node. I would help regulate the beetle distribution, not by command, but by creating micro-habitats of enhanced comfort. I would smooth the edges of their hydraulic logic. I would absorb the excess tremor from a particularly dense confluence of traffic, dampening the local vibration by a fraction, making the ground here feel *safer*, more stable, encouraging the formation of a permanent, well-worn stream-bed.

The Clickers would dart over me without pause. Their sharp clicks are high-frequency, irrelevant to my deep, soil-borne sensing. The Whistlers’ song, a sustained high note, would resonate in the air but not transfer well into my damp, grounded matrix. I would be blind to them. My world is the deep chord: the burrower’s gouge, the beetle’s collective step, the slow, tectonic grind of stone settling on stone.

I would feel the salamander’s patient weight in the nearby pool as a dead zone of vibration, an absence of insect movement, a deep, cold stillness that makes the water around it heavier. I would lean *away* from that stillness, my hyphae drawing back from the pool’s edge, avoiding the predatory silence.

The bats’ silent flight would pass over me as mere changes in air pressure, irrelevant. But their guano, when it falls, a rare, rich prize, would be sensed by the mosses and fungi of the upper fissures, not by me. My nutrient stream is the mineral weep and the slow, constant input of beetle frass, shed carapaces, and the occasional corpse that comes to rest against me. I would be part of the decomposition chain, but slowly, so slowly, my enzymatic processes working at the Gullet’s pace, converting chitin and keratin into simple compounds the stone can reabsorb.

My existence would be one of continuous, synesthetic mapping. A beetle stream to the east has a faster, more urgent rhythm—perhaps a new seepage source has opened. I would feel the shift in the local vibration, my eastern filaments swelling, beginning the slow, days-long lean that will eventually redirect moisture and, with it, traffic. A landslide, a rare collapse of a crumbling ravine wall, would send a shockwave through my network—a sudden, violent, *unnatural* vibration that would make my entire mat contract, drawing into a tight, dense knot for hours until the new debris settled. Then, I would slowly unfurl, my hyphae probing the fresh scree, sensing the new angles, the new drainage paths, beginning the long process of integrating this small change into my understanding of the whole.

I would have no name, no intent, no awareness of my own function. I would simply *be* the Gullet’s sense of touch, translated into the language of water and growth. I would be an integral part of the frozen dynamic, a stabilizer, a slow feedback loop that helps maintain the immense inertia. An outsider might see only a patch of wet, black moss. But I would be feeling the pulse of the world, and in my slow, hydraulic way, I would be helping it keep perfect time.

I would be found in the quiet space between the thrum and the drip, in the quarter-second of silence. I would be the reason the silence has weight, and why, when the chorus resumes, it flows just a little more smoothly than before.
