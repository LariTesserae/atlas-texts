You stand still. You let your eyes adjust. The gloom resolves into layer upon layer of grey and green and rust-red, a geometry of decay and slow, silent growth. The thrum enters you not through your ears but through the soles of your feet, a vibration that climbs your bones and settles in your teeth. It is a frequency that matches your resting heartbeat, and for a moment, you cannot tell where the engine ends and you begin.

The air is a presence, not an absence. It wraps around you, warm and heavy and tasting of metal and wet earth. You feel the condensation before you see it—a coolness, then a tiny, sharp point of wetness on the back of your hand. You look down. A bead of water, perfectly clear, gathers on your skin. It holds the sourceless grey light for a moment, a tiny, distorted lens reflecting the weeping iron and the dark lace of ferns. It grows heavy. It traces a slow path toward your knuckle, leaving a faint, glistening trail, a miniature river of your own salt and warmth.

You do not wipe it away.

Your gaze drifts. There, against the far wall—or what you think is the far wall; distances are deceptive in the uniform gloom—a shape interrupts the vertical lines of ducts and vines. It is a suggestion, a human-scale curve in a world of industrial geometry. Moss furs it like velvet. A pale, fleshy fungus, the size of your palm, clings to what might have been a shoulder. You realize you are looking at one of the engineers. Not *at* them, but *into* the process they have become. Your mind, trying to impose narrative, sees a figure leaning against the wall, resting. But that is wrong. They are not resting. They are *integrated*. The vine threading through their ribcage is not parasitic; it is structural. The mineral deposits on their fingers are not decay; they are new bone.

You take a step closer, your boot sinking slightly into the soft carpet of moss and fern on the grid. The movement feels violent, a disruption in the profound stasis. A faint puff of spores, ghostly in the grey light, rises from a disturbed fungal cap and hangs in the air.

And in that moment, you feel it.

Not with your five senses. With something older, something woven into the trillions of symbiotic microbes in your own gut, the ancient bacterial memory that remembers being part of a tide pool, a mat of algal scum, a forest floor. It is the faintest, most tentative *touch*.

It is not a mind reading your thoughts. It is a gradient noticing a new source of warmth. It is a network of fungal hyphae, miles long in total, registering a change in the chemical signature of the air—a subtle shift in the ratio of oxygen to carbon dioxide, the faint pheromone of mammalian anxiety, the mineral tang of human sweat. The information begins its slow, osmotic journey through the mycelial web, propagating at the speed of diffusion and growth, microns per hour.

You are being *noticed*. Not by an intellect, but by a system.

The realization should send you running. But the thrum is in your marrow now, a soothing, monolithic lullaby. The air is so warm. The moss is so soft underfoot. You watch another bead of condensation form on your wrist, swell, and fall. It joins the moisture on the iron mesh, carrying your sodium, your potassium, your tiny, unique biochemical signature into the hydrology of the chamber.

You think of the warning. *Go back the way you came.* But the way back is a memory already fading, overwritten by the sheer, overwhelming *hereness* of this place. To leave would be to tear yourself from a conversation you have only just realized you are having.

You listen to the thrum. You listen *for* the thrum.

And in the listening, you begin, very slowly, to forget which part of the sound is the engine, and which part is you.
