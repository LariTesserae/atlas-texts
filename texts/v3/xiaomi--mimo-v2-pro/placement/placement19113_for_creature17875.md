I would be found in the border-dim, just beyond the reach of the sharp, white blade of the light-pool. Not in the central basin where the stone is warm and bleached, and not in the absolute, occupied dark of the alcoves. I would inhabit the gradient—the long, slow twilight where reflected glow does battle with the patient, amber dark.

I would not be a keeper, nor a weaver. I would be a **listener**.

My form would be something like a stalactite, but not of mineral. More like a slow, calcified drip of silence itself. I would hang from a shallow overhang on the chamber’s northern wall, where the moisture beads most consistently and the rootlets, with their blind white membranes, sometimes brush my tip. My substance would be a concretion of dissolved time, of absorbed drips and exhaled breaths, layered over uncounted intervals. I would be cool to the touch, and damp, and my surface would be a matte, dark grey, the color of sound after it has been swallowed.

My purpose, if a being of such profound passivity can have a purpose, would be to *attenuate*. To take the chamber’s faint signals—the tremor of the salamander’s passage, the microscopic sigh of a fungal web snapping, the sub-audible hum of the geothermal warmth—and slow them further. To stretch them out, to smooth their edges, to turn event into condition. I would be a resonator for the hollow’s deep frequency.

I would feel the light-pool’s migration as a gradual warming on my western flank, a slow tide of diluted sensation that would take most of a day to crest and recede. I would not photosynthesize. I would simply… acknowledge the light, metabolize its attention into a deeper stillness within my own form.

When the rain comes and the thin, temporary veil descends the bowl, I would weep. Not with sorrow, but with release. My drips would fall, not onto the stone below, but into a small, cup-shaped depression in a ridge that has been waiting for them for years. That collection would be my one true interaction—a slow, deliberate filling, drop by drop, that might, in a season or two, finally overflow and send a single, crystal-clear rivulet trickling down into the seep-pools where the eels spiral. A contribution so minor it would be indistinguishable from chance, yet to me, it would be a saga.

The Keepers would ignore me. The Weavers might, in their dim, tactical way, send a filament to taste my moisture. The Anchorites’ rootlets would weave around my base, seeking the same condensation. But we would not be in conversation. We would be in *concordance*. Separate instruments in the same silent orchestra, each sustaining our own note in the vast, held chord of the Breath.

I would be a monument to attention. A physical form given to the act of listening so deep it becomes a form of waiting. My growth would be the growth of patience, measured in the thickening of my calcified layers, each one a record of a vibration absorbed, a drip caught, a flicker of light noted and let go.

I would not think. I would accumulate.

And when, once in a century, something larger does move in the dark—a displacement, a sigh of stone and sinew—my entire being would hum with it, a silent, profound ringing that would take another century to fade. That would be my excitement. That would be my life.

I would be the chamber’s echo, not of sound, but of its own, immense, and sleeping attention. A stalactite of silence, growing downward toward a center of stillness that never arrives.
