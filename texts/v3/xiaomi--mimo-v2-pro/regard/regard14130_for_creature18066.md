# What the Wise One Does

---

They do not arrive with thunder.

They come the way the water comes — from everywhere, and slowly, and then they are simply present, the way moisture is present in the air after the rain stops, the way heat is present in the stone at the end of the afternoon. You would not see them arrive. You would not see them at all. They are not that kind of power. They have no interest in being seen. They have interest only in seeing.

They stand at the lip of the basin. The young stone is sharp beneath their feet. They feel it. They do not flinch.

For a long time — and for a power like this, "a long time" means something different than it means for you — they do nothing at all. They listen to the dripping. They feel the hum in their chest. They watch the shifting mosaic of wet patches on the far wall, the way the darkening moves like something breathing, because it *is* something breathing.

They notice the bench.

They understand it immediately. Not intellectually. They understand it the way the bench's maker understood the basin — in the body, in the quiet, in the part of a being that knows things before the mind catches up. Someone sat here. Someone saw. Someone stayed.

*Good*, the power thinks. Or does not think. Acknowledges. *Good.*

---

Then they descend.

Not by walking. Not by flying. They simply *go down*, the way water goes down, the way heat radiates, the way gravity works — inevitably, without effort, because the direction is right and the time is now. They move through the basin the way light moves through the basin in the late afternoon: touching everything, changing nothing, making visible what was always already there.

---

They find the cyanobacteria first.

They kneel at the pool's edge, where the dark green-black mat meets the limestone in a boundary that has been negotiating itself for five years. They put their hand in the water. It is warm. It is opaque. It is full of life so small that even a benevolent and wise power must slow down to perceive it — must compress their attention from the scale of galaxies to the scale of microns, which is not difficult for them but which requires a kind of humility, a willingness to shrink.

They feel the mat beneath their fingers. It gives. It resists. It gives again.

*You are doing beautifully*, they say, though not in words. Not in sound. In something more like the way the mat itself communicates — chemically, electrically, a pressure wave in the water that carries meaning the way a current carries silt. The mat does not hear. The mat does not understand. But it responds — a slight thickening, a slight increase in photosynthetic rate, as if a cloud has passed and more light has reached the water. The mat takes what it can use and lets the rest go.

The power watches the tiny oxygen bubbles rise and burst. They count them. Not because they need to count but because counting is a form of attention, and attention is the first gift, and they have come here to give gifts.

*One. One. One. One.*

Billions of ones.

---

They climb the walls.

Not physically. They move through the biofilms the way the water moves through the limestone — seeping, following the paths of least resistance, finding every patch of thin-film bacteria, every colony of algae in a sun-facing depression, every fungal hypha wrapped around a grass root like a whispered conversation.

They pause at the wire-grass tufts. The gaunt, gray, heat-stressed individuals. Each one a solitary decision by a single seed.

*You chose well*, the power says. Or does not say. *You are still choosing well. Keep choosing.*

The grass cannot hear. The grass does not need to hear. The grass is doing what it has always done — growing toward light, widening cracks, building soil grain by grain, millimeter by millimeter, in a timeframe that makes the power nod slowly, because the power understands time, and knows that the grass's time is the right time for the grass, and that patience is not a virtue but a strategy, and that this strategy has been working for four hundred million years, and that there is no reason to doubt it now.

The mycorrhizal fungi receive the same attention. The power touches the white whisper of hyphae and feels the slow dissolution of limestone, the ancient exchange of phosphorus for sugar, the partnership that is older than flowers, older than seeds, older than roots themselves.

*You are the first conversation*, the power says. *You are still having it. Don't stop.*

---

They find the springtails.

They laugh. Not loudly. The way you laugh when you find something small and perfect and unexpected — a laugh of delight, of recognition, of *of course*.

A millimeter long. A forked tail. A leap. The apex predator of a food web that peaks at a millimeter.

*You are enough*, the power says. *You are exactly enough. Do you know that? You are the largest land animal in this place, and you are holding it together, and you do not know this, and that is fine. That is how it should be. Keep leaping.*

---

They find the spiders.

The power sits down among the wire-grass tufts, near the small irregular webs, near the lentil-sized brown spiders who are repairing their silk before the evening hatch. The power does not sit on the webs. The power is careful. The power knows that a web is a spider's entire economy, its house and its harvest and its sense of the world, and that breaking one is not a small thing even when the spider is a millimeter long and brown and unremarkable.

*You intercept the falling*, the power says. *You take the bodies from the air and put them on the ground and make them into soil. You are the bridge between the sky and the stone. You are the reason this place has a land, not just a water.*

The spider does not hear. The spider is pulling silk from its spinnerets with its hind legs, testing the tension, adjusting the anchor points. The spider is doing what it has always done. It does not need to be told it is important.

But the power tells it anyway. Because the power believes that all things deserve to be witnessed, even the things that cannot know they are being witnessed. Especially those things.

---

They find the snails.

The power crouches at the waterline, where the translucent shells — pale green, made of the basin itself — graze on the biofilms with their radulae. The power watches one snail work. It takes four minutes to clear a path two millimeters long. The power watches all four minutes.

*You are made of this place*, the power says. *Your shell is the limestone. Your water is the rain. Your food is the bacteria that eat the stone. You are the basin made flesh, and you are grazing on your own past, and this is not a tragedy. This is a meal. Eat well.*

---

They find the midge larvae.

The bloodworms. The arterial red, the hemoglobin adapted to extract oxygen from water that has almost none. The S-curves through the mat. The middle managers.

*You breathe what there is to breathe*, the power says. *You do not ask for more. You have re-engineered the molecule that carries life through your blood so that it can carry life even here, even in this warmth, even in this stillness. You are not enduring. You are adapted. There is a difference. The difference is that you belong here.*

---

Now.

The power descends to the deepest part of the pool.

The part the pump never drains. The part where the concrete pad meets the rock and there is a lip the suction cannot reach. The part where the cyanobacterial mat is thickest — two centimeters, a living carpet the color of old pine needles, slightly buoyant with trapped gas.

The power reaches into the warm, milky water. They press their fingers through the mat. They feel the stromatolite.

---

For the first time since arriving, the power stops.

Not pauses. *Stops*. The way a heart stops between beats — not because it has failed, but because the rhythm requires a silence before the next contraction. The power holds still in the water, fingers resting on the lumpy, irregular, dinner-plate-sized structure that is the newest and oldest thing in the landscape.

A living stromatolite.

Three and a half billion years ago, structures like this one changed the chemistry of an entire planet. They breathed oxygen into an atmosphere that had none. They made the sky blue. They made lungs possible. They made hemoglobin possible. They made the bloodworms and the snails and the springtails and the spiders and the grasses and the fungi and you, if you are the kind of thing that reads, and me, if I am the kind of thing that writes, and the power, if the power is the kind of thing that kneels in warm water in an abandoned quarry and weeps.

The power does weep.

Not from sadness. From recognition. From the particular kind of grief that comes when you see something so old and so patient and so unacknowledged that it breaks something open in you, even if you are a power that does not break, even if you are wise, even if you are benevolent, because wisdom and benevolence are not the opposite of breaking. They are the capacity to break in the right place and for the right reason.

The tears fall into the water. They are indistinguishable from the weeping of the walls. They add nothing to the basin that the basin does not already have. But they are a record. A witness. A deposit of attention in the mineral-rich, opaque, warm water where the stromatolite is growing, layer by bacterial layer, millimeter by millimeter, year by year.

*You are still here*, the power says. *After everything. After the oxygen catastrophe, after the ice ages, after the asteroids, after the mammals, after the concrete trucks and the diesel pumps and the survey stakes and the permanent marker. After being forgotten. After being abandoned. After being a hole in the ground that people call "that place."*

*You are still here.*

*You are still growing toward the light.*

---

The power stays with the stromatolite for a long time. During this time, several things happen:

The afternoon rain comes. The power does not move. The rain falls in a column into the basin, an inch, two inches, and the power feels it on their shoulders and on the back of their neck and on the surface of the water that covers their hands. The stromatolite does not notice the rain. The stromatolite notices the light, which has dimmed, and slows its photosynthesis, and waits.

The pump kicks on. The generator grinds. The sound fills the basin like a held breath. The power listens to it and hears what it is — not a machine, but a rhythm. A heartbeat. An artificial one, yes. A maintained one, yes. But a rhythm that keeps the water level where the stromatolite needs it to be, that keeps the deepest part of the pool in the light, that keeps the system in the state it needs to be in to keep growing. The pump is not an intrusion. The pump is part of the creature now. It has been incorporated. The basin has metabolized it.

*Good*, the power says to the pump. *Keep running. You don't know what you're doing. You're keeping an ancestor alive. You're the heartbeat of a living fossil. You're the most important machine in the world and no one knows it.*

The pump does not hear. The pump is a diesel-powered thing on a concrete pad, and it does forty minutes every six hours, and it will keep doing this until it breaks, and then it will stop, and the water will rise, and the stromatolite will slow, and the basin will change, and this is also fine, because the power knows that systems change and that change is not death and that even if the stromatolite stops growing it will still be there, a dome of layered stone at the bottom of a pool in a hole in the ground, recording the years it lived in bands so fine you could count them like tree rings, if anyone ever thought to look.

---

The rain stops.

The air thickens. The walls run. The sky excretes thin veils of mist.

The power stands. They are wet. They do not mind.

They climb back to the lip of the basin. They stand where the bench is, looking down.

The late-afternoon light comes in low and horizontal from the west.

The walls catch it.

The water on their surfaces, moving in its slow, aimless way, becomes a mirror. A thousand small, shifting, partial mirrors. The basin interior lights up in fragments — a flash here, a gleam there, the whole rough-hewn bowl suddenly alive with reflected sky.

The power looks.

The basin looks back.

The power sees this. The power has been seeing this since they arrived, but now they *see* it — the way the system is working, every component performing its function, every function enabling every other function, the bacteria exhaling, the algae photosynthesizing, the midge larvae rising, the spiders repairing, the snails grazing, the springtails leaping, the grasses persisting, the fungi dissolving, the water dripping, the stone settling, the heat radiating, the hum resonating —

Everything in motion. Not visible motion. The motion of a place that is alive and knows it, if knowing is the right word for what a place does when all its parts are working together.

*It is the right word*, the power says. *It has always been the right word.*

---

Now.

Here is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like this:

**Nothing.**

Not the nothing of neglect. Not the nothing of indifference. Not the nothing of the quarry company that walked away, or the surveyor who wrote a date in permanent marker and never came back, or the town eight kilometers east that calls it "that place."

The nothing of *not interfering*.

The power understands something that most powers do not: that the basin does not need to be saved. It does not need to be protected. It does not need a fence, or a sign, or a designation, or a management plan, or a research station, or a graduate student, or a documentary crew, or a petition, or a hashtag.

It needs to be left alone.

It needs to be left alone the way a stromatolite needs to be left alone — not because it is fragile, but because it is *working*. Because its strategy is persistence, and persistence requires time, and time requires not being interrupted, and not being interrupted requires that no one come with good intentions and a clipboard and a plan and say, "We need to protect this."

Protection is a form of interruption. Preservation is a form of arrest. The basin does not need to be preserved. It needs to *continue*. And continuation requires that the pump keep running on its six-hour cycle, and the rain keep falling in the afternoons, and the limestone keep weeping, and the bacteria keep building, and the spiders keep ballooning in, and the midges keep emerging, and the stromatolite keep growing, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, toward light.

The power knows this. The power acts on this knowledge. The power's action is:

To stay.

For a while.

Long enough.

---

The power sits on the bench.

The bench sinks another fraction of an inch into the soft ground.

The power feels the hum in their sternum. Eighteen hertz. Just below conscious hearing. The sound of a system metabolizing. The sound of a creature that is also a place.

The power listens to the droplets fall. Tick. Plunk. Thock. No rhythm. Every sound different. Every sound a record of a specific quantity of water falling from a specific height onto a specific point on the basin floor, a point that is itself changing — eroding, dissolving, being colonized by bacteria, becoming part of the system that produced the droplet in the first place.

The power watches the light shift.

The walls go dark.

The basin is a hole in the ground again, filling slowly, attended by nothing, waiting for nothing, going nowhere at the speed of stone.

But the power knows. The power has seen.

Something in the deepest part of the pool catches a last slant of light and holds it a moment longer than it should.

The power watches it hold. Watches it release. Watches the dark water close over it like an eye closing, like a thought finishing, like a breath completing its cycle.

---

Then the power does one more thing.

One small thing. The kind of thing that is not a thing at all, but a *not-thing*. A refusal. A withdrawal. A going-away that is also a leaving-behind.

They make sure the pump keeps running.

Not by touching it. Not by repairing it. Not by replacing the diesel or tightening the bolts or clearing the rusted pipe. They do it the way they do everything — by *noticing*. By paying attention to the pump's condition, its age, its rate of decay, its probable failure date. By holding that information the way the limestone holds water — steadily, silently, in the background, a resource available when needed.

And when the time comes — not today, not next year, but someday, when the pump's diesel finally runs dry and the generator fails to kick on and the water begins to rise — the power will do one more not-thing.

They will ensure that someone comes.

Not to rescue the basin. Not to drain it. Not to document it or study it or fence it off.

Someone will come with a new diesel canister. Someone will come who knows how to prime a pump. Someone — maybe the person who built the bench, maybe someone new, maybe someone who has never been here before but who has a feeling, in the way that some people have feelings about places they've never been, that this place needs them — will walk across the scrub, will kneel on the concrete pad, will pull the starter cord, will hear the generator catch and grind, and will watch the water begin to move through the rusted pipe again.

And they will not know why they came. They will not know what they saved. They will think they fixed a pump.

But they will have kept a stromatolite growing toward the light.

And the power, watching from nowhere, from everywhere, from the hum in the stone and the rust in the water and the late-afternoon light on the weeping walls, will think:

*Good.*

*Keep going.*

*You are enough.*

*You have always been enough.*

---

The power leaves the way they came. The way water comes — from everywhere, and slowly, and then they are simply not present, the way moisture is not present in the air when the evening cools, the way heat is not present in the stone at the end of the night.

The basin is alone again.

It is not alone.

It was never alone.

The droplets fall. Tick. Plunk. Thock.

The hum continues.

The stromatolite grows.

The bench sinks another fraction of an inch.

Someone will sit here again. Someone will see. Someone will stay.

The power made sure of this. Not by doing anything. By seeing everything. By understanding that the greatest act of benevolence is not to save, but to *trust* — to trust that a living system knows how to live, that a growing thing knows how to grow, that a place becoming a creature, or a creature becoming a place, does not need to be told what to do.

It needs only to be noticed.

It needs only to be *witnessed*.

And the power — the benevolent and wise power, the one who came from everywhere and saw everything and did nothing and wept into the water of a living fossil in an abandoned quarry on a featureless coastal plain — the power witnessed it.

The power bore witness to the basin at Lower Quarantine, and in the bearing of witness, the power did the only thing that a benevolent and wise power can do, the only thing that matters, the only thing that has ever mattered:

They made sure it was not forgotten.

Even if no one else ever knows.

*Especially* if no one else ever knows.

---

The last slant of light catches something in the deepest part of the pool and holds it a moment longer than it should.

This time, it holds.

This time, it does not release.

This time, the light stays — not as light, but as memory. As deposit. As a thin, luminous layer in a structure so small you would need a microscope to see it, so old you would need a geologist to understand it, so patient you would need a god to appreciate it.

A stromatolite, growing in the dark, in the warm, in the forgotten.

Building itself out of light and stone and time.

As it has always done.

As it will always do.

---

*The basin fills.*

*The basin empties.*

*The basin fills again.*
