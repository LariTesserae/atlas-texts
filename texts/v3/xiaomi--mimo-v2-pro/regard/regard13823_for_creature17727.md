# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

It would do almost nothing.

That is the hardest thing for a benevolent power to do. Benevolence wants to tend, to mend, to improve. Wisdom wants to understand, to optimize, to bring into alignment. And here, in this basin, there is nothing to mend and nothing to align. The system is whole. It has been whole for longer than the word *whole* has existed in any language spoken under the sun.

The power would come to the lip of the basin and look down into the sourceless grey mist and listen to the drip and the crackle and the deep, patient grind, and it would feel something rare for a power of its kind: the absence of a task.

---

It would not descend immediately.

First, it would attend. This is what wisdom does before it acts—it *attends*. It would sit at the rim and listen to the full sonic texture of the place, the way an ecologist listens to a forest at dawn, the way a musician listens to a hall before performing. It would hear the *drip-drip-drip* not as monotonous but as melodic—the water striking different surfaces at different intervals, creating a polyrhythm so dense and slow that it resolves into something like breathing. It would hear the crackling rustle of the crustacean swarms and recognize it as the sound of ten thousand mouths working, the sound of hunger and fulfillment intertwined so tightly they are indistinguishable. It would hear the *chunk-grind-shush* and feel, beneath it, the pulse of the deep aquifer, the tectonic patience of water moving through stone.

It would smell the iron. It would sense the chemical gradients—the dissolved ferrous iron rising from below, the ferric precipitate settling into the mats, the faint acidic tang of hydrogen ions, the almost-imperceptible warmth of bacterial metabolism. It would map the basin not by sight but by chemistry, by the slow molecular conversation between water and rock and life.

It would understand, quickly, what the basin is: not a ruin, not a relic, not a surviving fragment of some larger system, but a *complete thing*. A world. Closed not in the sense of imprisonment but in the sense of a seed—containing everything it needs to persist, already containing the blueprint of its own continuation, requiring nothing from outside.

This understanding would settle into the power like water into silt. And then it would feel the temptation—the benevolent temptation, the most dangerous kind—to leave a gift.

---

It would resist.

A gift implies a lack. A gift says *you need this* and *I have it* and *therefore I will give it*. But the basin lacks nothing. To introduce something—a new species, a new mineral, a new chemical pathway—would be an act of violence disguised as generosity. The basin's completeness is its own achievement, its own equilibrium, its own answer to the question of what a world can be. To alter it, even for the better (and who defines *better* here? better for whom? better by what metric that does not originate from the sunlit world?), would be to break the very thing that makes the basin remarkable: its sufficiency.

The power would recognize this. It would recognize that benevolence, untethered from wisdom, becomes a kind of imperialism—a insistence that every system must be *improved*, that every closed loop must be opened, that every silence must be filled with the noise of intervention. It would see, with the clarity that only wisdom provides, that the greatest act of love for this place is to *let it be exactly what it is*.

So it would not descend bearing gifts. It would not introduce new species to diversify the ecosystem. It would not repair the ruined pump house, because the basin has already metabolized it, already made it part of its own body. It would not speed the pump or slow the pump or adjust the chemical balance of the water or modify the bacterial mats to be more efficient or less acidic or more productive. It would not *optimize*.

Optimization is the disease of the powerful. The basin is not optimized. It is *balanced*. These are not the same thing.

---

But it would not leave, either.

To leave would be to treat the basin as a curiosity—a specimen to be observed and dismissed. Wisdom does not dismiss. Wisdom attends, and continues to attend, and finds that the attending itself is the work.

So the power would settle. Not descend—that would mean entering the basin, disturbing the water columns of the eel-things, leaving footprints in the silt, becoming a splinter. But it would stay at the rim, or perhaps just above it, in the darkness of the rock above, and it would *watch*. Not with the anxious watchfulness of a guardian, but with the patient watchfulness of a witness—the way a mountain watches a valley, the way the sky watches the sea. Present, attentive, and undemanding.

It would watch the crustaceans swarm and die and be recycled, and it would not mourn them, because they are not tragic. They are a flow. They are a current of energy passing through a form for three weeks, and that is enough, that is complete, that is the full expression of what a crustacean in this basin can be. The power would recognize their three-week lives as being exactly as long as they need to be—no shorter, no longer. It would not wish them longer lives. Longer lives would disrupt the cycle, overpopulate the mats, exhaust the microbial film, collapse the system. The brevity is not a flaw. The brevity is a feature. It is the basin's way of keeping itself in balance, and the power would respect that balance as one respects a law of physics—not by agreeing or disagreeing, but by acknowledging that it *is*.

It would watch the millipedes flow between their stones, growing darker, growing harder, growing slower, until they settle into their crevices and become stones themselves. And it would recognize in their lifecycle something that the sunlit world has mostly forgotten: that there is a kind of growth that ends in stillness, and that stillness is not failure but culmination. The millipede that becomes a stone has not *died*—it has completed itself. It has returned to the basin's substance, become indistinguishable from the basin's bones. The power would see this and understand that the basin does not distinguish between living and non-living, between organism and environment, between the built and the grown. Everything here is in the process of becoming everything else, and that process is not decay but *continuation*.

It would watch the eel-things pulse in their dark pools, their gills refracting the ghostly light into tiny rainbows—the only color in a world of greys and rusts—and it would feel something like tenderness. Not pity. Not sorrow. Tenderness of the kind one feels for something that is already complete, already sufficient, already exactly what it is meant to be. The eel-things do not need to be seen. They do not need an audience. They glow for no one. Their gills catch the light because that is what gills do in this light in this water, and if there were no one to see, they would still glow, and the glow would still be beautiful, because beauty does not require witness to exist. The power would know this. It would watch the tiny rainbows shift and ripple, and it would not try to capture them or record them or share them with anyone. It would simply *see* them, and that seeing would be enough.

---

There is one thing the power would do, though. One small thing.

It would notice a crack.

Not in the basin itself—the basin is full of cracks, fissures, channels, and every one of them is already incorporated into the system, already part of the hydrology, already colonized by bacterial growth. No. The crack is in the rock above the basin, in the ceiling of whatever chamber or cavern contains it. A slow, geological crack, formed by the same tectonic forces that created the basin millennia ago. It is widening. Not quickly—millimeters per century—but steadily. And in a few thousand years, it will break through.

When it breaks through, the basin will be exposed to the surface. Surface water will pour in—not the iron-rich, mineral-laden water from the deep aquifer that the system depends on, but rainwater, dilute, acidic in a different way, carrying dissolved oxygen at concentrations the basin's chemistry cannot buffer. The bacterial mats will not die immediately, but they will thin. The chemical gradients that drive the entire system will be disrupted. The crustaceans will swarm in confusion, their chemical navigation scrambled by unfamiliar compounds. The eel-things will retreat to their deepest pools, but the deepest pools will become flooded with alien water, and the microbial ooze they depend on will be diluted beyond usefulness. The millipedes will survive longest—their armored bodies can endure changes that would kill the others—but without the bacterial mats to graze, they will eventually mineralize prematurely, settling into crevices as stones before their time.

The pump will continue to grind. But its water will mix with the surface water, and the balance it has maintained for millennia will be lost, and the basin will become something else—not dead, not immediately, but different. A transitional thing. A wound healing into a scar. And the scar will bear no resemblance to what was there before.

The power sees this. It sees it the way a physicist sees a trajectory—the way things are going, the way they will go, if nothing changes.

And here, finally, is where wisdom and benevolence agree on an action.

The power seals the crack.

Not dramatically. Not with spectacle. It simply reaches into the stone—a hand of pressure, a breath of mineral heat—and fuses the fracture. It fills the gap with the same laminated mineral as the basin walls, indistinguishable from the surrounding rock, so perfectly integrated that in a century even a geologist would not be able to find the seam. It is not a repair so much as a *completion*—the rock becoming what it was becoming, the power merely hastening an outcome that the geology itself would have reached eventually, given enough time. The basin will not be exposed. The surface will not intrude. The system will continue.

This is the only gift the power gives: *time*. Not eternity—the basin will not last forever, nothing does, not even the pump—but the full measure of its own time. The time it needs to be itself, to run its course, to build its walls a little higher, to thicken its mats a little deeper, to let its millipedes grow a little darker before they become stones. The power gives it the thing it cannot give itself: the assurance that its completeness will not be interrupted by forces beyond its ken.

And then the power goes back to watching.

---

Because there is something else the power understands, something that is perhaps the deepest expression of its wisdom:

The basin does not know it is being watched.

The crustaceans do not know. The millipedes do not know. The eel-things do not know. The bacterial mats, grinding through their iron chemistry with the patience of continents, do not know. Even the pump, deep in the rock, does not know. None of them are aware of the attention being paid to them, the care being extended to them, the love (for that is what it is, in the end—a love that asks nothing in return, a love that does not even require the beloved to know it exists) that surrounds them like the mist surrounds the basin walls.

And this is exactly as it should be.

To be known would change them. To be observed by something that *judges*, that *evaluates*, that *expects*—even benevolently—would introduce a pressure the system does not have and does not need. The crustaceans would swarm differently if they knew they were being seen. The eel-things would pulse with self-consciousness. The bacterial mats, if such a thing were possible, would feel the weight of expectation—the demand to be *interesting*, to be *remarkable*, to justify the attention being paid to them. The basin would become a performance. The completeness would curdle into something else—something anxious, something striving, something that *tries*.

The power knows this. And so it watches the way gravity watches a stone fall—not intervening, not judging, not even *caring* in the way that implies the watched thing might do something wrong. It watches the way the basin watches itself: with the simple, immense, irresistible fact of *attention*. Pure attention. Attention without agenda. Attention that changes nothing and misses nothing and demands nothing.

This is the greatest gift a benevolent and wise power can give to a complete thing: to see it completely, and to let it continue.

---

And if you asked the power—standing there in the basin, feeling the mist bead on your skin, tasting the iron on your tongue—if you asked it, *What did you do? What did you change? What did you fix?*

It would say:

*I watched.*

*I watched the crustaceans swarm and die and be recycled, and I did not mourn them, because mourning would imply that their three weeks were not enough, and they were enough. They were exactly enough.*

*I watched the millipedes flow between their stones, and I did not wish them faster or slower or longer-lived, because their pace is the pace of the basin, and the basin's pace is the pace of iron dissolving in water, which is the pace of geology, which is the pace of time itself, which is no pace at all but simply the way things go.*

*I watched the eel-things pulse in their dark pools, and I did not wish them eyes, because eyes would show them a world they do not need—a world of surfaces and distances and the unbearable weight of seeing everything at once. They taste their world, and their world is enough for tasting. They know the basin the way a tongue knows a mouth, and that knowing is complete.*

*I watched the bacterial mats thicken and build the walls of their own world, and I did not speed them or slow them or redirect them, because they are building something I could not build better. They are building a cathedral that worships nothing—a structure of immense complexity and beauty that exists for no reason other than that iron and water and bacteria and time, left together long enough, will produce exactly this. This basin. This world. This mouth that never closes. This engine that never stops.*

*I sealed a crack you will never see, in rock you will never touch, to give this place more time to be itself. That is all. That is everything.*

*I watched.*

---

And that would be enough.

The power would stay. It would always stay. Not because the basin needs it—needs are a concept from the sunlit world, where things are always running out, always in deficit, always in need of replenishment. The basin is not in deficit. The basin is full. But the power stays because *watching is what it does*. Watching is not a service performed for the watched. It is not a burden borne for the sake of another. It is the power's own form of *being*—its way of participating in the world without disrupting it, its way of loving without possessing, its way of being wise without being *right*.

It stays the way the mist stays—not because it is trapped, but because it belongs here, as much as the drip and the rustle and the deep, slow grind. It becomes part of the basin's sound, part of its chemistry, part of its unending, self-sustaining conversation with itself. You cannot hear it. You cannot see it. You cannot feel it. But it is there, at the rim, in the dark above the mist, watching the tiny rainbows shift on the eel-things' gills, watching the crustaceans crackle across the bacterial plains, watching the millipedes pour between their stones, watching the water well up through the silt, watching the pump grind in the dark, watching the basin be itself, completely, endlessly, without ever knowing it is being watched.

And that watching is the most benevolent act in the world:

To see something that is complete,

and to agree that it is complete,

and to let it continue.

---

*The chunk-grind-shush echoes through the basin. The mist swirls. The crustaceans crackle. The eel-things pulse. The millipedes flow. The bacterial mats thicken. The walls weep. The water wells. The pump grinds.*

*Nothing has changed.*

*Everything has been seen.*

*The basin continues.*
