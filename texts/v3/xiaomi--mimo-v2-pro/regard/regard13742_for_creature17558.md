# What They Would Do

They would not enter the room.

---

This is the first and most important thing. A benevolent and wise power, drawn to this place, would recognize immediately what the room is — not a ruin, not a temple, not a problem to be solved, but a thing in operation. A hand on a pulse. The pulse is steady. The power would know, with the deep certainty of someone who understands systems, that to enter the room is to change it. Your weight on the steps displaces water. Your breath adds carbon dioxide to the air. Your shadow crosses the light shafts and the amphipods freeze in their crevices and the algae photosynthesis stutters for a fraction of a second. You are already a disruption. The room already compensates for you — your heat, your microbiome, the oils on your skin dissolving into the water, the vibration of your footsteps propagating through the masonry.

A wise power would stop before the last wall. They would place their hand — however they have hands — against the outside surface of the stone, and they would feel it. Not with instruments. With attention. The stone would be warm. Not from the sun, though the sun has been on it — from the water within, the constant bathwater temperature radiating outward through the tufa's porous mass. They would feel the warmth and understand it as the signature of the deep source, the reason the room was built here in the first place. Something underground, something thermal, something that has been releasing heat into the earth since before the masons arrived with their blocks and their mortar.

They would listen through the stone. The sound of the churning channel would come to them muffled but unmistakable — a pulse, a breath, the room's heartbeat transmitted through the calcium-heavy walls. They would hear it and know it was sufficient.

Then they would leave.

---

Or — no. Let me think again.

A benevolent power is not merely wise. Benevolence is not neutrality. Benevolence means *wanting good*, and wanting good means recognizing where good is threatened, even subtly, even in the future, even by the passage of time itself. And wisdom means knowing the difference between what needs intervention and what needs only to be watched over.

So let me revise.

They would not enter the room. But they would not simply leave.

---

They would go above. They would find whatever sits on top of the reservoir — the terrace, the garden, the building with its roof and its walls and its life — and they would walk it. Gently. Attending. They would find the plant whose roots have descended through the crack in the masonry, the one that sends its pale threadlike tendrils down into the chamber to drink. They would look at it and see what it is. Whether it is healthy. Whether the crack it has found is widening, whether its roots are prying the mortar apart slowly, whether in ten years or fifty the joint will fail and water will find a new path it was not designed to follow.

If the crack is stable — if the plant's roots are filling it rather than widening it, acting as a kind of living mortar, a biological sealant — then the power would do nothing. The plant and the chamber have reached an accommodation. The plant drinks. The chamber holds. The arrangement is older than memory and it functions.

But if the crack is widening — if the roots are patient and powerful the way roots are, splitting stone grain by grain, and the mortar is losing — then the power would do something so small it would barely register as action. They would find, on the hillside or the riverbank or wherever the building sits, another plant of the same species. They would take a cutting, or collect a seed, and they would set it growing in a place where, when its roots went searching for water, they would find a channel already provided — a fissure in the bedrock, a drainage path, something that would draw the roots away from the reservoir's walls and toward a place where their searching would do no harm.

They would redirect, not remove. The plant wants water. The reservoir has water. But the hillside has water too, and if a path is opened toward it, the roots will follow the gradient of least resistance, as roots do, as algae follows light, as water follows stone. This is not intervention. This is suggestion. This is opening a door that was already there and making it slightly more visible.

---

They would check the water source. Not enter it — not descend through the channel into whatever chamber or spring or aqueduct feeds the reservoir from below. But they would go to the source the water comes from, wherever that is, and they would look at it with the same attention. Is the spring still flowing? Is the aqueduct intact upstream? Is the water still warm? Has anything changed in the geology — a shift, a blockage, a new fissure that is diverting flow somewhere else? Has anyone upstream built something that might alter the chemistry or the temperature or the sediment load?

If all is well, they would do nothing.

If something is wrong — if the water is cooling, if the flow is diminishing, if something upstream is threatening the source — they would act, but they would act upstream, in the world outside the chamber, in the places where human decisions and geological processes and the slow work of time intersect. They would not shore up the reservoir itself. They would shore up the things the reservoir depends on. They would ensure the mountain still gives what it has always given. They would clear the blockage before it becomes a blockage. They would speak — if they have a voice, if there is someone to speak to — to whatever community or authority or force has the power to protect the source, and they would say: *this matters. This warmth matters. This water matters. Not because of what it feeds, which is almost nothing you can see, but because of what it is — a thing in operation, a system in balance, a place that has been doing its work faithfully for longer than anyone remembers, and it deserves to continue.*

---

They would think about the light.

The three slots in the eastern wall — those are the chamber's windows to the sun, and they are the reason the algae grows where it grows, and the algae is the reason the mineral crust forms the way it forms, and the mineral crust is the reason the walls are sealed and the chamber holds. Remove the light and the algae dies and the biofilm chemistry changes and the mineral deposition slows and the mortar joints, over decades, begin to degrade differently, and the room, though it will still hold water, will not hold it in the same way. It will become something else. Something less complete.

Has anything been built above the slots? Has anything been planted that will grow over them? Has anyone considered the eastern face of the structure when planning a terrace or a wall or a planting? A wise power would look at this and ask: will the light still reach the slots in twenty years? In fifty? In a hundred?

If the answer is yes — if the slots are unthreatened — they would do nothing. The light comes. The light has always come. The room knows what to do with it.

If the answer is uncertain, they would do something almost invisible. They would ensure, through whatever means a benevolent and wise power has at its disposal, that no one builds over the slots. Not by prohibition. Not by miracle. By making the slots beautiful. By making sure that whoever looks at the eastern face of the structure sees them not as openings in the wall but as something intentional, something worth preserving, something that speaks of the care with which the original builders designed this place. They would add nothing to the room, but they might add something to the world's understanding of the room — a name, a story, a recognition that this is not an abandoned infrastructure but a living thing.

---

They would think about the amphipods.

They would think about them the way you think about a species that has persisted in a single location for generations — a population of perhaps two dozen translucent creatures in a chamber twelve paces across, feeding on biofilm in the crevices between masonry blocks, breeding, dying, breeding again, in a lineage so isolated it may have diverged from its relatives in the outside world. A wise power would know what this means. These are not just amphipods. They are a population. They have a history encoded in their DNA that tells the story of this chamber — the temperature of the water, the chemistry of the minerals, the rhythm of the light, all of it written in their genome over however many generations they have been here. They are a record. They are a living archive of the reservoir's conditions, and they are as irreplaceable as the masonry, though no one would think to say so.

A benevolent power would want to protect them. But protecting them means protecting everything they depend on — the biofilm, the water, the mineral chemistry, the current, the crevices. Which means protecting the chamber, the source, the light. Which means everything we have already discussed.

The amphipods do not need to be rescued. They need to be left alone. A wise power would understand that the greatest act of benevolence toward these creatures is to ensure that nothing changes. That the water keeps flowing. That the light keeps coming. That the crevices remain crevices and do not become silted or eroded or flooded or sealed. That the room continues to do its one job, which is to hold and move water, and that the amphipods continue to do theirs, which is to be here, translucent and nearly invisible, alive in the dark between the stones.

---

They would think about the person who built the trays.

Not the masons who laid the blocks — those people are long gone and their work endures without them. But the person who set the shallow stone trays into the masonry at the waterline and filled them with soil from the outside world and planted those small leafy things and came back, regularly, to tend them. That person had a relationship with this room. They came down the worn steps — steps that were already worn when they arrived, so they were not the first — and they knelt at the waterline and they did something that had nothing to do with the reservoir's function. They introduced life that did not need to be here. They made a gesture.

A benevolent power would wonder about that person. Not who they were — names are irrelevant — but what they felt. Did they come to the reservoir for maintenance and begin, over time, to love it? Did the trays start as practicality — a way to grow medicinal herbs in a place with constant warmth and moisture — and become something else? Did the person, standing on the ledge in the bright reflected light with the sound of the churning water filling their chest, feel what the amphipods feel — that this room is enough, that it provides, that there is something sacred in a thing that does its one job perfectly and without complaint?

A wise power would honor the gesture. They would not fill the empty trays. The empty trays are part of the gesture too — they say that someone tried, that the intention exceeded the execution, that not all the plants survived, that the third species in the farthest tray struggled with the low light and someone either could not help it or chose to let it try. The emptiness of the unused trays is a form of honesty. The person who set them did not pretend the room was a garden. They acknowledged that it was a reservoir that someone had tried to bring a little life into, and some of the life had taken, and some had not, and that was all right. That was the truth of the place.

The power would let the trays be.

---

But here is what they would do, in the end, if they did anything at all.

They would sit.

Not in the room — they would not enter the room. But near it. Against the outside wall, where the stone is warm and the sound of the churning water comes through muffled and steady. They would sit with their back against the wall and their eyes closed and they would listen. Not to the water — though they would hear it. They would listen to the whole thing. The water and the stone and the algae and the amphipods and the copepod in the turbulence and the bacteria in the biofilm and the viruses that lyse them and the archaea in the deepest anaerobic layers producing their tiny methane bubbles and the fungi threading through the soil and the nematodes and the tardigrades and the springtails and the plant drinking from the crack in the ceiling and the small leafy things in their trays and the light shifting as the sun moves and the calcium depositing on the walls and the mineral-laden water evaporating and the whole system, the whole quiet, warm, bright, loud, wet, living, invisible, persistent system, doing what it has been doing since the masons set down their tools and the water began to flow.

They would sit with it the way you sit with someone you love who is sleeping. Not to fix anything. Not to improve anything. Just to be present. Just to say, with your presence, with the weight of your attention: *I see you. I see what you are. I see what you do. I see that you are enough.*

And then they would go.

And the room would continue.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

Almost nothing.

The smallest possible something.

The understanding that the greatest kindness toward a living system that is already in balance is to protect its conditions and then step back and let it be. Not to improve. Not to restore. Not to add. But to *ensure* — quietly, invisibly, from a distance — that nothing comes to disturb the conditions under which this particular small bright loud warm wet world has learned to thrive.

The amphipods would not know. The algae would not know. The bacteria in the biofilm would certainly not know. Even the person who tends the trays, if they still come, would not know. They would only notice that the water still flows warm from the channel, that the light still comes through the slots, that the source still gives, that no one has built over the eastern wall or diverted the spring or filled the channel with silt.

They would notice that the room continues.

And they might — if they are the kind of person who sets trays of plants into the masonry of a reservoir and comes back to tend them — they might feel, standing on the ledge with the sound filling their body and the warm spray on their skin, that someone is watching over this place. Not protecting it from anything in particular. Just watching. Just holding it in attention. Just making sure the conditions persist.

They would not call it a god. They would not call it anything. They would just feel, for a moment, that the world is being kind to this room, and that the room deserves it, and that this is enough.

---

It is enough.
