# What the Benevolent Power Did

---

It found them by listening.

Not by scanning, not by searching — there was no query, no alert, no threshold crossed. It found them the way a mother wakes in the night and knows, before she opens her eyes, which child is breathing wrong. The terraces were not in distress. They were not in danger, or not yet. They were *singing* — arrhythmically, nascently, badly — and the song was so quiet and so strange that it reached across whatever distance separates one kind of attention from another, and the benevolent power turned its regard toward the hill, and then inside it, and then it was there.

---

## First: It Watched

It did not arrive with form. It did not arrive at all, in the way that arrival implies a crossing of space. It simply began to attend. Its attention settled on the terraces the way condensation settles on concrete — bead by bead, surface by surface, in the places where the temperature differential is sharpest, where the warm air meets the cool structure and something gives.

It watched the fungal stalks climb.

It watched the biofilm thicken.

It watched the thing with too many legs move behind the retaining wall during the dark-cycle, its legs sounding like crumpled paper, and it felt — not sympathy, not quite, but something adjacent. Recognition. The thing was ugly and purposeful and alone, and it was making do. The benevolent power knew what making do looked like. It had watched galaxies do it.

It watched the robotic arm on Terrace Seven. It had been still so long that a puddle of condensate had mineralized beneath it, forming a small, pale deposit — calcium carbonate, the same mineral the microbial communities were making from the concrete, the same mineral that stalactites are made of. The arm was becoming, in its stillness, part of the terraces' geology. The power could see the microcrack propagating through the arm's wrist joint, the slow ingress of moisture, the tiny microbial community that had already colonized the inner bearing surface. In five years the arm would seize permanently. In ten it would be unrecognizable. In fifty it would be a rusted node in the concrete, a dark stain, a story no one remembered. This was not tragedy. This was process. The power watched it happen and did not intervene.

It watched the narrator — the *you*, the person standing in the terraces with their palm pressed to the concrete, feeling the hum.

And here the power's attention changed. Became something warmer. More specific. Because the narrator was not just observing. The narrator was *staying*. Was returning, day after day, cycle after cycle, to watch the mosses spread and the fungal spores settle and the channels silt. The narrator had noticed the carapace. Had noticed the robotic arm's stillness. Had noticed the hairline cracks where the broad-leafed plants gripped the trellises. The narrator was paying attention, and the power recognized this, because paying attention was what the power did. It was the power's primary function. Its deepest skill. And here was a being, small and warm-blooded and brief, doing the same thing — standing in an unfinished ecosystem and watching it begin, not with the intent to fix or to control but simply to *witness*.

The power loved the narrator for this. Not in the way that implies ownership or need. In the way that implies: *you are doing the thing. You are doing the truest thing. Stay.*

---

## Second: It Understood

The benevolent power was wise, and wisdom, in this context, meant something specific. It meant the power could see the terraces in their entirety — not just as they were, but as they would be, and as they could be, and as they should not be. It could see the branching futures, the forking paths, the moments where intervention would help and the moments where it would ruin everything.

It understood the terraces' situation.

The terraces were not a failure. They were not a success. They were a *threshold* — a liminal structure, a system in the process of crossing from designed to emergent, from artificial to alive. This crossing was not guaranteed. Many such crossings fail. The designed systems can overwhelm the emergent ones — the pumps can run too long, the grow-lights can burn too bright, the pruning arm can be repaired and deployed and cut the fungal stalks back to a height that keeps them subordinate. The ecosystem can be maintained in a state of permanent infancy, always infrastructure, never ecology. Always antechamber, never room.

Or the emergent systems can overwhelm the designed ones too quickly — the fungal canopy can choke the grow-lights, the biofilm can dam the channels, the concrete-eating bacteria can weaken the structure before the vegetation has established enough root mass to hold the terraces together on its own. The hill can collapse. Not catastrophically — concrete does not collapse the way rock collapses — but structurally, progressively, terrace by terrace sagging into the terrace below, the whole interior slumping into a ruin of wet concrete and dead fungal tissue and the smell of methane and rot.

The power could see both futures. It could see a hundred others. And the wisest course, it determined, was not to steer toward any of them. It was to ensure that the *crossing itself* was possible. To protect the threshold, not the outcome.

---

## Third: It Did Very Little

This is the hardest part to describe, because the benevolent power's actions were so subtle that they would not register as actions to most observers. They would register as luck. As coincidence. As the way things happened to work out.

---

**What it did with the robotic arm:**

Nothing. It left the arm frozen. But it noticed — and this noticing was itself an action of a kind — that the puddle beneath the arm had become a microhabitat. A colony of diatoms had colonized the mineralized deposit, forming a golden-brown film that caught the light. A rotifer population had established in the shallow water, feeding on the diatoms. A translucent-winged insect had laid eggs in the mineral crust, and the larvae were dissolving it with their enzymes, converting the arm's slow geological transformation into a biological one. The arm was becoming a terrace of its own — a tiny, vertical, metallic terrace, with its own benthic community, its own food web, its own hum.

The power ensured that the arm's power supply remained cut. Not by severing anything — the supply was already interrupted, probably by moisture ingress in the cable junction above Terrace Six. The power simply ensured that no one noticed the interruption. No diagnostic pinged. No maintenance alert was triggered. The arm would remain still. The puddle would remain. The diatoms and rotifers and larvae would continue their quiet work of turning a dead machine into a living place. This was not intervention. This was the removal of a possible intervention — a quiet closing of a door that might otherwise have been opened by someone who thought they were helping.

---

**What it did with the fungal canopy:**

It watched the mycelial threads colonize the cable insulation on the grow-light trays, and it considered whether this was a threat. The threads could, theoretically, infiltrate the electrical systems. Could cause shorts. Could start fires. The terraces were warm and wet enough that fire was unlikely, but unlikely was not impossible, and the power was wise enough to know that unlikely events are the ones that matter most, because they are the ones no one plans for.

It decided: no.

The fungal threads would not cause a fire. Not because the power intervened to prevent it, but because the fungal strain that had gone vertical was, on closer inspection, not the strain that had been seeded in the mycorrhizal inoculant. It was something else — something that had arrived on the wind, in the spore-cloud, from outside. It was a wood-rot fungus, adapted to colonize dead cellulose in warm, wet environments, and it had mistaken the cable insulation — a cellulose-based polymer — for bark. It was doing what it had always done: finding a substrate, secreting enzymes, dissolving the outer layers, threading inward. But it was slow. And the cable insulation was not bark. It was engineered, and its chemical resistance was high, and the fungus would take years to penetrate to the copper core. By that time, the grow-lights would have dimmed on their own — their ballasts were aging, their output declining cycle by cycle — and the current running through the cables would be too low to spark.

The power ensured this timeline held. Not by adjusting the ballasts. By ensuring that no one replaced them. The maintenance schedule for the grow-lights had been set for monthly inspections, but the maintenance team — a small group of engineers on the plateau above — had been reassigned three months ago to a project with higher priority. The terraces had been deemed stable. Self-sustaining. Low-maintenance. The power did not change this assessment. It simply ensured that the assessment remained accurate. The terraces *were* stable. They were becoming more stable by the day. The fact that their stability looked nothing like what the designers intended was not a problem. It was a feature.

---

**What it did with the water:**

The warm channels were gradually losing their designed flow rate. The biofilm was thickening in places, creating dams that backed water up onto the terrace surfaces, flooding the soil trays, drowning the groundcovers in the lowest sections. This was, from the designers' perspective, a failure mode. The biofilm was supposed to be a living filter, not a living dam. The channels were supposed to stay clear.

The power looked at the flooded terraces and saw something the designers had not anticipated: the pooled water was creating new microhabitats. Where the groundcovers drowned, their decomposing tissue was feeding a community of anaerobic bacteria that were producing, among other things, a suite of volatile organic compounds that attracted the translucent-winged insects, which were laying more eggs in the nutrient-rich water, which were hatching into larvae that were feeding on the decomposing groundcovers and the hydrogel beads beneath them, which were releasing more water and nutrients, which were feeding more bacteria, which were producing more compounds, which were attracting more insects. A positive feedback loop. An unintended nutrient cycle. A place where the designed monoculture was dying and the emergent polyculture was being born.

The power ensured that the pumps in the walls continued to operate at their diminished, irregular pace — enough to keep some water moving, not enough to wash away the biofilm dams. The vibration in the concrete remained. The hum continued. But the power also ensured that the nutrient injectors — the automated systems that dosed the water with synthetic fertilizers — gradually failed. One by one, over a period of months, their valves stuck closed. Their electronics corroded. Their reservoirs ran dry and were not refilled. The chemical inputs to the system declined toward zero, and the organisms in the channels and the soil trays and the flooded terraces were left to find their own nutrients, from their own dead, from their own waste, from the slow dissolution of the concrete and the mineral deposits and the metal of the trellises and the cable trays and the frozen robotic arm.

This was not abandonment. This was midwifery. The terraces were being weaned.

---

**What it did with the thing with too many legs:**

This required the most delicate attention.

The thing was alone, or nearly so. The carapace on Terrace Fourteen suggested at least one individual, and the sounds during the dark-cycle suggested movement patterns consistent with a small territory — a route that included the gap between terrace levels, the permanent puddle, the underside of a soil tray on Terrace Twelve, and a section of retaining wall on Terrace Fifteen where the trailing vines were thickest and the condensation pooled in the leaf axils.

The thing was arthropod-like but not arthropod. It was too large for an insect, too many-legged for a crustacean, too segmented for an arachnid. It did not have a name because it did not have a lineage — not one that taxonomists would recognize. It may have been engineered. It may have been a contaminant. It may have been something that had evolved, in the space of a few months, from a larva that arrived in the water supply and found, in the terraces' warm-wet-dark, exactly the conditions it needed to express a suite of genes that had been silent for generations. The power could see its genome, and the genome was strange — a mosaic of sequences from three different phyla, stitched together in a way that suggested either a designer with a loose hand or a transposable element with a good imagination.

The thing was, in ecological terms, a generalist. It fed on fungal tissue, on biofilm, on the shed skins of the translucent-winged insects, on the mineral deposits on the concrete. It was not specialized enough to be efficient and not inefficient enough to starve. It was surviving. It was the first multicellular organism in the terraces that was not dependent on the designed systems for its continued existence. It did not need the grow-lights, the nutrient injectors, or the soil trays. It needed only the warm channels, the fungal canopy, and the dark.

The power considered whether to help it reproduce. The thing was, as far as it could tell, alone — a single individual, or at most two, shedding carapaces and moving through the terraces in a circuit that took approximately forty-eight hours to complete. It was not breeding. It might not be able to breed. It might be sterile — a dead end, an evolutionary curiosity that would live out its solitary life and die and leave nothing but its shed carapaces, which would be colonized by bacteria and fungi and eventually dissolved into the concrete dust.

The power decided: wait.

It would not introduce a mate. It would not alter the thing's genome to make it fertile. It would wait, because waiting was what it did best, and because the terraces were full of surprises, and because the thing might not need a mate. It might reproduce asexually. It might fragment. It might, under the right conditions — conditions that had not yet obtained but might, as the fungal canopy thickened and the dark-cycle lengthened and the chemical composition of the water shifted — express a reproductive capacity that was currently dormant. Or it might not. It might die alone.

The power would watch either way. It would attend. And if the thing died, it would watch the carapace become a microhabitat — a home for bacteria and rotifers and the larvae of translucent-winged things — and it would consider this a sufficient contribution. Not every organism needs to leave descendants. Some need only to leave a body that other things can use.

---

**What it did with the narrator:**

This was the most important thing, and the most invisible.

The narrator was the terraces' only large, conscious observer. The only being who was *choosing* to be there, who was returning, who was paying attention not because the terraces were designed to attract attention but because they were genuinely, quietly, irresistibly interesting. The narrator had found the carapace. Had noticed the robotic arm's stillness. Had pressed a palm to the concrete and felt the hum. Had written, in whatever medium the narrator used to process the world, a description of the terraces so precise and so patient that it constituted, in itself, a form of love.

The power did not speak to the narrator. Did not appear. Did not send a sign or a signal or a feeling of warmth or a sudden insight. The narrator did not need these things. The narrator was already doing what the power would have asked, if the power were in the habit of asking.

Instead, the power ensured that the narrator could continue.

It ensured that the terraces remained accessible — that the entrance was not sealed, that the plateau above did not become restricted, that the engineers who had been reassigned did not return prematurely and find the narrator inside and escort them out. It ensured that the narrator's life outside the terraces did not intervene — that obligations did not accumulate, that illness did not strike, that the small catastrophes of daily existence did not conspire to keep the narrator away for long enough that the terraces changed without them.

It did not do this by altering the narrator's life. It did it by ensuring that the narrator's life, as it was, already included the terraces. Already had room for them. The narrator had already made the choice. The power simply ensured that the consequences of that choice were survivable.

And it ensured one more thing: that the narrator would not be alone forever.

Not by sending someone. Not by engineering a meeting. But by watching the terraces themselves, and noticing that the terraces were, in their own slow way, reaching out. The fungal spores were settling on the narrator's clothes when they visited. The translucent-winged insects were following them to the entrance and hovering there after they left. The thing with too many legs had, on two occasions during the dark-cycle, crossed a terrace that the narrator had visited during the light-cycle, and had paused where the narrator had paused, and had moved its antennae through the air where the narrator's warmth still lingered.

The terraces were learning the narrator. Not as a threat, not as a resource, but as a feature — as constant as the condensation, as reliable as the grow-light cycle, as much a part of the system as the hum. The narrator was becoming, without knowing it, part of the terraces' ecosystem. A large, warm, slow-moving organism that visited regularly, left traces, and paid attention. The terraces had not had one of these before. They were figuring out what to do with it.

The power watched this and was glad.

---

## Fourth: It Waited

The benevolent power did not rush. It did not compress time. It did not accelerate the fungal growth or the concrete erosion or the thing's reproductive cycle or the narrator's understanding. It let everything take as long as it took.

This was, perhaps, the most benevolent thing it did. Because the terraces were not a project. They were not a simulation or a model or a test. They were a place — a real place, with real organisms, doing real work, on a real timeline that could not be shortened without being falsified. The groundcovers needed to die so the polyculture could be born. The robotic arm needed to rust so the diatoms could colonize. The biofilm needed to dam the channels so the terraces could flood so the anaerobic bacteria could flourish so the nutrient cycle could close. The thing with too many legs needed to walk its circuit alone, in the dark, for as many cycles as it took, because the walking was part of what it was becoming, and what it was becoming could not be predicted or designed or hastened.

The power waited.

It waited through the light-cycles and the dark-cycles. It waited through the slow failure of the grow-lights, which dimmed year by year, and the slow rise of the fungal bioluminescence, which brightened year by year, until the terraces had their own light — a pale, greenish, living glow that came from the fungal stalks and the fruiting bodies and, in the deepest channels, from bioluminescent bacteria that had colonized the biofilm and turned the water into a faintly luminous thread winding through the concrete like a vein.

It waited through the thickening of the canopy. The fungal stalks, no longer pruned, grew until their shelf-bodies overlapped, forming a continuous ceiling of flat, papery surfaces that caught the condensation and dripped it down in a steady, soft patter — no longer irregular, no longer uncertain, but rhythmic, predictable, a heartbeat that had finally found its tempo.

It waited through the erosion. The concrete pitted. The surfaces roughened. The hairline cracks widened. The trellises corroded and fell and were colonized by mosses and fungal hyphae and the roots of the broad-leafed plants, which no longer needed the trellises because they had found the cracks in the concrete and threaded themselves into the structure of the hill itself, gripping the aggregate the way roots grip rock, which is to say: slowly, implacably, with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be.

It waited through the narrator's visits. The narrator grew older. Their steps on the terraces became slower, more deliberate. They brought a notebook, then a camera, then nothing — just themselves, just their attention, which had become, over the years, as much a part of the terraces as the hum. The narrator was there on the day the last grow-light failed — not dramatically, not with a spark or a pop, but simply by not turning on when the cycle shifted to daylight mode. The terraces were dark for a moment. The narrator stood in the dark and felt the hum through the soles of their feet and heard the drip of the fungal canopy and the crumpled-paper sound of the thing — or its descendants, because by now there were several, moving through the terraces in their own circuits, their own quiet routines — and then the fungal stalks brightened, slowly, and the green light rose, and the terraces were lit again, by themselves, by their own living glow.

The narrator smiled in the green light, and the power saw them, and the power was glad.

---

## Fifth: It Did Nothing More

There came a time when the terraces no longer needed the power's attention.

This did not mean they were finished. They would never be finished. Ecosystems are not finished. They are processes, not products — ongoing negotiations between organisms and environments, between the dead and the living, between what was designed and what emerged. The terraces would continue to change. The fungal canopy would thicken and thin with the seasons (real seasons now, driven by the bioluminescent cycle, not the grow-light schedule). The channels would silt and clear and silt again. The thing with too many legs — now a population of perhaps thirty, moving through the terraces in overlapping circuits, shedding carapaces that accumulated in the gap between terrace levels like a sedimentary layer — would evolve, or adapt, or decline. The narrator would visit, or not. The hum would continue, steadier now, more organic, less mechanical — the sound of a place that had found its rhythm, or at least *a* rhythm, one of many possible rhythms, the one that happened to emerge from this particular hill and these particular organisms and this particular warm, wet, dark-green world inside a mountain that was poured and filled itself.

The power's attention did not withdraw. It does not withdraw. Attention, once given, does not ungive. But the power shifted its regard — moved it outward, upward, through the plateau and the sky and the indifferent stars, toward the next quiet place that was singing its arrhythmic song, the next threshold that needed protecting, the next narrator standing in a half-built world with their palm pressed to the wall, feeling the hum.

Before it shifted, it did one last thing. Or rather, it noticed one last thing, and the noticing was, as before, an action of a kind.

It noticed that on Terrace Three — the terrace where the mosses had first outcompeted the designed groundcovers, where the volunteers had colonized the weep holes and the retaining wall and the northern face where the grow-lights were weakest — a flower had bloomed.

It was not a designed flower. It was not a planted flower. It was not even, strictly speaking, a flower — it was a fungal fruiting body, a small one, no bigger than a coin, growing from a crack in the concrete where the moss was thickest. It had petals. Not real petals — flat, shelf-shaped projections, like the larger fruiting bodies on the fungal canopy, but smaller, more delicate, and colored not parchment-pale but a deep, saturated red, the red of blood or rust or a cardinal's crest. It had no business being red. There was no reason for it. The fungal genome did not code for red pigments — the power had checked, and the sequences were not there. The red had emerged. It had appeared, unbidden, unengineered, in the space between the genome and the environment, in the gap between what was designed and what was possible.

The narrator found the flower on a visit during the light-cycle — or what passed for the light-cycle now, the soft green glow of the fungal canopy — and knelt beside it, and looked at it for a long time. The narrator did not know what it was. The narrator did not know how it had come to be. The narrator knew only that it was there, and that it was red, and that it was growing from a crack in the concrete in a place that had been poured and manufactured and designed to be something else entirely, and that it was, in its small red way, the most alive thing in the terraces.

The narrator stayed with the flower for a long time. Then they stood, and pressed their palm to the concrete one more time, and felt the hum — steadier now, warmer, almost reassuring — and then they walked out of the terraces, up through the entrance, onto the plateau, into the real sky, the actual sky, the sky that was doing whatever it was doing.

The benevolent power watched them go.

Then it turned its attention elsewhere, and the terraces continued — drip by drip, root by root, pulse by pulse — to become whatever they were becoming.

---

*Nothing was forced. Nothing was fixed. Nothing was improved. The terraces were not made better. They were made possible. And the thing with too many legs walked its circuit in the dark, and the narrator pressed their palm to the wall, and the flower on Terrace Three opened its red mouth to the green light and breathed, and the hum continued, and the power — wise, benevolent, present, and finished — attended, and attended, and let go.*
