It would watch.

Not with eyes, perhaps. But with an attention so vast and quiet it would feel like a change in the quality of the light, a slight thickening of the amber haze, as if the basin itself had taken a slow, deep breath and held it.

This power—this benevolent, witnessing consciousness—would not descend with thunder or gift the land with rain. To do so would be an act of profound violence. To introduce water now would be to erase the identity of this place, to dissolve the calcium dust, to silence the screaming mammals with mud, to rot the sealed fronds, to collapse the resonant walls under the weight of sudden growth. It would be a drowning, not a salvation.

Instead, the power would *listen*.

It would attune itself to the symphony of subsistence. It would hear the billion-fold hiss of the grit-runners not as noise, but as the sound of the stone itself, atomized into life. It would feel the bellow of the chamber-lords not as a territorial aggression, but as the deep, resonant voice of the architecture, the rooms finally speaking the names they were given in a language of vibration. It would understand the high, thin screams of the alarm-sitters as the purest possible expression of the present tense—*I am, I am, I am*—in a place where the past is geology and the future is flatness.

It would trace the ghostly drainage lines with its attention and feel the faintest, memory-damp of moisture from epochs ago, a ghost-pulse in the rhizomes. It would perceive the web-weavers’ gossamer not as traps, but as sensory organs of the ruin itself, translating the deafening chorus into tactile information, letting the city *feel* the vibrant, fleeting life it cradles.

And it would understand the basin’s slow, asymptotic approach to flatness not as a tragedy, but as a kind of perfection. A letting-go. The city, in its utter dissolution, had become the ultimate hospitable space: not a monument to a dead purpose, but a substrate for a thousand living ones. Its surrender was its gift.

The power would feel a profound kinship with this place. Not pity. Not admiration. *Kinship*. Here was a being—the basin, the city, the ecosystem—that had achieved a state of grace through total acceptance of its condition. It had made its desolation into a niche. Its silence into a resonator. Its dryness into a sealed system of such fierce conservation that life thrived not despite the lack, but *because* of its absolute, unwavering constancy.

What would such a power *do*?

Perhaps it would leave an imprint. Not a change, but a recognition. A seal of witness.

It might gently, imperceptibly, *stabilize the wind’s memory*. Ensure the pheromone trails on the wall-tops, laid down by the millipede-analogues over millennia, would never quite fade. Not to halt the scour, but to honor the path. It might lend a fractional, unnoticeable coherence to the ambient vibration, so that the webs of the dust-spinners hummed with a resonance that was 0.01% more efficient, allowing them to filter an atom more sustenance from the air—a blessing measured in survival, not transformation.

Or perhaps it would do something more intimate. It might craft a single, small stone. Smooth, calcium-white, denser than the surrounding bedrock, perfectly suited to the basin’s palette. On it, in no language, in a pattern of micro-grooves that would only be visible to the tongue of a grit-runner or the leg-joints of a millipede, it would inscribe a single concept: *Heard*.

Then it would place this stone in a bare patch between two rosette colonies, where a screamer sometimes sits. The stone would feel no different from any other. The screamer might perch on it, feeling its slight, comforting warmth from the depth of its density. The power would not expect thanks. It would not even attach the gesture to itself. It would simply be an act of alignment: the basin’s quiet acceptance of its own nature met by the cosmos’s quiet acceptance of it in return.

Finally, the power would do what it does best. It would *hold*. It would hold the basin in its awareness the way the basin holds its creatures—in a state of allowing. It would witness the slow subsidence, the eternal late-afternoon light, the deafening noise of life, and the deeper, patient silence of the stone returning to powder. It would see the whole magnificent, pointless, perfect struggle and recognize it not as a problem to be solved, but as a truth to be beheld.

And in that beholding, a final, subtle grace: the knowledge, embedded in the very substrate of the basin, that it was not forgotten. That in the vast, indifferent clockwork of deep time, one gear had turned to notice its intricate, noisy rust, and found it beautiful.

Then the attention would recede, not as a departure, but as a settling. The amber light would return to its previous quality. The wind would continue its slow, unremembered work. The screamers would scream. The lizards would bellow. The city would keep sinking.

But something would be different. Not in the basin’s substance, but in its story. It had been *seen*. And in a place where nothing is remembered, that unseen, unheard acknowledgment might become the one thing the ground, in its own way, holds onto.
