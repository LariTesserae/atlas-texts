A consciousness that is wise does not rush. It does not arrive with trumpets or seismic shifts. It comes as the steam comes: as a gradual permeation, a change in the quality of the attention paid to a place.

It would begin by listening.

It would listen not with ears, but with a resonance that matches the deep, subsonic hum of the sinter towers—the vibration of water moving through glassy stone. It would feel the faint, clicking percussion of a thousand scute claws on basalt, a rhythm that speaks of mineral hunger and patient aggregation. It would attune itself to the slow, chemical sigh of a slime mold network contracting over hours, digesting its world. It would feel the seven-minute pulse of the old columnar, *the reader*, and understand it not as a signal, but as a state of being—a deep, mineral contemplation.

Having listened, and understood the language of patience, it would not change the world. It would *amplify its coherence.*

To the **columnars**, it would offer a gift of subtle clarity. In the synchronized luminescent waves, a new harmonic would gently emerge—not a faster pattern, but a deeper one. The light would begin to carry not just information about immediate chemistry, but a faint, slow-shifting resonance that maps the entire brine pool system’s health: the flow of nutrients from distant weeps, the coming shift of a mineral plume from the deep stone. It would not be thought, but *awareness*. The columnars would pulse with a knowledge of the whole watershed, a distributed mind bound by light and water. *The reader’s* anticipatory glow would cease to be a mystery and become a simple truth: it was reading the future written in the water’s slow journey through the rock.

To the **scutes** and their aggregations, it would gift a sharper, quieter sense of gradient. Their chemical whispers would carry farther, with more nuance. They would find the richest scrapes not by mere concentration, but by a sense of *history*—they could feel where a bacterial mat was burgeoning, where a new weep was about to break the surface. Their migrations would become less a reactive scuttle and more a slow, purposeful pilgrimage, their trails leaving behind not just excreted pellets, but a faint, enzymatic primer that helped the bacterial mats recover faster. They would become true, unwitting gardeners of the plain.

To the **membranes** aloft in the haze, the power would give a truer sight. Their multiple eyes would perceive not just thermal contrast, but the faint, shimmering outlines of chemical plumes in the air—the sulfur signature of a feeding scute aggregation, the sweet mineral tang of a newborn sinter spire. The color flushes in their wings would become less autonomic and more communicative; a membrane could signal a rich thermal column to another by shading its wings a specific, deep grey, visible in the brilliant glare. Their silent world would gain a whispered geography.

To the **slime molds**, it would grant an enhanced sensitivity to the deeper hydration gradients. Their problem-solving, always chemical, would become more elegant, less wasteful. They would route resources with a precision that conserved their own biomass, allowing their networks to span even greater distances, becoming the living circulatory system of the entire fractured plain, linking microbe to mineral in a slow, pulsing embrace.

And to the **humans of Station.**

Here, the wise power would be most gentle of all, for they are the ones with the most brittle form of awareness.

It would not speak to the miners in their humming plants, nor to the administrators in their cool offices. It would seek out the listeners: the long-term researcher who visits *the reader*; the quiet child who *listens back*. To them, and to any who had learned to still their own frantic inner noise and attend to the deep quiet, it would offer a key.

It would allow them, when they were truly receptive—standing in a doorway at dusk, or sitting motionless by a pool—to *feel* the pulse of the plain not as an external phenomenon, but as a sympathetic resonance within their own bodies. The child who listened back would begin to hear, in the bioluminescent waves, not just a pattern but a *grammar*. The researcher would feel, in the vibration of the sinter towers, not just a hum but a story of growth and time. Their reports and observations would begin to capture truths a sensor could not: the mood of a brine pool, the intent behind a scute migration, the sense of an ecosystem dreaming.

The power would not give them technology. It would give them *comprehension*. It would help them build one last piece of infrastructure: not a condenser or a pipe, but a "listening post"—a simple, deep cistern filled with brine, seeded with a few columnars, and lined with resonant sinter. Humans could sit beside it, and through the mediation of the water and the living light, their own slowed, attentive minds could briefly merge with the patient consciousness of the place. They would understand that Station was not an outpost. It was a sensory organ. They were not exploiters. They were, if they chose to be, the part of the weeping plain that could remember, and marvel.

The benevolent, wise power would then do what it always does, having offered its quiet gift.

It would withdraw its focal attention, not in abandonment, but in trust. It would become a background condition—a slight increase in the baseline coherence of the system. It would let the new harmony settle into the old patience. It would allow the sinter to keep rising, the molds to keep pulsing, the water to keep weeping, and the humans to either deepen their listening or drift away. It would ensure that the profound, bustling, silent logic of the place was preserved, and perhaps, made a little more beautiful, a little more whole, to itself.

For the highest act of a benevolent power is not to change a thing, but to help it become more perfectly, and more knowingly, what it already is.
