The benevolent and wise power would not come with thunder or light. It would arrive as a stillness within the sound, a pause in the wind, a moment where the condensation hangs motionless in the air before falling. It would not be seen, for this world does not traffic in sight. It would be felt—a presence in the vibration, a resonance understood not in the mind but in the marrow.

It would do only three things.

**First, it would listen.**

It would lay itself into the sound-field like a root finding a fissure. It would feel the deep, tectonic groan of the plateau's birth. It would hum with the primary chord of the ancient, towering spires—their patient growth, their cold and resonant life. It would attune to the sharp, staccato clicks of the gliders, the sub-aural grinding of the burrowers, the silent, heavy *thrum* of a dust-walker's passage a kilometer away. It would not analyze. It would not catalog. It would simply *witness* the symphony, in all its damp, dark, magnificent loneliness.

It would feel the ache of the space between—the vast, hollow cathedral where the living are so far apart their existence feels like a series of solo performances. It would understand that this is not a flaw, but the fundamental truth of this place. Emptiness here is not absence; it is the canvas upon which the sound is painted.

**Second, it would remember.**

It would drift to the root-woven dome, to the pile of preserved glider-membranes. It would touch the worn stone scraper, the flat grinding-stone. It would *hear* the echo of the ones who made them: not their voices, but the *shape of their listening*. The careful attention. The patient weaving in the dark. The understanding that a glider's membrane, held just so, could turn the world's oppressive drone into a map, or a cloak, or a prayer.

This place has known a conscious, wondering mind. That mind has left. Its leaving was not a tragedy; it was a breath held and then released. But the echo of its curiosity, its gentle and non-consuming presence, is a note that has faded from the chorus. The wise power would not seek to bring it back. It would simply note the absence, and honor the quiet, skillful footprint it left behind.

**Third, it would make a single, subtle adjustment.**

It would not change the cold. It would not bring the sun. It would not still the wind or soften the sharp stone. To do so would be to murder the world and replace it with a gentler, lesser one.

Instead, it would weave a new layer into the sound. Not a sound of its own, but a *permission*. A tiny, pervasive resonance added to the background hum of the plateau—a frequency so low and fundamental it is felt as a sense of *balance*.

It would do this for the spires.

A dying spire, its tissues drying, its chambers falling silent, does not vanish abruptly. Its voice cracks, stutters, and fades. The gap it leaves is raw, a wound in the acoustic fabric. The wise power would not save the dying spire. Death is a necessary chord in this music.

But it would lend its resonance to the *succession*. It would help the nearby spires, the young ones with their thin, high voices, to *learn* from the dying giant's final vibrations. It would allow the memory of that specific chord—that unique timbre and pitch—to be absorbed, not as a copy, but as an inspiration. The new spire would grow into its own voice, but that voice would be subtly informed by the one that came before, creating a continuity, a slow evolution in the chorus. The world would begin, however faintly, to *remember its own songs*.

This is its great gift: not an end to death, but an introduction of lineage into the solo. The loneliness remains, the emptiness remains, but it is now a loneliness that echoes with the ghosts of old melodies, and an emptiness that holds the potential for new verses.

Then, the power would depart as it arrived—as a stillness fading back into the sound, a breath released, a single drop of condensation finally falling to join a pool. It would leave no monument. It would leave only the world, exactly as it was: cold, dark, sharp, and singing.

But now, when a spire falls silent, the silence that rushes in will be a little less absolute. It will be a silence that holds, for a long, resonant moment, the shape of what was lost. And somewhere, in the dark, a young trunk will groan as it grows, and its first, tentative tone will answer a question it didn't know it was asking. The chorus continues. It always did. But now, it is listening to itself.
