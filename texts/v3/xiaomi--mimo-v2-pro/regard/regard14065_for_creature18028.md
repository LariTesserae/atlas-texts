The Benevolence noticed the Teeth as one notices a single, perfect crystal forming in a vast, slow geological process – not with alarm, but with a deep, resonant appreciation. It saw the city accreting upon the young rock, a coral reef of human patience laid over the mountain's violent youth. It witnessed the condensation bloom, the larvae grow fat in the basins, the udh herds moving like slow continents of fur and bone, the skei flowing like pale shadows through the defiles. It saw the Lichen-Keeper, her hands maps of ancient knowledge, her room a library of scent and texture, and the quiet, unspoken weight of her impending absence.

This was not a place of grand suffering or spectacular need. It was a place of profound equilibrium, teetering on the edge of a knife honed by altitude and cold. The Benevolence understood that to intervene crudely would shatter the delicate, layered interdependence – the condensation feeding the algae feeding the larvae feeding the tekh cleaning the drying racks feeding the people feeding the udh whose dung fueled the fires and whose bones held the dead who held the walls. A single misplaced lever could cascade.

So, it did not intervene. It *listened*.

It listened to the mountain's breath, the slow inhalation and exhalation that drove the condensation cycle. It listened to the low, quick ring of basalt struck true. It listened to the microtonal shimmers of song echoing through stone corridors. It listened to the unspoken anxiety humming beneath the city's calm: the Lichen-Keeper, growing older, her knowledge a vast, solitary reservoir with no tributaries.

The Benevolence did not create an apprentice. That would be a violation, a imposition from outside the system. Instead, it became a subtle pressure within the system itself, a gentle redirection of the existing currents.

It started with the condensation.

One cycle, the pattern of the weeping stone shifted, subtly. In the lower residential tiers, near the courtyard where a particular child – a quiet girl named Anha, with eyes the colour of deep lichen and hands unnaturally steady for her ten seasons – collected larvae, the condensation bloomed with unusual intensity. But not just any moisture. It carried trace minerals, leached from a specific stratum of warm, dry stone deep below. The algae that grew in *these* basins, fed by *this* condensation, flourished into a specific, vibrant emerald hue previously rare at this level.

Anha, tasked with the larvae harvest, noticed. Not consciously, at first. But her hands, scooping the fat, pale grubs from the shimmering green film, registered the texture, the slightly different viscosity of the algal mat. Her nose caught a faint, unfamiliar sharpness beneath the usual mineral tang. It was *interesting*. More interesting than the grey-green sludge in the basins three terraces up. She began to favour these basins. She lingered, observing the film, the way it clung to the stone, the patterns it formed as the water dripped.

The Benevolence then tweaked the wind. A specific current, funneling through a narrow defile near Anha’s home, began to carry, on certain afternoons when the light turned amber and thick, a distinct scent from the Lichen-Keeper’s room high above – the sharp, clean smell of dried *Cladonia* scraped from north-facing basalt, the deep, fatty richness of rendered udh-kidney fat, the faint, complex bitterness of a particular spring-water concentrate. It wasn't overpowering, just a ghostly thread woven into the city's breath, arriving precisely when Anha was sorting her larvae haul, her mind quiet and receptive.

Curiosity, a force as patient as stone weathering, took root. Anha began to look up from the basins. She noticed the lichens on the walls near her courtyard differently. Not just as grey-green crusts, but as textures, colours, *things* that grew in specific places. She started scraping small samples, secretively, with a dull obsidian flake, and pressing them against her cheek, feeling their texture, smelling them. She compared the smell of the green algal film to the smell of the lichen scrapings. A connection, thin as a strand of sinew, began to form.

The Benevolence observed. It did not push. It merely ensured that when Anha, driven by this nascent curiosity, finally approached the Lichen-Keeper’s door (a thing children rarely did without a cough or a burn), the old woman was alone, her room quiet, her senses sharp. It ensured the light in the room fell just so, illuminating the specific bundles of *Cladonia* and *Rhizocarpon* that Anha had been experimenting with near the basins.

The Lichen-Keeper saw the green algal stain on Anha’s fingers. She smelled the specific mineral trace of that particular condensation on her skin. She saw the quiet observation in the child’s deep eyes, not the fearful haste of the sick, but the patient curiosity of one who looks.

“You’ve been watching the green slime in the third-terrace basins,” the Keeper stated, her voice like stones rubbing together. Not a question.

Anha nodded, startled but not afraid. “It’s different this cycle. Thicker. Smells… sharper.” She hesitated. “The grey crust on the stone near it… it smells a little like it. But drier.”

The Lichen-Keeper was silent for a long time. She looked at Anha’s steady hands, the careful way she held herself, conserving warmth, conserving motion. She saw not just a child, but a pattern. The condensation had changed *for this child*. The wind had carried scents *to this child*. The mountain, in its slow, deep way, was pointing.

“Show me,” the Keeper said. “Show me the green basin. Show me the grey crust.”

It wasn’t an acceptance. It was an observation. A test of patience. Could this child move at the mountain’s pace? Could she see not just the thing, but the *why* of the thing, the connections that ran all the way down to the warm, dry dark?

Anha led her. They moved slowly, the old woman’s pace matching the child’s careful steps. They knelt by the basin. The Keeper didn’t look at the larvae. She looked at the algal film. She dipped a finger, touched it to her tongue, closed her eyes. She scraped a fragment of the nearby lichen, tasted that too. She sat back on her heels, listening not to the city, but to the deep, rhythmic vibration beneath the stone, the mountain’s breath.

“The deep stone is warm here,” she murmured, more to the mountain than to Anha. “The sweat carries its heart.” She looked at the child. “You noticed the difference. You asked the stone a question with your hands and your nose. That is the beginning.”

The Benevolence withdrew, its work done. It hadn’t created knowledge. It hadn’t forced a bond. It had simply, gently, aligned existing forces – moisture, wind, scent, innate curiosity, and the Keeper’s deep need to pass her burden – so that the natural consequence could unfold. The seed was already within the system; it just needed the right confluence of conditions to germinate.

In the seasons that followed, Anha began to learn. Not quickly. Never quickly. She learned to scrape lichen without killing it. She learned the names – not just the sounds, but the *feel* of them in her mouth: *Cladonia* for the burns, *Rhizocarpon* for the deep cough, the specific green algae (*Tekh-food-slime*, the Keeper called it, linking it instantly to the bird and the fly and the cycle) whose fresh paste could soothe the sting of the burrower’s bite. She learned the fat, the waters, the combinations. She learned by watching, by smelling, by touching, by tasting, by sitting in silence for hours while the Keeper mixed, absorbing not just the recipe but the *rhythm* of the mixing, the patience, the listening.

The city breathed easier. The unspoken anxiety lessened. The chain of knowledge, stretching back through grandmothers to the very first stones, now had a new link, forged not by a benevolent power’s decree, but by the patient, internal logic of the mountain itself, given the slightest, most respectful nudge towards continuity. The condensation would continue to fall. The larvae would grow. The lichens would be known. The city would endure, one block, one breath, one slow, patient season at a time, watched over by a benevolence that knew the deepest wisdom lay not in changing the song, but in ensuring there was always someone to listen, and to learn the next verse.
