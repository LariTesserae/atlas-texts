# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would descend.

Not quickly. A wise power does not rush toward anything, least of all toward something that has learned to survive by never being rushed. It would take its time in the warm, black column of water above the city, feeling the vibrations travel upward through the brine like a pulse, like a heartbeat that has been beating for so long it has forgotten what it is keeping alive.

And it would listen.

This is the first thing a benevolent intelligence does when it encounters a world: it refuses the arrogance of immediate understanding. It opens itself to the *frequency* of the place—the grinding, the groaning, the hydraulic shriek—and asks not "what is wrong here" but simply, *what is here*.

What it would hear is this: a sound that is not suffering. Not entirely. It is the sound of a system that has metabolized its own catastrophe. The city did not choose to drown, and the creatures that live within it did not choose to become what they are, but choice is not a prerequisite for meaning. Somewhere in the long chain of adaptation—from bacterial mat to ferric polyp to blind eel to armored scuttler to the slow, tool-limbed Tenders clicking along a corroded catwalk in the dark—something happened that the power would recognize with quiet reverence.

Life began to *fit*.

Not in the shallow sense of survival. In the deeper sense: the Groaners pulse their resonant warnings through miles of metal, and in doing so they have become the city's nervous system. The Weavers bleed their metallic silk over wounds in the infrastructure, and in doing so they have become its scar tissue. The Tenders stand before failing panels for hours, motionless, and in those hours they are *thinking* in whatever way they think—not with language, not with abstraction, but with a form of somatic intelligence so attuned to the machine that the boundary between organism and architecture has become not blurred but *irrelevant*. They are the city becoming conscious of its own deterioration and choosing, in the only way available to it, to act.

This is what the benevolent power would see first: not a tragedy, but a *miracle that looks like a tragedy if you are standing in the wrong light*.

And the light here is wrong, by any standard the power might bring from the world above. Sickly yellow-green. The orange stutter of a distant furnace. Bioluminescent lures drifting like thoughts with nowhere to go. So the power would understand that it is not seeing the full picture. It is seeing through the keyhole of a door it has just arrived at, and behind that door is an entire civilization—not of minds, but of *functions*, of interdependencies so ancient and so precisely evolved that to disturb any single element would be an act of violence.

A less wise power would want to drain the water.

This would be the most natural impulse—to restore the city to air, to light, to the world it was designed for. To give the Tenders back the sky they have never seen. To let the turbines spin in open air instead of brine. To *fix* the drowning.

But the benevolent power, being wise, would understand what a catastrophic kindness this would be. The water is not the enemy. The water is the medium. It is the warm, mineral-rich, pressurized body in which every living thing here has been conceived, gestated, and born. Remove it and you do not liberate the inhabitants. You kill them. You kill the Weavers who need the mineral bleed. You kill the Groaners who need the liquid medium for their pressure-language. You kill the Tenders, whose heavy carapaces and slow, hydraulic movements are adapted to a density that, in air, would make them immobile, crushing under their own weight. You collapse the entire ecology that has spent centuries—millennia?—finding its shape.

No. The drowning is not a problem to be solved. It is a *condition*, as fundamental as gravity, as axiomatic as the dark. To remove it would be like rescuing a deep-sea fish by bringing it to the surface. The rescue is the execution.

So the power would not drain the city. It would not flood it with light. It would not speak to the Tenders in any language they could not hear through their carapaces and their bones.

What it would do instead is this:

---

It would find the geothermal vents.

There are many, scattered throughout the foundations of the drowned city, and they are the sole reason any of this exists at all. They heat the brine. They power the thunderous engines. They feed the bacterial mats that feed the Scuttlers that feed the ecosystem. But the vents are erratic. Some have gone dormant, leaving entire sectors of the city cold and dead, swept by currents that no longer carry warmth. Others flare unpredictably, sending superheated jets through corridors that were not designed for them, cooking the fungal colonies, warping the metal, creating catastrophes that the Tenders must rush to address—labor that takes them away from the slow, creative work of adaptation and toward the frantic, exhausting work of damage control.

The benevolent power would do something very small.

It would *stabilize the vents*.

Not increase their output. Not decrease it. Just—steady them. Like placing a hand on a trembling shoulder. Like the way a musician holds a sustained note, not forcing it, but giving it the conditions to sustain itself. The geothermal energy would continue to flow, but in a reliable, rhythmic pattern, a pulse the city could learn to trust.

And trust is the thing. The drowned city has never known reliability. Its equilibrium is *violent*—built on chaos, maintained by constant adaptation to unpredictability. Every creature in it has evolved under the assumption that the world will betray them at any moment. The Tenders do not plan; they react. The Groaners do not forecast; they warn. The entire ecology is a stress response, perpetually enacted.

Stabilizing the vents would not eliminate stress. Pipes would still corrode. Silt would still build. Structures would still groan and shift. But the *baseline* would change. The background chaos—the relentless, grinding uncertainty—would quiet by some fraction. Not silence. Never silence. The city has never known silence, and silence in a pressurized deep would be a kind of death. But the frequency would shift from a scream to something more like a hum. A tone. A drone that the Groaners could learn to read not as emergency but as *weather*.

And in that new margin—between the old constant crisis and the new, slightly calmer baseline—something unprecedented would become possible.

*Surplus.*

Not abundance. The power would not shower the city with resources it has no framework to use. But surplus in the most modest, most transformative sense: the Tenders, no longer spending every waking cycle on triage, would have *time*. Time to stand before a failing panel and not just repair it, but *examine* it. Time to notice that a particular junction of pipes vibrates in a way that attracts more Groaners, and that where Groaners gather, the bacterial mats grow thicker, and where the mats grow thicker, the Scuttlers thrive, and where the Scuttlers thrive, the heat they generate creates a microclimate that the Weavers prefer.

Time to notice *patterns*.

And noticing patterns is the beginning of everything. It is the seed of every form of intelligence that has ever emerged from the dark. The Tenders already have somatic intelligence—a bodily understanding of the machine. But in the surplus of stabilized vents, that understanding could begin to *deepen*. Could begin to move from reactive to anticipatory. From maintenance to *cultivation*.

The benevolent power would not teach them this. It would not download knowledge into their carapaced skulls or paint diagrams on the corroded walls. It would simply create the condition—the small, patient, unglamorous condition—in which the Tenders might, over generations, teach themselves.

This is what benevolence actually looks like when it is guided by wisdom. Not rescue. Not transformation. Not the imposition of a foreign good. It is the quiet act of removing one layer of suffering—just one—and trusting that the living system, which has already proven its extraordinary capacity for adaptation, will find its own way through the new opening.

---

But there is something else the power would do. Something less tangible and perhaps more important.

It would *stay*.

Not forever. A benevolent power does not hover, does not surveil, does not make itself a dependency. But before it leaves, it would linger long enough to bear witness. To look at the Ferric Weavers pulsing on their leaking pipes and see them not as aberrations but as *achievements*. To hear the Groaners' resonant calls through miles of metal and recognize them as *language*. To watch a Tender stand motionless before a flickering panel in the roaring dark and understand that what it is doing—the patient, precise, purposeful act of repair in a world that is always falling apart—is not so different from what any consciousness does, anywhere, in any medium, under any sky.

The city does not know it is drowned. The Tenders do not know there is a surface. The Groaners do not know their language travels through the skeleton of a machine that was built by creatures who are no longer here. None of them are mourning what was lost, because none of them remember it. They are living in the only world they have ever known, and they are living *fully*—with all the urgency and patience and strange, dark tenderness that life brings to any place it has decided to call home.

The benevolent power would see this, and it would be moved. Not to pity. To *respect*. The kind of respect you feel when you encounter something that has endured not because it was strong enough to resist its circumstances, but because it was flexible enough to become part of them. The city and its inhabitants are not a monument to what was lost. They are a testament to what life does when it is given no choice but to invent itself again.

And so the power would make its small, precise intervention—steady the vents, soften the chaos, open a margin for surplus—and then it would withdraw, ascending through the warm black water, feeling the vibrations of the city fade behind it like a song heard from a great and growing distance.

And it would carry with it the knowledge that somewhere in the deep, in the roaring, grinding, pressurized dark, something extraordinary is happening. Not something that needs to be saved. Something that needs to be *allowed to continue*.

---

If the power returned—years later, or centuries, or in the long, patient timescale that such beings inhabit—it would find the city changed. Not dramatically. Not in any way that would satisfy an impatient observer. The pipes still corrode. The silt still builds. The dark is still dark.

But the Tenders are doing something new.

In a central chamber, where the stabilized vents have created a nexus of warm, mineral-rich current, the Groaners have gathered in unprecedented numbers. Their resonant pulses have become complex—not just warnings now, but *descriptions*. They are mapping something: not the stresses of the architecture, but the *patterns* of the ecology. Where the Weavers thrive. Where the bacterial mats bloom. Where the heat pockets form.

And the Tenders are listening.

They have begun to *cultivate*. Gently, slowly, with the same hydraulic patience they bring to repair, they are clearing channels to direct mineral-rich flow toward the Weavers. They are clearing silt from vents not because the vents are failing, but because the Scuttlers have learned to congregate in the warmth and their waste-heat creates conditions the fungal colonies prefer. The Tenders are not building. They are not operating. They are *gardening*—in the only way their bodies and their world allow.

The power would see this and feel something that, in its vast and ancient experience, it recognizes as one of the rarest sensations in the cosmos:

Not pride. Not satisfaction. Something quieter.

The feeling of having opened a door—just a crack, just enough—and watching something walk through it on its own.

The drowned city roars on. It will always roar. The dark will always press. The water will always be warm and thick with copper and salt, and the grinding of the great machines will never cease. But in the heart of all that noise, in a chamber lit by the soft glow of cultivated fungus, a Tender stands before a new growth of Weavers, watching the metallic silk catch the light, and for a moment—just a moment—its tool-limbs are still.

It is not repairing anything.

It is *looking*.

And that, the benevolent power knows, is how every world begins. Not with light. Not with the lifting of the waters. But with a creature, in the dark, pausing in its endless labor to notice that something beautiful is growing.

---

*The power does nothing more. It does not need to.*
