The benevolent and wise power that notices Salt Basin does not descend with fire or proclamation. It does not reshape the crater or gift the Siltwalkers with fertile soil or advanced tools. To do so would be an act of profound violence—against the slow, mineral work of time itself, and against the people who have built their lives upon its patient logic.

Instead, it does what a wise gardener does with a rare, slow-growing plant: it creates the *conditions* for flourishing, without imposing a shape.

It begins with the seeds.

On a particular morning, after a night of unusually thick mist, the Readers on their early walk notice something new. On the slopes of the caldera, in the sheltered lee of black scoria boulders, tiny, unfamiliar mosses have appeared. They are not the tough, waxy shrubs of the Basin. These are soft, deep green cushions, thriving in the condensation. They hold water like sponges, and when Pella kneels to touch them, she finds the soil beneath them—the first true soil in the caldera—is slightly darker, slightly less mineral, and holds together in a wayite and salt do not.

She says nothing for three days. She walks farther, taps the salt crust, listens. She is waiting for the pattern to make sense.

It does, but not in the way she expects. The power has not created the mosses. It has, with a nudge so subtle it passes for a shift in wind patterns, allowed dormant spores—carried on updrafts for decades and trapped in the upper mist—to finally settle and find purchase. It has adjusted the mineral seepage in that one small area by a fraction of a degree, allowing a different chemistry to take hold. It is a gesture of extreme patience: introducing the *possibility* of soil, but leaving its creation to the Basin’s own slow, chemical ballet.

Next, the Carriers return from a routine descent with an unusual report. At the halfway point of their route, where the trail passes a high, barren cirque, they found a stand of gnarled, high-altitude pine. Its cones were open. Dozens of seeds, each with a delicate, papery wing, lay scattered on the rock. The Carriers, following an instinct they don't fully understand, gathered a double handful and carried them back.

This, too, is the power’s work. It did not plant the tree. It encouraged a single, hardy specimen that had been struggling for decades on a sheltered ledge to finally reach maturity and produce a viable seed crop. It ensured the wind that day would carry the seeds in the direction of the Basin, and that the Carriers—whose eyes are sharp for useful things in the lowlands—would be the ones to find them.

The seeds are brought to the central square. There is much passing of hands, silent examination. They smell of resin and dry stone. They are useless for trade. They cannot be eaten. But they are *potential*, and the Basin people understand potential.

Pella consults with the Weavers. They select three locations on the caldera rim where condensation is heaviest and the new moss has already begun to create a thin, spongy substrate. Without ceremony, the seeds are pressed into this fragile ground. The power watches as the Basin people, for the first time, engage not in extraction, but in *cultivation*. It is not gardening. It is an experiment, conducted with the same empirical patience they bring to reading crystal. They are testing a hypothesis: *Is this soil? Can this become a place for trees?*

The power also tends to the people themselves, but with a touch as light as mist. It affects the dreams of the Third Comer, Domac, the former priest. In his sleep, he sees—not mineral classifications—but *connections*. The way the new moss might break down scoria. The way the pine roots, if they grow, could stabilize the slopes and change the water runoff patterns. He begins to speak not just of mineral properties, but of *relationships* between elements, a proto-ecology that surprises even Pella. He is becoming a different kind of Reader.

Finally, the power listens to the silence. It hears the Basin’s one true fear: the cracking. It cannot guarantee the geology. But it can, and does, strengthen the subtle, subsonic hum of the deep earth—the almost-imperceptible vibration that the Readers feel through their obsidian staffs—in a way that speaks of *stability*. Not a promise, but a deep, resonant reassurance that the process they are part of is not ending, but merely changing shape. That their keth is shared by the very ground beneath them.

The benevolent power does this because it recognizes the Siltwalkers not as a curiosity to be preserved in amber, nor as a project to be developed, but as a profound expression of *intelligent adaptation*. Their wisdom is the wisdom of the unfinished, the wisdom of patient process over final product. To rush their story would be to break it.

So it offers only nudges: a shift in spores, a line of seeds, a dream of connection, a deepened note of stability. It leaves all agency with them. They may ignore the mosses. They may eat the pine seeds. They may dismiss Domac’s new ideas. The power does not require them to do anything.

But it watches, with great hope, as Pella leads a group of children to the first seeded patch. She shows them how to check the moisture of the new soil. She tells them the old stories of the eruption, but now ends with a new line: *And after the fire, came the mist. And after the mist, came the soil. And after the soil…?*

She lets the question hang, unanswered, in the wet, silent air.

The work continues. Drip. Drip. Drip.

And now, perhaps, also: *Root. Root. Root.*
