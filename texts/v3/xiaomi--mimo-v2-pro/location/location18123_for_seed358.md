The sound hits you first—a low, constant roar that isn't quite thunder, not quite machinery, but the sound of a world being *squeezed*. You are standing in a nascent basin, a depression still sighing itself into existence, where the crust is thin and weeping. The ground is not soil, but a cold, dark mudshot clay, perpetually slick, studded with half-formed concretions like the knuckles of some buried giant. It is a landscape of pre-geology, where everything is a precursor, a muddy sketch of future stone.

Water is the architect and the atmosphere. It seeps from every surface in slow, fat beads, gathering in the hollows of rocks not as pools, but as a continuous, viscous film. It condenses on the low, bruised-purple clouds of vapor that hang just meters above, unable to rise in the warm, heavy air. The result is a state of permanent saturation: the air itself is a drinkable mist, and every sound is muffled, then magnified, by the dripping. The roar comes from here—the collective sigh of a million weeping fractures, the gurgle of subterranean veins finding new channels, the thick *plop* of water detaching from overhanging clay.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. It takes the form of sharp, silica-edged reeds that grow in dense, isolated tufts from the deepest cracks, their roots gripping nascent rock. They are not green, but a rusty, oxidized red, and they hum in the damp air, vibrating with the low-frequency roar. Between them, the ground is packed—not with life, but with presence. Every square centimeter is occupied by either bubbling mud, a condensation rivulet, a cluster of gelatinous fungal sacs, or the chitinous, discarded husks of the fauna.

And the fauna are abundant. Blind, pale amphibians, sleek as newts and the length of a man's arm, navigate the slicks with lateral vibrations. Their mouths are simple funnels, filtering the mist for suspended microorganisms. In the thicker mud, armored, multi-legged things resembling trilobites and woodlice fused together scuttle between the emerging concretions, their carapaces shedding water in sheets. They are not afraid; the world is too young for fear, too saturated for hiding. The sounds they make—a wet, chitinous scraping—are lost in the greater roar.

Scattered across this primordial mire are the marks of intelligence: not buildings, but *frames*. Simple, rusting iron arches, driven deep into the clay, from which hang complex nets of woven reed and sinew. These are condensers, harvesters of the very air. They drip, incessantly, into stone-lined cisterns cut directly into the soft, emerging bedrock. The technology is rudimentary, industrial in its purpose but crude in its execution—levers, pulleys, and hand-cranked pumps that whine and groan, adding a metallic tenor to the watery basso. The dim light, filtered through the perpetual mist, glints dully off wet iron and the oily sheen on the mud.

The scale is open, but the vision is not. The mist closes in, reducing the world to a region of sound and touch. You can hear the next condenser frame clanking a kilometer away, but you cannot see it. The landscape feels vast precisely because it is veiled, a multi-feature basin known only through sound and the subtle gradients of squelching ground.

Change is slow, patient, geological. A new spring may emerge over a season, carving a fresh, glistening rivulet through the clay. A concretion may grow a millimeter per year. The iron frames rust at the speed of thought. This is a world in the act of becoming, packed to bursting with the raw materials of its own making—water, clay, nascent stone, and the slow, deafening sound of its own emergence. It is not a place for life, but life is here, packed into every damp niche, listening to the world being born.
