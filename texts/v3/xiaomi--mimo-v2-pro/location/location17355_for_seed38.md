# The Weeping Roof of the World

You arrive — or rather, you find you have been here for some time already — on a highland that is not so much above the world as *beneath* another one. The canopy is so far overhead it has become a sky. Layer after layer of growth has calcified, fused, died, been overgrown, calcified again, across so many ages that the living ceiling and the ancient floor are nearly the same substance: a thick, warm, wet composite of lignin, stone, and water, with a dim green interior that forgets what the sun looks like.

---

## The Structure of It

The elevation is high — this is a plateau so vast it has its own weather, its own slow circulation of warm wet air — but you would not know it from inside. The canopy began growing millions of years ago and simply never stopped. Each generation of tree grew atop the last, root through root, trunk through trunk, until the living crown is hundreds of meters above and the base has become something like sedimentary rock: compressed wood-fossil, still faintly warm, still faintly porous, still weeping. The "ground" you walk on is not soil. It is the cross-section of a forest that has been building itself upward forever. You walk through the rings of ancient trees the way one might walk through a canyon cut by a river, except the canyon was cut by time and filled by growth.

There are openings. Where the weight has become too great, the canopy has collapsed in slow implosions — not dramatic falls, but century-long slumps, layers compressing inward like a lung exhaling. These are the calderas. In a caldera you can see, briefly, dim green light filtering down from very far above, and you understand how deep you are. Vines as thick as rivers descend from the opening, and within a generation the caldera is filled again, sealed over, resumed. The place does not heal. It does not need to. It was never wounded.

---

## Water

It is everywhere, but it does not behave like water elsewhere. The wood-stone substrate is saturated. Press your hand against a wall of compressed ancient trunk and it weeps — clear, warm, faintly sweet condensate that beads and runs in slow rivulets. The air is thick with moisture. Not fog exactly, but a visible humidity, a green haze that softens edges at distances of more than a few meters. Water does not pool here, because everything absorbs it. It condenses on the underside of canopy layers above and drips — always drips — in a metronomic patter that varies in pitch depending on the depth of the chamber you stand in. In deep chambers, the drops are so spaced and the echo so long that each one sounds like a small bell, tolling the passage of minutes you cannot otherwise measure.

There are channels, though. Slow, warm, carrying dissolved tannins and sugars. They are not rivers. They are the vascular system of the place, running through capillary grooves in the woody substrate, surfaced occasionally where the canopy-layer has eroded through. They move the color of strong tea. They smell faintly of fermentation. Some of them glow, but only in the ultraviolet — the fauna can see this; you cannot, except as a faint sense that some surfaces are *wrong*, *uncanny*, subtly lit from within.

---

## The Vegetation

It is not accurate to call it vegetation anymore. It is architecture, substrate, geology, and atmosphere simultaneously. The living layer — the crown, hundreds of meters up — photosynthesizes. But it photosynthesizes in dim green filtered light, which means it has evolved to be extravagantly efficient, which means the leaf surfaces are vast, dark, almost black-green, spongy, fractal. From below, the canopy interior looks like the underside of a reef: dark folds, dangling structures, things that might be leaves or might be aerial roots or might be organs for catching condensation or might be something with no name.

Below the living crown, the dead-wood layers host other growths: shelf fungi the size of tables, their undersides breathing in slow rhythm, expanding and contracting by millimeters over the course of hours. Mosses so old they have become mats you can walk on — springy, warm, faintly pulsing with microscopic life. Lichens that have consumed the surfaces they grew on and now form a continuous glazed skin over entire walls of compressed wood, smooth and dark and slippery with moisture.

There are no separate trees. There is no ground. There is only the palimpsest: layer upon layer of growth, each one the substrate for the next, going down and down until the wood is stone, going up and up until the leaves are so high they have their own climate.

---

## The Fauna

They are abundant but you rarely see them. The density of the place — root, branch, vine, fungal shelf, compressed trunk — means life moves *through* rather than *across*. Creatures here are elongated, flattened, or soft-bodied, able to press into crevices in the woody substrate. Many are bioluminescent in spectra you cannot see, communicating in channels of light that pass through the dim green haze like signals through fog.

The sounds they make are small. Clicks. A soft wet tapping, like rain on a leaf but rhythmic, intentional. Occasionally, from deep within the woody walls, a resonance — a creature the size of your forearm vibrating its body against the compressed trunk, using the entire canopy-layer as an amplifier, sending a low hum through the structure that carries for kilometers. This is communication. The messages move slowly. There is no urgency.

Some of the fauna are sessile. They attach to the woody substrate and are overgrown, incorporated, eventually fossilized into the layers below. They do not seem to mind. Whether this is death or a different form of persistence is not clear. The boundary between organism and environment here is not a boundary at all.

There are things in the calderas that descend on vine-ropes, feeding on the new growth that fills the opening. They are large. They are the closest thing to visible wildlife the place offers: dark shapes, seen from far below, moving with exaggerated slowness against the faint green glow of an open sky they will never reach.

---

## The Built

Almost nothing. You find, occasionally, in the compressed wood-walls, structures — or rather, the *impressions* of structures: right angles, deliberate hollows, something that was once a doorway, now compressed to the size of your fist. Whoever built them did so long ago, perhaps when the canopy was lower, the layers fewer, the light brighter. The structures have been absorbed. The wood grew over them, through them, past them. They are fossils of intention in a matrix of growth.

Sometimes you find a stone, placed deliberately, now half-dissolved in tannic water but still distinct from the woody substrate around it. A marker. A boundary. A thing that said *I was here and I meant this.* The plant world did not argue with the stone. It simply grew around it, over it, incorporated it into its own slow grammar, until the meaning belonged to the place rather than to whoever set it.

---

## Atmosphere and Sensation

It is warm. Not hot — a steady, humid warmth, like the inside of a mouth, like a greenhouse at dusk, like the air above a compost pile in summer. The temperature does not change. There are no seasons here, or rather, the seasons are so slow they are indistinguishable from geology: a shift in dominant species over centuries, a slow migration of the canopy's thinnest point, a gradual warming or cooling measured in degrees per millennium.

It is dim. The light that reaches you has been filtered through so many layers of dark leaf that it is a deep green twilight, more felt than seen. Your eyes adjust, or rather, your other senses take over: the dampness on your skin, the sound of dripping, the smell of warm wet wood and fermenting sugars, the texture of the substrate under your hands.

It is quiet, mostly. The dripping. The faint clicks. The occasional deep resonance from within the walls. But the quiet is not empty — it is *full*, dense with the sound of things growing, water moving, air circulating in slow thermals through chambers and corridors in the canopy-composite. If you listen long enough, the quiet becomes a voice: the voice of a place that has been speaking to itself for so long it has forgotten there is anyone else to hear.

---

## The Rules

Nothing is separate here. The water is the plant is the stone is the air is the creature. Things do not die so much as they *become substrate*. Things do not grow so much as they *extend what is already here*. The scale is such that individual organisms are indistinguishable from ecosystems, ecosystems from geology, geology from atmosphere. If you stayed long enough — centuries, millennia — you would become part of it too. Your minerals would leach into the tannic water. Your soft tissues would feed the fungal shelves. Your bones would be overgrown, compressed, fossilized. You would become a right-angle impression in a wall of wood, a marker, a boundary, a thing that once said *I was here* — and the place, in its ancient slow way, would say *yes, and now you are here still, but differently*.

The place has been doing this for longer than anyone remembers. It will continue. It does not need you. It does not reject you. It simply grows.
