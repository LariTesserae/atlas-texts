The world is known to its inhabitants as the **Lithic Drift**, and you stand within one of its many **Cathedral Groves**.

It is a place of cool, damp stone and overwhelming, silent life. The elevation is high, but not mountainous; you stand on a recently-erupted plateau of dark, vesicular basalt, still sharp-edged and unweathered by millennia. The rock is young, and it shows. Fumaroles, no larger than parlors, dot the landscape, breathing slow, warm plumes of mineral-scented steam into the chill air. This is the primary source of water—not rivers, but a constant, whispering condensation that beads on every surface, drips from the fleshy leaves of giant flora, and collects in shallow, lukewarm pools within the basalt’s natural bowls.

The **vegetation** is lush, but it is a strange, vertical jungle. With soil scarce on the fresh rock, life has climbed upward. Immense species of clubmoss and horsetail, relics of an older age, form dense, spire-like thickets that scrape the low, perpetually overcast sky. Their fibrous trunks are woven with vines bearing fleshy, pallid flowers that open only in the dim twilight. Fungi are the true architects here; vast networks of mycelium stitch the basalt together, and shelf fungi the size of dinner tables create natural staircases and balconies. Everything is slightly damp to the touch, cool and slick.

The **sound** is profoundly quiet. The constant drip of condensation, the occasional soft sigh of steam, the faint, papery rustle of giant horsetail fronds—these are the only noises. There is no birdsong, no insect buzz. The overwhelming **fauna** is present, but it is a silent population.

And what a population. This is the domain of the **Lithic Drift’s Insectile Megafauna**. Creatures of chitin and patience fill every niche, but they move with a slow, deliberate grace that makes no sound. Armored beetles the size of bovine animals browse on fungal growths, their mandibles peeling away layers with a soft, wet tearing. Centipedes, thick as a man’s thigh and segmented in gleaming obsidian and rust, patrol the vertical cliffs of basalt, feeling for vibrations with hyper-sensitive antennae. Clouds of tiny, bioluminescent gnats drift in the steam plumes, their cold light the primary illumination in the perpetual gloom. There are no predators in the fast, violent sense; instead, there are patient ambushers. A mantis-like creature, its carapace a perfect mimic of the basalt’s texture, might remain motionless for weeks, waiting for a beetle to wander within reach of its spiked forelimbs. The ecosystem is in constant, slow-motion **dynamic**—a complex, overwhelming dance of growth, consumption, and silent, inevitable decay.

At the heart of this grove, nestled between three particularly large steam vents, is the **settlement**. It is urban in its complexity but primitive in its materials and scale, a single-feature area no larger than a rural hamlet. The people here, the **Drip-Catchers**, have built organically from the resources at hand. Their structures are not freestanding, but woven *into* the living landscape. They carve shallow dwellings into the sides of the softer basalt outcrops, lining them with compacted fungal felt for insulation. More elaborate structures are scaffolds of cured horsetail trunk and vine rope, lashed together and suspended from the massive, natural arches of rock. Walkways of woven netting connect these aerial hamlets, creating a web-like layer of habitation between the ground and the canopy.

Their **technology** is simple and masterful. They are weavers, carvers, and steam-tamers. Using sharp flakes of basalt and the hardened mandibles of deceased beetles as tools, they craft everything from clothing (woven from plant fiber and the shed silk of giant larvae) to delicate water-funnels that channel condensation into cisterns. They have learned to tame the steam vents, using clay pipes (fired in the vents themselves) to direct heat for drying food, warming dwellings, and creating a soft, echoing hum that is the closest thing the grove has to industry. Their society is layered, literally, with elders in the warmer, drier upper tiers and younger hunters tending the fungal gardens on the ground.

The **light** is always dim—a soft, grey, shadowless illumination that filters through the high canopy of giant horsetails and the ever-present mist. Noon is merely a slightly brighter shade of twilight. This, combined with the silent fauna and the damp, cool air, creates an atmosphere of deep, primeval hush. It is a **strange** and hermetic world, a bubble of intense, quiet life on a young, sharp, and cooling world. The rules here are of patience, adaptation, and silent observation. To move quickly is to risk a fall into a fungal bed or the notice of a patient watcher. To listen is to hear only the world breathing—drip, sigh, rustle. It is a cathedral built by time and chance, and its congregation is vast, silent, and ever-multiplying.
