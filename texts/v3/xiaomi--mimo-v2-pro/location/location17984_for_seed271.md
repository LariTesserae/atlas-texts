The basin is a shallow depression in ancient clay, perhaps a lake’s last breath held for ten thousand years. It is not large—you could walk its perimeter in the time it takes for the high, pale sun to move the width of two fingers held at arm’s length. The air is warm, but not brightly so; it carries the smell of damp soil and old stone, a scent that has settled here and never fully leaves.

The ground is a mosaic of desiccated clay polygons, each a foot or so across, their cracks deep and shadowed. Where the clay has given way, it reveals a substrate of smooth, water-rounded pebbles, grey and ochre and dull green, polished by currents long vanished. In the center of the basin, this gravelly bed dips into a shallow, permanent puddle. Its water is clear, still, and no deeper than a palm’s width, perfectly reflecting the uniform pale sky. It is fed not by a stream, but by the slow, seeping condensation of the dawn mists that gather in the low place each cool morning before the sun warms the air. The edges of the puddle are fringed with a thin rime of salt and mineral deposits, left behind as the water retreats by millimeters each season.

Vegetation exists only in small, stubborn congregations. A few clusters of low-growing reed with flat, blue-green blades rise from the muddier margins of the central puddle. Their roots hold tight clumps of dark, peaty soil together. Further out, upon the cracked clay, grow cushions of a succulent plant, its leaves like fat, dusty pearls, storing the intermittent moisture for long, quiet weeks. Nothing flowers. Nothing grows tall. The vegetation is a punctuation, not a sentence.

Around the basin’s rim, half-buried in the clay or nestled among the pebble beds, are the stones. They are not natural. Their shapes are too regular—some smoothed into shallow, elliptical dishes, others stacked into low, tottering cairns no higher than a knee. One, near the largest patch of reeds, is a pillar of the same local stone, worn featureless by an eternity of rain, its top scooped into a shallow bowl that catches the rare rainfall. These are not ruins. They are remnants. They have been here so long they have become part of the geology, their purpose—grinding, stacking, catching—long forgotten, their edges softened into the same grammar as the surrounding pebbles.

The light is dim, not from cloud cover, but from a constant, high haze that diffuses the sun into a sourceless glow. There are no harsh shadows, only soft gradients of light on the pale clay and stone. The sound is the sound of absence: a faint, almost sub-audible hum of stillness, interrupted only by the occasional *plink* of a single drop of condensate falling from a reed blade into the central puddle, a sound that seems to hang in the air for a moment before dissolving.

There is no animal life. No insects chirr in the sparse grasses, no birds call overhead. The only movement is the imperceptible creep of the sun’s haze-muted light across the clay polygons, and the slow, rhythmic breathing of the puddle, rising and falling with the seasons’ glacial pulse. This place is in equilibrium. The water level is stable, the clay cracks further by a hairsbreadth each century, the stones settle a grain deeper. It is a pocket of the world that has found its final, quiet state—a warm, damp, empty room, furnished only with its own ancient, settled stillness.
