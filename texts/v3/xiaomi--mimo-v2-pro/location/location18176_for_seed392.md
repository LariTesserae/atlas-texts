The light is the first and last thing you know. It is not sunlight, though it is bright—almost painfully so—and it comes from everywhere. It presses down from a sky of uniform, pearlescent glare, and it rises in a faint, wet sheen from the ground itself. There is no source, no shadow. The world is a bowl of luminous haze.

You stand on a causeway. It is made of something like fused ceramic, pale green and slightly porous, sweating a constant dew. It is one of many, a network of broad paths that rise only a hand’s breadth above the true ground. That ground is a mire—not mud, but a dense, spongy mat of pale roots and fibrous moss, perpetually saturated. Water wells up between the woven fibers, pooling in shallow, still lenses that mirror the blank sky. The air is thick and hot, carrying the scent of wet stone, warm greenery, and a faint, sweet rot, like fruit left in the rain.

The vegetation is abundant but does not grow from the mire. It grows from the structures. Everything here is built. The causeways, the low, bulbous domes that cluster like fungal colonies, the arching bridges that span sluggish, canal-like channels of clear, warm water—all are made of the same pale ceramic, or of a dense, resinous timber that sweats sap, or of a smooth, bone-like material. And from every joint, every crevice, every carved gutter, life spills.

Thick, rubbery vines with heart-shaped leaves, a deep, almost black green, crawl up the sides of domes. Out of the tops burst explosions of flora: waxy, trumpet-shaped flowers in feverish yellows and reds, their interiors slick with nectar that drips continuously onto the paths below. Feathery mosses in electric blue coat the leeward sides of walls. Fungus, too—great shelves of it, some pale as parchment, others pulsing with a faint, bioluminescent glow in the deeper alcoves. The world is a terrarium, and everything in it is cultivated, curated, *placed*. There is no wild soil. The plant life is grafted, woven, and rooted directly into the fabric of the place.

The fauna is everywhere, and it is part of the architecture. Things like oversized, iridescent beetles with crystalline wings methodically polish sections of the ceramic walls, leaving behind whorls of slick moisture. Long, sinuous creatures with too many jointed legs and smooth, eyeless heads glide through the canal-water, their bodies undulating against the glass-smooth sides. They seem less like animals and more like maintenance systems. In the air, things that resemble floating seed pods—translucent, bladder-like—drift on imperceptible currents, trailing sticky filaments that collect condensation from the bright haze. The sound is a low, continuous hum—not mechanical, but biological. The drip of water, the chitinous click of a beetle turning, the slow, wet tear of a vine pulling at its moorings. It is a quiet that is full of tiny, patient noises.

The feeling is of equilibrium, but a strange one. Nothing seems to decay, but nothing seems to grow, either. The vines are always this length. The flowers are always this open. The water always weeps from this exact crack in the causeway. It is a frozen moment of rampant growth. A jungle, captured in resin and set to hum forever.

And then the wrongness seeps in, like the damp. Look at the edge of a dome where it meets the path. The line isn't quite straight. It seems to *breathe*, subtly, swelling and receding a millimeter in a rhythm too slow to track. The geometry of a nearby arch, when you try to sketch it in your mind, refuses to hold—a right angle that seems obtuse *and* acute at the same time. The light casts no shadows, yet you feel a constant, gentle pressure from all sides, as if the very space is crowded, though the paths are empty. The roots in the mire beneath the causeway sometimes arrange themselves, for a fleeting second, into patterns that almost resemble script before softening back into a tangle.

This is a place that was made entirely by hand—primitive hands, using tools of stone and shaped wood to grow and weave this ceramic and biomass into existence. They built a perfect, stable ecosystem, a paradise in a bowl of light. And in their perfect, hand-wrought creation, they baked in a fundamental instability. The reality here is soft, impressionable. It remembers the touch of its makers, and it sometimes echoes it in ways that are not quite right. The bright, wet, hot, quiet world is a monument, and it is dreaming. It dreams it is still being shaped, and in that dream, the shapes sometimes forget how to hold.
