# The Throat

There is a place where the old shield volcano finally gave up. Not in violence — the violence ended a hundred million years ago — but in a long, slow sigh. The cone collapsed, the flanks weathered into broad patient curves, and the high plateau that remains is smooth as bone, scored with shallow gullies that haven't changed their course in epochs. Everything up there is tan and ochre and the grey-brown ofite under a sky that is always overcast, always hazing the sun to a pale smear. The rock is warm to the touch. It has been warm to the touch since before anything living had hands.

At the center of the plateau — and this is so small that you could walk past it, that a person *has* walked past it, that the first person to see it only looked down because they stumbled — there is a hole.

Not a crater. A throat.

It is perhaps forty meters across and descends into darkness at an angle steep enough that you cannot see the bottom. The walls are columnar basalt, hexagonal pillars pressed together like the cells of a hive, worn glass-smooth by uncountable years of hot wet wind breathing upward from below. The stone is dark, almost black, streaked with pale mineral deposits — calcium, lithium, something iridescent that might be vanadium sulfide — laid down in slow rings like the inside of a geode.

The wind breathes. It is continuous. It is warm and thick and smells of sulfur and green things and hot mineral, a smell that clings to the back of the throat. The air temperature at the lip of the throat is fifteen or twenty degrees warmer than the surrounding plateau, and it only gets warmer descending. The humidity is extraordinary — you can see it, a trembling column of vapor rising from the hole and dispersing into the overcast, feeding the permanent cloud layer that dims the whole plateau. There is no rain here. There are no streams. There is no surface water anywhere on the high ground. But the throat exhales moisture ceaselessly, drawn from geothermal aquifers deep in the mountain's roots, and this is enough.

## The walls

The vegetation grows on the throat's inner walls, and it is *lush* — almost obscenely so against the barren plain above. From the lip, looking down, you see a helix of green winding into the dark, following the narrow ledges and cracks between the basalt columns. The plants are almost all the same type: a dense, fleshy, dark-leafed moss-analog that grows in thick mats three or four centimeters deep, interspersed with long pale root-tendrils that hang downward into the vent like hair. There are ferns — small, tightly coiled, growing in the joints between columns. There are fungal shelves, bracket-like, fanning out from crevices in shades of ochre and dull orange. There are clusters of what might be called flowers, though they have no petals — just swollen translucent bulbs the color of amber, each one the size of a fingertip, clustered on thin stems, faintly glowing with their own weak bioluminescence.

The green is so dense it looks wet. It *is* wet. Touch the moss and water wells up between your fingers — not from the sky, not from any stream, but condensed from the rising air, pulled out of the vapor column and held. The plants have evolved for this. They are condensation-harvesters, their leaves angled and textured to channel microscopic droplets downward to their roots. They do not need rain. They do not need a river. They breathe the throat's exhalation and are fed.

Lower — and you would need ropes, or a reckless disregard for your own safety — the vegetation changes. The light from above fades quickly. The ferns give way to pale, nearly white plants with enormous surface-area leaves, thin as paper, angled to catch every photon of the dim ambient glow. Further still, the bioluminescence of the amber-bulbs becomes the primary light source, and the plants shift again: leafless, pale, almost translucent, feeding on chemical gradients rather than photosynthesis, their roots thrust deep into the mineral-rich basalt. The lowest visible levels are carpeted in a kind of living paste — a thick biofilm of cyanobacteria and sulfur-oxidizing microbes, inches deep, glowing a faint and sickly green, processing the volcanic gases that seep up through the rock.

## The settlement

Somebody lives here. *Many* somebodies.

The settlement clings to the throat's inner wall about fifteen meters below the lip, built into and onto the columnar basalt like a wasp nest grafted onto a cliff. The construction is stone — the same basalt, broken into blocks and fitted without mortar, using the natural ledges and joints of the columns as foundations. The buildings are small, dark, and dense: stacked two and three high where the ledges allow, connected by narrow stone stairways and simple rope bridges strung across gaps in the rock face. Some structures are half-cave, carved back into the softer stone between columns, their doorways dark rectangles in the black wall. Others are freestanding, huddled together so tightly their walls touch, sharing load-bearing columns, their roofs flat and used as platforms for drying moss and tending small gardens of edible ferns.

It is, by any reasonable measure, a town. Perhaps two hundred people, maybe three, living in a vertical warren that extends down about thirty meters of the throat's wall. There is a kind of central plaza — a wider-than-usual ledge, reinforced with stacked stone — where people gather. There are storage rooms cut into the rock. There are communal ovens, fueled by dried biomass and the ambient heat of the basalt itself. There are places where the stone has been smoothed and shaped into troughs for catching condensation — the only reliable source of drinking water, channeled from the moss-mats above.

The construction is primitive in method but sophisticated in form. These people have been building here for a long time. The oldest stones at the base of the settlement are worn round, their edges softened by centuries of wet wind. The newest stones, laid to repair a collapsed stairway or extend a ledge, are sharp and angular, still showing the fracture-marks of hand tools — simple basalt hammers, bone wedges, fire-and-water cracking techniques. There is no metal. There is no fired ceramic that I can see. But the stonework is precise, the load-bearing logic sound, the layout of the town clearly the product of accumulated knowledge passed down through generations.

They are quiet people, in the way that people who live in vertical spaces tend to be — sound carries strangely in the throat, echoes and distorts — but the place is *not* quiet.

## The noise

It is loud. Not cacophonous, not painful, but *loud* — a dense, layered, persistent wall of sound that fills the throat from wall to wall and rises with the vapor into the overcast sky.

The foundation of it is the wind: a constant low moan as the geothermal updraft moves through the throat's constriction, shifting pitch as eddies form and break against the stone. Over that, the wet sound of dripping — everywhere, always, condensation running down the basalt columns and pattering into the moss. The moss itself rustles when the wind gusts, a soft papery sound, and the hanging root-tendrils sway and tap against the stone with a sound like rain on a tent.

Then: the animals.

---

## The fauna

The throat is *full* of life, and most of it has wings.

Insects dominate. There are clouds of small flying things — not quite gnats, not quite moths, something with four translucent wings and a body the color of the moss, nearly invisible against the green walls. They swarm in the updraft, riding the warm air, and their collective hum is a physical presence, a vibration in the chest. There are larger insects too: beetle-analogs the size of a thumb, armored in dark chitin, crawling methodically through the moss-mats and feeding on the biofilm. There are wasp-analogs — slender, amber-bodied, nesting in the cracks between basalt columns, building paper-thin nests from chewed moss-fiber. Their buzz is higher, sharper, cutting through the general hum.

Bats. Or something like bats — small, echolocating, with elongated fingers and membranous wings, roosting in the deepest accessible crevices and emerging in chittering spirals to hawk the swarming insects. There are hundreds of them. Their calls are rapid clicking sounds, layered into a texture that sounds like static, like a river heard from far away.

Birds. A dozen species at least, all small, all adapted to the vertical: short-tailed, strong-footed, with curved beaks for prying insects from crevices. They nest on the ledges above and below the settlement, and the townspeople tolerate them — perhaps encourage them, because the birds eat the wasps, and the wasps sting. The birds sing. Not beautifully. Thin, whistling calls, competitive, territorial, constant.

Lizards. Pale, almost white, with large dark eyes adapted to the dim light. They bask on the warm basalt at the throat's lip and descend into the greenery to hunt. They are fast and silent and everywhere.

And further down, in the dark — things I can only infer from sounds that rise with the vent-wind. Chittering. A rhythmic clicking, slower and deeper than the bats. Once, what might have been a scream, long and descending, swallowed by the echo before it resolved into anything identifiable. The townspeople do not go below the third bridge. This is not a rule I was told. It is a rule I can see in the way they move, the way their eyes track downward when they stand on the lowest ledges, the way children are gathered in when they drift too close to the edge.

## The life of the place

Everything is in motion. The moss grows visibly — or nearly so — swelling and thickening in the constant humidity, then harvested by the townspeople on a rotating schedule, dried on the rooftops, used for food, fuel, fiber, insulation. The wasps build and rebuild their nests endlessly, chewing through the dried moss that was itself harvested from the living moss that fed on the wasps' exhalations. The bats emerge and return in shifts, following some rhythm of insect availability that pulses with the geothermal venting below. The birds argue. The lizards hunt. The wind breathes in and breathes out.

The town shifts with it. People are awake at all hours — there is no sharp day/night boundary here, only the slow fluctuation of the dim overcast sky above and the steady bioluminescent glow from below. Work happens in cycles keyed to the condensation patterns: when the updraft is strongest, the moss drips fastest, and the water-troughs fill, and people collect and store. When the updraft weakens — every few hours, for reasons tied to pressure changes in the deep rock — the moss dries slightly, the insects swarm closer to the walls, the bats descend, and the bird-nests become active with feeding. The townspeople fish for insects too, using sticky fibers drawn from the root-tendrils, strung on thin stone hooks. Protein.

Children play on the rope bridges. Old people sit on the warm rooftops and work the moss-fiber into cord. Someone is always hammering stone somewhere, the sharp crack carrying up and down the throat with startling clarity, cutting through the hum and the dripping and the wind.

## What it feels like

Standing at the lip, looking down into the Throat, you feel the warm breath on your face and smell the sulfur-and-green smell, and you hear the hum rising, and you see the dim green helix descending into darkness lit by amber glints, and the stone beneath your feet is warm and smooth and *old* — so old it has forgotten what it was before it was stone — and above you the sky is a flat grey lid, and around you the plateau stretches out empty and dry and tan and silent, and down there, in that small and hidden place, two hundred people are living in the wall of a hole in the world, and everything is breathing, and nothing is still.
