The city of **Salt Basin** clings to the lip of a high, young caldera, a crater so recently formed that the rock at its rim is still sharp enough to cut. Here, at nearly 13,000 feet, the air is thin but paradoxically heavy with water. This is a landscape of weeping stone and silent, mineral work.

**Terrain & Formation:** The caldera itself is barely a century old, a punch-hole in a range of ancient, rounded mountains. The interior slopes are a chaotic spill of black scoria and pumice, shattered and angular, showing no sign of wind or water smoothing. Erosion is just beginning: shallow, vertical gullies have been etched by sudden runoff, like cracks in drying clay. The basin floor, a mile across, is a flat, blinding white plain of crystallized salts and silts, perfectly level and unnervingly stable.

**Water:** Despite the altitude, water is omnipresent. Superheated springs bubble up through fissures in the caldera floor, their steam rising to meet the cold, dry air. This creates a perpetual, low-lying mist that hugs the white plain, condensing on every surface. At night, the temperature drop is dramatic. Every shard of rock, every wooden beam in the city, weeps with thick, warm droplets that collect in cisterns and irrigation channels. The sound is a constant, gentle **drip-drip-drip**, a slow percussion in the near-silence.

**Vegetation & Ecology:** Life is sparse and strategic. Tough, waxy-leaved shrubs with deep taproots cluster around the warm, mineral seeps. They bear small, hard berries but no flowers. There are no birds, no insects, no mammals. The only sounds are the wind, the dripping water, and the occasional hollow *clunk* of a settling rock. The ecology is in arrested development, waiting for soil to form and seeds to arrive on the wind.

**The City & Its People:** Salt Basin is a town of perhaps two thousand souls, built from the very materials of its birth. The structures are low, thick-walled, and made of cut blocks of the sharp, black volcanic rock, mortared with a durable clay from the basin floor. Roofs are shallowly pitched and channeled to direct the constant condensation into cisterns. There is no metal to speak of; tools are of shaped stone, obsidian, and dense, imported hardwood. The primary industry is **salt-mining and mineral collection**. The white plain is harvested for its salts, sulfates, and crystalline minerals, which are traded with distant lowland settlements for grain, cloth, and simple ceramics.

**Atmosphere & Light:** The light is normal, clear and sharp at this altitude, but the ever-present steam mutes it to a diffuse, milky glow. Shadows are soft. The air smells of wet stone, sulfur, and clean, cold ozone. The feeling is one of profound **isolation and equilibrium**. The caldera is geologically spent; it will not erupt again. The water cycle is closed, recycling through mist and seep. The people live in a stable, unchanging relationship with their environment, their primitive technology perfectly matched to the unchanging, harsh conditions. Time feels suspended here—a young, raw landscape held in a permanent, quiet state of becoming. Nothing decays quickly in the dry, sterile air, and nothing grows quickly in the thin, mineral soil. The city is a quiet archive of its own recent birth, forever mining the evidence of its violent creation.

It is a place of sharp edges and soft light, of wet silence and patient labor, suspended between the violence of its past and the stillness of its future.
