You are standing on a continent of cultivated stone.

The first thing you notice is the heat—a deep, saturated warmth that rises from the ground itself, a gentle but immense engine of transformation. The light is normal, a steady, unfiltered sun hanging in a hazy, brass-colored sky, but its heat is absorbed and slowly radiated by the land below.

The land is vast, a gently undulating plain stretching to horizons that shimmer with thermal distortion. It is almost entirely built. Yet, this is no city of metal or mortar. The "built" environment is a single, continuous, biological-mineral substrate. Imagine a substance like oolitic limestone, or the fused skeleton of a coral reef the size of a subcontinent, but with a slow, metabolic purpose. The ground is made of interlocking, vaguely hexagonal plates of a porous, calcium-like material, ranging in color from bleached ivory to a deep, rusty ochre. It is not rock grown over, but rock *grown*. This is the world's only technology: a cultivated, planetary-scale lithic biogenesis, now dormant and complete in its foundational phase.

Erosion is emerging, embryonic. The plates are sharp-edged where they meet, their patterns still crisp. But here and there, in the gentle depressions and channels between the plates, the first stages of dissolution are visible. Condensation, the primary source of the dampness, collects in these micro-valleys at night. It is not rain so much as a pervasive, nightly weeping of the atmosphere onto the warm stone. This moisture, slightly acidic from atmospheric gases, is etching the first hairline fractures, dissolving the faintest bevels into the plate edges. A continental sculpture garden, just beginning to feel the chisel of time.

Water exists almost solely as this transient moisture. There are no rivers, no lakes. Instead, in the larger concavities of the terrain, the nightly condensation pools for a few dawn hours—shallow, warm, milky with dissolved minerals—before the rising sun drinks it back into the air. These ephemeral tarns are the only interruption in the vast, sparse landscape.

Vegetation is sparse, clinging to life in these temporary wetlands. Not grasses or trees, but extremophile mats—a rubbery, dark green-black biofilm that spreads over the damp plates, and low, bulbous structures like lithops or stone plants, their fleshy bodies storing the mineral-rich water. They are more geology than botany, their growth imperceptibly slow, their colors blending with the ochre and ivory of the substrate. They are the only soft things in a world of hard, warm angles.

There is no animal life. No birds, no insects, no scurrying things. The dynamism is entirely abiotic: the cycle of condensation and evaporation, the slow chemical dance of erosion, the shifting patterns of heat haze, the rare, startling *tink* of a plate fracturing from thermal stress deep within the crust—a sound that echoes in the immense silence. The silence is not empty; it is a low, physical hum. It is the sound of heat expanding matter, of faint breezes sighing across millions of porous plates, creating a continent-sized, Aeolian whisper just at the edge of hearing.

The rules of this world are thermal and metabolic. Everything is process. The built foundation is not inert; it is the stabilized corpse of an ancient, world-engineering biology, now subject to the slower, grander cycles of physics and chemistry. You are walking on a completed organism, feeling its fading warmth, watching the first patient agents of entropy begin their work on a canvas of continental scale. It is not a place of events, but of states—a vast, silent, hot cathedral in the first moment after the final stone was set, just as the first mote of dust begins to settle.
