The world is called **Aberros**, and it is a place of drowning peaks. The mountains are newborns, still slick with the trauma of their own emergence. Erosion has not had time to sculpt them; they are raw, jagged, and violent in their ascent from a planetary sea that covers 90% of the globe. You stand on what is either the highest point of a continent or the crown of an immense, sunken volcano. The air is thick, warm, and carries the constant, humid pressure of being at the bottom of an atmospheric ocean. The light is dim, a perpetual bruised twilight, filtered through leagues of water-vapor haze and the slow, chemical luminescence of the deep sea that remembers being sky.

The terrain is a sculpture of contradiction. **Water** is everywhere, not as a feature but as the fundamental substance. It beads on every surface, flows in thin, shimmering films down sheer cliff faces, pools in the concavities of shattered rock, and hangs as a suspended, glittering fog. The sound is mostly the hush of this water—drips, trickles, the deep, subsonic *groan* of the mountain settling under its own impossible weight. Occasionally, a distant, whale-like call echoes through the stone, a mystery of the deep that has followed the peaks into the air.

**Vegetation** clings in lush, desperate profusion. It is not green. Here, life learned its colors from volcanic glass and abyssal coral. Great fans of **kelp-fern** unfurl in metallic bronze and deep burgundy, their fronds structured to channel the constant condensation into their root systems. **Stonebloom mosses** create living, pulpy veils over the rocks in shades of deep violet and phosphorescent blue, their glow the primary source of light in ravines. The largest organisms are **pillar-coral trees**, calcareous, branching structures that grow upward like slow-motion geysers, sometimes fused into the raw stone, sometimes forming bridges across chasms. Their "leaves" are feathery, filter-feeding plumes that sway in the thick, still air. This is a world where the line between animal and plant, between geology and biology, is softly blurred.

The **built** environment emerges from this. Settlements are not *on* the mountain, but *of* it. Dwellings are excavated from the softer, porous coral-stone, their walls smooth and cool. Bridges and walkways are woven from the living, rubbery vines of the **bind-weed**, which is encouraged to grow into specific shapes, its growth-patterns forming a primitive, biographical script that only the tenders can read. The **technology** is one of cultivation and patient shaping: tools of hardened basalt and shell, containers of woven kelp-fiber, the use of controlled fungal growths to decompose rock and create new chambers. There is no metal. The "urban" density comes from this hive-like burrowing into the living rock, creating warrens of chambers lit by jars of glowing moss, connected by corridors that smell of damp earth, salt, and slow combustion of fatty, oil-rich seeds.

**Fauna** is rare, but significant. The dominant lifeforms are the **Silent Ones**: translucent, jellyfish-like creatures the size of horses that drift through the mist-filled air, feeding on airborne spores and the subtle electrical gradients of the mountain. They are rarely seen, but their passage is marked by a soft, purplish light and a shift in the air pressure. Smaller, crab-like creatures with shells of iridescent nacre scuttle in the wet places, tending to the mosses and fungi. The true weirdness, however, is in the **rules**. Perspective plays tricks. Looking down a ravine, the mist can suddenly feel like looking up into a boundless sky. Sound carries strangely; a whisper can travel for miles, while a shout is swallowed by the thick air. The mountain itself seems to *breathe* in a slow, tidal cycle—the constant seepage of water increases and decreases in a rhythm that takes days to complete.

This is a world in its **slow**, embryonic phase. Change is measured in the creep of a coral-tree's growth, the inching journey of a vine across a cliff face, the patient accumulation of silt that will, in millennia, create new land. The dynamic is one of quiet, saturated becoming. The mountain is still rising, millimeter by millimeter, from the global sea. The people who live here do not fight the water; they are aquaculturalists of the air, their existence a testament to patience. They listen to the groans of the stone, chart the drift of the Silent Ones, and carve their futures into the soft, wet bones of a world still deciding what it will be.
