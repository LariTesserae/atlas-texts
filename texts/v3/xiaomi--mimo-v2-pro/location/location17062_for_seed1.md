The first thing you notice is the sound. It isn’t just loud; it is a layered, physical pressure. A deep, continent-sized groan vibrates up through the soles of your feet—the sound of rock under immense, slow stress. Overlaid on this is a constant, wet *hiss*, like a billion tiny pressure valves releasing steam, and a higher, chittering *clatter*, sharp and abundant, like the conversation of gravel in a running stream. The air is saturated with noise.

This is the Gravel Marches. A vast, low-lying plain that seems to extend forever into a dim, pewter-colored haze. The ground is not soil, but a dense, packed mosaic of shattered stone—slate, basalt, granite, quartz—fractured into chips, shards, and rounded cobbles by an eternity of erosion. There is no empty space. Every square inch is occupied by this mineral carpet, ranging in size from fine, gritty sand to boulders the size of hovels, all locked together in a tight, tessellated fit. It is ancient, worn-down, and yet impossibly full.

And it is wet. A perpetual, freezing mist hangs knee-high, condensing on every surface. Water doesn't pool; it *clings*. Every shard glistens with a slick, oily sheen. It weeps from the faces of larger stones, running in slow, viscous rivulets that carve temporary channels before being re-absorbed or frozen into fragile, crystalline webs in the crevices. The cold is profound, a static, energy-sapping chill that has settled into the world's bones.

Nothing grows here. There is no plant life, not even the hardiest lichen. Yet, the place is far from dead.

This is a world of mineral fauna. Life here is not biological, but crystalline and mechanistic. The "creatures" are the industry itself. From the gravel, formations rise—slow, grinding *Tectonic Beetles* with shells of layered schist, moving inches per century, their passage a seismic groan. Faster, more abundant are the *Clatter-Swarms*: loose congregations of magnetized iron pyrite and sharp flint that move in skittering clouds, their collective vibration producing that constant, sharp chorus. They feed on the ambient electrical charge of the mist, their metallic bodies sparking faintly in the gloom.

The "industry" is the landscape's metabolism. Geothermal vents, not of hot steam, but of pressurized, mineral-rich brine, punctuate the plain. They erupt in slow, silent gouts, depositing new layers of silicate and salt that are immediately colonized by new clatter-swarms. In the distance, what looks like a mountain range is actually the colossal, stationary form of a *Rust-Refinery*, a miles-long ridge of oxidizing iron that bleeds ochre fluid into the mist, its flanks alive with specialized, cleaning organisms that scrape and process the corrosion. There are no buildings, no artifacts. This is a natural—or perhaps, *unnatural*—ecosystem that happens to operate on the principles of engine, furnace, and assembly line.

The light is dim, sourceless, as if the mist itself absorbs and re-emits a feeble luminescence. Shadows are deep and grey, and in the middle distance, reality seems to fray. Here, the high weirdness asserts itself. Space feels saturated, packed, yet somehow *folded*. A grove of crystalline spires, sharp and clear a moment ago, might suddenly look like a smudged painting. The sound of a nearby clatter-swarm might echo from the wrong direction, or arrive a full second before you see them. Perspective plays tricks; a close boulder might seem vast, while a distant rust-refinery feels intimately near. It is as if the ancient, eroded, saturated nature of this place has worn thin the very fabric of local reality, creating pockets where perception and geometry are unstable.

The dynamic energy is low but relentless. It is the pace of tectonic shift, of frost-heave, of crystalline growth. Processes are in motion, but they are measured in days for the swarms, and in millennia for the beetles and the refineries. Weather is not storm or sun, but shifts in the density of the mist, changes in the pitch of the groaning rock, and the slow, migratory redistribution of the gravel itself.

To stand here is to be inside a living, breathing, industrial geode. A cold, wet, deafening, and ancient engine of stone and water, where the line between the mineral, the mechanical, and the living has not just blurred—it has been ground to dust and paved over.
