The place is called the **Gypsum Needle**, and it is a single, immense formation of crystalline gypsum—selenite—that has thrust upward from the dry heart of a continental salt flat. It is not a mountain range, but a singular spire, a jagged, translucent fang rising from the blinding white plain. From a distance, it looks like a sliver of frozen lightning. Up close, it is a cathedral.

**Terrain & Materials:** The base of the Needle is a scree of sharp, milky crystals, young and unweathered, crunching underfoot like broken glass. The ground is not soil, but compacted salt and anhydrite, hard and pale. The spire itself is not solid, but a labyrinth of internal fissures and channels, where light refracts into cold rainbows on the walls. There is no standing water anywhere. The moisture that might have existed in this geology was chemically bound or evaporated millennia ago. The lushness here is not of water, but of life that has learned to live without it.

**Vegetation & Ecology:** Life clings to the spire in dense, vertical mats. This is a **lithophytic jungle**—a community of rock-eating flora. Great, layered fans of **dry-rot fungus** spread across the gypsum, their mycelia drawing minerals directly from the crystal, their fruiting bodies tough and parchment-like. Between them hang curtains of **xerophytic moss**, evolved to pull trace humidity from the thin, warm air that circulates through the Needle’s hollows. Their color is a deep, muted green, almost black in the shadows. Lichen, in scabrous silver and burnt-orange, paints every surface.

**Fauna:** Abundant, but silent. The fauna is overwhelmingly arthropodal. **Glasswing moths**, with scales as clear as the selenite, drift through the air currents, feeding on the mineral-rich spores of the fungi. **Salt-scorpions**, pale and elongated, hunt in the crevices, their carapaces translucent. Higher up, near the spire’s blunted peak, colonies of **vein-bats** roost in crystal cavities, navigating by echolocation too high for human ears to perceive—the source of the profound, oppressive silence. There are no birds, no large mammals. The food web is closed, internal, and patient.

**Settlement & Built Environment:** The spire is not just inhabited; it is *modified*. A civilization, now long-absent or perhaps never sapient in a way we'd recognize, has shaped the Needle. The "urban" element is a **carved infrastructure**. Tunnels have been bored, not with tools, but by guiding the natural cleavage planes of the crystal. Rooms and halls emerge where big crystals have been pried away, leaving geometric voids. Bridges of fused selenite shards span internal chasms. It is organized, dense with purposeful alteration, yet shows no sign of metal, fire, or machinery. The constructions feel more like the work of an industrious insect colony than a city, yet on a grand, architectural scale. It is a metropolis grown, not built.

**Atmosphere & Light:** The air is warm and still, thick with mineral dust motes that hang in the shafts of normal, unfiltered sunlight. The light inside the Needle is extraordinary—it filters through the translucent walls, fills every chamber with a diffused, luminous glow, like being inside a lantern. Shadows are soft and rare. The silence is total, broken only by the faint, occasional *tink* of a crystal contracting in the heat, or the papery rustle of a fungus curtain shifting.

**Rules & Weirdness:** The weirdness is subtle, structural. Things are *too* ordered. The crystal lattices follow patterns that seem almost mnemonic. The fungal growths sometimes form perfect, repeating spirals that are too large to be accidental. There is a sense of **slow, geological intention**. The place feels not young in its formation, but young in its *revelation*—as if it was recently uncovered, exposed to the sky for the first time. Change is glacially slow; a fungal mat may advance an inch per year. The dynamic is one of imperceptible growth and silent, crystalline adjustment.

To stand in the central nave of the Gypsum Needle is to be inside a living mineral. You are surrounded by the soft luminescence of rock, the dense, quiet press of parasitic life, and the ghost of an architecture that whispers of patience and pressure. It is a silent, warm, dry, and utterly alien greenhouse, holding its breath under a sun that never sets on its stillness.
