The first thing you understand is the cold. It is a dry, needling cold that works through layers, a cold that has had millennia to perfect its technique. You are on a plateau, high enough that the sky is a deeper, thinner blue, and the sun is a bright, distant disk that offers light but little warmth. The air is thin enough that your breath comes in deliberate pulls, each exhalation a sudden, fleeting cloud of vapor that vanishes into the immense emptiness around you.

This is a young world, or at least a young part of it. The rock underfoot is raw, almost angry. Great slabs of granite and basalt jut from the ground at sharp angles, their surfaces unweathered, unsmoothed. There is no soil, only grit and scree between the larger formations. The erosion parameter reads as "emerging," and you see it everywhere: this is a landscape in the first, violent stages of being born. Fissures split the rock not with the gentle curves of water-carved valleys, but with the sharp, sudden violence of tectonic rupture. The ground itself feels like it might still be cooling.

And yet, there is water. The parameter is high, but it doesn’t feel like a marsh. It is a *presence*, a constant. It weeps from the sheer cliff faces in glittering, slow-motion trails that freeze into intricate veils of ice halfway down their descent. It condenses on the cold rock at night, leaving dark, slick patches that glitter come morning. You hear it, too: a deep, subterranean gurgle, the sound of vast aquifers shifting far below. The air holds a metallic, mineral tang, the scent of wet stone and ozone.

But the true contradiction, the element that stitches this impossible place into a coherent, functioning system, is the industry. And it is everywhere, in a way that feels less like an invasion and more like a symbiotic organism. Not buildings, no. The parameter said "none" for built structures, and it’s true—there are no houses, no shanties, no foundations. The industry is *of* the place. Colossal, rust-red mechanisms are fused to the cliff faces, their riveted hulls humming with a low, visceral vibration. They are drilling rigs, but of a strange, archaic design, their derricks like skeletal fingers probing the wounds in the earth. Their purpose is clear: the geothermal veins. They tap the deep, hot water, the very source of the weeping cliffs.

Pipes, thick as ancient trees and sweating with condensation, snake across the plateau, anchored to the rock by great iron stakes. They do not carry oil, but superheated, mineral-rich water and steam. The air around them shimmers. The sound is a constant, low-frequency thrum—a factory’s heartbeat, but with no walls, no roof. Just the machine, exposed and elemental, powered by the planet’s own heat against the frigid air.

This is where the life comes in. The fauna is abundant, but it is not a life you recognize. Great, shaggy creatures with reflective coats, like bison crossed with mountain goats, roam in small, skittish herds. They lick the mineral crusts from the warm pipes, their breath steaming around the hot metal. In the air, things that look like manta rays made of slate and iron glide on the thermal updrafts from the vents, their undersides glowing with a faint, biological luminescence. They are not birds, but something older, something that has learned to feed on the heat and the condensation. Smaller, six-legged things with iridescent carapaces scuttle between rocks, harvesting algal mats that grow in the warm, wet microclimates around leaky valves.

The emptiness, the vast space between presences, is real. The scale is regional, but the density is near-void. You can see for miles across this shattered plateau, and the only signs of movement are the distant herds, the gliding sky-filters, and the slow, purposeful pump of the rigs. There is no clutter. It is an ecosystem of stark, functional relationships.

Light is dim, not from cloud cover, but from the angle of the sun and the sheer, light-absorbing blackness of the basalt. Shadows are long and sharp. The dynamic activity is the eternal, slow-motion ballet: the tectonic grind that lifts the plateau, the weeping water that seeks to wear it down, the industrial probes that tap its energy, and the hardy, strange life that has adapted to the seams between all these forces. It is mostly quiet, the silence broken by the mournful whistle of wind over a vent, the distant clang of a pressure valve releasing, and the low, constant growl of the world being both built and bled, here at its cold, raw, mechanical heart.
