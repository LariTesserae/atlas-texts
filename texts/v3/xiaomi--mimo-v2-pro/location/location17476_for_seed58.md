# The Cauldron Towns

## Entry

You descend through a throat of stacked iron grating that serves as both stairwell and ventilation shaft. The air thickens with each turn — not merely warm but *radiant*, soaking into the walls themselves, which are cut basalt so young that the chisel marks still glitter with fresh mineral fracture. Water beads on every surface. Not flowing water — condensation, the weeping of a cool upper world meeting a hot lower one, gathering in fat drops that fall with irregular, echoing plinks into channels carved along the edges of every walkway. These channels feed downward into cisterns. The town drinks from its own sweat.

## The Chambers

At the bottom of the grate-stair you emerge into the first of a series of connected chambers — perhaps six or seven, no two the same shape, all roughly cathedral-sized but not cathedral-tall. The ceilings press low, fifteen or eighteen feet at most, giving every space the feeling of a room rather than a cavern. They were not carved for grandeur. They were carved for *use*, and the stone was removed as fast as possible, leaving irregular walls with a brutal, almost contemptuous geometry — flat where the drill-bit walked, scalloped where it stopped, fractured where the rock simply gave way and the miners said *good enough*.

The chambers connect through short, narrow passages. You must duck. The stone around each passage opening has been worn smoother than the surrounding walls — not by water but by shoulders, by the passage of human bodies over what might be months or might be decades. It is impossible to tell. The basalt does not record time the way softer stone would. It holds its edges. The erosion here is geological youth: nothing has had time to round anything. Every corner is a knife. The only softness is where human contact has intervened.

## Construction

The settlement is built *into* and *onto* the chambers in dense layers. Platforms of welded steel plate are bolted directly into the basalt, creating second levels — lofts, storage decks, sleeping berths stacked three high with only enough clearance to roll into them. Pipe-work runs along every ceiling: copper, iron, a few lengths of ceramic. Some carry water. Some carry something hotter. The pipes radiate warmth you can feel on your upturned palm, and where they join or elbow, the fittings are wrapped in rag insulation that has gone dark with moisture.

There are no doors. Fabric partitions — heavy canvas, oilskin, layered burlap — divide living spaces from working spaces from nothing-spaces. They hang from rails bolted into the ceiling and can be drawn or pushed aside. The effect is of a town that is also a single continuous interior, a place with rooms but no private architecture. You sense the density not through crowding — there are not enough people for true crowding — but through *accumulation*. Every surface is used. Hooks hold tools. Shelves hold jars. Ropes hold bundles. The walls themselves are studded with driven iron pins, each one bearing something: a lamp, a coil of wire, a drying rag, a small shrine to nothing identifiable.

## Industry

The deeper chambers are the active ones. You hear them before you see them — not loud, but persistent, a rhythmic mechanical coughing that resolves, as you approach, into piston-driven pumps. They are moving water. Or trying to. The pumps sit in shallow basins cut into the chamber floors, and the water they move is warm, faintly mineralized, tinged with something that makes it slightly viscous. It sheens the stone with a pale residue. The pumps are old but maintained: newer bolts in older housings, replacement gaskets of vulcanized rubber wedged into fittings designed for leather. They run on compressed air piped from somewhere higher up — you can hear the hiss of it leaking from imperfect joints, a sound so constant it has become silence.

Beyond the pump chamber, a larger space serves as a workshop. Metal lathes. A forge that burns some pressurized fuel, probably gas drawn from deeper strata, producing a flame so hot it is nearly invisible — just a ripple in the air above a nozzle, and the intense blue-white pinpoint where it meets steel. The forge heats the whole chamber to a temperature that borders on medical. Workers — if that is the right word; *occupants* might be better — move slowly, deliberately, pausing often. They are making parts. Replacement parts for the pumps, the grating-stairs, the pipe fittings, the bolt-holds that keep the platforms from sagging. This is not a town that exports. It maintains itself. Every act of production feeds back into the infrastructure of continued existence.

## Absence

No animals. None. Not even insects, which is wrong for a damp environment, which tells you something about the air or the water or the heat — something hostile to anything without hands and a reason to be here. There is vegetation, but barely: in the cistern chamber, where the collected condensation pools in shallow stone basins, a species of pale mushroom grows in clusters so uniform they look cultivated. They may be. They are the only living thing other than the people, and they are eaten, and they are not enough to call an ecology.

The light comes from lamps — oil, burning with a yellowish, sulfurous cast that makes the basalt walls look bruised. Some chambers have more lamps than others. The deep workshop is bright by necessity. The sleeping berths are dark. The passages between are navigated by memory and by the residual glow from whichever chamber you have just left. There is no system. Light is a resource allocated by urgency.

## Atmosphere

The quiet here is not silence. It is the *envelope* of sound that replaces silence when you are underground and surrounded by stone: the drip of condensation, the pump's arrhythmic cough, the muffled clank of metal on metal from the workshop, the almost sub-audible groan of thermal expansion in the rock itself. These sounds layer into a texture. You stop hearing them as individual events. They become the ground. Against this ground, voices carry strangely — the low ceilings bounce them forward, compress them, so that a conversation in the next chamber arrives as a murmur with edges, words impossible to parse but tone absolutely clear.

The heat is inescapable. It comes from below, from the rock itself, from whatever geothermal gradient drives this place's existence. There is no cooling strategy except wet rags on the neck and slow movement and the knowledge that the upper chambers are slightly less brutal than the lower ones. Sweat does not evaporate. The air is too saturated. You remain wet. The condensation on the walls and the condensation on your skin are the same process, the same thermodynamic negotiation, and after a while the boundary between your body and the town's body — this sweating, breathing, dripping architecture — feels thin.

## The Rules

You do not go deeper than the workshop. This is understood. The deepest passage is gated with a lattice of iron bar that could be opened but is not. Behind it, the air is hotter still, and there is a sound that might be water moving in volume or might be something else — a resonance in the stone, a pressure, a venting. No one mentions it. The gate is not locked. It is simply closed, and the understanding that it stays closed is as structural as the bolt-holds in the basalt.

You maintain the pumps. If the pumps stop, the cisterns go stagnant, and stagnant water in this heat becomes something other than water. You do not test this. The rhythm of the pump-cough is the rhythm of the town's continued permission to exist.

You do not waste the mushrooms. They grow slowly. They are the only thing that grows.

You do not complain about the heat. This is not a rule spoken aloud. It is a rule enacted by the fact that no one does, and the fact that to do so would be to name something that cannot be changed, which in a place this small and this dense and this close to the bone of the world would be a kind of fracture that the stone could not afford.
