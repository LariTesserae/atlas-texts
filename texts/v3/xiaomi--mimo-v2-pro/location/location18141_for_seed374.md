The first thing that hits is the sound. It’s not a noise, but a pressure—a relentless, grinding harmony of a million chisels on stone, a billion tiny abrasions, layered into a roaring, static-filled wall. It’s the sound of the world being made, and it never, ever stops.

You stand on a plain of low, sweating mounds. The air is thick, warm, and heavy with moisture that doesn’t fall as rain but simply condenses, coating every surface in a slick, mineral-rich sheen. Droplets weep from the undersides of every jagged overhang and gather in shallow, lukewarm pools between the ridges. This is the water: not a river or a sea, but a constant, pervasive seepage, a world perpetually on the edge of saturation.

But this is no soft, earthen marsh. The terrain is raw, unfinished geology. The mounds are not hills, but nascent formations—embryonic tors, half-extruded pillars, and sloping aprons of freshly fractured basalt and glistening obsidian. Erosion has barely begun its work. The shapes are brutal, sharp-edged, and provisional, like a sculptor’s first rough knocks from the quarry face. They rise from a low-lying basin, offering a regional vista of similar, densely packed stumps and plateaus, all under the same dim, diffuse light.

Upon this raw mineral canvas, the "urban" life unfolds. But these are not buildings of brick and beam. They are **constructs**.

The constructors are the abundant fauna: swarms of chitinous, multi-limbed creatures the size of a human forearm. They have mandibles like carbide-tipped files and limbs ending in rasping, scoop-like appendages. They are everywhere, a living, scuttling carpet across the stone. They do not eat the rock; they *process* it. With frantic, deafening industry, they scrape, grind, and harvest the minerals, secreting a binding agent from thoracic glands. They shape this amalgam into the structures that define this world.

The constructs rise in dense, layered warrens. They are not planned cities, but grown ones—a coral reef of stone. Tubes, domes, vaulted corridors, and leaning spires are fused together in a dizzying, overlapping complexity. The walls are thin, porous, and veined with the weeping moisture. The architecture is purely functional: breeding chambers, moisture-collecting cisterns, lookout spires, and vast, vaulted halls where the ceaseless grinding sound is somehow amplified into a physical vibration. The constructors live inside these creations, tending them, expanding them, repairing the constant micro-fractures from their own activity.

There is no plant life. There is no soil. The only biology recognized is animal and mineral. The energy source seems to be geothermal—the warmth of the planet's nascent crust—and the chemical bounty of the sweating rocks themselves. The constructors' life cycle is intertwined with their construction; their eggs are laid in mineral-rich, moist niches deep within the warrens.

The world is static in its dynamism. The scale is open; you can see dozens of these growing warrens from any high point, their spires and tubes clustering the basin floor. The equilibrium is not one of peace, but of constant, balanced production. For every spire erected, a nearby mound is scraped down to its foundation. The light is dim, filtered through a perpetual haze of rock dust kicked up by the industry, giving the air a soft, granular quality.

To be here is to be inside a living, breathing quarry. The smell is of wet stone, ozone, and the faint, acrid tang of the constructor's secretion. The deafening sound is the voice of the world—a lullaby of creation, a static hymn of a planet building itself, forever, in the warm, wet dark.
