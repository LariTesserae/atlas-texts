You are in a drowned city.

The first thing is the sound. It is not the sound of water, though water is everywhere—pressurized, warm, and dense as syrup. It is the sound of *industry*, a grinding, chattering, hydraulic shriek that travels through the liquid medium with brutal efficiency. It is the sound of a million joints articulating, of pistons firing in the dark, of vast gears meshing somewhere in the crushing beyond. It is deafening not in volume but in vibration; you feel it in your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes, in the resonant cavities of your bones. It is the sound of a place that has never known silence.

You are not swimming. You are standing, or rather, your boots find purchase on a gritty, ferrous floor. The water—a warm, mineral-heavy brine that tastes of copper and salt—supports you, its density making movement a slow, resistant push. The pressure is a constant, firm embrace. Above, below, and on all sides: dimness. Light here is a rare, localized phenomenon. A sickly yellow-green glow emanates from clusters of bioluminescent fungus clinging to superstructures, casting long, wavering shadows. Occasional flares of industrial light—sparks from a distant weld, the hot orange throat of a submerged furnace—paint strobing tableaux against the rust and silt.

This is a place *built*, not grown. The vegetation parameter is almost zero, but life is here, in simpler, stranger forms. The fungus is the primary "flora," forming pulsating, veined mats on every surface. Rarer are the animals: blind, eel-like creatures with bioluminescent lures that drift through the currents like lost thoughts; armored crustaceans that scuttle in the gutters, feeding on bacterial sludge and metallic runoff. They are sparse, navigating a world not designed for them.

The city itself is a maze of industrial plumbing and habitation blocks, all corroded, pitted, and settled into a state of grim permanence. It is a vertical warren of catwalks, pressure-sealed ducts, and cylindrical chambers, layered and interconnected. The architecture is functional, brutal, and utterly saturated. Pipes as thick as ancient trees run in parallel lines, weeping mineral deposits. Great turbines, their blades slowly turning in the current, are embedded in the walls like fossilized giants. This is an urban space, but one built for function, not comfort—a factory-complex that became a town that became a tomb that somehow, against all odds, *persists*.

The turbulence is relentless. This is not a still, abyssal plain. Powerful, chaotic currents surge through the corridors, stirred by geothermal vents, the operations of the city's own vast engines, and perhaps the gravitational pull of a troubled sea above. Silt storms boil up without warning, reducing visibility to inches. Structures groan and shift. New channels are carved by erosion over centuries; old ones collapse. The city is in a constant state of slow-motion demolition and accidental renovation. It is a system in violent equilibrium, a bazaar of decay and relentless mechanical function.

The rules of this world are written in pressure and corrosion. Things that are weak, soft, or temporary are crushed or dissolved. Only the dense, the armored, the permanently anchored survive. Energy comes not from a sun, but from the planet's own heat, tapped by geothermal vents and converted by thunderous, unseen engines into the electricity that flickers in dying grids and powers the ceaseless machinery. The few inhabitants—if any remain—are likely as slow, dense, and patient as the crustaceans, moving through the warm, deafening dark with the current, tending to systems they may no longer understand, in a city that is less a home and more a perpetually failing machine they are symbiotically entwined with.

It is a pocket of furious activity embedded within the crushing void of the deep. A hidden, roaring organ in the body of a drowned world.
