# The Spire Tundra

## The Plants That Hold the Sky

The first thing you would notice, if you could see, is that the world is not dark in the way a cave is dark. It is dark in the way a winter afternoon never quite brightens — a perpetual bruised twilight that never commits to night or day. The sun is a rumor, a faint warm smudge behind permanent overcast, if it is a sun at all and not some other body entirely. The light that reaches the surface is a gray residue, enough to distinguish shapes at close range, not enough to read by, not enough to cast a shadow.

The second thing you would notice is the sound.

But the plants come first. They must, because they are everywhere. They are the substrate of this world, the material from which nearly everything else is derived, and yet they are almost nothing *like* plants as you might know them. Imagine a boreal forest distilled to its loneliest elements: not a forest at all, but a scattering. A *sparsity* of colossi.

They rise from the high plateau at irregular intervals — singly, or in clusters of two or three — and they are enormous. Each trunk is a pillar twenty to forty meters in diameter, tapering slightly as it climbs, barkless, with a skin like fused cartilage, pale gray-brown, slightly translucent in places, so that in the dim light you can almost see the slow movement of fluid inside. Some reach four hundred meters. Some reach higher. Their canopies are not canopies in any leafy sense; they are *structures*, vast latticed fans of woody tissue that spread laterally from the crown, branching and rebranching into networks so fine they resemble frozen nervous systems or river deltas seen from great height. These lattices catch condensation. They drip.

The spacing is what makes the landscape feel haunted. Two spires might stand a kilometer apart. Between them: nothing. Or nearly nothing — a floor of coldite, thatiteiteiteiteiteiteite fine-grainedite powderedite rockiteite — cold,ite hard,ite sharp underfoot where young ridges of stone break through. The geology here is *new*. The plateau is still being born. You can feel it in the way the ground is not quite settled, the way certain ridges have edges that haven't been worn soft, the way scree fields are made of angular fragments with surfaces so fresh they are almost glossy. Erosion hasn't had time to round anything. The mountains behind the plateau — and there are mountains, dark saw-blade silhouettes visible against the marginally lighter sky — are sharp as broken glass.

So the plants fill the world. Vegetation accounts for nearly all of the living mass. And yet the spaces between them are vast, and hollow, and the density of presence per cubic kilometer is almost nothing. You walk between spires and you are *alone* in a way that feels geological. The emptiness has weight.

## Moisture and Cold

The cold is not the dry cold of a frozen desert. It is *damp* cold, the cold of a world where moisture is always present but never abundant. Condensation forms on everything. The latticed canopies of the spires collect it and release it in slow, irregular rain — not rain from clouds, but a secondary rain, an under-rain, falling from hundreds of meters up in fat cold drops that land with a sound like slow applause on the stone below. Pools form in the hollows between rock ridges. They are shallow and still and so cold their surfaces develop a skin of ice-slush that cracks and reforms as the slow rhythm of dripping continues. Some of these pools drain into fissures in the young rock and disappear. Some freeze entirely and persist as flat white eyes staring up at the overcast sky.

The air itself is thick with suspended moisture — not fog exactly, but a *heaviness* in the atmosphere, a wet weight that makes breathing feel deliberate. On the skin it settles as a film, not wet enough to drip, cold enough to ache. Ice crystals form on the upper surfaces of things — on the latticed canopies, on the pale cartilage-bark of the spire trunks, on the angular edges of exposed stone — and they persist because the temperature rarely rises enough to melt them. The spires are crowned with ice. They glitter faintly in the near-dark, catching whatever residual light exists and refracting it into tiny cold sparks.

There are rivers, of a sort. But they are not visible rivers. They run *under* the stone, through the young fissures and cracks of the new geology, and you hear them before you encounter them — a sub-bass vibration in the ground, a feeling in the bones of your feet. Where the stone opens wide enough, you can look down and see dark water moving fast over smooth rock, cold and clear and utterly black. The plants send root-tendrils into these crevices. You can see them sometimes, pale and muscular, gripping the rock walls of the fissures like fingers curling around the edge of a table.

## The Sound

Now: the sound.

It is *loud*. This world is louder than any landscape has a right to be, and the sound comes from the plants.

The spires *sing*.

It begins with the cold. As temperature fluctuates — and it does fluctuate, driven by cycles that may be diurnal or tidal or something else entirely — the enormous trunks of the spires contract and expand. The cartilage-skin creaks. The latticed canopies flex and groan. Deep within the trunks, resonance chambers — hollow cavities in the woody tissue, some large enough to stand in — amplify the vibrations of the structure and emit them as low, sustained tones. Not a single tone. A *chord*. Each spire has a different timbre, a different pitch, determined by its size, its age, the shape of its internal cavities, the moisture content of its tissues. When the wind rises — and the wind *does* rise, because the scale of this plateau is continental enough to generate its own weather — the sound becomes extraordinary. The latticed canopies catch the air and vibrate like the strings of some impossible instrument. The resonance chambers howl. The whole landscape hums and throbs and drones at frequencies you feel in your chest and your teeth and the fluid of your inner ear.

And the animals add to it.

## The Fauna

They are abundant, and they are loud, and they live in the dark.

The most visible — if "visible" means anything in this light — are the *gliders*. They are broad, flat-bodied creatures, roughly the size of a large eagle but shaped more like a manta ray, with a membrane stretched between elongated limb-bones. They launch from the latticed canopies of the spires and ride the sound — *ride the sound*, not the wind, or not only the wind. Their membranes are taut and thin and they resonate with specific frequencies. A glider adjusts the tension of its membrane to couple with the tone of a nearby spire, and the vibration provides lift, or at least stability, allowing it to glide long distances between the spires with almost no effort. They call to each other in sharp, high clicks that cut through the low drone of the plants like needles through cloth. You hear them constantly — a staccato layer of percussion above the sustained bass of the spires.

There are burrowers, too. Things that move through the root-networks of the spires, feeding on the moist tissues, leaving tunnels that collapse slowly behind them. You hear them as a scratching, a gnawing, a faint wet grinding from beneath the surface of the rock. Some are large. How large is hard to say; you only hear them, and the size of the tunnels, where they open onto the surface, suggests animals as big as a human torso.

And in the pools and underground rivers, things move. You see ripples in surface water that shouldn't have ripples. You hear splashing from crevices. Once, looking down into a fissure where dark water moved, you saw something pale and long — very long — shift in the current and disappear.

The larger fauna — and there is larger fauna, evidenced by tracks in the coldite dust, broad padded prints arranged in a pattern that suggests an animal that walks on six or eight limbs — is rarely encountered. The density is too low. You are alone on this plateau, and so are they. You might walk for days between encounters. The tracks suggest something heavy, deliberate, and unafraid. Something that lives in the spaces between the spires and has adapted to the sound, the dark, the cold.

## The Structures

They are old. Or — not *old* exactly. *Brief.*

Scattered across the plateau, usually in the lee of a spire where the wind is less and the dripping from the canopy provides a reliable water source, you find the remains of structures. They are small. A single wall, knee-high, made from fitted fragments of spire-tissue — the woody material of fallen lattices, cut with rough tools and stacked without mortar. A circular foundation, perhaps four meters across, filled now with coldite dust and a thin crust of ice. In one place, a more complete structure: a low dome, barely tall enough to crouch in, its walls woven from the long fibrous roots of a spire that has since died and collapsed. The weaving is skilled. Someone sat here, in the dark, in the cold, and *wove*.

There are tools. Simple ones. Blades chipped from the angular stone of the young geology, sharp as the landscape that produced them. Scrapers. A pointed implement that might have been used for piercing or sewing. A flat stone with a shallow depression worn into it, as if something was ground there repeatedly.

No metal. Nothing fired. Nothing inscribed. The people who made these things worked with what the landscape offered — stone, root, the tissues of fallen plants — and they did not stay long, or they did not leave much, or both.

In one shelter, the most complete one, you find something else: a pile of glider-membranes, carefully folded, dried and preserved by the cold. Dozens of them. Their resonance properties, even dried, are remarkable — you hold one up and it *hums* faintly in the ambient sound-field, vibrating in sympathy with the drone of the nearest spire. The people who lived here understood the sound. They collected it. Perhaps they used it. Perhaps the membranes were instruments, or tools, or clothing, or something that has no word in a language built for warmer, quieter worlds.

## Weather and Motion

The plateau is not still.

The weather cycles come in surges. A pressure builds — you feel it in your ears, in the increased tension of the spire-canopies, in the way the gliders go quiet and the burrowers go deep. Then the wind arrives, a sustained blast from one direction, cold and wet and carrying ice-crystals that sting exposed skin. The spires sing louder. The canopy-lattices thrash. The under-rain intensifies, driven sideways by the wind, pooling in new places, flooding some of the shallow crevices. This lasts for hours. Maybe days. Time is difficult in the dark.

Then the wind drops. The sound shifts — the spires settle into a lower register, almost soothing, almost meditative. The gliders relaunch. The dripping resumes its slow applause. A period of relative calm, during which the condensation thickens and the temperature drops further and the ice on the angular rocks grows another millimeter.

Then the cycle begins again.

The geology, too, is active. The plateau is still rising. You hear it — *feel* it — as a deep, subsonic groan from far below, a tectonic sigh. Occasionally a ridge of stone shifts, and a new edge is exposed, sharp as a knife, and a new crevice opens, and a new underground river is born or redirected, and the root-tendrils of the nearest spire slowly, over the course of days, extend toward the new water source.

The spires themselves are in motion, though their pace is geological. They grow. They compete — not actively, but through presence, their root-networks interlocking underground, their canopies spreading to catch what moisture and what faint light exists. A spire that grows too close to another will eventually, over decades, block its neighbor's access to the sparse resources. The weaker spire dies. Its tissues dry and harden and its canopy collapses, and the sound it made — its unique voice, its chord — goes silent, and the silence is noticeable, a gap in the chorus, an absence that the surviving spires and the animals and the weather rush to fill.

## The Feeling

You stand on this plateau and you are cold, and damp, and your eyes ache from the effort of seeing in the dark, and the sound is enormous — a living, breathing, *singing* enormity that never stops, that is the world talking to itself — and you are *alone* in a way that feels ancient. The spires rise around you like the pillars of a cathedral that has no walls, no roof, no congregation. The air is wet and cold and heavy and smells of stone and growing tissue and ice. The ground under your feet is sharp and new. Somewhere far below, water moves through cracks in the young earth. Somewhere above, a glider clicks in the dark. Somewhere in the distance, an animal you will never see leaves tracks in the dust between the singing giants.

The world is alive. It is almost *entirely* alive — a vast, cold, dark, singing body of plants and the things that eat them and the things that eat those things, all of it moving, all of it *loud*, all of it spaced so far apart that the living world feels like an empty world, a hollow cathedral, a chorus sung by soloists who cannot see each other in the dark.

And it is still being born. The stone is still sharp. The rivers are still finding their paths. The spires are still growing, still learning their voices, still reaching upward in the dark toward a light that never quite arrives.
