The world is called the Plateau of Condensers. It is a vast, high-altitude continental slab, a table of basalt and iron ore so immense that its edges have their own weather systems. The air is thin and achingly cold, but supersaturated with moisture—a constant, weeping mist that clings to every surface, collecting in heavy droplets that hang from sharp, young rock formations before freezing into delicate rime. The ground is not truly wet, but glazed. A deep, resonant hum is the first thing you notice, and it never stops.

This is a world of engineered geology. The “lush” vegetation is not biologic, but industrial. Forests of heat-exchanger towers, vast as redwoods, rise from the plateau, their surfaces finned and ribbed to maximize condensation. They are connected by a network of aqueducts, not of stone, but of riveted, groaning iron, weeping rust-colored tears from every joint. The “leaves” are automated radiator panels, opening and closing with slow, hydraulic sighs, releasing plumes of steam that add to the perpetual mist. The sound is a layered cacophony: the deep hum of sub-plateau turbines, the clanking rhythmic piston of water pumps, the hiss of a thousand pressure valves, and the constant, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of condensate dripping into collection funnels.

The emptiness is profound. The scale is such that a single Condenser-Tower, with its surrounding lattice of catwalks and frost-coated pipes, can be kilometers across, yet appear as a solitary, spindly monument in an immense grey void. The infrastructure—the urban element—is sparse and functional. There are no streets, only service viaducts for the movement of machinery. Platforms and gantries are bolted directly to the bedrock, hosting squat, windowless barracks and workshops. The people, when you see them, are bundled figures in heavy, insulated gear, moving with purpose between control sheds or inspecting the vast, shuddering flywheels of generators. They are not settlers; they are custodians, or perhaps prisoners, of the system.

The young, sharp geology is at war with the industrial scale. Immense, recently fractured cliffs of black basalt have been drilled and honeycombed to house turbines. Fresh talus slopes, still angular and unweathered, are smothered under pipelines. The environment is changing only in the slow, glacial pace of entropy—rust, frost-heave, the infinitesimal creep of metal fatigue. Nothing grows, nothing decays biologically. The only cycle is the engineered one: water vapor condenses on the cold towers, is collected, purified in roaring filtration plants, heated in core reactors deep within the plateau, and then vented back into the sky to begin again. It is a closed, perfect, and deafening system.

The weirdness is in the fusion. It is a landscape that has *become* a machine. The rock is part of the coolant system. The atmosphere is a working fluid. The sound is its heartbeat. There is no animal life because there is no niche, no food web, no silence in which to hear a call. The normal, cold light of a distant sun filters through the industrial haze, casting long, distorted shadows of the towers across the weeping rock, making the entire plateau look like a single, colossal, and terribly lonely organism, forever busy with a purpose that has no need for life, only for the relentless, unchanging conversion of energy and the collection of tears from stone.
