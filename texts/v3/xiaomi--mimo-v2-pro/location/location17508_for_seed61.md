# The Weeping Cloister

---

The city lives inside a single cavity.

Not a cavern in the romantic sense — no vaulted cathedrals of stone, no bioluminescent wonderland. A *cavity*: a wound in limestone that never fully closed, carved wider over uncounted years by the patient chemistry of water dissolving calcium carbonate molecule by molecule, then abandoned by the water as it found new paths. The result is an irregular room roughly the size of a town square, roughly the shape of a collapsed lung, roughly as old as the idea of permanence.

And it is *loud*.

---

## The Sound

You notice this first and you never stop noticing it. The acoustics of the cavity are ruthless. Every surface is curved or faceted in ways that collect sound and return it, slightly altered, slightly delayed, slightly layered over the next arriving wave. The limestone itself rings — it is thin in places, honeycombed, almost bell-like where water once hollowed channels through it and left behind sheets of stone no thicker than a fingertip. When water strikes these sheets, they sing. A high, thin, glassy note that never quite repeats and never quite stops.

But the main source of the sound is the seep. Water enters the cavity from above — not as a stream, not as a drip, but as a *sweat*. The ceiling and upper walls perspire constantly. Condensation forms on the cool stone, gathers, runs, falls. Thousands of tiny rivulets descending thousands of slight irregularities, each one finding its way down to the floor and into the channels carved generations ago. The drops strike pooled water, strike stone, strike each other. In the echoing cavity, what would be a whisper in the open air becomes a sustained, ambient roar — a white noise that is not white, that has texture, that has rhythm, that sounds almost like breathing if breathing were done by something the size of a building.

The inhabitants do not shout over it. They have adapted. Their language — what language they have — is gestural, tactile. Hands against shoulders, fingers tracing shapes on skin. The sound is not a problem to be solved. It is the air they live inside.

---

## The Walls

The limestone is old. Not ancient in the way of granite or gneiss, but old in the way of something that has been *settled* — that has found its final shape and is content to keep it. The erosion here is not active destruction. It is the weathering of a thing that has already arrived. Every surface is smoothed, rounded, softened. The edges where water once carved are now blurred, like features in a half-remembered dream. Mineral deposits — calcium carbonate, traces of iron, veins of something darker and more crystalline — stripe the walls in horizontal bands that record the water table's slow retreat over centuries.

In places, the stone is translucent. Thin sheets of calcite, laid down in still water long ago, now hang from the ceiling like membrane. Where the dim light filters through them, they glow faintly amber, faintly warm, though they are not warm.

The floor is uneven, sloped toward a central depression where the deepest water collects in a pool no wider than a bedroom. The water is clear and impossibly still between the impacts of falling drops. The pool has no visible bottom. The limestone beneath it continues downward, dissolving, dissolving, but so slowly that no living memory records it changing.

---

## The City

They have built into the cavity the way barnacles build into the hull of a ship: densely, without plan, without apology.

Structures cling to the walls in tiers — shelves and platforms and terraces, hewn from the living rock with hand tools. Chisels of antler and hardite stone. Hammers of water-smoothed cobbles bound in fiber. The work is primitive in material and astonishing in ambition. Stairways have been cut into the vertical faces, narrow and steep, worn glossy by generations of bare feet. Rooms have been hollowed into the thicker sections of wall — shallow alcoves for sleeping, deeper chambers for storage, passages that connect one level to another in ways that suggest no architect but rather a slow, accretive intelligence, the way a termite mound is designed by no single termite.

The structures press close. Doorways face each other across gaps barely wide enough to walk through sideways. Platforms overlap, creating partial ceilings over lower platforms. In the densest sections, near the center of the wall where the stone is thickest, the built environment is layered three or four times — rooms within rooms, walls that serve as floors for the level above, carved channels in the stone carrying seep-water through the settlement like plumbing that was never invented, only *followed*.

There is no wood. There is no plant fiber. Building material is stone, and whatever can be made from the creatures that live in the deeper passages (see below). The aesthetic is mineral: everything is the color of limestone, or the color ofite, or the color of water, or the color of shadow.

---

## The Inhabitants

They are few, by count. Perhaps sixty, perhaps eighty. But they are *dense* — they cluster, they huddle, they live in each other's spaces. Privacy is not a concept the cavity has room for. They sleep in shifts. They eat in groups. They move through the settlement like blood moves through a capillary: always in contact with the walls.

They are small. Not stunted — *proportioned for this*. Their frames are narrow, their hands wide, their fingers long. Their eyes are large and slow to adjust to the rare moments of brighter light. Their skin is pale, almost translucent in places, and cool to the touch. They have been here a long time. Not these specific individuals — the people, the culture, the line. They do not remember arriving. They do not remember a before.

Their tools are simple. Stone blades for cutting. Bone awls for piercing. Cord woven from the shed skins of the blind cave eels that move through the deeper water channels (see below). They have no metal, though there are deposits of iron in the wall-bands — they have not yet thought to heat stone, and there is almost no fuel for fire in the cavity. A single small hearth exists in the driest upper alcove, fed with dried eel-skin and fat, used sparingly, kept alive more as a point of ritual warmth than for any practical purpose. The smoke rises and is absorbed by the porous ceiling. The light it casts is dim and orange and reaches only a few meters before the limestone drinks it.

---

## The Fauna

Almost nothing.

The blind eels are the most visible: pale, eyeless, perhaps a handspan long, moving through the water channels that thread the lower walls. They are slow and taste of mineral and old water. The people catch them with woven traps set at channel junctions. The eels are food, and their skins are material, and their oil feeds the single fire.

There are insects — tiny, white, almost invisible. They live in the cracks of the stone and eat whatever organic residue accumulates: shed skin, eel bones, the occasional fungal growth that appears on walls near the waterline. The fungi are thin and pale and do not last long. They are not vegetation. They are *almost-alive* — the boundary between mineral deposit and organism, ambiguous, growing in sheets that resemble the calcite formations around them until you notice the faint web of hyphae at their edges.

In the deepest water, there are things that might be worms or might be something else. Long, thin, pale. The people do not eat them. They do not speak of them.

---

## The Atmosphere

The air is cool and wet and close. Humidity is near-total. Everything is damp. Skin is damp. Stone is damp. The air itself seems damp, as though you could wring moisture from it by closing your fist. Breathing is easy but *present* — you are aware of the air entering you, aware of its weight, its mineral taste.

The dim light comes from several sources. The calcite sheets in the ceiling transmit a faint glow from somewhere above — not sunlight, not directly, but something that has been filtered and diffused through so much stone that it arrives as a gentle amber luminescence, enough to see by if you let your eyes adjust, never enough to see clearly. The hearth contributes its small orange pool. And there is a phenomenon the inhabitants call (in their gesture-language, which translates roughly as) *the shining*: certain minerals in the wall-bands fluoresce faintly when water passes over them, a cold blue-green light that appears and vanishes as the seep-patterns shift. It is not reliable. It is not bright. But in the deepest hours of the dim cycle, when the hearth is lowest and the amber glow from above is weakest, the shining is enough to walk by.

The smell is stone and water and the faint organic tang of sixty bodies living in a closed space with poor ventilation. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a place that has been occupied so long that the smell has become indistinguishable from the place itself.

---

## The Stillness

Nothing changes.

This is the deepest truth of the cavity. The seep rate is constant. The temperature does not vary. The pool does not rise or fall. The eels do not multiply or diminish. The people do not grow or innovate or decline. The structures are maintained, repaired, replaced in kind — never expanded, never abandoned. The sound is the same sound it has always been, the same roar, the same rhythm, the same breathing.

Time passes, but it passes like water through limestone: invisibly, imperceptibly, measurable only by what it leaves behind — the slow rounding of an edge, the slow deepening of a footstep in stone, the slow accumulation of calcium deposits on the ceiling that will, in some unimaginable future, close the cavity entirely and seal it shut.

But not yet. Not for a long time yet.

The city breathes. The water falls. The people move through their dense, layered, weathered home in the dark, touching each other's shoulders, tracing meaning on skin, listening to a sound that has always been there and will always be there and means nothing and means everything.

---

*They do not know they are underground. They have no word for "above." The concept of a surface — open air, horizon, sky — would be as foreign and as useless as the concept of dryness. This is not a place beneath the world. This is the world. It has always been this size, this shape, this loud. It is complete.*
