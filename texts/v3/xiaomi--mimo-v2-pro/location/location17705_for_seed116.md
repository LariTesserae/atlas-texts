# The Reservoir at Kathedral Peak

You would not find it from the air. From the air there is only mountain — one of the world's great stumps, a massif of rotten limestone and gneiss so old it has forgotten what it was before the folding. Its flanks are bald with age, scoured to a kind of mineral patience, the slope angle barely perceptible after millions of years of settling. The stone is the color of a bruise in its final healing stage: yellow-gray, aching toward white. Nothing grows on the outside. The air at this altitude is the temperature of a held breath. The wind has nothing to catch on.

But there is a seam. On the eastern face, where the massif shows a fracture line older than most mountain chains, a crack runs vertically for two hundred meters. It is exactly the width of a maintenance corridor, because that is what it is. At its base, a steel door the color of old pennies. From this door, if you pressed your ear to it — and at this altitude, in this quiet, you would — you would hear the mountain's interior *roaring*.

---

## The Interior

Step through, and everything changes.

The first thing is the heat. It is not the dry warmth of the outside world, the cauterized thinness of high-altitude sun. It is *wet* heat, the kind that wraps your skin in a film before you've finished your first breath. The air carries a mineral sweetness — ozone and limestone and something vegetal, something ancient and fermenting. Your glasses fog. Your shirt darkens. You are breathing water now, not air.

The second thing is the sound. It fills you from the inside before you can locate its sources. There is the low, perpetual groan of pumps — massive diaphragm pumps, you will later learn, sunk into the bedrock far below, drawing water from a reservoir that has been here as long as the mountain itself. There is the hiss of steam from pressure-release valves along the ceiling pipes. There is the chitter and splash of a hundred species of creature doing what creatures do. And beneath all of it, a kind of bass vibration, the whole mountain humming with contained water, with a system in continuous motion.

You are inside a cavity. A *constructed* cavity — though "cave" is not the right word. Think of a cathedral that has been built by someone who loved caves, or a cave that has been engineered by someone who loved cathedrals. The walls are not natural stone. They are lined — have been lined for centuries, perhaps longer — with a ceramic composite, a smooth dark glaze over what was once raw rock. The glaze is cracked in places, and in those cracks, life has taken hold: bright mosses of impossible green, fungal shelves the color of rust, thin-rooted ferns that drink from the constant condensation running down the walls.

The space is enormous. Not in the way of a landscape — this is not a place you can survey. It is enormous in the way of an interior, a single room so vast that its far walls disappear into haze. The ceiling arches high overhead, vaulted and dripping, hung with the stalactitic deposits of centuries of mineral-laden moisture. Industrial pipe runs crisscross the upper darkness, some thick as tree trunks, some thin as capillaries, all sweating, all humming. Flood lamps are mounted at intervals along the pipes — sodium-vapor, by their amber cast — giving the whole space a perpetual golden half-light, the color of late afternoon trapped forever.

---

## The Water

The floor of the reservoir is not a floor. It is water.

A body of water so deep and so still at its center that its bottom has never been mapped. The pumps draw from it; the pipes return condensate to it. Rain falls inside the mountain — a continuous, gentle rain, generated by the temperature differential between the warm lower air and the cool ceiling ceramic. Droplets form on the stalactites and the pipe runs and the glaze, gathering into rivulets, running down the walls, dripping from every surface with a rhythm that is almost musical, almost maddening. The sound of this rain is the foundation of the reservoir's acoustic world: a thousand tiny impacts, a soft and endless applause.

The water is warm — heated geothermally, though the mechanism has been engineering so long that the distinction between natural and artificial has dissolved. The surface steams faintly in the amber light. Near the edges, where the water meets the ceramic retaining walls, it is shallow enough to see the bottom: a substrate of gravel and fallen mineral deposits and — remarkably — a kind of silt that might be soil, might be ancient organic matter compressed beyond recognition. In this shallows, things grow.

---

## The Vegetation

The reservoir's edge is a riot. Not a planned riot — there is clearly a *history* of planning here, visible in the geometric terracing of the retaining walls, in the terraced planting beds that step down toward the waterline, in the drip-irrigation lines that run along each terrace like veins. But the planning has been overtaken. The vegetation has long since outgrown its beds.

Water hyacinth floats in dense, drifting mats along the margins, their lavender flowers catching the sodium light and glowing like small lanterns. Between the hyacinths, broad-leafed aquatics — species unrecognizable to any botanist, bred or evolved in this enclosed biome over spans that make the word "cultivar" quaint — unfurl pads the size of dinner tables. Submerged fronds wave in the gentle current of the pump circulation, visible as dark green shadows beneath the steaming surface. On the terraces themselves, ferns dominate: tree ferns, their fronds arching over the walkways that connect the terraces; maidenhair ferns in the crevices between ceramic tiles; something like a selaginella but the size of a shrub, covering the retaining walls in a continuous green fur.

And the mosses. Mosses are the reservoir's true vegetation, the substrate upon which all else depends. They coat every surface that is wet, which is every surface. They turn the ceramic walls into velvet. They soften the pipe runs. They fill the cracks in the walkways. They are the color of emerald in the direct light of the flood lamps, the color of shadow everywhere else. They are the mountain's long, slow attempt to reclaim what was taken from it when the cavity was hollowed and lined and filled.

Higher up, where the terraces give way to the vertical walls, epiphytes cascade: orchids with waxy, heat-adapted blooms; bromeliads that catch the perpetual rainwater in their rosettes and become tiny ecosystems of their own; hanging mosses — *Tillandsia*, or something descended from it — that drape from the pipe runs in curtains of silver-green, filtering the amber light into something dappled and shifting.

---

## The Fauna

Life here is *loud*.

The reservoir supports a trophic web of baroque complexity, all of it contained, all of it in motion. The water teems. In the shallows, translucent shrimp — pale, eyeless, evolved for this darkness — scull through the silt in clouds. They are eaten by fish: small, scaled, brightly colored, with the oversized fins of aquarium breeds run feral over generations. Larger fish patrol the deeper water, shapes you glimpse as shadows and wakes in the steam-hazed middle distance. Something moves in the very deep water, something large enough to create a current when it surfaces, but it surfaces rarely, and you do not see it clearly.

On the terraces, in the ferns, the noise is primate: not monkeys, but something that fills the same niche. Small, arboreal, six-limbed — the reservoir's most conspicuous weirdness, though not its deepest. They are the color of the mosses they live among, with bright black eyes and hands that grip with a precision that suggests descent from something engineered. They chatter. They scream. They throw things — mineral nodules, fruit husks, occasionally live insects — at each other and at anything that moves below their territories. They are abundant in the way that songbirds are abundant in a healthy forest: you are never out of earshot of them, never alone in the green.

Insects fill the remaining air. Moths the size of hands circle the flood lamps in slow, drunken orbits. Beetles as long as fingers trundle across the walkways, armored and indifferent. Midges form clouds in the humid upper air, and where the midges swarm, the bats follow: echolocating, threading the pipe runs at speed, filling the dark upper vault with clicks so rapid they blur into a continuous static.

Frogs sing from every wet surface. Their calls are varied, overlapping, a polyphony that starts at what might be dusk — though there is no dusk here, no day, no night, only the constant amber glow — and builds into a wall of sound. The frogs are the reservoir's circadian rhythm, the closest thing this place has to a clock. They sing when the pump cycle shifts, when the rain intensifies, when the temperature fluctuates by half a degree. They are the system's most sensitive sensor array, and they announce every change in a chorus that ranges from subsonic thrum to a high, glassy whistle.

---

## The Machines

The infrastructure is old. Not antique — there is nothing decorative about it — but old in the way of things that have been maintained continuously by hands that understood them less and less with each passing generation. The pumps are the oldest components: massive, cast-iron bellows mechanisms sunk into chambers far below the waterline, driven by a geothermal engine that taps the mountain's residual heat. They operate on a cycle that is almost biological in its rhythm — inhale, pressurize, exhale, rest — and their labor is what keeps the water circulating, keeps the rain falling, keeps the air warm.

Along the terraces, agricultural machinery sits in various states of use and abandonment. Harvesters with articulated arms, designed to service the planting beds, now stand half-swallowed by the ferns they were meant to tend. Their paint is blistered, their joints seized, but their tracks still run, and the six-limbed creatures use them as highways between terrace levels, swinging from arm to arm with casual acrobatics. Nutrient dispensers line the walkways, their hoppers empty, their dispensing heads clogged with moss. Someone — something — still fills them, occasionally; you find fresh pellets in the trays, but the pattern is irregular, perhaps instinctive rather than intentional.

The pipe runs are the reservoir's circulatory system, and they are alive in every sense that matters. Some carry water. Some carry steam. Some, you suspect, carry something else — a gas, a nutrient mist, an antifungal agent — because in places the vegetation thins and the ceramic is clean and the air smells sharp, chemical, corrective. The pipes hum and knock and occasionally shudder with pressure changes that ripple through the entire system. When a valve opens somewhere in the deep, you feel it in your chest before you hear it.

---

## The Atmosphere of the Place

What lingers, after the heat and the sound and the green — what you carry out with you, if you leave, which is not certain — is the sense of *age*. Not deterioration. Not decay. The reservoir is not falling apart; it is settling. It has been here long enough that the distinction between the built and the grown has become meaningless. The ceramic walls are covered in living skin. The machinery has been colonized by the ecology. The rain falls because of the pumps and the rain feeds the moss and the moss absorbs the vibration of the pumps and the vibration carries through the walls and the walls support the ferns and the ferns drip and the drip fills the reservoir and the reservoir feeds the pumps. The loop has closed. The mountain breathes.

It is loud, and it is sparse — sparse in the way that a rainforest canopy is sparse: the spaces between are vast, the presences are scattered across a volume too large to comprehend, and yet the density of *life per unit of attention* is overwhelming. You cannot take it in. You focus on a single terrace, a single fern, a single frog, and the rest recedes into haze and steam and sound. The place is a pocket — you could walk its perimeter in a day, if the walkways were clear — and yet it contains, within its ceramic shell, a world.

The weirdness is not in any single feature. It is in the totality: a mountain that has been hollowed and filled and sealed and left to run, and run, and run, until the running *became* the mountain. No one is in charge. No one has been in charge for a very long time. But the pumps cycle, and the rain falls, and the frogs sing, and the six-limbed things chatter in the ferns, and the deep water moves with something slow and patient and enormous, and the whole place hums with the quiet, ancient, industrious certainty of a thing that has forgotten it was ever supposed to stop.
