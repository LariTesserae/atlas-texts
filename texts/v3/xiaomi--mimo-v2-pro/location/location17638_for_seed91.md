The sound arrives first, and it is a presence. Not a roar, but a deep, ceaseless vibration that works its way into bone. It is the sound of the forest breathing.

You are in a valley, though the valley itself is largely absent. The slopes are there—steep, sharp-edged walls of dark, fractured basalt—but the floor is not soil or rock. It is a mat. A living, spongy, fibrous mat of mycelial threads and rhizomorphic cords, thick as a mattress, woven over everything. The air is not air, but a dense, cold, damp medium, tasting of crushed ferns, wet stone, and the sharp ozone of mechanical decay. Condensation beads on every surface, trembling with the constant vibration.

This is a fungal forest. Total vegetation, not in the sense of leaves and chlorophyll, but of hyphae and fruiting bodies. The "trees" are colossal stalked fungi, their caps broad and tiered like umbrellas, shedding spores in slow, glittering cascades that drift in the dim light like fog. They are not green; they are umber, ocher, deep violet, and ghostly pale. Between them, lattices of thinner fungal growth fill the sparse spaces, a web of life so complete it has become the architecture. The ground is a sponge; every step releases a puff of spore-mist and the scent of deep earth.

The light is dim, filtering from somewhere far above—a distant, icy surface—through chasms in the basalt. It is a blue-grey twilight, never bright, never fully dark. In this half-light, the landscape extends. It is not a cave, but a network of sunken valleys and collapsed lava tubes, an open subterranean region miles across. The scale is regional, a fungal metropolis without inhabitants.

And here, the other rules of the world assert themselves. The deafening sound is not from industry, but from the forest itself. The constant vibration is the resonant frequency of the mycelial network. It hums with a low, seismic tone, a product of millions of tons of fungal mass shifting and growing, contracting and expanding in the cold. It is the sound of patient, slow-motion growth on a geological scale. This is the dynamic: slow. Change here is measured in centuries. A new stalk might push an inch in a year. The condensation drip that carves a new channel in the basalt takes a decade.

And yet, there are marks of something else. Scattered across the fungal floor, half-absorbed into the living mat, are the remains. Rusted iron beams, their ends corroded to lace, protrude from fungal shelves. A massive gear, its teeth softened by rust, sits like a strange altar. Here and there, the vague, rounded shapes of machinery are visible, entombed in the growing mass. This is the built environment: scattered artifacts of an industrial past, now being digested. The technology—engines, generators, vast mechanical presses—is 1.8/3, but it is dead tech. It is fossilizing. The dampness seeps into every circuit, the cold slows corrosion to a crawl, and the fungi slowly enfold it all. Perhaps the vibration was once mechanical, but now it is organic. The forest has learned the frequency and sings it back.

Fauna is rare. Occasionally, a pale, eyeless thing—salamander-like, or perhaps a giant, sightless insect—scuttles between the fungal trunks, feeding on the spores. Its sounds are lost in the ambient hum. The ecology is simple: the fungi feed on the mineral-rich basalt and the lingering hydrocarbons from the old machines, broken down by specialized enzymes. The moisture from above sustains them. The cold preserves and slows.

It is a place of immense, quiet power. A slow, deafening, damp cathedral of decay and growth, where the memory of industry is being peaceably consumed by the patient, all-encompassing life of the fungal world. The only law is absorption. The only future is more of the same, forever, in the dim, humming cold.
