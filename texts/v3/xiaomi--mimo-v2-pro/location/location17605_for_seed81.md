The basin holds a metropolis meant for a different climate, or a different species, or perhaps for no species at all—a city of broad avenues and skeletal towers that now bakes under a sun it was never designed to reflect. The heat is not merely air temperature; it radiates up from the cracked, dark stone of the plazas, and down from a sky the color of a fading bruise. There is moisture everywhere, but it does not pool in lakes or rivers. Instead, it weeps. It beads on the flanks of rust-streaked iron pylons, drips in slow, fat drops from the lip of a cantilevered roof a hundred meters up, and rises in ghostly veils of steam from grates set into the street. The air is a hot, wet cloth on the face. The ground is always damp, a permanent slickness that feeds no moss, no vine, only a faint, greasy iridescence in the deepest cracks.

There is no soil, only a manufactured substrate of compacted slag and fused ceramic, now weathered into gentle, settled contours. The few things that grow are not alive in any way you recognize. They are mineral crusts, bone-white and fractal, that bloom in the sheltered undersides of balconies. They are slow, syrupy seeps of silica from weeping walls, forming glassy, translucent curtains over doorways. There are fungal analogues, but they are made of braided metal filaments and sulfur deposits, clustering around the warm, humming vents of subterranean machinery. They pulse with a faint, internal heat, but they do not breathe.

The city’s infrastructure is immense, but hollow. Its scale is regional, a vast grid of identical hexagonal hab-blocks marching toward the hazy, heat-shimmered horizon, connected by soaring bridge-ways and deep, shadow-cut canals that hold only vapor. The density is an illusion of form. You can stand in a central plaza, surrounded by architecture of staggering complexity—arched buttresses, filigreed flywheels the size of houses, towering stacks that once plumed with fire—and hear only the hollow, wet *tock* of a single condensation drop falling into a drain. The silence is profound, broken by the low, almost sub-audible groan of metal expanding in the heat, the distant *sigh* of steam from a fissure, and the occasional, startling *clank* from deep within the earth, as if some great, dormant mechanism is shifting in its sleep.

The ecology, such as it is, is one of process, not biology. The heat drives a slow, relentless water cycle: moisture rises from some deep, geothermal aquifer, condenses on the cool, high faces of the empty towers, trickles down their flanks in controlled channels—engineered waterfalls that now run unguided, etching new paths—and is collected in the vast, subterranean cisterns that hum with residual industrial power. This cycle is the city’s only engine. It powers the dim glow of amber emergency lights that still flicker in some corridors, and the sluggish rotation of a single, colossal fan somewhere far below, which stirs the hot air but cools nothing.

Life is rare, but present. Things move in the periphery: sleek, multi-limbed creatures of polished carapace that scuttle from shadow to shadow, drinking condensation and licking mineral salts. They are not animals, but possibly autonomous maintenance drones, long out of contact with their masters, running on fading power cells and performing their last, meaningless routines. High above, at the very edge of the dim light, you might see a flutter—a membranous, heat-resistant thing, more kite than bird, riding the thermal updrafts with silent, mechanical grace.

The world’s rules are those of a finished system running down. Everything is settled, weathered, in a state of high-temperature, high-moisture equilibrium. The built environment is not being reclaimed by nature; nature never had a claim here. Instead, the city is being slowly, strangely mineralized. It is a thermodynamic engine, a place where water, heat, and engineered material interact in a slow, weird ballet of erosion and accretion. The strangeness lies in the emptiness of the intention—the grandeur of the design, hollowed out and left to the mercy of its own lingering, wet heat. It is a ghost limb of a civilization, still twitching with the memory of industry, sweating in the perpetual, dim afternoon of its afterlife.
