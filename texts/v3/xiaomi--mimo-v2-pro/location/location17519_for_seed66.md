You stand on the upper tiers of Khol-Thyrsa, the Weeping City, and the world breathes around you.

It is a high place, a vast continental shelf of basalt and older, darker stone, but it is not dry. Water is ever-present—not as rivers or oceans, but as a perpetual, shimmering exhalation. It seeps from the porous rock in glittering rivulets, condenses on every surface in a cold dew, and hangs in the air as a fine, cool mist that catches the light. The ground underfoot is dark, polished basalt, channelled with millennia-old gutters that guide the endless weeping into deeper cisterns. The sound is a constant, layered susurrus: the drip, trickle, and patter of water, underscored by a deeper, resonant hum.

For this is a city. Not built, exactly, but *grown* and *excavated* over epochs. The architecture is a fusion of geology and industry. Towers of fused black stone and dark, vitreous ceramic twist towards the sky, their surfaces slick with moisture and etched with patterns that are half-geometric conduit, half-natural mineral veining. These are not inert structures. They are the exoskeleton of a vast, slow metabolism. From countless vents and pores, not smoke, but a warm, mineral-rich vapor sighs out, joining the mist. The air smells of ozone, wet stone, and a faint, metallic tang like blood or copper.

There is no green here. No blade of grass, no lichen. The 0.3 vegetation parameter has been fulfilled by something stranger. Life here is faunal, overwhelming, and mineral. The "flora" is the city itself, and the fauna are its mobile children. Great, slow creatures—like ambulatory geodes or crustaceans sculpted from chalcedony and iron—inch along the broad avenues, their crystalline carapaces scraping a gritty counterpoint to the water's music. Smaller things, swarms of glass-shelled insectoids, skitter over the weeping walls, filtering nutrients from the mineral-rich condensation. Their collective clicking and chiming adds a high, persistent layer to the soundscape. This is the overwhelming fauna: a lithic ecosystem, where biology has been replaced by a complex, self-perpetuating mineralogy. They are the reason for the city's industry; their shed carapaces, their excretions, the very geothermal energy they help regulate, are the resources processed in the humming forges deep below.

The scale is continental, and the erosion is profound. This is not a new settlement. It is a landscape in perfect, settled balance with itself. The towers are weathered into smooth, organic shapes, their forms dictated by millennia of mist-wind and mineral deposition. The city's "infrastructure" is a network of canals, aqueducts, and geothermal vents so old they appear natural. The warmth (1.5) comes not from a sun, which is often obscured by the thick, luminous mist, but from the planet's own heart, channeled upward by the city's deep roots.

Light filters down, normal but diffuse, refracted through the ever-present moisture into a shadowless, silvery glow. It illuminates the vast, layered density of the place. It is crowded, but not chaotic. Every surface has purpose, every space is utilized, yet it all feels inevitable, like the structure of a sponge or a bone. The sound (1.7) is loud, but it is a *structured* loudness: the bass hum of the deep engines, the mid-range chatter of water, the high-frequency clatter of the fauna. It is the sound of a living, working organism.

The dynamic is slow, almost imperceptible. Change happens in the drift of mist, the millimeter-per-year growth of a new crystalline spire, the gradual migration of a herd of basalt grazers across a plaza. The industry (1.9) is not one of frantic production, but of patient, endless recycling and refinement. It maintains the balance. The weirdness is low because, to its inhabitants, this is simply reality. The logic is complete: a wet, high, ancient world where water, stone, and motion have converged into a single, vast, weeping, breathing, living city. To walk its tiers is to walk through the circulatory system, the nervous system, and the skeleton of a being whose lifespan is measured in geological time.
