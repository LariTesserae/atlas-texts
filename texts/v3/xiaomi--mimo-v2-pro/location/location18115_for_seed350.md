# The Engine Garden

You arrive by nothing. There is no road in. The land — if it is land — appears to have only recently risen from whatever came before it, and already it doesn't want to stay.

---

**First: the sound.**

Before you see anything, the sound finds you. Not approaches — *finds*, the way a hand finds a nerve. It is total, and it is industrial, and it is biological, and it is geological, and it has no clean separations between any of these. What you hear is a churning. A droning. A mechanical grinding married to a liquid roar married to something that might be the inside of a throat. There are frequencies below hearing that you feel in your ankles, in your jaw, in the fluid behind your eyes. If there were animals here, they would have learned not to exist. There are no animals. There never will be, not in any lasting way. The sound has claimed that niche — the niche of *moving thing that occupies the auditory space of a living creature* — and it will not yield it.

You try to shout to yourself. You can't hear whether you succeed.

---

**What you can see** (though barely):

The light is the color of a bruise. Not dark — not *dark* dark — but a kind of suffocated amber-green that suggests a sun exists somewhere, or existed, and is trying to push its light through an impossibly thick medium. The medium is water. The medium is rain. The medium is vapor rising off everything that is about to become everything else. There is no horizon. There is only a gradient from murk to heavier murk, and within that gradient, shapes.

The shapes are trees.

Or: the shapes are *what trees do here*, which is not the same thing.

They rise from water that is between ankle-deep and chest-deep depending on where you step, and depending on the moment — the water level seems to have opinions, seems to breathe in and out on a cycle too fast to chart. The trees are enormous. Not tall — no, they spread *outward*, their trunks forking low, almost at the waterline, into a canopy that extends horizontally for forty, sixty, a hundred meters before it tangles into the canopy of the next tree. Between the trunks, aerial roots descend in curtains, some thicker than your body, some hair-thin, all of them slick and black and trembling with the vibration of the sound. The leaves — if leaves — are broad, leathery, almost plastic in texture, a deep wet green so dark it's nearly black. They hold the rain without dropping it. The rain has been falling so long that every surface is coated in a film of living water, and on that film, something is growing: mosses, or algae, or something that is neither, something bright chartreuse and slightly phosphorescent, feeding on the dim light that filters down, competing with the canopy for every photon.

The water is not clear. It is the color of brewed tea — warm, tannic, opaque — and it moves constantly. Not in waves, exactly. In *surges*. Pockets of current that rise and subside without wind, without apparent cause, as if the ground beneath the water is shifting. Occasionally the water rushes laterally, as though the entire basin has tilted by a degree, and then corrects itself. Your footing — where you can find footing — is on something that is not soil. It is a dense, fibrous mat of roots and decaying vegetation so compressed it has the consistency of felt. It shifts under you. It is warm. The whole place is warm — not hot, not oppressive in temperature alone, but warm the way a body is warm, warm in the way that makes you understand it is *alive*, that the heat is metabolic.

---

**Then: the ruins.**

They are almost invisible. You stumble against the first one — a wall of concrete, or something that was concrete once, now colonized by the green-black moss so thoroughly that it reads as organic until you scrape a patch and find the mineral beneath. The structures are scattered. Not a city. Not even a village. More like the *idea* of architecture deposited here by floodwater and left. A single stairway that descends into the water and continues below it, its railing wrapped in roots that have pried the rebar outward. A rectangular depression in the root-mat that might have been a foundation, its corners softened by growth. A column, leaning, half-submerged, thick with lichen, connected to nothing, holding up nothing, standing because the vegetation around it has grown into a lattice that props it up, so that the column and the vines are now a single structure, and neither can be removed without collapsing the other.

And then — not scattered, not ruins — the **machinery**.

It rises from the water where the trees thin, or where the trees have *made room* for it, which is stranger. Engines. Vast, rusting, iron-and-steel engines, their flywheels taller than you, their pistons thick as old-growth trunks, bolted to platforms of concrete that have cracked and tilted but not fully surrendered. They run. *They are running.* The sound — the sound — much of it is *them*. The pistons churn. The flywheels spin at low, grinding RPM. Steam — or what looks like steam but smells of iron and ozone and something sweetly rotting — hisses from joints and valves and cracks in boiler casings. But there is no smokestack. There is no coal pile. There is no visible fuel source. The engines are fed by pipes — massive, corroded pipes — that descend into the water, and what they draw up is the warm brown water itself, or something *in* the water, something dissolved or suspended, something the engines combust or catalyze or simply *process*, because the exhaust that rises from them is not smoke but a shimmer, a warping of the air, a place where the light bends and the trees behind the engines seem to shift position, or number, or species.

There are more engines deeper in. You can hear them — different registers, different rhythms, overlapping, creating beats and interference patterns in the sound that make your inner ear ache. Some of the machinery is connected by drive belts and shafts to other machinery, creating a chain of force transmission that extends into the murk beyond sight. What are they *making*? What is being produced? You look for conveyor belts, for output, for product, and find nothing except — the trees. The vegetation. The engines are running and the jungle is *lush*, is *aggressively, impossibly fertile*, and when an engine sputters and misfires, the canopy above it thins, and when it resumes, the growth surges. The engines are not separate from the ecology. They are the heartbeat of it. They are pumping something into the root-mat, or into the water, or into the air, and the jungle is drinking it.

---

**Now: the wrongness.**

It was always there, but you couldn't focus on it until this moment, because it is the kind of wrongness that hides behind attention — the more directly you look, the more it behaves. So you look sideways. You let your eyes unfocus. And you see:

The trees have too many trunks. Or: the trunks you counted a moment ago have changed position. Not moved — you didn't see them move — but *changed*, as if the tree has rearranged its architecture while you were looking at the engine, as if the act of observation was the only thing holding it in a fixed state, and now that you've looked away and back, it has *settled into a different configuration* that is equally plausible, equally stable, equally a tree, but not the *same* tree.

The water reflects things that are not above it. You look down and see, in the brown murk, a reflection of a sky that is not the sky you're standing under — lighter, bluer, with clouds of a shape you don't recognize — and then the reflection shivers and becomes the dark canopy again, and you can't be sure you saw what you saw.

The ruins — the scattered concrete structures — when you pass one and then circle back, there is sometimes a new one. Not newly built. *Already old.* Already colonized. Already leaning at the same angle of decay as everything else, as though it has been here as long as the rest, and you simply failed to see it before. Or it was not there before. Or both. Both can be true here. The place does not require consistency.

The engines, when you are close to one, make sense. There is a valve, a piston, a flywheel — mechanical, comprehensible, even beautiful in the brutal way of old industrial machinery. But when you step back, and there are two or three engines visible at once, the geometry of them starts to suggest something. The pipes connect in ways that, if you mapped them, would form a shape. The pistons fire in a sequence that, if you could remember the pattern, might be a rhythm. The whole assembly of machinery, spread across this drowned basin, might be a single machine — might be a single *organ* — performing a function you cannot name because it operates at a scale and in a dimension you do not have access to.

The reality breakdown is not dramatic. It is not a shattering. It is a *softening*. A place where the rules of space and objecthood and cause and effect have been soaked in warm water until they've gone limp, and anything can reshape itself around anything else, given enough time, which this place has — it has *all the time*, it has *too much time*, it is drowning in time the way it is drowning in water.

---

**The turbulence:**

Every thirty seconds, or every hour — time is unreliable — something shifts. A section of root-mat tears free and migrates, carrying a small concrete ruin with it, the whole island drifting across the water until it collides with a tree trunk and accretes. An engine that was running smoothly seizes, and the canopy above it withers in minutes, leaves going grey and papery and sloughing off into the water, and then something in the water — some reaction, some catalyst — triggers the engine to restart, and the growth returns, but *different*, a different species, a different leaf shape, as if the jungle has forgotten what it was before and is improvising. A pocket of water begins to boil, not with heat but with *activity* — the surface roiling, the tea-brown liquid becoming opaque white, and within it shapes forming and dissolving: structural shapes, columnar shapes, things that look like they *want to be* buildings, that are trying to *remember* how to be buildings, but can't quite hold the form, and collapse back into water. This is what the turbulence is: creation and destruction happening simultaneously, at every scale, with no net direction. The land is still forming — it is *young*, the erosion hasn't even begun to smooth it — and yet it is already drowning, already overgrown, already ruined. The sequence of events here is not past-present-future. It is all-of-these-at-once, layered, dense, saturated.

---

**What it is:**

This is a place where something broke. Not broke *down* — broke *open*. A seam in whatever substrate holds reality together split, and what leaked through was water and growth and noise and engines — the raw *concept* of industry married to the raw *concept* of nature, without the mediating layer of purpose or design. The machines don't produce anything because there is no one to produce *for*. The jungle doesn't grow toward light because the light is insufficient and the growth doesn't care. The sound doesn't communicate because there is nothing to hear it. Everything here is operating at full capacity, all the time, for no reason, and the result is not chaos exactly — it is *excess*. Too much. Too many presences per volume. Too much happening per moment. The density is not crowding — there is space — but the space is *full*, is *saturated* with process, with change, with the deafening, murky, warm, root-choked, engine-thrumming fact of *existence*, here, in this low place, where the water never drains and the light never brightens and the machines never stop and the trees never stop and the ground is always rearranging itself under the water and the water is always rearranging itself under the rain and the rain is always falling, has always been falling, will always be falling, in the place where everything that has no reason to exist has come to exist anyway, and will not stop.
