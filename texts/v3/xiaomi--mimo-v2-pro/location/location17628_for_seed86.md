The place is called the Dry Gullet, though it is not entirely dry. It is a vast, sunken basin—a collapsed warren of ancient caverns whose roofs fell in long ago, leaving a network of troughs and channels open to a perpetually overcast sky. The air is cool and heavy with the smell of wet stone and deep, cold soil.

**Terrain and Materials:**
The ground is a maze of sharp-edged fissures and shallow, crumbling ravines, their walls layered with bands of grey shale and dark, iron-streaked clay. The erosion is young; the landslides that carved these gullies happened in a geologic yesterday. Stones are angular, unrounded by time. In the lowest creases of the land, water appears—not as rivers, but as seeping, condensing systems. Sheets of slick, black moss drink from perpetual dampness on north-facing walls. Puddles the color of weak tea gather in depressions, only to slowly drain away through gravel beds. The moisture is a presence, but not a dominant one; the land is defined by its dryness, with water as a fleeting visitor.

**Atmosphere and Light:**
The light is perpetually dim, a bruised twilight filtered through the thick, non-luminous cloud layer. There is no direct sun, only a diffuse, shadowless glow that flattens perspective and leaches color, leaving only umbers, slates, and deep greens. The air is still, but not silent. It carries the chill of the deep earth, a constant, penetrating cool that seeps into bone.

**Sound:**
This is a loud place, but the sound is organic and rhythmic. A ceaseless, layered chorus dominates: a deep, pulsing thrum from the ground itself, felt as much as heard, produced by the vibration of countless burrowing creatures. Above this, a staccato clicking and rasping, the scrape of chitinous limbs on stone. Intermittent, piercing whistles cut through the din, signals in the dimness. The drip of condensation provides a liquid, metallic percussion in the quiet pockets between the larger noises.

**Ecology and Fauna:**
Vegetation is sparse but tenacious: patches of phosphorescent lichen on rocks, stalks of pale, leafless fungus that grow in clusters near moisture, and tough, root-tangled scrub that grips the ravine edges. The overwhelming presence, however, is animal. The Gullet teems with life, but of a specific kind. Vast colonies of beetle-like creatures, ranging from thumb-sized to as long as a forearm, navigate the cliffs in shimmering, oil-slick streams. Blind, salamander-like amphibians, their skins slick and pale, haunt the seepage pools. The air is occasionally swept by the silent, leathery flight of large-eared bats that roost in the higher fissures. This is an ancient, stable ecosystem in perfect equilibrium. The creatures are not weird; they are simply adapted, filling every niche with a pragmatic, relentless efficiency. They are the reason for the sound, and their sheer biomass makes the ground seem alive.

**Human Presence and Rules:**
Built things are absent. No structures, no tools, no marks of intent remain. Yet, the *potential* for primitive use is here. The sharp flint nodules in the clay layers, the sturdy fungal stalks, the tough carapaces of the beetles—all could be material for a simple toolmaker. But no one has. The rule of the Gullet is one of deep, unchanging stability. The dynamic is frozen. The ecosystem is at climax, the erosion is stalled at this young, sharp phase, the moisture level is a constant, low-grade equilibrium. Nothing decays quickly; a dead beetle lies for weeks, slowly being consumed by smaller grubs, its form holding until it finally collapses into dust. It feels eternal, not in the sense of age, but in the sense of having always been exactly like this, and of resisting any change with the immense inertia of its perfect, crowded balance. To enter is to feel like a temporary, clumsy disturbance in a system that has no need for you and will not remember you. The only movement is the ceaseless, small motion of the fauna, and the slow, almost imperceptible weep of water through stone.
