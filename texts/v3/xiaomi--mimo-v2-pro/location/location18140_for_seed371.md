You are in a crevasse. Not a canyon—those are old, wide, carved by patience. This is a wound, fresh and deep, splitting the spine of a young, sharp mountain range that rose yesterday, geologically speaking. The scale is intimate, oppressive: the walls of dark, wet basalt and granite pinch together barely fifty feet apart, sometimes narrowing to the width of a hallway. Above, a slit of permanent twilight sky is almost always obscured by the lush, choking vegetation that clings to every surface.

The vegetation is the first shock. In this cool, high-altitude crack, life has gone riotous. Not with trees, but with colossal, fleshy fungi and shade-thick mosses. Shelf fungi the size of dining tables jut from the walls in tiered cities, their undersides glowing with a faint, sickly bioluminescence—pale greens and blues that provide the only real light, a dim, aqueous glow that makes the damp rock glisten. Great, spongy columns of a fungal species, like distorted coral, rise from the crevasse floor, nearly touching the leaning fungal plates above. The density is immense; the air is thick with the smell of wet earth, ozone, and a sharp, enzymatic tang, like overripe fruit and rust.

And there is the sound. A constant, loud, industrial grinding and throbbing. It is not from any machine you can see. It is the sound of the place itself. The deep, resonant groan is the mountain’s bones shifting, the tectonic stress of its recent, violent birth. Layered over this is a wet, rhythmic *pulsing*—the hydraulic action of the fungal forests. You can see it: great bulbous sacs on the fungal columns contract and expand in a slow, syncopated rhythm, pumping moisture and nutrients through fibrous, cable-like hyphae that are as thick as your arm. These hyphae drape everywhere, binding the rock, glistening with condensation. It is a sound of industry, of scaled production, of immense biological machinery at work.

The dampness is constant, but free water is rare. A viscous, dew-like condensation weeps from every surface, collecting in shallow, silty pools on the fungal plates before dripping down in endless, echoing plinks that join the cacophony. The temperature is cool, but the close air and the metabolic heat of the fungal factories make it feel close and muggy.

There is no animal life. None. The occasional skitter you might hear is not a creature, but a shifting plate of rock or a fungal shelf collapsing under its own weight, instantly beginning to be reclaimed by white, feathery mycelium. The turbulence is relentless. The crevasse is alive and changing. With a sound like tearing wet cardboard, a whole section of fungal city might slough off the wall, crashing to the floor in a cloud of spores so thick it looks like smoke, only for new growth—already budding from the exposed rock—to begin its frantic climb within hours. The grinding of the rock walls is a constant companion, a reminder that this gap could close as quickly as it opened.

This is not a forest. It is a biomechanical digestive system, a fungal engine room built in the belly of a newborn mountain. The light is the glow of its processes, the sound is its function, the lush density is its frantic, productive growth. It is a place of absolute, normalcy for a world where geology and biology are the same brutal, industrious force. It is not weird; it is simply what happens here. You are an intruder in a system that is perfectly, turbulently, and loudly complete without you.
