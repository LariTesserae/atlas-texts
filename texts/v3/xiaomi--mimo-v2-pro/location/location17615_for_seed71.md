# The Thinning

---

The plateau has no name anyone can agree on, and the disagreement has lasted longer than most geological features on younger worlds. It is enormous. It is old beyond the useful meaning of that word. And it is dissolving—not into ruin, but into something gentler and more troubling.

## The Ground Itself

Imagine stone that has been under pressure since before the concept of pressure existed. Then imagine the pressure slowly, over timescales that would embarrass most planets, letting go.

The surface of the plateau is a dark, dense basalt-and-granite body scoured almost perfectly flat by repeated glaciations that came and went and came and went until the glaciers themselves grew tired and left for good. What remains is an undulating plain of wind-polished gravel, perhaps four thousand meters above any sea that might have once been relevant, extending in every direction to a horizon so distant that curvature—or what passes for curvature here—becomes a matter of faith.

The gravel is not uniform. Where frost-heave has had its longest, most patient way, the ground has sorted itself into polygons: dark basalt fragments ringed by lighter granite chips, each pattern three to eight meters across, tiling the surface like the hide of some impossibly vast creature. In places these polygons are pristine, almost decorative. In places they warp. There are zones where the polygons stop being hexagons and start being shapes that don't have names—not because no one has named them, but because the shapes themselves seem to shift when you're not looking directly at them. Locals, when asked, will say: *yes, it does that*.

Deeper down, the plateau is more aquifer than mountain. Water moves through it—not across it. The rock is so old, so fractured, so gently permeable after these aeons that moisture percolates downward through kilometers of stone in patterns that hydrologists would call "incoherent" and that the inhabitants simply call *the circulation*. It comes up elsewhere. Sometimes days later. Sometimes, reportedly, before it went down.

## Water

There is almost no standing water on the surface. Look across the plateau and you will see no lakes, no rivers, no streams. Just stone and gravel and wind and fog.

But the plateau is not dry. It is *damp* in a way that saturates everything without ever presenting itself as water. The air carries moisture at near-constant saturation. Fog banks form in depressions, linger for days, and dissipate without producing rain—or produce rain that evaporates before reaching the ground, or reaches the ground and immediately disappears into the polygon gravel as though the earth is thirsty. Frost forms on every exposed surface each night, painting the gravel fields in white lace that melts by midmorning and is gone.

And in certain places—always in certain places, never anywhere else—water breaches the surface. Springs, the locals call them, though the word doesn't capture the violence of emergence: a crack in the gravel suddenly full of dark, mineral-heavy water that wells up and spills outward in thin, cold sheets before vanishing again into the ground fifty meters downhill. These springs appear and disappear on cycles that no one has fully mapped. Some have been active for centuries. Some appear, flow for a season, and are never seen again. The mineral deposits they leave behind—pale rings of calcium and iron oxide on the dark stone—are the only bright color on the plateau for kilometers in any direction.

It is around these springs that life gathers.

## The Moss Kingdoms

Scattered across the vast, dark, gravel-plain like oases in a desert—but oases that sprawl outward for hectares, thick and vivid and wet—are the plant communities. The locals have no collective word for them. Each patch has its own name, its own character, its own small story.

They are, at first glance, astonishingly lush for a place this cold and this high. *Sphagnum* and relatives, thick as mattresses, dark green to almost black, so saturated with water that walking on them means sinking to the knee. Liverworts in sheets of deep jade. Lichens in colors that shouldn't exist at this latitude—vivid rust, ice-blue, a gray so pale it looks like powdered bone—growing on every stone surface the moss hasn't already claimed. Dwarf shrubs with waxy, wind-resistant leaves that grow in dense, low mats, never rising more than a hand's breadth above the moss. Grasses that grow in single, isolated tussocks, each one a small monument to persistence, surrounded by meters of bare gravel before the next tussock appears.

There are trees. Some of the larger patches—those near the most reliable springs, those in sheltered depressions—support stands of *something* that qualifies as tree only by the most charitable definition: wind-stunted, no taller than a person, trunks twisted and flanged, roots spreading laterally across the moss surface because there is permafrost below and the roots cannot go down. They grow so slowly that a trunk the thickness of a wrist might be centuries old. Their bark is black. Their leaves, in what passes for summer, are dark and thick and almost leathery, photosynthesizing with a grim, efficient reluctance.

But—and here is the thing that takes time to notice—the patches are *isolated*. Stand in a plant community and look outward and you will see, across one, two, five kilometers of bare gravel, another green smudge on the horizon. Between them: nothing. Stone. Wind. The slow, silent dissolution of a world.

This is what *sparse* means here. Not thin vegetation. *Island* vegetation, scattered across a sea of naked rock so vast that each patch is its own small, complete world, cut off from every other by distances that take days to walk.

And the isolation is not just spatial. The patches are *different from each other* in ways that shouldn't occur if they're part of the same ecosystem. One patch might be almost entirely sphagnum. The next, kilometers away, might be all lichen, with only a few mossy outcrops. A third might be dominated by a shrub species that exists nowhere else on the plateau. Botanists who visit—and they are rare—come away unsettled. The communities don't follow gradient logic. They don't sort by altitude or moisture or substrate in any consistent way. Each one seems to have *decided* what it wants to be, independently, without reference to its neighbors.

The locals shrug. *They grow where they can*, they say. *They become what the ground allows.*

## The Settlements

There are, by most estimates, between twenty and thirty settlements on the entire plateau. Each is separated from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of kilometers. Between them: gravel, fog, polygons, silence.

Each settlement is built around a spring, or where a spring once was, or where the inhabitants believe a spring will eventually be.

The construction is stone. Always stone—there is nothing else to build with. The blocks are dressed basalt and granite, cut with tools of bone and antler and copper so pure and soft it bends in the hand. The joints are sealed with a mortar that the inhabitants manufacture fromiteiteite andite and and and localite plantite calc and andite and mineral spring sediment—aiteite paste that hardens over years into somethingite chemically almost identical to the living bedrock. Walls are thick: a meter, sometimes more. Roists are flat stone slabs laid over timber frames of the twisted, wind-stunted trees, carried from the nearest moss patch, which might be days away.

The architecture is low. Nothing in a settlement rises more than two and a half meters above the ground surface. The buildings are partially buried—the ground has been excavated to create sunken courtyards and semi-subterranean chambers that use the thermal mass of the permafrost to maintain a stable, if cold, interior temperature. Doorways are narrow. Windows are small, thick, and made of sheets of translucent membrane—organ membrane, stretched and dried, from animals that have not existed on this plateau for longer than anyone can remember, replaced only with painstaking slowness using treated plant fiber.

The settlements are organized. Roads—if you can call them that—are paved with fitted gravel, curving in slow arcs between structures. The layout is not grid-based but radial: each settlement spreads outward from a central point, usually the original spring or a distinctive feature of the bedrock. Ring-walls of stone enclose the outermost structures, not for defense—there is nothing to defend against—but for wind, and perhaps for other things.

Some settlements are in ruin. Perhaps a third of them show signs of abandonment: roofs collapsed, walls tumbled, gravel slowly reclaiming the courtyards. But the ruin is not dramatic. It is slow, gentle, almost peaceful—a building losing its roof over the course of a century, a wall subsiding as the permafrost beneath it shifts by millimeters per year. The settlements don't fall. They *settle*. They return to the ground.

Those that remain inhabited are maintained by populations that might number in the dozens. Small enough that every person knows every other person's footsteps. Small enough that a single argument can alter the social landscape for a generation.

And the people—they build. They maintain. They repair. They do so with a slowness that matches the plateau itself. A mason might spend an entire season replacing a single cracked lintel. A farmer—that is, a moss-tender, someone who coaxes the plant patches to expand toward the settlement—might work for years to extend a lichen colony by a few meters. Everything here operates at the pace of geology, even the human things.

But there is something else about the construction. Something about the angles.

## The Drift

The walls of the oldest structures are not straight.

Not because they were poorly built. Not because of subsidence or frost-heave, though those things happen too. The walls are not straight because *straightness is not a stable property in this place*.

A wall built perfectly level and true in the morning will, by evening, exhibit a tilt so slight that only careful measurement reveals it. Not in any consistent direction—the tilt varies from wall to wall, from room to room. Over weeks, the tilt accumulates. Over months, it becomes visible to the naked eye. Over years, the building has its own lean, its own character, as individual as a face.

The inhabitants have adapted. They build *expecting* the drift. A master builder, laying a foundation, will deliberately introduce a counter-lean, calculated from experience and oral tradition, designed so that by the time the building has aged into its settling, the walls will have drifted *back* toward plumb. It doesn't always work. Some buildings end up with a permanent, gentle slant that the occupants accept as simply the way that building is. Children growing up in a tilted room develop a fine sense of balance that outsiders find uncanny.

But the drift goes deeper than architecture.

The ground polygon patterns shift—not quickly, but they shift. A path that was straight last year now has a slight curve. A landmark stone that was due east of the settlement gate is now slightly northeast. Not much. Millimeters. Centimeters, over longer periods. But *it moves*, and it moves in directions that don't correspond to any known force.

And there are zones.

Stand at the edge of a settlement and look outward across the gravel, and the horizon is distant and flat and reassuringly vast. But there are places—random, unpredictable, drifting slowly across the plateau like weather systems made of geometry—where the distance *contracts*. Where you walk toward what should be a five-kilometer-distant landmark and arrive in what feels like an hour. Where you can hear, faintly, sounds from a moss patch that should be two days' travel away: the drip of water, the settling of saturated moss, a low hum that might be wind through a shrub canopy.

The locals call these zones *nearings*. They are not dangerous, exactly. But they are disorienting. Compasses—which the inhabitants make from magnetite and floating bone needles—spin slowly in a nearing. The polygons on the ground become irregular, then lose their pattern entirely, then reform into *different* patterns that no one remembers having seen before.

The settlements have learned to mark nearings. Cairns of white stone—granite, chosen for its light color against the dark basalt—are placed at the boundaries, and moved as the boundaries move. A nearing that drifts toward a settlement is watched carefully. If it arrives, the affected buildings are abandoned—not because they become unsafe, but because *they become unreliable*. A room inside an active nearing might be larger on the inside than the outside. A doorway might open onto a courtyard that isn't where you left it. Children find this exciting. Adults find it tiring.

The nearings always pass. Eventually. Days, weeks, months. They drift through and away and the geometry restabilizes, though not always to exactly the same configuration as before.

## Sound

The plateau is quiet.

Not silent—there is always the wind. A low, steady, lateral wind that moves across the gravel with a faint hiss, like the world is slowly exhaling. It rarely rises to a howl. It rarely drops to nothing. It is a constant, the background frequency of this place, and after long enough here, people stop hearing it the way they stop hearing their own heartbeat.

But beyond the wind: quiet. No birdsong. No insect hum. No rustle of animal movement in the moss. The plant patches are silent places—water dripping, moss settling under its own weight with faint, wet sounds, the occasional crack of a frost-loosened stone. But nothing *moves through* them. Nothing hunts. Nothing calls.

The absence of animals is not partial. It is total. There are no mammals, no birds, no reptiles, no amphibians, no fish in the subterranean waterways, no insects, no spiders, no worms. The soil—the thin organic layer beneath the moss—is almost sterile: fungal hyphae, bacterial mats, decomposing plant matter, and nothing else. No nematodes. No tardigrades, even, though they were looked for.

This is not a recent absence. The oldest settlement records—carved in stone, in a script that is more pictographic than alphabetic, recording events in sequences measured in generations—do not mention animals. There are no depictions of fauna in any settlement art. There are no words for animal in the local language, which is otherwise rich in terms for types of silence, types of fog, types of stone surface.

The moss patches don't seem to miss them. Reproduction is vegetative, mostly—rhizome spread, fragment budding, spore dispersal by wind. Pollination, if it occurs at all, is anemophilous. The plants have never needed anything alive that isn't themselves.

The settlements are quiet, too. The inhabitants speak, but less than people elsewhere. Conversations are low, brief, often supplemented by gesture or by the slow, deliberate act of showing—leading someone to a place, pointing at a thing, doing a task together in shared silence. The architecture of social space reflects this: rooms are small, intimate, designed for two or three people at most. There are no large gathering halls. If the settlement needs to address itself, it does so in the open air, in the central courtyard, with the wind carrying the words away almost as soon as they are spoken.

It is a quiet place, and the quiet is not emptiness. It is *fullness of a different kind*—the fullness of wind on gravel, of stone settling, of water moving through deep rock, of moss growing so slowly that its movement is indistinguishable from stillness.

## The Deep Softening

But there is something else. Something the inhabitants discuss only in certain moods, in certain rooms, with certain people.

The plateau is old. Immeasurably, fundamentally old. It has been planed down to near-flatness by forces that have themselves long since ceased to exist. The bedrock is kilometers thick, but the *upper surface*—the zone where the rock has been exposed to atmosphere, to frost, to the slow infiltration of water, to whatever processes have been at work here for longer than anyone can conceive—has changed. Not eroded in the usual sense. *Softened*. The rock at the surface has become subtly, undeniably *different* from the rock below. More permeable. More responsive. More *receptive*.

The inhabitants believe—and their belief is supported by generations of observation, carved in stone records that take entire walls of semi-subterranean chambers to contain—that the plateau is approaching something. Not an ending. A *thinning*. The boundary between the surface and whatever lies beneath—or beyond, or *inside*—is becoming less firm. The nearings are symptoms. The drift is a symptom. The polygons shifting without cause, the springs appearing and disappearing, the moss patches that grow in defiance of all apparent environmental logic—these are the surface expressions of a deepening softness in the fabric of the place.

The oldest records speak of a time when the nearings were rarer, smaller, shorter. The rate has been increasing. Not linearly—there are long plateaus of stability, followed by sudden expansions. The current generation has experienced more nearings than any generation in the recorded history.

The inhabitants are not afraid. Fear is not the right word. They are *attentive*. They watch. They maintain their walls and their moss patches and their springs and their quiet, deliberate lives, and they watch. They speak of the softening the way one might speak of a season that is approaching—not with dread, but with the calm, focused awareness of people who have lived alongside a slow, vast, incomprehensible process for so long that it has become as natural as wind, as stone, as the quiet.

There is a phrase, used sparingly, that translates roughly as: *the ground remembers what it was before it was ground*.

No one can say what the plateau was before. The records don't go back that far. The stone itself, when examined, shows no fossils, no layered history—just deep, uniform, ancient crystalline basalt, a body of rock so old it predates its own ability to record what happened to it.

But the softening continues. The nearings grow. The geometry loosens. The springs multiply. The moss patches, in their slow, wordless, animal-less way, grow thicker, darker, more vivid, reaching toward something they can sense but cannot name.

And the people build their walls at odd angles, and lay their stones with counter-leans, and walk their gravel paths with the quiet, unhurried gait of inhabitants of a place that has always been coming to an end—not violently, not catastrophically, but with the patient, inexorable gentleness of stone finally, after all this time, letting go.

---

*The plateau has no name. It doesn't need one. It is the only thing here.*
