Here is the world that emerges from those parameters.

The sky is a pale, persistent blue, lit by a sun that feels distant and small. The light is normal—clear, shadow-defining—but it falls upon a landscape that has long forgotten the meaning of change. This is a place called the Sill. It is not a valley or a plain, but the worn crown of an unimaginably ancient mountain range, eroded over eons until only its highest, flattest, most stubborn heart remains. You stand on a plateau of such immense, regional scale that its edges are not horizons, but gradual, imperceptible fades into a haze of distance. The elevation is extreme, but the feeling is not of height, but of exposed, primordial depth. The air is thin and cool, carrying no moisture, no scent of growth, only the dry, mineral taste of rock dust.

The ground is a dense, layered field of stone. It is not sand, but a brittle, intricate lace of oxidized iron and pale quartz, shattered into tessellated polygons by a billion years of thermal breathing that stopped long ago. These polygons, some as wide as a house, fit together in a near-seamless mosaic, a shattered skin over the bones of the world. Between them, in hairline fractures, you might see a darker, glassier stone—a primordial basalt, a memory of the first cooling. The density is high; every square inch is occupied by this fractal conversation of mineral.

And there is the fauna. The term feels wrong. They are the Overwhelming Presence. They do not move, not in the way living things do, yet the air, the ground, the very space between mineral flakes is *saturated* with them. They are static geometries imposed upon reality. Some are perfect spheres of silent, rotating force that hover a hand’s breadth above the stone, their surfaces mirroring a distorted, cleaner version of the landscape. Others are frozen lattices of faint, hard light, extending from the ground in crystalline arrays that defy the eye’s attempt to parse their dimensions. They intersect. They occupy the same space as the rock without disturbing it. Their scale is vast—a single geometric ghost might stretch from your feet to the distant haze, a silent, immobile scaffolding woven into the fabric of the place. They are the reason for the high weirdness: here, reality is not broken, but *settled* into a state of layered, non-interacting truths. A rock is a rock, and it is also the anchor-point for a looping, transparent helix of what might be time, frozen into structure. The two states coexist, dense and passive, in eternal equilibrium.

Sound is nearly absent. The only quiet whispers are the occasional, subliminal creak of the world’s deep tectonic patience, and a faint, high-frequency hum—or is it a mental pressure?—emitting from the geometric fauna. There is no wind. The atmosphere is still, sealed.

Upon this mosaic, under the watch of the silent shapes, are the built marks. They are scattered, faint, and humble. Perhaps a circle of weathered stones, not arranged for habitation but for some forgotten, primitive observation. Elsewhere, a single slab of darker basalt, tilted so that its face aligns perfectly with the annual arc of the pale sun, its surface pecked with constellations that do not match the current sky. A low, curving wall that protects nothing, built from the local polygonal flakes with simple ingenuity, its mortar long powdered to dust. These are the traces of a people—perhaps the first people—who understood this place as both sacred and final. Their technology was primitive, attuned to stone and alignment. They did not build cities here. They built markers, acknowledgements. They learned, perhaps, to listen to the hum of the geometries, to map their intersections, to accept the world’s final, unchanging form.

Nothing decays, because decay requires change. Nothing grows. The low vegetation—0.6 on a scale of life—is itself a contradiction. In certain sheltered hollows where the polygons dip, there are patches of something that might be called lichen, but it is not biological. It is a slow, crystalline growth of mineral, a fractal bloom of copper silicate and purple fluorite, spreading microns per century. It is sparse, yet beautiful, and utterly inert.

This is the Sill. A primordial height, flattened by time, crowded with the silent, overwhelming architecture of a reality that has layered itself into permanent stillness. It is not dead. It is finished. It breathes in epochs, and the scattered artifacts of its brief, primitive inhabitants are merely the last, quiet punctuation at the end of a very long sentence. The water is gone, locked in deep, dry mineral veins. The temperature is cool, a heat-death warmth. The dynamic is zero. Here, on the roof of a dead world, under the gaze of eternal geometries, change has finally, completely stopped.
