The sky is the color of wet slate, low and heavy, pressing down on a world that seems half-dissolved. This is the Basin of Murmurs, an ancient, shallow depression where the very rock has been weeping for eons. Water is everywhere, but not in rivers or lakes. It seeps, drips, and condenses. Every surface is slick with a perpetual film of moisture. Great, rounded boulders, smoothed to a soft gloss by unimaginable time, are streaked with dark, wet veins. From fissures in their faces, slow, constant trickles of water emerge, not with the sound of a splash, but with a soft, endless *hiss*, as if the stone itself is exhaling.

Vegetation is absent. There are no roots, no leaves, no green. The ground is a mosaic of dark, waterlogged clay and vast, shallow pools of still, tea-colored water that reflect the dim, silvery light. The only "plants" are mineral formations: forests of delicate, translucent gypsum crystals that jut from the clay in sparse clusters, and sprawling, brittle mats of iron-red concretions that crackle underfoot like eggshells. The air is cool and so saturated with moisture that breathing feels like drinking.

Yet, this is no silent, dead world. It is deafening. The sound is not of wind or life, but of water and stone in constant, slow-motion conversation. The dripping is a million tiny impacts, a white-noise roar that fills the head. Beneath it, from deep within the earth, comes a low, resonant *groan*—the sound of bedrock, saturated and heavy, shifting its ancient weight. Occasionally, a larger concretion mat will collapse into a pool with a sudden, brittle *crash* that echoes briefly through the haze.

Life is here, but it is not life as recognized. The fauna is abundant and strange, perfectly adapted to this mineral world. In the shallow pools, colonies of **glass-eels**—translucent, silicon-based creatures—filter mineral flakes from the water, their bodies chiming faintly as they brush against each other. On the crystal spires, **condensation lice**—tiny, slow-moving arthropods with shells of calcium carbonate—lick moisture from the facets. The most visible creatures are the **mud-skimmers**, flat, multi-legged beings that skate across the surface tension of the pools, their undersides trailing filaments that absorb dissolved minerals. They leave no wake, only faint, geometric ripples. Predation is a slow, chemical affair; a mud-skimmer might become encased in a sudden, rapid crystallization triggered by a rival's secretion.

Scattered across this vista are faint marks of a prior or peripheral intelligence. Not buildings, but **cairns**—carefully stacked arrangements of the most resonant, bell-like stones. Some are simple piles; others form low, circular walls that seem to channel the weeping water into specific patterns. They are old beyond measure, their edges rounded by the same patient erosion that shapes the boulders. No tools lie nearby, only the occasional, perfectly spherical stone that fits a mud-skimmer's manipulator limb.

The world is active. The groaning of the rock is a constant dynamic. Weather cycles are local and strange: a patch of ground might suddenly belch a column of warm, steamy air, creating a brief, swirling vortex that whips condensation into a tiny, roaring cyclone before dissipating. The mud-skimmers migrate in slow, pulsing waves toward these events, harvesting the briefly activated minerals. The ecosystem is in motion, but the pace is geologic, patient, and deeply, profoundly settled. This is a world at the end of its creative time, still profoundly alive in its own slow, wet, mineral way, humming with the deafening sound of its own ancient, stable decay.
