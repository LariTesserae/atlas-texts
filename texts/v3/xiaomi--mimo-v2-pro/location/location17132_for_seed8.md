The ground is not so much ground as a consolidation of time and moisture. It is a world of mud and shallow water, where the distinction between land and water has been arguing for millennia and has reached a weary, saturated truce. The elevation is low—so low that the air itself seems to pool in the depressions, thick and warm (2.4/3) and carrying the mineral breath of wet stone (2.3/3).

You stand on a vast, gently undulating plain of primordial mud (erosion: 2.9/3). It is ancient mud, the final, refined product of mountains that rose and were worn away so long ago their memory is only a slight thickening in the sedimentary layers. The color is a deep umber, shading to charcoal grey where it is perpetually soaked. It is not sticky, but smooth and cool an inch below the sun-warmed surface. Cracks are rare; the water table is a hand’s breadth down, and weeps constantly from any exposed face.

Here and there, the mud gives way to islands of rock. They are not boulders, but the polished, exposed skulls of the bedrock itself—dark basalt and granite, smoothed by eons of rainfall and chemical dissolution into forms that are more organic than mineral. They look like sleeping animals, or the rounded vertebrae of some colossal, buried beast. Every surface is dimpled with potholes and etched with flowing grooves where water runs in the frequent rains, tracing paths of least resistance that have been the same for ten thousand years. From the shaded undersides and deep pores of these stones, water weeps steadily, feeding glistening black mosses and creating a constant, soft *drip-drip-drip* that is a fundamental rhythm of this place.

Vegetation is sparse (1.2/3), a deliberate afterthought in this landscape of stone and water. There are no trees. Instead, on the slightly higher, firmer mud banks, grow stands of reedy, pale-green horsetails, their prehistoric silhouettes sharp against the sky. Patches of a spongy, lime-green moss colonize the weeping rocks, forming thick, wet cushions. In the shallows of the countless ponds and trickles, long ribbons of dark green algae drift in the slow currents, their strands occasionally breaking free to form rafts that meander across the open water. The absence of dense foliage opens the landscape up (scale: 2.1/3, density: 1.3/3), giving it a regional, melancholic breadth. You can see for miles across the shimmering plain, to where distant rock islands look like a scattering of dark seeds.

The fauna (1.6/3) is abundant but largely unseen, a presence felt and heard more than observed. The sound is loud (2.0/3), a layered chorus of survival. The constant drip of water provides the baseline. Over this, the high, electric buzz of unseen insects—the tiny, resilient inheritors of this humid world—creates a shimmering haze of sound. From the deeper ponds comes the occasional, resonant *plonk* of a frog, its call hollow and echoing. In the sky, broad-winged, solitary birds with long, forked tails wheel silently on the warm updrafts, their sharp eyes scanning the water for the quick flash of a scaled side. In the mud, trails tell of things that drag themselves from pool to pool: the looping path of a gastropod, the delicate, paired prints of a wading bird, the sinuous, pressed-down channel of something that preferred to slide.

The light is normal (2.3/3), but it has a quality defined by the air. The warm, condensing atmosphere catches the sun, making it a white, hazy disc. The light is bright but diffuse, casting soft-edged shadows and making every wet surface gleam with a patient, perpetual shine. There is no sharpness here, only gradation.

Nothing is built (0.4/3). No stone is stacked, no path is paved. The only artifacts are the slow, geologic arrangements of the stones themselves and the faint, transient marks of life. This is a world operating at a pace (dynamic: 0.8/3) that makes human presence feel like a frantic, irrelevant buzzing. Change here is measured in the millimeter’s creep of mud over a century, in the slow, sure collapse of a rock island into its own reflection, in the imperceptible shift of a watercourse that has flowed the same way since the last ice age receded.

To be here is to feel the profound, humid weight of deep time. It is a landscape that has finished becoming and is now simply *being*, a quiet, loud, wet, and ancient meditation on erosion and endurance. The world’s rules are written in water and stone: everything flows, everything is worn down, and in the end, everything settles into a vast, patient, and saturated equilibrium.
