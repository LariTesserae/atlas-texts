# The Reservoir

You stand on a ledge of fitted stone three handspans above the waterline. The room is perhaps twelve paces across and seven tall — not a cavern, not a tunnel, but a chamber, deliberate, enclosed. Every surface is made. The floor, the walls, the corbeled ceiling arching overhead — all of it laid by hand, block upon block, the mortar lines thick and uneven in the way of early masonry. No natural rock is exposed anywhere. The stone is a pale tufa, calcium-heavy, and it sweats. Condensation beads on every surface and runs in thin rivulets down toward the water that fills the lower third of the room.

The water is warm. Not tepid — genuinely warm, like bathwater left to sit in a sun-struck cistern, though there is no sun here. It rises from somewhere below, fed by a channel cut through the base of the far wall, a rectangular opening barely wide enough to admit a crouching person. The flow is constant and audible: a thick, churning sound, the water forcing itself through the narrow throat of the channel and spreading into the chamber with a low roar that fills the space entirely. It is loud here. The sound has nowhere to go but back on itself, reflecting off the stone ceiling and walls until the air itself seems to vibrate with the pressure of it. You would need to raise your voice to speak to someone standing beside you.

The water enters through that channel and exits through another — a weir cut into the near wall at a slightly higher elevation, so the room maintains a steady level, always just below the ledge where you stand. Overflow spills through the weir and is gone. The water in the chamber is clear but not still. It moves in slow gyres, turning on itself, carrying with it a fine pale sediment that catches what light there is and holds it.

And there is light — startlingly bright for a room with no sky. Three long slots have been cut through the upper wall on the eastern side, angled upward through the mass of the structure toward the outside world. They are too narrow to see through, but they channel daylight downward in three distinct shafts, each one dense with suspended moisture, so that the beams are visible as solid planes of white-gold light cutting across the room. They strike the water's surface and refract, filling the lower space with a shimmering, restless illumination. The upper walls and ceiling receive no direct light but are painted with it indirectly — reflected off the water, off the wet stone — so that the entire room is bright, brighter than most rooms you have stood in above ground. There is no corner dark enough to hide anything.

Vegetation here is minimal but insistent. The waterline marks a band of green — thin films of algae coating the stone in slick, bright mats where the light reaches. A single root system descends from a crack in the ceiling masonry, pale and threadlike, dangled into the water by some plant growing on the structure above. It has no leaves down here, only the roots, which sway gently in the current. Further along the western wall, someone has set shallow trays of cut stone into the masonry at the waterline, and in these trays a few small plants grow — leafy, low, water-fed. Not a garden. Not really. More like a gesture toward one. The plants are spaced far apart. Most of the trays are empty, their soil dark and damp.

There is no animal life. No fish in the water — it moves too fast through the channel, perhaps, or the space is too small, too sealed. No insects. Once, you might see a tiny translucent shrimp-like thing drift past in the current, but it is gone before you are sure of it. The room belongs to water and stone and the sounds they make together.

The air is warm and heavy with moisture. You breathe water as much as air. Your skin is filmed with it within moments. The stone underfoot is slick wherever the spray from the channel reaches, and the masonry above the waterline is furred with a fine pale mineral deposit — calcium, left behind as the warm water evaporates from the surfaces. It gives the upper walls a soft, encrusted texture, almost like frost, but warm to the touch. The whole room smells of wet stone and the faint alkaline tang of dissolved minerals.

The channel churns. The overflow spills. The light shifts slowly as the sun moves outside, and the three bright columns of illumination creep across the water and up the western wall over the course of the hours. The process is steady, predictable, and unceasing. This room was built to do one thing — to hold and move water — and it is doing it, and it has been doing it for a long time, and the masonry shows no sign of stopping. The blocks are fitted well enough to last. The mortar holds. The channels have not silted closed.

There is a set of shallow steps cut into the ledge where you stand, descending into the water — three steps, then the floor drops away. They are worn smooth on their treads, the edges rounded by uncountable instances of feet descending to check the channel, to clear a blockage, to harvest whatever small plants grow in the stone trays. No one is here now. But the steps suggest a regularity of use, a maintenance rhythm, an intention that persists even if the hands that carried it out are absent.

The room is small enough that you can take it in at a glance. There is nothing hidden. There is no depth to explore beyond what you see. It is a single functioning object — a chamber, a reservoir, a junction in some larger system of waterworks that extends beyond these walls in directions you cannot see. What matters here is only what is present: the water, the light, the sound, the warmth, the smooth worn stone under your feet. Everything is known. Everything is close. The world beyond the walls is irrelevant to the small bright loud warm wet room that is doing exactly what it was made to do.
