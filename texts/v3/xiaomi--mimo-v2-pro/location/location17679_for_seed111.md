# The Cauldron of the Calling Dark

You would not find this place by looking for it. You would find it by almost falling into it.

---

**The Rim.**

At 4,700 meters the mountain has not finished becoming. The rock is very young — geologically speaking, a wound still scabbing. Basalt ridges thrust upward in angular plates, some of them sharp enough to draw blood if you brace a hand wrong, slick with a black mineral sheen that hasn't had centuries of rain to round into something hospitable. Frost has begun its work in the crevices but hasn't had time to do much. The edges of the formation are violent and definite: cliff faces that terminate in clean breaks, as though the stone were snapped rather than eroded. In places you can see the glassy striations of the last eruption — not old. Decades, not centuries.

The wind at the rim is thin and cutting and smells of sulfur and cold iron. The sky above is the particular blue-black of extreme altitude, bruised at the edges by a slow-banking wall of cloud that has been rolling toward this peak from the west for what looks like days. The cloud will arrive. It always arrives. It is the engine of everything below.

Where the rim breaks open — a notch on the eastern face, barely two meters wide — there is a crack in the basalt that leads down.

You go down.

---

**The Descent.**

Thirty meters of near-vertical fissure, hand-and-knee work in the dark. The rock is wet. Not flowing — *weeping*. Moisture has condensed on every surface in a continuous film, and the fissure funnels wind upward in a slow, warm exhalation that carries sound with it. As you descend, the sound grows.

It is not one sound. It is a density of sound.

First you hear the dripping: condensation releasing from stone in irregular pulses, striking ledges and pooled water below with a pitch that varies by depth. Then you hear the rustling — a dry, papery, *many-jointed* rustling, like a library of old maps being shuffled by uncountable hands. Then you begin to hear the calls.

The calls are what will stay with you.

---

**The Cauldron.**

The fissure opens into a chamber approximately 180 meters across and perhaps 90 meters from floor to ceiling — cathedral-scale, but contained, bounded on all sides by dark basalt walls that rise to a dome where the cloud-water condenses and falls in a perpetual fine mist. The floor is a shallow bowl of broken scree andite rubble, ankle-deep in places, with a braided network of tiny streams running toward a central depression where the water collects in a black, still pool maybe fifteen meters wide. The pool is fed from above and from dozens of seepage cracks in the walls, and it loses water to the rock beneath it — how deep it goes, no one in this place has ever known or wondered.

There is almost no light.

What light exists comes from three sources: the faint phosphorescence of a lichen-like organism that grows in thin, vein-like traceries along the upper walls of the dome, emitting a blue-white glow so dim it takes minutes for your eyes to register it; the occasional bioluminescent flash from within the pool itself, brief and amber, like something turning over in sleep; and — if you time your visit with the season — a two-week window in late autumn when the sun's angle drops low enough to strike through the eastern fissure and send a single blade of grey-gold light across the cauldron floor for approximately forty minutes each afternoon. The creatures orient to this light. It is the only time you will see the floor clearly.

Otherwise: near-darkness. Wet darkness. A darkness that breathes.

---

**The Fauna.**

They are everywhere.

The walls are furred with them. From a distance, in the faint bioluminescent glow, the cauldron's interior looks almost *textured* — like the inside of a living throat, ridged and glistening. Move closer and the texture resolves into bodies.

They are small — most of them between the size of a thumb and the size of a hand. They are not one species. They are a community, a parliament, an *occupation*. The largest contingent is a winged invertebrate, six-limbed, with a flattened body and membraneous wings that fold against the dorsal surface like closed umbrellas. Their carapaces are dark — deep brown, near-black — and slightly translucent, so that when the bioluminescent lichen catches them at the right angle you can see the slow peristaltic movement of their organs within. They roost on the walls in layers, stacked and interlocking, gripping the stone with hooked tarsal claws and gripping each other with the same. When disturbed — when you descend, for instance, and dislodge a handhold — a ripple moves through them. Not a flight response. More of a *shudder*. A collective intake of breath. Then they resettle.

They are the source of the rustling sound. Thousands of wings adjusting. Thousands of bodies shifting weight.

Among them, larger creatures move. A slow, pale salamander-analog, eyeless, perhaps 30 centimeters long, that navigates the wall crevices with lateral undulation and feeds on the winged invertebrates by simply *opening its mouth* and waiting. They walk into it. It is not a hunt. It is an arrangement. The salamander's skin is translucent to the point of being nearly invisible against the wet stone; you can see its pale liver, its slow-beating heart.

In the pool, something larger. You don't see it clearly. You see its effects: the occasional amber flash, a disturbance in the surface-film of condensation that radiates outward in concentric rings, a low subsonic vibration that you feel in your molars more than hear. The pool-thing is the reason the winged invertebrates do not drink from the central water. They drink from the wall-seepage, the dripping condensation, the tiny braided streams. They avoid the pool. Everything avoids the pool.

At the edges of the cauldron floor, where the scree meets the walls, there are denser colonies — organisms that are harder to classify. Clusters of what might be fungi or might be animals: bulbous, moist, pulsing faintly, each the size of a closed fist, anchored to the rock by root-like filaments. They emit a faint, sweet, rotting smell. They are eaten by something. At night — not that there is day here, but during the deeper periods of darkness when even the lichen seems to dim — you can hear the *chitinous, methodical crunching* of something feeding on them. You will not see what. You will hear its mouthparts.

And in the air — always in the air — the calls.

The winged invertebrates vocalize. This is the part that will stay with you. They produce sound by scraping specialized ridges on their second and third limbs together — a stridulation — amplified by the resonance of the cauldron's dome. Individually, each call is a thin, bright, almost musical *tik*, like a single drop of water hitting a crystal glass. But there are thousands of them, and they do not call in unison. They call in *patterns* — cascading, overlapping, rising and falling in waves that move across the walls like weather systems. The sound bounces off the dome and returns to them slightly altered, and they adjust their timing in response. The result is a soundscape of extraordinary complexity: a shimmering, clicking, rippling roar that fills the cauldron at all hours, louder during the mist-heaviest periods when the cloud spills over the rim and feeds the condensation, softer when the air dries for brief intervals. It is not cacophony. It has structure. It has something that, if you are inclined to pattern-recognition, begins to sound like *syntax*.

Whether it *is* syntax — whether the calls mean anything beyond territorial assertion or mating display — is not a question the cauldron is interested in answering.

---

**Vegetation.**

Almost none. The lichen on the upper walls is the primary autotroph, supplemented by chemosynthetic bacterial mats in the pool margins that metabolize the sulfur compounds seeping up from below. A few thin, rootless grasses grow in the crevices where the fissure-light reaches, pale and etiolated, more white than green, like things that have forgotten the sun. They are grazed by the winged invertebrates, which descend in spiraling columns during the light-hours to feed, and the grasses never grow more than a few centimeters before being consumed. A cycle of almost — the grasses attempt, the invertebrates arrive, the grasses are reset. It is not a struggle. It is a rhythm.

---

**The Mood.**

The cauldron is wet and dark and *full*. It has the density of a held breath. The creatures press against every surface, fill every crevice, layer upon layer, and the sound they make is constant and enveloping and strange — not hostile, not welcoming, simply *present*, the way a river is present, the way a mountain is present. You are inside something. You are inside something that has been here — in this configuration, this arrangement of predator and prey and dripping stone and stridulating dark — for not very long, geologically speaking. The rock remembers when it was molten. The creatures do not remember anything. They are new here. Everything is new here. The sharp edges of the basalt are proof: nothing has had time to smooth them, nothing has had time to accumulate, nothing has had time to *settle*.

And yet it feels settled. It feels complete. The cauldron has arrived at its fullness quickly, the way a room fills with smoke — not gradually but all at once, the way saturation works, the way a threshold is crossed. The fauna is overwhelming not because it is large but because it is *everywhere*, every niche occupied, every surface claimed, every cubic meter of air inhabited by wings and calls and the smell of damp chitin.

You sit at the edge of the scree, in the near-dark, and listen. The calls wash over you. The mist settles on your skin and on your clothes and on the sharp stone beneath you. Somewhere in the pool, something shifts, and the amber light pulses once, twice, and goes out.

The cauldron does not notice you. The cauldron has never noticed anything. It is too busy being itself — young and strange and full and loud and dark and wet and *alive*, so alive it hums, so alive the stone itself seems to breathe with the slow collective exhalation of ten thousand small bodies settling deeper into sleep.

---

You climb back out through the fissure. The wind at the rim hits you like a door opening into winter. The cloud has arrived; it pours over the western edge of the mountain in a slow grey river and spills down into the cauldron behind you, feeding the mist, feeding the calls, feeding the dark.

You are wet. You are cold. You are loud with the memory of sound.

The rock beneath your hand is sharp and young and still growing.
