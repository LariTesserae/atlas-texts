# The Weeping Terraces of Vassuul

You arrive—or you are already here, which amounts to the same thing—standing on a broad shelf of dark stone that extends in every direction like the floor of an enormous, ruined ballroom. The stone is warm underfoot. Not sun-warm. It radiates from within, a low geological heat that would be comforting if the air weren't already so oppressively hot that your skin begins to slick with moisture within moments.

There is no sun. There is no visible source of light at all, and yet the world is not fully dark. A pallid luminescence clings to the air itself, unlocatable, equal in all directions, casting no shadows—or rather, casting shadows so faint and so diffuse that they seem to arrive from everywhere at once, giving every surface a soft, grey, dreamlike overexposure. The sky, if it is a sky, is a low dark ceiling of cloud or smoke or something denser, something that seems not so far above the highest structures. It has a texture like wet linen. It moves, slowly, in directions that don't correspond to any wind you can feel.

## The water

Water is the dominant fact of this place. Not as rivers, not as rain, not as any form you'd recognize from a living landscape. The stone beneath you is saturated. Step anywhere on the terrace and your foot leaves a dark print that fills slowly with a sheen of moisture, the rock sweating from below, from inside, from a water table that must sit inches beneath the surface. Pools form in every depression—not puddles, exactly, because they have a strange stillness, a surface tension that seems too strong, the water sitting in basins like mercury, like something that doesn't quite want to be here.

In places, water runs in thin vertical threads down the faces of buildings—not streams, not even trickles, but steady weeping lines that descend twenty or thirty feet and never accelerate, never pool at the base, simply feeding into the stone and vanishing. Other walls are slick with a continuous film of moisture that catches the shadowless light and makes the buildings appear to breathe, to expand and contract with each slow pulse of condensation. The air itself is thick with humidity. You can taste it—flat, mineral, faintly alkaline—and you can see it when you look toward the horizon, which is not a line but a gradient, the world fading upward into haze and then into nothing.

Some structures have roofs that collect the moisture. Funnel-shaped capstones of carved stone channel water down interior columns, and inside these buildings you can hear the sound: a low, continuous *dripping*, steady as a heartbeat, the only rhythmic noise in a landscape that is otherwise profoundly quiet.

## The city

Because this is a city. That much is unmistakable, even in the dim half-light, even though the architecture is unlike anything in any living tradition.

The buildings rise in irregular clusters across the terrace, spaced wide apart—far wider than any functional city would allow. Each structure stands alone in a field of dark, wet stone, separated from its neighbors by fifty, a hundred, two hundred yards of empty, glistening floor. The effect is of a graveyard, or a forest of monuments, each one occupying its own private darkness.

They are built from the same stone as the ground—a dense, fine-grained basite, almost black when dry, dark grey when wet, which is always. The construction is sophisticated: interlocking blocks fitted without mortar, the joints so precise that water beads on the surface rather than seeping between. Some walls lean at angles that should be structurally impossible, curving inward or cantilevering outward with no visible support, as though the stone has a memory of its own geometry and holds itself in place through some principle older than gravity.

The buildings vary in scale but share a common grammar. Narrow vertical slits for windows—or not windows, exactly, but openings that run the full height of a wall, letting the pale light through in thin luminous bars. Doorways that are too tall, too narrow, proportioned for something with a different relationship to passage. Roofs that are either flat and pooled with still water, or peaked in steep angles that shed moisture in those strange vertical threads. No stairs visible on any exterior—though some structures have ramps that spiral slowly upward, wide enough for a cart, worn smooth by something that used them over an unfathomable span of time.

Some buildings have collapsed. Their ruins are notable not for the destruction but for how *orderly* it is: blocks that have fallen remain in their original orientation, stacked neatly where they landed, as though the building simply lay down rather than breaking apart. Moss grows on none of them. No vegetation of any kind has colonized the ruins. The stone simply weathers, slowly, its edges rounding, its faces smoothing, returning to the terrace from which it was cut.

Here and there, larger structures dominate. A cylindrical tower, windowless, perhaps a hundred feet tall, its surface carved in a continuous spiral groove that channels water in a slow corkscrew down its full height. A low, sprawling complex of interconnected chambers, open to the air now, its roof long gone, its interior visible: rows of stone shelves or benches, all empty, all the same size, too narrow for sleeping, too high for sitting. A bridge—or what was a bridge—that spans a shallow basin full of that mercury-still water, its arch sagging slightly in the middle, its railings carved with patterns that might be script or might be erosion, you cannot tell.

There is no decoration. No murals, no statues, no symbols affixed to walls. The buildings are purely themselves—pure structure, pure shelter, pure void—and yet they are clearly *made*, clearly the product of intention and craft. Whoever built this city built it to last, and it has lasted, but it has lasted past the point of any living memory. The stone remembers. Nothing else does.

## The absence

This is what strikes you most, once the initial strangeness fades. The *absence*.

There are no people. No bodies. No remains of any kind—not bones, not implements, not refuse. The interiors of the buildings are empty. The shelves and benches hold nothing. The streets—if the wide stone paths between buildings deserve that name—hold no litter, no lost objects, no evidence that anything has ever walked them except the slow accumulation of condensation.

There are no animals. You would expect, in a place this wet, some sign of life—algae on the standing water, insects in the air, something. There is nothing. The water is clear to the point of invisibility. The air is clean. The stone is bare. If you press your hand to a wall and hold it there, you feel the warmth of the rock and the steady seep of moisture, but you feel no vibration, no pulse, nothing that suggests life beneath or within. The city is metabolically inert. It sweats, but it does not breathe.

And yet.

The dynamic quality of the place is undeniable. Things are *happening*. Water is always moving, always condensing, always weeping downward. The light, sourceless as it is, seems to shift in subtle ways—not brightening or dimming, but *rearranging*, as though the geography of illumination is slowly realigning. The low clouds move. The haze thickens and thins. Somewhere in the middle distance, at the edge of sight in this flattened, humid atmosphere, something might be rising—a new structure, a column of steam, a fold in the terrace you hadn't noticed before. Or it might be nothing. The eye cannot settle here. The landscape resists being watched.

## The edges

The city does not end cleanly. Walking outward from any point, the buildings become less frequent—the gaps between them widening from hundreds of yards to quarter-miles, to half-miles, the structures shrinking or sinking or simply stopping, until you are standing on an empty expanse of warm, wet stone that extends to the haze in every direction, featureless and vast and yet somehow still *urban*, still part of the plan, still bearing the stamp of construction. The terrace itself is built. The entire basin is a floor. Whoever made this place made everything, all the way down.

At the lowest points—and this city occupies a depression, a basin, a place that collects water the way a bowl collects rain—the stone gives way to shallow lagoons of impossibly clear water, warm as blood, their bottoms invisible in the grey light. You can wade into them. The stone slopes gently. The water reaches your ankles, your knees, your waist, and never gets deeper. You can walk out for what feels like miles, always waist-deep, always on smooth stone, always in that sourceless light, and the city is behind you now, a dark irregular line on the horizon, and ahead there is only water and haze and the faintest sense that the world curves downward, that you are walking into a depression that has no bottom, or whose bottom is the place where water and stone are the same thing, where condensation becomes geology, where the city's foundation dissolves into the water table that feeds it.

You turn back. The city waits, patient and wet and dark.

## The weird

Something is wrong with the geometry.

You notice it gradually. A street that you walked down in one direction seems longer when you walk back. A building that appeared cylindrical from one angle appears flattened, almost two-dimensional, from another—as though it exists only in certain perspectives, only when observed from the correct orientation. The spiral tower can be seen from every point in the city, no matter how far you walk or what structures stand between you and it. It is always visible, always in the distance, always the same apparent size.

The shadows, faint as they are, occasionally move in ways that don't correspond to the objects casting them. A column's shadow bends. A doorway's shadow falls *upward*. You catch these things in peripheral vision and they resolve when you look directly, but the resolution feels wrong, feels like a correction, like the world is smoothing itself out in response to your attention.

Time is difficult to track. You sleep—or you think you sleep—and wake to find the light unchanged, the moisture unchanged, the heat unchanged. You feel rested but cannot say how long you rested. You feel hungry but cannot remember when you last ate. The city provides no food. The water is drinkable—you test this with great reluctance—but it tastes of nothing, slides down your throat without registering, satisfies no thirst even as it fills your stomach.

The air carries sounds that shouldn't exist in a place with no animals and no wind: a distant, low *hum* that might be the stone vibrating, or might be the water table resonating, or might be something else entirely—something living in the substrate, something that predates the city, something that the city was built to contain, or to feed, or to worship. The hum is not constant. It fades in and out over spans of minutes or hours, and when it fades, the silence is so absolute that you can hear your own blood moving through your ears.

## The rules

Nothing rots here. Nothing decays. The city weathers, yes—the stone rounds, the edges soften—but organic matter, if you were to leave something behind, would simply *stop*, would enter a state of perfect preservation, held in the warm wet air like an insect in amber. The city is a museum of itself, exhibiting only itself, forever.

Nothing grows here. The water is too pure, the stone too inert, the light too wrong for photosynthesis. Seeds would lie dormant. Spores would remain spores. The absence of vegetation is not a failure but a *condition*, a fundamental rule of the place: this is a landscape of mineral and water and construction, and it has been so for so long that biology has become an irrelevant category.

Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. The haze at the horizon is not a boundary you can cross. Walk toward it and the city stretches with you, new buildings materializing in your peripheral vision, the terrace extending, the water deepening and then shallowing again, always returning you to the center, always returning you to the city. You are inside something. You have always been inside something.

The city is awake. The city is watching you with every one of its too-tall doorways and its weeping walls and its shadowless light. It does not wish you harm. It does not wish you anything. It simply *is*, and you are inside it, and the water is rising very, very slowly, and the hum is getting louder, and the stone beneath your feet is warm, and it has always been warm, and it will always be warm, and this is home now.

This has always been home.
