# The Foundry Ranges

---

The mountains here are wrong. Not metaphorically — structurally. They rise in plates and shards of raw basalt and granite that haven't yet learned how to be mountains, haven't weathered into the comfortable archetypes of peak and ridge and saddle. They lean at angles that suggest arrested thrust, as though the tectonic forces that pushed them upward simply stopped mid-motion and forgot to finish. The rock faces are sharp. They cut. Fault lines gape open a half-meter wide in places, exhaling warm, sulfurous breath into air that otherwise stays just above freezing. The geology is *emerging* — you can watch it, if you're patient, the way you can watch a potter's wheel turn and sense the shape arriving before it's arrived.

This is a place that is still becoming.

And yet people are already here. A lot of them.

---

## The City of Koralath

Koralath doesn't settle the mountains so much as it *attaches* to them — a sprawling municipal organism of iron catwalks, timber scaffolding, and concrete anchors bolted into stone that's only been stone for a few hundred years. The city extends across roughly forty square kilometers of mid-elevation terrain (if "mid-elevation" can describe a band of ledges and tilted plateaus averaging 4,200 meters above the nearest sea). It is a town-sized thing with city-sized ambitions, layered vertically as much as horizontally. Some neighborhoods exist entirely under other neighborhoods. The lowest levels sit inside the fault-clefts themselves, lit by foundry glow and humming with the labor of turbines.

The built environment is industrial in the most literal and immersive sense: this is a city whose primary civic sound is the rhythmic percussion of compressors and stamp-mills. Smokestacks punctuate the skyline at irregular intervals, each one a different height, each one venting a slightly different shade of grey. Electrical cabling runs in visible bundles along exterior walls and across open-air bridges, sparking faintly in the damp. Everything mechanical here is *big* — overscaled, overbuilt, designed to be serviced rather than replaced. The aesthetic is not sleek. It is riveted, welded, shored-up with secondary beams. Infrastructure is layered on top of infrastructure. You can read the city's expansion history in its cross-sections: the oldest sections are buried deepest, already half-incorporated into the living rock by mineral seep and time.

Koralath is sparse for an urban place. The structures are large but the spaces between them are vast — broad courtyards of naked stone, open plazas where the wind has its way. Population density is low relative to the built footprint. Many buildings sit half-empty, or quarter-occupied, or serve purposes that seem to have more to do with anchoring the city structurally than housing anyone. You get the sense that Koralath was built for more people than currently live here, or perhaps built for people who haven't arrived yet.

## The Light

It is never bright.

The sun exists — you can find it if you climb above the cloud-line, which sits permanently at around 5,000 meters, a flat grey lid pressed onto the upper peaks. But within the habitable band where Koralath sits, daylight is a rumor. The light is the light of overcast, of fog, of perpetual crepuscular dimness. It has no clear source and no clear direction. Shadows barely form. Colors flatten into muted families of grey-green, iron-brown, oxidized copper, and the particular off-white of wet concrete.

At night, the city compensates. Arc lights on tall poles cast overlapping cones of harsh, buzzing illumination that turn the fog into a visible body — a breathing, drifting, luminous atmosphere. The effect is theatrical and slightly nauseating. You walk through pools of light and valleys of near-total darkness. The fauna has adapted to this rhythm more readily than the people.

---

## The Moisture

It is not a wet place. It is a *damp* place. There is a difference.

Rain falls intermittently — short, cold squalls that sweep horizontally across the ledges and then stop, leaving everything slick and glistening but never truly soaked. More persistent is the condensation. The temperature hovers near the dew point constantly, which means that every metal surface in Koralath is perpetually beaded with water. Rust is a civic enemy. The sound of dripping is everywhere — a constant, irregular metronome that punctuates the deeper industrial drone. Puddles form in the concavities of unfinished rock. Small, unnamed streams appear in the fault-clefts after rain and vanish within hours, leaving mineral stains that are themselves slowly being studied by the city's geological surveyors.

The moisture feeds what vegetation exists. And what exists is — against expectation — lush, in its own stunted and tenacious way.

---

## The Vegetation

It grows in defiance of altitude, cold, and light. Moss is the primary colonizer — thick, bright green carpets of it that cover every north-facing surface and turn the lower ledges into something almost pastoral, if pastures grew vertically. Ferns appear in sheltered clefts, their fronds curled tight against the cold but unfurling slowly, so slowly, in the rare moments of filtered sunlight. Lichens crust the older stonework in fractal patterns that the city's engineers monitor, because the lichens are doing something strange to the structural concrete — dissolving it, yes, but also *reinforcing* it, binding it with a biological matrix that has no business being as strong as it is.

The vegetation is not abundant by lowland standards, but at this altitude, in this light, every patch of green feels deliberate. Earned. The plants here have a quality of *persistence* rather than vitality. They are lush the way a single surviving garden in a ruin is lush — defiant, slightly eerie, and more alive than they have any right to be.

---

## The Fauna

And this is where the description begins to curdle.

The animal life of the Foundry Ranges is abundant and *varied* in a way that doesn't track with the ecosystem. Species that should not coexist here do. Alpine ibex pick their way along the highest catwalks of Koralath, unafraid of the machines. Bats roost in the smokestacks and fly out at dusk in columns so dense they look like dark, living smoke. Insects — vast, slow-moving insects with too many segments — cluster around the arc lights and drop their shed exoskeletons on the plazas like brittle confetti. Rats in the lower levels have grown to a size that is discussed but not formally acknowledged. Birds call from the scaffolding — birds with calls that sound like machinery, or perhaps the reverse.

The fauna is not *balanced*. It is not an ecosystem in the ecological sense. It is a *collection* — a dense, overlapping assemblage of creatures drawn here by something and held here by something, and nobody in Koralath can agree on what that something is. The animals behave as though the city is a habitat, and the city behaves as though the animals are a resource, and the two arrangements coexist without ever quite reconciling.

Some of the fauna is not fauna.

---

## The Sound

During the day, the dominant sound is industrial: the low, arrhythmic thud of heavy machinery echoing off rock faces, the whine of generators, the clank of metal on metal from repair crews working on the endless infrastructure. This is layered with the dripping, the wind (which makes a thin, singing noise as it passes through the fault-clefts), and — underneath everything — a hum. The hum is not mechanical. It comes from the rock itself. It vibrates through the soles of your feet. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is *there*, the way your own heartbeat is there, and after a few days in Koralath you stop hearing it unless someone mentions it.

At night, the sound shifts. The machines throttle down. The fauna wakes. And the fog — which is always present but somehow louder at night — begins to carry sounds that are not quite echoes and not quite voices. The residents of Koralath call this *the ringing*, though it doesn't ring. It murmurs. It rustles. It sounds like a language heard through a wall, parsed just below the threshold of comprehension.

The sound level averages around 1.5 on most scales: not cacophonous, but never silent. Koralath is a loud-enough place. You can sleep through it. You cannot ignore it.

---

## The Weirdness

Here is the thing about Koralath that the tourist board doesn't advertise and the geological survey cannot explain:

The mountains are not finished, and while they are unfinished, the world around them is not entirely real.

It is not that reality *breaks* here, exactly. It *thins*. It develops grain, like wood, and if you look at anything — a wall, a face, the horizon — for too long, you begin to see the grain. The lines along which reality would fracture if it were stressed. Things are *almost* right. A shadow falls at a slightly wrong angle. A catwalk extends five meters further than it did yesterday, and the extra section looks identical to the rest, same rivets, same rust pattern, same drip-marks, but no one built it. Water flows uphill in two of the fault-clefts. Not always. Not reliably. But often enough that the plumbing in Lower Koralath has been redesigned twice.

The fauna is part of this. Some of the animals are *compositional* — assembled from the grammar of animal-ness but not from any specific species. A thing that moves like a cat and sounds like a bird and has the eyes of something that has never seen sunlight. These creatures are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *present*, in the way that furniture is present. They belong to the space rather than occupying it.

The residents of Koralath cope. They have developed a pragmatic relationship with the instability. There are rules — folk rules, not laws — about what to do when something changes. Don't look at it directly. Don't mention it to someone who wasn't there yesterday. If a new door appears in your wall, open it only during daylight hours. If a new door appears in your wall and it leads somewhere you've been before, *that* is when you contact the Survey.

The Survey is Koralath's geological-slash-reality-assessment bureau. They monitor the mountains and the world-skin that stretches over them like a membrane over a wound that hasn't closed. They issue reports that are technical, careful, and deeply unsettling. The latest one noted that the rate of geological emergence — the speed at which new rock is forming — has increased by 0.03% over the last cycle. The report did not say what this implied. The report did not need to.

## The Tempo

Everything here is slow.

The mountains rise slowly — millimeters per year, though the Survey suspects the rate is accelerating in ways that don't correspond to known tectonic models. The city expands slowly, adding a wing here, a bridge there, mostly in response to needs that emerged decades ago. The fauna migrates slowly, seasonally, in patterns that trace spirals across the ledges rather than straight routes. The fog moves slowly. The dripping is slow. The rust is slow. Even the weirdness is slow — changes accumulate gradually, a door appearing over the course of a week, a new species arriving over the course of a season, the hum deepening by a quarter-tone over the course of a year.

This slowness is not peaceful. It is *patient*. It is the patience of a process that knows it will finish eventually and is in no particular hurry to do so, because what it is building is large and the materials are still arriving and the blueprints — if they exist — have not yet been fully read.

Koralath sits in the body of a world that is still drawing its own breath. The residents feel it. They are careful people, slow themselves, given to long silences and long views. They build heavy because the ground is uncertain. They light the fog because the sun cannot reach them. They listen to the hum and do not discuss what it might be saying.

They are waiting. They don't know for what. But the mountains know, and the mountains are patient, and the mountains are not finished, and when they are finished — 

No one in Koralath finishes that sentence.

They just keep building.
