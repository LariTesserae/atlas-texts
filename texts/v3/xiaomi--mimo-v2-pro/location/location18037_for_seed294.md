The world is a city of glass, drowned and dreaming.

You stand on what might be a plaza, or a broad, shallow roof, or the back of some gargantuan, dormant thing. The material underfoot is not stone, not metal, but a dense, vitreous substance—like obsidian stretched thin and cooled in a deep, silent rush. It is dark, but not opaque; beneath its surface, faint, slow-moving luminosities pulse like the circulation of a cold, grey bloodstream. This is the substance of the world: a single, continuous, constructed substrate, ancient beyond measure, eroded into soft, rounded contours. Everything is made of it. The low benches, the occasional fractured pillars, the very ground beneath the endless water—it is all one piece, a sculpture abandoned by a god who thought only in terms of flow and form.

And it is submerged. The water is not a feature of the landscape; it is the landscape’s atmosphere. You are immersed in it. It is cold, achingly cold, a chill that seeps not into flesh but into the mind’s conception of warmth. It is perfectly clear, yet thick with a faint, silvery tint, like liquid moonlight. Visibility extends for miles, fading not into murk but into a blue-grey, sub-aquatic haze. There is no sun, no sky. The light is a dim, diffuse radiance that seems to emanate from the water itself, or perhaps from the slow-glowing veins within the glass terrain below. It is a 10,000-year-old twilight.

The turbulence is not in storm or current, but in the medium itself. The water *trembles*. Constantly. A low, seismic hum vibrates through the glass plain, and with it, the liquid shivers in chaotic, standing waves. Geysers of finer, almost particulate glass erupt silently from fissures, rising in slow-motion plumes before dissolving, raining down as a glittering silt that accumulates in drifts. This is the world’s dynamism: a perpetual, chthonic shaking that endlessly sculpts and resculpts the upper layers of the construct. New arches buckle and melt from a wall. A spire, eroded to a needle, finally loses its century-long battle with gravity and bends like taffy, collapsing into a new, soft dune. The erosion is primordial, the settling of a form into its final, dreamlike state, yet the shaking never lets it rest. It is a paradox of deep time and constant change: a grand, static ruin in the process of becoming another grand, static ruin.

Life is here, but not as we know it. The "fauna" are the glass structures themselves. Or rather, they are the patterns that move *through* them. Schools of delicate, filament-like structures—part crystalline growth, part autonomous current—drift in the trembling water, pulsing with the same slow light as the ground. Larger, slower presences glide near the bottom: vast, manta-like formations of layered glass, their edges frayed and regenerating, filtering the glittering silt. They make no sound but a faint, harmonic *tinging* as their bodies resonate with the world’s deep hum. They are abundant, filling the middle distances, yet the scale of the world dwarfs them. The space between them is vast, watery emptiness.

There is no vegetation. There is no technology. There is no "weirdness" in the sense of the bizarre or the uncanny. This is simply the state of things. The logic is absolute: a mineral world, fully made, flooded, ancient, and alive with the slow, glassy processes of its own eternal decay and rebirth. The only sounds are the deep, vibrational drone, the occasional, distant *crack* of shifting substrate, and the high, ethereal chime of the fauna brushing against each other. It is mostly quiet. The cold is absolute. The light is dim. You are a fleeting warmth in a city of glass, at the bottom of an ocean of time.
