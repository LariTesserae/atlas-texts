The city of Vash is remembered only by its bones, and the water that fills its streets.

It is a place of permanent twilight, the sky choked by a dense, unbroken layer of cloud that swallows the sun. The only illumination comes from the faint, perpetual glow of the world itself: a cold bioluminescence from the deep water below, and the warmer, sporadic lanterns of life that cling to the structures. The water is everywhere—not as a flood, but as the new ground level. The upper stories of sunken towers and the peaks of submerged domes breach the surface like the archipelago of a forgotten archipelago. Between them, the water is dark and warm, a liquid atmosphere thick with suspended life.

The architecture is stone and ferro-concrete, ancient and settled. Erosion has long since rounded edges, smoothed inscriptions, and turned grand avenues into silted canals. The city’s form is in balance with the water; the surviving structures are those strong enough or lucky enough to have become permanent reefs. Growth has been layered upon them for generations. Corals in muted shades of rust, ocher, and deep violet encrust every surface. Sponges form polyps and shelves. Long, sparse fronds of a seagrass called “drown-kelp” wave from what were once windows and balconies, their bioluminescent tips pulsing slowly in the current.

This is an urban reef. The density is high; every cubic meter of the water column and every square meter of hard surface teems with presence. Shoals of small, silver-sided fish flow through hollowed-out atriums like living rivers. Larger, slower shapes—gentle, filter-feeding rays the color of bruised twilight—glide between the spires. Eels with patterns like cracked marble watch from crevices in old masonry. The sound is a low, omnipresent hum: the collective crackle of shrimp, the distant song of something large moving in the abyssal channels that were once subway tunnels, the soft, rhythmic push of water through gills and against fins. It is quiet, but occupied.

The air above the waterline is warm and heavy, tasting of salt and organic decay. The breeze is slow. Here, on the rooftops and the caps of the tallest ruins, a different kind of sparse vegetation takes hold: tough, salt-sprayed grasses, and a few stunted, woody shrubs that somehow root in cracked concrete. Flying fauna claim these heights—birds with dark, waterproof plumage that dive for fish, and colonies of bat-like creatures that echolocate through the perpetually dim airspace, snatching flying insects from the thick air.

There is no technology. The people, if they ever were, are gone. Their works are now just geography, the scaffolding for a new and bustling ecosystem. The world operates on biological pace: predator and prey, the drift of plankton clouds at dawn (a relative brightening of the grey), the spawning corals that turn the water milky once a cycle, the relentless, weathering work of the salt and the time. It is a settled place, not in the sense of tranquility, but in the sense of a system that has found its parameters—a warm, dark, wet, and densely peopled (by other forms of life) state of existence, moving slowly under its blanket of cloud.
