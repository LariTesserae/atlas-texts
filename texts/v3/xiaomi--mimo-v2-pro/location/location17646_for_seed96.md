The first thing you notice is the sound: a low, omnipresent *hiss*. It is not loud, but it fills the air, the sound of a world condensing. Water wells up from everywhere and from nowhere. It beads on the sharp, black angles of the stone, runs in thin, silver rivulets down faces carved with geometric precision, and pools in perfect, still basins that reflect the bright, sourceless sky. The air itself is cool and saturated, a mist that never quite becomes fog, tasting of wet slate and ozone.

This is not a land shaped by wind or patient river, but by hands. The terrain is a series of ascending terraces, carved from a dark, basalt-like rock. The scale is regional—a valley system—but every inch of it is *occupied*. The terraces are not rough-hewn; they are fitted with an obsessive, primitive precision. Blocks are joined without mortar, seams so tight they are visible only as darker lines against the wet stone. Water, forced upward by immense, unseen pressure, seeps from these seams, weeping steadily down the intricate faces of the structures. There are no plants, no soil, no natural substrate. The ground is a paved, channeled, and sculpted mineral world.

The brightness comes from the water and the stone. The dark rock, when perpetually wet, gains a deep, obsidian sheen. The shallow, engineered canals that direct the endless weeping catch the bright light and shatter it into a million shifting mirrors. The atmosphere is luminous, glacial, and silent except for the constant, soft *hiss* of condensation and the drip-drip-drip of water finding its ordained paths.

The builders are gone, or perhaps they were never here in a way we would recognize. Their tools were simple: levers of a dense, dark wood (the only non-mineral material, and it too is petrified, waterlogged), copper-alloy chisels worn smooth, polishing stones. Their craft was total. They did not build *upon* the landscape; they *replaced* it. The high elevation and young, sharp features of the original geology—those jagged peaks and unstable scree slopes—have been wholly subsumed. In their place is this silent, terraced, hydrological machine.

There is no animal life. The ecology is one of mineral accretion and dissolution. Slow, patient processes govern here: the gradual smoothing of a once-sharp corner by a millennia-old drip, the infinitesimal creep of calcite deposits forming a new, ghostly lace over a carved channel. The dynamism is geological, a clock measured in water wearing stone.

It is a packed, yet open place. You can see far across the valley, terrace upon terrace ascending into the mist, but there is no empty space. Every surface is worked, every flow of water is directed. The density is of information, of intent, not of clutter. It feels less like a city and more like a single, vast, circuitous sculpture designed for the sole purpose of moving water through shaped stone.

Weirdness here is quiet and profound. It is the strangeness of a world that has abolished its own nature. The rules are simple: water rises, stone is carved, and everything that *is*, is made. It is a landscape of profound artifice, weeping forever in the cool, bright air, a monument to a craft that sought not to imitate nature, but to supersede it with a quieter, wetter, and more deliberate order.
