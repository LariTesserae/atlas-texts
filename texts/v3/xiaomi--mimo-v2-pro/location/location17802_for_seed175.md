You have found a cirque—a high, hollowed bowl carved into the mountain’s flank long ago, now settled into its final form. The air is cool at this elevation, but a persistent, gentle warmth radiates from the rock itself, a geothermal memory stored in the ancient stone. The sky above is a bright, normal blue, but the light within the bowl feels softened, diffused by the constant mist that breathes from the weeping walls.

The scale is intimate, cathedral-like, but the emptiness is profound. The sheer, curved cliffs of weathered granite and darker basalt rise around you, their faces slick with a permanent, slow-motion weeping. Water doesn’t fall here so much as *emerge*—a million tiny mouths sighing clear, warm droplets that gather in rivulets, tracing lazy, mineral-rich paths down the rock. The sound is the dominant presence: a pervasive, soft *hush*, the collective whisper of water on stone, punctuated by the occasional, heavy *plink* of a larger drop falling into a pool.

The floor of the cirque is a mosaic of stone and life. Between scattered, rounded boulders smoothed by millennia of mist, the ground is saturated. Here, vegetation takes the form of a lush, deep-green carpet of sphagnum moss, spongy and cool underfoot, drinking the ambient damp. From this mat rise strange, sparse sentinels: not trees, but towering, primitive flora. Great, ribbed pitcher plants, some as tall as a person, their lurid throats gaping to catch the constant condensation. Stands of ghostly, leafless stalks that might be horsetails from a forgotten age, their joints beaded with moisture. Further out, in the slightly drier patches, clusters of ferns uncurl in slow motion, their fronds heavy with droplets.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects, not even a ripple in the pools from a swimming thing. The silence, when the water’s whisper fades for a moment, is absolute and ancient. Yet the place is not lifeless; it *breathes*. The moss exhales dampness. The pitcher plants slowly digest the mineral-rich mist. The ferns grow in a timescale of seasons felt, not marked.

The strangeness is in the balance. It is a closed system, a world unto itself. The warm rocks, the constant water, the slow-growing flora—they exist in a state of established, perfect equilibrium. Change happens here, but at a geologic pace. A new fissure weeps a little more each century, a moss colony inches over a boulder over generations. You sense there are rules: do not rush. The damp stone is slick. The pitcher plants, while passive, offer a sticky, sweet-smelling doom. The pools are deceptively deep.

And there are artifacts, if you look. Not structures—nothing built. But in the center of the cirque, arranged in a broken, random-looking circle, are a dozen stones. They are not native to this bowl; their type is different, smoother, a pale grey. Some are upright, others slumped. They are utterly worn, their surfaces weathered into soft, ambiguous curves. Are they natural? Were they placed? Any carvings they might have held have been dissolved by ages of mist into faint, swirling suggestions. They feel like the first and last mark of a mind that came, considered, and left—or was absorbed—long before metal or complex tools. They are the punctuation in a long, silent sentence written by water and time.

You stand in a room made by the mountain, furnished by patience, and lit by a soft, eternal gray light filtered through mist. It is a place that has already said its piece, and now simply continues, in warmth and wetness and profound quiet.
