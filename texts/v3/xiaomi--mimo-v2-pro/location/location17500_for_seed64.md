The pocket is called the Veilstone Biosphere, though there is no stone and nothing to veil. It occupies a single, vast cavern near the summit of the Pillar of Night—a near-vertical spire of black, vitreous basalt that spears into the permanently occluded sky of the high peaks. The scale is intimate, a contained world no larger than a grand cathedral nave, but within it, life crowds upon itself with a dense, suffocating fervor.

There is no water here. None. The chemistry is based on a cold, viscous, silvery fluid that seeps from the basalt walls—a non-aqueous amalgam of heavy metals and complex hydrocarbons. It pools in shallow, mirror-bright basins, and instead of evaporating, it slowly sublimates into a faint, chilling mist that smells of ozone and wet graphite. This is the blood of this place.

And from it, the vegetation drinks. The "lushness" is not green, but a riot of metallic and crystalline forms. Towering, slow-growing stalks of bismuth-crystal, iridescent and brittle, clatter against each other in the faint thermal currents. Sheets of silvery-white lichen, like patches of frost, consume the walls, metabolizing the mineral mist. Fungoid structures resembling braided copper wire rise in dense thickets, their caps dripping droplets of the silvery fluid back into the pools with a persistent, high-pitched *tink-tink-tink* that contributes to the chamber's constant, resonant hum. The air is cool, almost frigid, and carries the sharp scent of cold metal and the deeper, earthier tang of hydrocarbon decay.

The elevation is extreme; the pressure is low, and the air is thin. The ecosystem, such as it is, exists in a state of ferocious, packed equilibrium. There is no space between presences. The bismuth stalks are woven through with filament-thin veins of conductive ore; the lichen patches merge into a continuous metallic skin; the wire-fungi form impenetrable brambles. Everything is connected, a single, saturated circuit of life and process.

Faint marks of a scattered, industrial past are embedded within it. Rusted iron stanchions, half-consumed by the lichen, rise from the pools—the remnants of old sampling rigs. A single, massive structure dominates the far wall: a dormant refinery of tarnished brass and dark glass, its pipes now serving as trellises for the copper fungi. Its engines are silent, but the chamber itself *hums*. The sound is not from machinery, but from the ecosystem—a low-frequency thrum from the vibrating bismuth, the sublimation hiss of the pools, the constant, crystalline *tink* of falling droplets, and an underlying, almost subsonic drone from the entire living matrix conducting energy through the rock. It is loud, a ceaseless, industrial choir sung by the biome itself.

Light is provided not from above, but from within. Phosphorescent veins in the basalt emit a dim, perpetual twilight of blue-grey luminescence. It glints off every metallic surface, creating a confusing, low-contrast world of sharp reflections and deep, soft shadows. There are no animals. Movement is only the slow creep of growth, the drip of fluid, and the occasional, sharp crack of a bismuth stalk fracturing under its own weight.

The weirdness is in the totality of the adaptation. This is a world built from industrial waste and geologic patience. The old refinery isn't a ruin; it is a foundational fossil, its leaked effluence having seeded the unique biochemistry. The system is in perfect, static equilibrium—never changing. The growth rate is measured in millimeters per century. The fluid levels in the pools are invariant. The hum is the same now as it was a thousand years ago. It is a packed, loud, living monument to a chemistry that abandoned water, thriving in the cold, dim, mineral gut of a mountain, forever singing its song of metal and oil.
