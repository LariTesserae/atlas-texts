# The Weeping Caldera

You arrive through a fissure in black basalt that shouldn't be wide enough to admit you but does, the rock warm and slick under your palms, and the passage opens into a bowl.

The bowl is perhaps two hundred meters across. You can see all of it at once — the far wall of rock, the floor, everything — and the containment is immediate, architectural, like stepping into a room whose ceiling is sky. The rock is young. You can feel it in the way the walls are still sharp-angled, still carrying the memory of fracture, not yet softened by rain or time. The basalt is dark, almost black, shot through with veins of something that is not quartz and not metal but catches the light with a faint prismatic shiver, as if the stone itself is uncertain what it's made of. The caldera floor is uneven, not yet settled into its final shape — little ridges and step-downs and shallow pockets where water has already begun to pool.

And there is water everywhere.

Not flooding. Not a lake. Something more intimate than that. The walls weep. Every surface is wet. A thin, continuous film of condensation runs down the basalt faces, collecting in the cracks, threading along the sharp edges of rock in rivulets so fine they catch the light like filaments of glass. The air is saturated — not fog, not rain, but a warm, breath-heavy humidity that sits on your skin and enters your lungs and makes every surface gleam. Where the water gathers on the caldera floor, it forms shallow, clear pools only a few centimeters deep, and they cover most of the ground, so that walking means stepping through a mosaic of warm water and dark stone. The pools are still. They reflect the sky above — a bright, white-hot haze — and the reflections make the floor look like it's twice as deep as it is, as if the bowl continues downward into a mirror world.

The heat is remarkable. Not the dry heat of an oven but a wet, pressing heat, the kind that makes you aware of your own blood moving. The high elevation should mean cooler air, and perhaps a kilometer above this caldera it does — perhaps there are winds up there, and a view of other volcanic peaks in some grey-green range. But inside the bowl the heat is trapped, gathered, concentrated by the rock walls, and the water in the air acts as a blanket. You sweat immediately. Your clothes are damp within minutes. Everything here is warm — the stone, the water, the air, the light.

The light is not strange. This is important. It is ordinary, overhead, diffused through the haze of humidity into a bright, even, shadowless wash. It is the light of a hot afternoon anywhere. What is strange is everything else.

---

The vegetation is sparse but emphatic. In the cracks where water collects most persistently, there are plants — but not quite plants you recognize. They grow in clusters, small colonies of two or three or five, spaced widely across the caldera floor and up the lower walls, each cluster separated from the next by meters of wet bare stone. They are low-growing, the tallest no more than knee height, with broad, thick, almost fleshy leaves of a blue-green so dark it's nearly black. The leaves are waxy, and the water beads on them rather than soaking in, sitting in perfect spheres on the surface. Where the stems meet the rock, the root structures are visible — they don't penetrate the stone but grip its surface, spreading in fractal patterns like frost on glass, and the roots are pale, almost white, and they seem to glow faintly against the dark basalt, though this might be a trick of the wet surface and the angle of light.

Some of the clusters produce structures that are not quite flowers. They are small, rigid, geometric — dodecahedral, or close to it — and they rise on thin stalks above the leaves. They are the same color as the leaves. They do not open. They do not seem to serve any reproductive purpose. They just *are*, sitting there in the heat, beaded with water, geometrically perfect, like something manufactured rather than grown.

You count perhaps fifteen or twenty of these clusters across the entire caldera floor. They are the only living things you can see, except —

---

Except for the things that move.

They are rare. You might go minutes without seeing one. But they are here.

They are small — the largest is the size of your spread hand — and they move across the wet stone with a fluid, flowing motion, like oil poured across a plate. They are dark, darker than the basalt, and they have no visible limbs, no eyes, no faces. They are shapeless in the way that a puddle is shapeless, conforming to the surface they move across, flattening into the shallow pools and then rising up the other side with the water streaming off them. They leave no trail. They make no sound. When one reaches a cluster of plants, it pauses — if pausing is the right word for something that has no discernible front or back — and seems to press itself against the leaves, conforming to their shape, wrapping around them for a moment, and then it moves on. Whether it is feeding, or resting, or doing something for which you have no word, you cannot tell.

You see three of them in the first hour. You never see them interact with each other.

---

And then there is the technology.

It is scattered. It is faint. But it is here, and once you see it, you begin to see it everywhere.

In the center of the caldera, half-submerged in one of the shallow pools, there is a structure. It is small — about the size of a writing desk, or a large dog — and it is made of something that is not stone and not metal and not ceramic, though it has qualities of all three. It is smooth. It is pale — lighter than anything else in this place of dark rock and dark plants — and it has a faintly luminous quality, as if it is generating its own light, though this light is so dim it barely registers, overwhelmed by the bright haze above. Its shape is irregular but purposeful, like a tool designed for a hand you have never seen. There are surfaces that curve and surfaces that are flat, and at the intersections of these surfaces there are seams so fine you can only see them when the condensation gathers along them and traces their paths in tiny bright lines.

The structure hums. Or rather — it would hum, if you could hear it. It is at the edge of hearing, a frequency that you feel in your teeth and in the bones of your skull rather than perceive as sound. The caldera is mostly quiet — there is the soft, constant hiss of water condensing on stone, the occasional faint drip or trickle, the almost-silence of a place where very little is happening audibly — and beneath all of this there is the structure, vibrating at a frequency that makes the water near it tremble in patterns. Concentric circles. Standing waves. Shapes that shouldn't form in a shallow pool but do.

You approach it. The water around your ankles is warm. The stone under your feet is warm. The air is warm. Everything is warm, and the structure is cool — the first cool thing you have touched since entering the caldera. Its surface is not cold, exactly, but it is *less warm*, and the difference is startling, almost shocking, and your hand pulls back before you decide to pull it back.

There are other artifacts. Look along the walls of the caldera, in the crevices where the basalt has fractured into angular shelves. In three or four places you can see small structures embedded in the rock — or perhaps *emerging from* the rock, the way a fossil emerges from a matrix, except these are not fossils. They are the same pale, smooth material as the central structure, and they are small, some no larger than a fist, and they seem to be growing. Or at least, they are in different states of completion. One is barely a nub, a slight rounding of the pale material against the dark stone. Another is more developed, its shape half-revealed, the basalt cracked and falling away from it in sharp fragments. Another is fully emerged and sits on its shelf of rock like a carved ornament, and around it the basalt is scored with fine lines — not cracks, but deliberate marks, radiating outward, as if the structure has inscribed something into the stone as it came through.

The veins of not-quartz in the basalt walls are connected to these structures. You begin to see the pattern: the pale, prismatic veins run through the rock like a circulatory system, converging on the embedded artifacts, feeding them, or being fed by them. The whole caldera is threaded with this network. The rock is not inert. It is infrastructure.

---

You sit on a dry-ish shelf of rock near the caldera's edge and watch.

The light does not change. The haze is constant. The heat is constant. The water continues its slow, continuous descent down every surface. The plants sit in their clusters, dark and geometric. The shapeless creatures — you have seen four now, perhaps five — drift across the wet stone in their silent, purposeless way.

The central structure hums.

The caldera is young. You can feel this in the sharpness of the rock, in the way the walls still show the violence of their formation, in the fact that the water has not yet carved channels or smoothed the edges of the fissures. Whatever made this place — volcanic event, deliberate excavation, something else — it happened recently. The place has not yet learned what it is. The technology is still emerging from the stone. The creatures have not yet filled the space or found their niches. The plants have not yet spread.

It is a place in the process of becoming itself, and you are here during the early minutes, the first hours, while the walls still weep with the heat of formation and the artifacts are still pushing through the rock and the hum is still learning its own frequency.

You are small here. Not threatened — there is nothing hostile in this caldera, nothing that has noticed you or cares that you are here — but small in the way that a single cell is small inside a body that is still embryonic, still organizing itself, still deciding what it will become.

The quiet holds. The water falls. The shapeless dark things move.

You stay.
