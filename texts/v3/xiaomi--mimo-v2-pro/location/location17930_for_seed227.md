# The Hollow of Roan

You find it by accident, or perhaps it finds you — a seam in the low coastal headland where the cliff face has split along some ancient fault and never quite closed. From outside, it looks like nothing: a crevice wide enough to turn sideways through, its lip beaded with condensation, its breath smelling of basalt and salt and the green-dark scent of moss that never sees direct light.

Step through.

The noise hits first and does not stop. Somewhere behind the inner wall — you never see where, the geometry of the place resists clear lines of sight — water moves in volume. A tidal channel funnels through a constriction deep in the rock, and the ocean's pulse drives it with a low, unceasing roar that you feel in your jaw, your sternum, the soft architecture of your inner ear. The sound is the place. It is not accompaniment. It is not background. After a few minutes you forget that silence ever existed and begin to experience the roar as a kind of texture, a pressure you inhabit rather than hear. You learn to read vibration instead of sound — the difference between the surge's deep throb and the higher shudder where water strikes a particular stone.

---

The chamber opens — but *open* is the wrong word. It does not open so much as it **accumulates inward.** You are in a throat of rock that descends slightly, curving so that you cannot see the entrance behind you, and the space widens unevenly into a series of pockets and ledges and half-rooms, each partially walled by folded sheets of dark basalt, each holding its own pool or puddle or slick of moisture. The walls weep. Condensation gathers on every surface, runs in slow threads, collects in hollows that have been deepening for thousands of years — long enough that their rims have rounded into smooth lips, long enough that the rock has taken on the polished look of something that has been touched by the same gesture ten million times.

This is the erosion. Not violent. Not dramatic. Simply **old.** Every edge has been softened. Every surface carries the matte sheen of weathering that has outlasted its own memory. The stone is warm beneath its wetness only in the way that all stone at this depth holds the faint ghost of geothermal patience — not warmth as you know it, but the absence of the cold that grips the land above. Here, the cold is held at one remove. The rock insulates. The water retains. It is still cold — your breath clouds, your fingers ache after a while, the moisture on the walls would freeze if the air moved faster — but it is the cold of a cellar, not the cold of an open field. Settled. Chronic. Unbothered.

---

And here is the contradiction that gives the place its character: it is **lush.** Not in the way of a forest or a jungle — there is no canopy, no layered ecology, no chorus of competing species. The lushness is singular, monolithic, almost liturgical. Moss covers everything. *Everything.* The ledges, the walls, the fallen slabs of stone, the surfaces of the pools themselves where the water is still enough. It grows in textures that vary with the light — and the light varies, in its meager way.

Because there is light. Not much. At the entrance crevice, a thin gray wash falls in, carrying no warmth, no color, just the fact of photons. It reaches perhaps twenty feet inward before it thins into a kind of permanent dusk that is only slightly less dark than the deeper chambers. The moss nearest the entrance is a gray-green, almost silver, the color of lichen on a skull. As you move inward, it darkens through jade, through emerald, through a green so deep it reads as black — a green that exists only because it has adapted to light levels that would render most photosynthesizers functionally blind. Ferns grow from the ledges in sparse, elongated sprays, their fronds stretched thin and pale as if reaching for something they have learned to do without. Small, fleshy-leaved plants — you might call them succulents if you were generous — cluster in the drip-zones where condensation channels pour a reliable thread of water. They are the color of liver, of dark garnets, of blood in low light.

The ecology is not empty but it is **singular.** You do not see many species. You see one moss, perhaps two, and they have colonized so completely that there is no room and no need for variety. The ferns are one kind. The succulents are one kind. Life here has not diversified; it has **specialized,** performing the same trick so perfectly and for so long that the trick has become the entire world.

---

The remains are there. You find them on the second visit, or the third — the first time through, the darkness and the noise conspire to make you miss them entirely.

Small structures. **Stone niches,** cut into the walls at roughly waist height, each about the size of a box that might hold a forearm. Their edges are hand-chipped — you can see the percussion marks if you hold a light close, irregular, patient, the work of someone hitting one rock against another over and over until a hollow appeared. Some are dry inside. Some hold a small pool of condensation, black and still. Some have a smooth stone placed at the back, or a bundle of something fibrous that has long since compressed into a dark mass.

There are not many. Perhaps a dozen in the whole complex of chambers. They are scattered, not arranged in any pattern you can discern, and they are weathered into the walls so thoroughly that they look almost geological — as if the rock had grown around them, or as if the rock itself had decided to remember something in the shape of a niche.

There is also, in the widest chamber, a low wall. Knee-high. Dry-stacked basalt, no mortar, no tooling beyond the rough fracture-planes of the stones themselves. It does not divide anything. It does not hold anything back. It runs for about ten feet and stops. It has been there so long that moss has covered it entirely and small ferns have rooted in its crevices, and its top stones have settled into the ones below with the slow certainty of gravity acting on millennia. You sit on it because it is the right height for sitting. It feels like it was built for sitting. You wonder if someone sat here and listened to the water and watched the dark and felt the green growing over everything, including themselves.

No one tends these structures. No one has tended them for a long time. They are not ruins in the way of fallen temples or collapsed cities. They are **inclusions** — features of the rock, like veins or nodules or the small crystal pockets you sometimes find inside a broken stone. They belong to the place the way the moss belongs, the way the water belongs, the way the sound belongs. They are the place remembering that hands were here once.

---

Time moves slowly in the hollow. The moss grows a millimeter a century — or less. The condensation follows the same channels it has followed since the last ice age rearranged the coastline. The tidal surge pumps in and out on a schedule of hours, not minutes, and its rhythm has been so constant for so long that it no longer constitutes change. It constitutes **being.** The hollow breathes with the ocean the way a sleeping creature breathes — not because it decides to, but because breath is what it is.

You notice, if you stay long enough, that there is a slow cycle beneath the apparent stillness. The pools rise and fall not just with the tides but with the seasons — a few inches, perhaps, over months. The ferns on the northern ledges grow slightly more vigorously in the darker half of the year, when the condensation increases. The moss at the entrance lightens and darkens with the angle of the sun across months, turning pale gold for a few weeks in midwinter when the light, such as it is, rakes in at its lowest. But these cycles are so gradual, so attenuated, that you would need to leave and come back and leave and come back to perceive them as cycles at all. In the moment, everything is permanent. Everything has always been this way.

---

The fauna — yes, there is fauna, though you would be forgiven for thinking otherwise. Insects, primarily. Small, dark, wingless things that move through the moss like thoughts through a quiet mind. You see them only when you stop moving and look for a long time. They are beetles, or things shaped like beetles, with bodies so dark they absorb what little light there is and return nothing. They feed on the moss. Something feeds on them — you find the occasional shed wing-case or the desiccated remnant of a spider leg caught in a thread of condensation — but the food web is thin, nearly theoretical. An ecosystem on the edge of metaphor.

Once, in the deepest chamber — the one where the sound is loudest and the green is almost black — you see something move in one of the pools. Something pale. Something that might have been a eyeless fish or might have been a trick of the wavering reflection of your light on water disturbed by the tidal vibration. You look for it again and it is not there. You tell yourself it was nothing. You are almost certain you are right.

---

You leave the hollow the way you entered it — sideways, through the crevice, back into the cold bright shock of the open cliff with its wind and its gray sky and its wide, empty horizon. The sound cuts off behind you like a door closing. The silence is so sudden and so complete that you stand for a moment, blinking, unmoored.

Your clothes are damp. Your fingers smell of basalt and green. In the crease of your palm you find a tiny fragment of moss, dark as a pupil, still alive, still growing, patient beyond anything you have a name for. You could keep it. You could put it in your pocket and carry it out into the world.

You press it back into the wet rock at the mouth of the crevice, and it holds.

You walk away, and the hollow continues without you — dark, green, roaring, old, and utterly indifferent to whether anyone knows it exists.
