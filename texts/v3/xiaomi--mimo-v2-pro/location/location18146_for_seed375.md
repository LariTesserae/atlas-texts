The city is called Mnem, though no one speaks its name here. It stands in a shallow basin of pale, powdered stone—the dust of a forgotten mountain range, ground to talc by winds that no longer blow. Silence is the architecture. It builds the space between the stone spires, fills the empty plazas, and lies thick in the arched doorways.

You are in a district, a cluster of structures no larger than a monastery close. The buildings are not built so much as *exuded* from the ground: bone-white mineral formations that rose, crystallized, and hollowed themselves into towers, arches, and smooth, curved walls. Their surfaces are sharp-edged, geologically young, showing the fresh facets of recent emergence. They are utterly devoid of tool-marks, of hinges, of any sign of intentional construction. They are urban in their density and organization—a tight labyrinth of lanes and courtyards—but organic in their genesis, like a coral reef lifted into the air.

The light is a constant, dim silver, sourceless and cool. It doesn’t cast shadows; it seeps into things, giving the white stone a faint, inner luminescence, like old bone. There is no sun, no moon—only a high, permanent overcast of fine, silicate haze that filters the world to monochrome.

Life is sparse and strange. A thin, grey lichen grows in whorled patterns on northern-facing walls, feeling like cold velvet to the touch. Further in, in a small, bowl-like courtyard, a single organism dominates: a "whisper-grass." It’s not grass, but a field of tall, silica-thin filaments, each one a pale blue, growing from the cracked paving stones. They do not rustle. They are too rigid, too fine. They simply *are*, a silent, frozen shock of color. The only fauna are the "dust-motes"—not insects, but tiny, slow-drifting aggregations of mineral particles that move with a faint, electrostatic purposiveness, clustering in sheltered corners before dissolving again. A larger creature, a "still-hare," sits motionless in the shadow of a spire. It is the color of the stone, its fur more like a coating of fine gravel. Its eyes are closed. It breathes perhaps once a minute. It is not hiding; it is participating in the silence.

The dynamism here is not in spectacle, but in imperceptibly slow, powerful processes. You can *feel* it in the air—a cool, heavy pressure that isn't wind, but the atmosphere itself expanding and contracting with the planet's deep, tidal breathing. You can *see* it in the stone. Watch a single, sharp edge of a rooftop for an hour. A tiny flake of mineral, no larger than a grain of sand, will suddenly detach and fall, dislodged by the slow, relentless thermal shift. The city is eroding, but on a timescale that makes stone feel like water. The whisper-grass is growing, but upwards, millimeter by millimeter, century by century. The dust-motes reassemble. The still-hare, on some cycle tied to the planet's core, will open its eyes, take three precise hops to a new patch of shadow, and freeze again for days.

This is a sealed system. The low elevation and cool temperature hold the thin atmosphere close. There is no water cycle; the chemistry is silicate, saline, and dry. Any moisture is locked in the crystalline matrices of the stone or the lichen's tough tissues. The urban forms are not a legacy of builders, but of a slow, geological puberty—a planet's crust trying on the shapes of habitation for size, with no one to inhabit them. The rules are few: stillness is equilibrium, motion is erosion, sound is a vacuum. To walk here is to feel like the loudest, most dynamic, and most temporary thing in the world, your heartbeat a drum in the cathedral of silence.
