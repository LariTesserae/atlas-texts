# The Condensation Terraces

You are standing inside a hill that believes it is a mountain.

It was poured, not born. The slope beneath your feet is aggregate andite — a proprietary composite that has not yet learned to weather. Its surface is smooth where the molds were pulled and rough where the scaffolding clamps bit in. There are no streambeds carved into it. No talus. No lichen. The concrete is barely a season old in places, still sweating calcium, still off-gassing its chemical youth. Water beads on it everywhere, not from rain but from the atmosphere itself, which is thick and warm and sits against the skin like a held breath. Condensation runs in vertical threads down every pillar, every retaining wall, every unfinished parapet — collecting in gutters that were designed to channel it but haven't been calibrated yet, so it overflows in places, pooling in little glycerine-clear ponds on terraces that step up and up into a ceiling you can't quite see.

The ceiling is there. This is an interior. But the mist softens its geometry into a pale, shifting grey, and the grow-lights strung across it on cable trays have been dimmed to a late-afternoon amber that makes everything feel older than it is. The light is normal — deliberately, almost aggressively normal. Whoever designed this space wanted you to feel like you were outside, or wanted the plants to think they were. It is convincing enough to be disorienting. You keep forgetting you are inside a structure on a highland plateau, inside a hill that is itself inside something larger — though what that something is, the terraces don't say.

---

The vegetation is everywhere and it is *managed* in the way that a river is managed — with channels and releases and an ongoing, slightly anxious attention. Every terrace holds a different biome in miniature. On the level nearest the entrance, low groundcovers with rubbery, moisture-wicking leaves form a tight mat over manufactured soil beds. The beds are thin — maybe four inches deep — set into trays that slot into the terrace flooring like drawers in a cabinet. You can see the undersides in places where the trays are unfinished, exposed girders and conduit, the industrial skeleton of the hill. Above the groundcovers, tiered planters hold broad-leafed species with thick stems that lean against trellises made of perforated metal. Water runs down the trellises too, and the plants have sent roots into the perforations, gripping the metal the way epiphytes grip bark. Higher still, on the middle terraces, something tree-like grows — though it is not a tree. It is a vertical fungal stalk, pale and slightly translucent, that branches into a canopy of flat, shelf-shaped fruiting bodies the color of old parchment. These catch the condensation and drip steadily onto the terrace below. The sound this makes is a soft, irregular patter — one of the few sounds in the space. It is not unpleasant. It is the sound of a place that is slowly, quietly filling.

Between the terraces, water moves through open channels cut directly into the concrete. The channels are narrow — hand-width — and the water in them is warm to the touch. It smells faintly of copper and green chlorophyll. In some channels, a slick biofilm has formed: a thin, living skin of algae and bacteria that processes nutrients and feeds them back into the irrigation system. This was designed. The biofilm is a feature, not a flaw. It turns the channels into slow, low-tech kidneys — filtering, cycling, keeping the water in motion without pumps. But there are pumps too, hidden in the walls, and you can feel them when you press your palm flat against the concrete: a low, irregular vibration, like a heartbeat that hasn't settled into rhythm. The whole hill hums with this unfinished pulse.

---

You do not see the industrial apparatus directly, but you hear it in the silences between the drip and the hum. Somewhere behind the terrace walls — behind the curtain of trailing vines that hangs from every overhang, behind the manufactured soil and the trellises and the fungal canopy — there are machines. Not large ones. The scale here is small, intimate, room-sized. The sounds they make are domestic: a click, a whir, a short mechanical exhalation as something opens or closes. A valve adjusting. A grow-light shifting on its track. A nutrient injector pulsing once, twice, then falling quiet. These are the sounds of a caretaking system — automated, patient, not entirely finished. Some of the machinery is visible where the vegetation hasn't yet filled in. You can see a robotic arm on a rail above Terrace Seven, its end effector shaped like a pair of pruning shears, frozen mid-task. There is a puddle forming under it. It has been still for a while. Whether it is broken or waiting is unclear.

The built environment extends down to the molecular. The soil is manufactured — mineral aggregate, hydrogel beads, a slow-release nutrient matrix — and it behaves like soil, darkening when wet, cracking in thin plates when it dries in the rare spots where the condensation doesn't reach. But it does not smell like soil. It smells like a greenhouse supply store: sterile, mineral, faintly synthetic. The organisms growing in it have not yet transformed it. They are young, and the soil is younger, and the relationship between them is still transactional rather than symbiotic. Give it a year. Give it five. The soil will become something else. But right now, it is still a product, still in its packaging in some essential way, and the plants growing from it seem to know this — they root shallowly, grip loosely, lean on their trellises the way travelers lean on temporary railings.

---

Fauna is almost absent. A few insects — tiny, translucent-winged things that might be gnats or might be something engineered — hover in the warmest corners where the grow-lights cluster. They move erratically, as though they are not entirely sure how to fly. There are no birds. No frogs. The channels are too young for fish. On Terrace Fourteen, near the fungal canopy, you find the shed carapace of something — a beetle, maybe, or a small crab — hollow and dry, caught in a crack between two concrete panels. It is the only evidence of a larger animal you will see. Whatever laid it here is gone or hiding. The terraces are not hostile to life, exactly, but they are not yet *for* life in the way that a forest floor or a riverbank is for life. They are for the idea of life. The infrastructure of it. The promise.

---

The strangeness here is quiet and accumulative. It lives in the contradictions. A hill that was poured. Soil that was manufactured. Water that condenses from air that was heated by machines to make it condense. A fungal tree growing from a metal trellis rooted in a concrete terrace inside a built mountain on a high plateau above a world you have not seen and may not be able to reach. Nothing here is hidden, exactly — the concrete is honest about being concrete, the channels are open, the machinery is not disguised — but the whole system is oriented toward a future state: a moment when the vegetation will be lush enough to hide the infrastructure, when the soil will be alive enough to forget it was manufactured, when the condensation will have worn the concrete into something that looks like stone. That moment has not arrived. You are standing in the antechamber of an ecosystem. The door is open. The room is being built around you as you stand in it, drip by drip, root by root, pulse by pulse.

The warmth is steady. The light is kind. The mist softens your edges. Somewhere above, the ceiling holds, and above that, the plateau holds, and above that, the sky — real sky, actual sky — is doing whatever it is doing, indifferent to the hill that is growing inside itself far below. You could stay here. It would be easy to stay here. The terraces are not designed to keep you in, but they are designed to make you want to remain — to watch the biofilm thicken, to hear the next pump-cycle, to see whether the robotic arm on Terrace Seven will move again, to find out what hatched from the carapace on Terrace Fourteen, to wait for the moment when the concrete finally, finally begins to erode.

It hasn't started yet. Everything here is beginning.
