You stand on a ridge of raw, dark basalt, a fractured spine of the world tilted toward a sky of bruised pewter. The air is thin and cuts sharply in your lungs—it carries the electric taste of ozone and cold stone. This is a high place, impossibly high, where the planet’s crust has been shoved upward in a recent, violent adolescence. Erosion has barely begun its work; the slopes are jagged, angular, littered with scree that slides in whispering avalanches at your touch. The scale is regional, a vast mountainous province seen from its highest pass, but the density is low. Between the knife-edge ridges lie immense, quiet valleys of emptiness, floors of powdered regolith and shattered rock.

And yet, there is life.

It is not a life you recognize. The vegetation is lush, but it does not drink water. There is no water here—not in streams, not in the air, not in the cells of the plants themselves. The chemistry is wrong. Instead, great crystalline forests climb the slopes. Their “trunks” are pillars of intergrown quartz, feldspar, and stranger, piezoelectric minerals that hum with internal stress. Their “leaves” are thin, razor-sharp laminations of mica and obsidian, rustling against each other with a sound like a thousand chisels on glass. They photosynthesize, but not with chlorophyll. Their tissues are doped with metallic salts and semi-conductor compounds; they drink the dim, grey light and convert it into a slow, cold electrochemical gradient. From a distance, they look like forests of daggers and dark mirrors, swaying with an immense, grinding slowness. The ground between them is not soil, but a gritty dust studded with smaller, bulbous lithophytes—things like granite cabbages and agave forms made of flint.

The sound. You feel it in your teeth and bones before you hear it. A deep, omnipresent **roar**, not from a single source, but from the entire landscape. It is the sound of the world’s newness, the sound of rock under colossal tension, of minerals growing and shearing at a molecular level. It is the sound of the piezoelectric forests discharging built-up static in endless, crackling chains. It is a deafening, geological white noise that swamps all other sensation. In this sound, the faint scratching of the scree is swallowed. The slow, glassy rustle of the crystal trees is but a faint overtone.

Then, the weirdness asserts itself.

The air *stutters*. For a half-second, the jagged horizon ahead duplicates itself, a shimmering afterimage offset by ten feet, before snapping back with a nauseating lurch. A patch of granite cabbage on a nearby boulder suddenly inverts its coloration, light becoming dark, in perfect silence. Your own shadow does not fall consistently; it seems to pool and stretch toward the cold, dim sun in ways that defy the angle of light. This is not an optical illusion. It is a fundamental shudder in the local rules of space and perception. Reality here is thin, still cooling from its violent uplift, and prone to cracks. The immense, patient sound seems to be the pressure pushing through those cracks.

Scattered across this trembling, mineral forest are the marks of inhabitants. Not animals—there are none. The fauna parameter is a stark zero. But there are structures. Small, primitive shelters built into the lee of massive boulders or at the base of the largest crystal trees. They are made of carefully stacked basalt slabs and bound with a tough, fibrous resin harvested from the lithophytes. The tools found nearby are simple: hammer-stones of dense gneiss, sharp obsidian flakes, crude basalt axes. There is no metal. The beings who built these were few, and their stay was tentative. A single clay pot, unfired and crumbly, holds a collection of oddly regular quartz crystals. A slab of shale bears scratched tally marks and spirals that mirror the growth patterns of the mica-leaves. They came, perhaps, to study the shimmer. To listen to the roar. To try and read the breaking of the world, and then they were gone, or they ceased to be. Their remains are faint, scattered, already being swallowed by the slow, mineral growth and the occasional reality stutter that folds a wall section into slightly the wrong space.

The light is perpetually dim, a gloaming state, as if the sun is forever just below the ridge. The temperature is a penetrating, dry cool that saps warmth steadily. Change happens here on a geological clock—the trees grow millimeters per century, the mountain rises imperceptibly, the cracks in reality may heal or widen over eons. It is a world of immense, raw forces, but executed with a slow, deliberate patience. It is a deafening, silent (for all sound is one sound), lush, barren, newborn, and ancient place. It is a reality under construction, and the construction site is alive, strange, and utterly indifferent to the soft, temporary things that might try to witness it.
