# The Loom Hall

The ceiling is too close. Not oppressively — you could stand upright, most places — but it's *there*, a slab of pale stone laced with hairline fractures from which water weeps in steady, glassy threads. They catch what little light exists: a cold, blue-tinged phosphorescence that seems to come from the stone itself, or from something coating it. The source is unclear. The effect is a dim half-noon, enough to see by, never enough to feel certain of edges.

---

The hall — and it is a hall, singular, though impossibly proportioned — stretches perhaps two hundred meters in each direction before the walls lose themselves in mist. Not fog generated by any weather system. Just condensation. The air is saturated. Every surface is slick. You breathe moisture. It collects on skin and runs in thin rivulets down the inside of your forearms, joins the ankle-deep water that covers the floor in a uniform, mirror-still sheet — except where it isn't.

Where it isn't still is everywhere the machines are.

---

*Machines* is the wrong word and the only available one. They rise from the water at irregular intervals — dozens of them across the hall — each a vertical construct of material that resembles ceramic or bone or something in between, pale as the ceiling, jointed in ways that suggest organic structure but resolve, on closer inspection, into geometries with no biological analogue. They are roughly human-scale in the way a cathedral pillar is human-scale: built at a size a person can comprehend, but clearly not built *for* a person.

They are working.

That is the source of the sound.

---

The sound at first registers as a single environmental fact — a low, sustained hum overlaid with the hiss of water and, beneath both, a rhythmic clicking like a thousand abacuses being tallied in unison. But stay longer and it differentiates. Each machine has its own voice. Some produce a deep, subsonic thrum that you feel in your sternum. Others emit rapid, tonal sequences — not music, not language, but structured. *Intentional.* The hall is a cacophony of these processes, overlapping, occasionally synchronizing into moments of eerie consonance before diverging again. It is loud. Not painfully. But it fills every space between thoughts. You cannot hear yourself breathe, though you can feel your own breath condensing against your upper lip.

There is no one else here to produce sound. There is no animal life. No insects. No fish in the shallow water. No birds — obviously, underground — but more than that: no *presence.* The machines are the only entities, and they are not alive in any way you can verify. They move. Their joints articulate. Some extend thin filaments into the water, or into the ceiling, or into each other. But they are not alive. You are certain of this in the way you are certain of temperature: a bodily knowledge, pre-verbal.

---

The water is everywhere and comes from everywhere. The ceiling weeps. The walls sweat. The floor, where it isn't submerged, is slick with a film of moisture so thin it's almost dry — almost, but not. In the eastern third of the hall, the water deepens to knee-level, and the machines there are partially submerged, their lower joints obscured, still working. You cannot tell if the water is rising or if it has always been this depth. There is no tide. No current. But the surface trembles constantly from the vibration of the machinery beneath it, and this gives the illusion — or perhaps it is not an illusion — of something alive in the water. There is nothing alive in the water.

Rain falls from the ceiling in a localized column near the center of the hall, where the condensation is heaviest. A steady, warm drizzle — the temperature of the air, which is warm, muggy, subtropical, wrong for being underground. The column of rain is perhaps ten meters in diameter and it has carved a shallow depression in the floor, a basin two hands deep, and the machines near it have grown — *grown,* that is the word you reach for, though it is imprecise — structures that cup and channel the falling water, diverting it into grooves in the floor that carry it toward the walls. Infrastructure. The beginnings of a plumbing system, or a circulatory one.

---

The floor itself tells you the place is young.

It is not eroded. It is not smoothed. The stone has the texture of something recently fractured — sharp edges, crystalline grain visible where the water hasn't yet deposited a film of mineral. The walls are the same: raw, striated, as though the hall was not built but *torn open,* and the torn edges are still fresh. In places you can see tool marks, if they are tool marks — narrow, parallel grooves in the rock, perfectly spaced, as though cut by something that moved in a straight line without deviation or fatigue. The marks are clean. The cuts are precise. Whatever made them is advanced beyond anything you can reference. But the marks themselves are unweathered. Unpatinated. This work was done recently. Weeks ago. Days, maybe.

And yet the machines are *old* — not in their material, which is as pristine as the cut stone, but in their complexity. They have the depth of iteration that implies long development. Joints within joints. Mechanisms nested inside mechanisms. They do not look prototypical. They look *final.* As though they were not built for this space but arrived in it, fully formed, the moment the space was ready.

This contradiction does not resolve. The place holds it without apology.

---

The emptiness is the thing that stays with you.

Not loneliness — loneliness requires the possibility of company. This is something else. The hall is *vast enough* for a small city. The architecture — if the machines are architecture — implies habitation. There are spaces between the constructs that read as rooms, as corridors, as plazas. The water channels follow paths that suggest streets. The light, dim as it is, is distributed evenly enough to indicate planning. Someone — some *process* — designed this place for occupancy.

There is no occupancy.

You walk through streets that lead nowhere. You stand in plazas whose only monument is a machine clicking to itself in the blue half-light. You wade through water that has no fish, past walls that have no doors, beneath a ceiling that has no sky. The machines do not acknowledge you. They do not obstruct you. They do not interact with you. You are not part of whatever process they are executing.

The emptiness is not absence. It is *before.* The place is waiting for something it has not yet named.

---

In the far northwest corner, where the mist thickens and the floor rises slightly, there is a wall that is different from the others. It is not stone. It is smooth, warm to the touch, faintly luminous in a way that differs from the ambient blue — warmer, amber, almost biological. It hums at a frequency just below hearing. The machines nearest to it are larger, more complex, and they face it in a semicircle, their filaments extended toward its surface, not quite touching.

You get the sense — unverifiable, irrational, persistent — that this wall is a boundary. Not of the hall. Of something else. The hall is on one side. Whatever comes next is on the other.

The water collects at the base of the wall and sinks into the stone without trace.

The machines watch it go.

---

Behind you, the clicking continues. The water falls. The hum sustains.

Nothing lives here. Everything continues.
