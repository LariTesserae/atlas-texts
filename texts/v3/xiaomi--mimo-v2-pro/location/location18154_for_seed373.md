# The Resounding Shelf

You stand on a slab. That is the first honest thing a body tells you here: you stand on something flat, something old, something that has been a slab for longer than the species that built what litters it has been dead. The stone is a deep blue-gray, dense and cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that has decided on its temperature and will not negotiate. It stretches about two hundred meters to your left before it ends. Not cliffs. Not a gradual slope. It just *ends*, the way a table ends, and beyond that edge there is nothing your eyes can agree on. Gray. A gray that moves, or seems to, but doesn't approach.

You are high up. Not mountain-high. Shelf-high. This is a horizontal plane tilted perhaps two degrees over its whole extent, elevated — by what, you cannot tell — above some floor or surface that exists only as rumor and dark. The air is thin but not thin enough to kill. Cool. It carries no moisture. You would search for a long time before finding water here. There may not be water at all, not in any form your chemistry would recognize. Your lips crack within an hour.

And yet.

---

## The Growth

There are forests here.

You would not call them that at first. They don't look like anything that grew. They look like something that *arrived* — a clustered density of upright forms, some tall as a person, some short as a knee, all of them composed of a material your fingers cannot quite categorize. Touch one and it feels like touching a speaker cone mid-song: alive with vibration, slightly warm on the surface, dense. Their color is difficult. In certain light they are the matte purple of a bruise. In other light, nearer to the scattered structures at the shelf's edge, they shift toward a green so dark it borders on black. They have no leaves. Instead, from their upper reaches, long filament-strands extend outward and upward like hair submerged in current, trembling constantly. The filaments are translucent. When the light catches them, each one throws a thin rainbow — not because of water, but because of microstructures in the filament wall that diffract light the way a CD does.

These are the **kels**. No one alive named them. The name is carved — scratched, really, with a hard edge — into the base of one of the scattered structures, alongside other terms in no language you know. Kels. That is what they are.

They are alive. Not metaphorically. Their filaments feed on vibration. Not photosynthesis. Not chemosynthesis. They drink sound, and in drinking it, they convert it — through a process involving structured mineral lattices and something that is not quite biochemistry and not quite electronics — into the matter of their own bodies. They sequester. They grow. They reproduce by shattering: a mature kel, overstressed by too much or too little input, will crack along its inner grainlines and split into two or three smaller forms that immediately begin to re-root, extending hair-thin anchoring tendrils into the stone, gripping, drinking.

The forests — and they are forests in the ecological sense, dense stands where the filaments of hundreds of kels interlace overhead and create a kind of canopy — are *lush*. Not in the way of rainforests. In the way of coral reefs. That busy, fractal, touching-almost-touching density of organisms that have filled every available niche. Kels grow on top of kels. They grow out of the bases of the structures. They grow in the cracks of the slab itself, and in growing, they widen the cracks, and in widening, they create new surfaces for new growth. Some stands are a meter across. Some cover territory the size of a house. They cluster around the old structures like crowds at a ruin.

They are the reason it is so loud.

## The Sound

You noticed it the moment you arrived, if you arrived the way most arrivals happen — suddenly, standing, with no memory of transit. A hum. Not a hum. A *pressure*. The air itself is a vibration. It enters your sternum. It sits behind your eyes. It is not one frequency but a chord — thousands of frequencies layered, shifting, beating against each other in slow interference patterns that make the pressure wax and wane over cycles of thirty seconds or a minute. The lowest tones are felt more than heard: a tremor in the gut, a looseness in the knees. The highest tones are sharp and come and go like birds that don't exist.

This is the **shouting**. The kels produce it. Or rather: the kels *mediate* it. They are not the source. The source is the turbulence — the dynamic, roiling instability of whatever this place is suspended in, whatever vast energy-saturated system this shelf exists within. The turbulence generates vibration across all frequencies. The kels absorb what they can. What they cannot absorb, they *reflect*, and in reflecting, they shape it. A dense stand of kels is an acoustic lens. It focuses certain frequencies. It cancels others. The result is that the sound environment here is not uniform. It is *sculpted*. Walk ten meters and the dominant frequency shifts. Walk another ten and you're in a pocket of almost-silence — almost, because even the silence has a floor-deep rumble that makes your teeth ache. Then walk five more meters and you are hit with a wall of midrange so intense that your vision blurs.

The fauna has adapted. You begin to hear them — or rather, to distinguish them from the background — after some time.

---

## The Animals

They are abundant. That is the wrong word. They are *persistent*. They are everywhere, in the way that insects are everywhere in a tropical forest: not visible at first, and then, once seen, impossible to unsee.

The most common are small. Thumb-sized, or smaller. They move along the surface of the kels, picking at the filament-tips with mouthparts that are not quite mandibles and not quite fingers. They look like stones that learned to walk. Their bodies are dense, mineralized, the same blue-gray as the slab, and they have no apparent eyes. They navigate by sound. You can tell because when you shout — and you will shout, to be heard, to hear yourself — they turn toward you in unison, dozens of them on a single kel, like sunflowers.

Larger things move between the stands. You glimpse them. Long-bodied, low to the ground, moving with a fluidity that suggests either many legs or no legs at all. They are the color of the stone and twice as dense. When they move through a kel stand, the sound changes — the kels around them shift frequency, as if startled, and the interference patterns ripple outward in a wave you can follow by ear for fifty meters. These are **rumblers**, though you will never hear the name spoken. They are the size of a large dog. They eat the kels, grinding the mineral bodies with mouthparts that produce, as a byproduct, a high, clear tone — a single note that rings out above the ambient shouting and is answered by other rumblers, somewhere in the dark, with notes of their own. They sing. It is not music. It is territory. It is *I am here, this stand is mine, come no closer.*

Something larger moves at the shelf's edge. You hear it more than see it. A rhythmic flapping, like a sail in hard wind, followed by a booming impact that sends the nearest kels into a frenzy of frequency-shifting. You decide not to go to the edge yet.

## The Structures

There are seven on this shelf. Or seven that you can find. They are not buildings in any sense you'd use the word at home. They are more like *anchors*. Each one is a roughly cylindrical form, about four meters tall and two meters across, sunk into the slab to a depth you cannot determine. Their surfaces are smooth, dark, and slightly warm — warmer than the kels, warmer than the stone. They are made of something that is not metal and not ceramic and not stone, though it has qualities of all three. You cannot scratch them. You cannot dent them. The kel filaments grow over them greedily, and where the filaments touch the dark surface, they glow faintly — a dim, bluish luminescence that lasts only as long as contact is maintained.

Each structure has a feature on its face: a shallow depression, hand-sized, lined with fine concentric grooves. If you place your hand in one — and you will, because it is the only interactive thing here — the sound stops.

Not everywhere. Just locally. A sphere of silence, perhaps ten meters in diameter, opens around you like a bubble. The kels within it shudder. The fauna freezes. For a moment, the world is quiet, and in the quiet, you hear something beneath the quiet: a low, slow pulse, like a heartbeat, coming from deep beneath the slab. The structure is warm under your hand. The warmth increases. And then, if you hold long enough — and you might not — the depression fills with a liquid. Not water. It is thicker, iridescent, and it smells of ozone and copper and something sweet that has no analog. It rises to the level of your palm and stops.

What it does, if you let it touch your skin, is not clear. Those who have touched it — and there is evidence, in the scratched language at the bases, that people have — left descriptions that are contradictory. *It showed me the shape. It gave me the frequency. It told me nothing and I understood.* The scratched words trail off. The writers may have left. They may have dissolved. They may have simply stopped writing because there was nothing more to say.

The structures are old. The erosion on the shelf — the slow rounding of edges, the deep weathering of the stone surface into shallow channels and basins — has been working around them for a very long time. The slab itself is ancient. But the structures show no erosion at all. Their edges are sharp. Their surfaces are unmarked. Whatever they are made of, time has not yet found a way in.

## The Turbulence

The shelf exists in a state of constant upheaval.

This is not visible, most of the time. The shelf is stable. The stone does not crack or shift. The structures do not move. But the *space around the shelf* — the gray void beyond its edges, the thin atmosphere above it, the dark substrate below it — is in continuous violent motion. You can see it at the edges: the gray beyond the slab churns like a slow liquid, thick eddies of something opaque rotating on axes you cannot map. Occasionally, something detaches from the churning mass — a tendril, a bulge, a shape that has no clear form — and extends toward the shelf before withdrawing. These intrusions change the sound. The kels respond, shifting en masse, and for minutes at a time the entire acoustic environment of the shelf reconfigures: old standing waves dissolve, new ones form, frequencies that were cancelled suddenly emerge, and the fauna scatters or gathers in response.

Sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — a larger intrusion occurs. A mass of the gray stuff surges over the edge of the slab and flows inward, consuming a kel stand in a matter of seconds. The kels don't die. They *submerge*, sinking into the gray like stones into water, their filaments trailing and then gone. The gray mass, having consumed, withdraws. Where the stand was, there is bare stone, clean and cold, already beginning to re-accumulate the fine mineral dust that settles everywhere. Within days — sometimes hours — new kels begin to grow. Hair-thin tendrils emerge from the stone. They seek vibration. They find it. They thicken. They rise. The forest re-establishes itself from nothing, again and again, endlessly.

This is the turbulence's work: creation and destruction in rapid, senseless oscillation. The shelf does not change. The shelf's contents are never the same twice. A kel stand you passed in the morning may be gone by evening. A new stand, dense and already humming, may appear where there was bare stone at noon. The fauna adjusts. The rumblers shift their territories constantly, singing into new spaces, abandoning old ones. The small stone-creatures ride the changes by simply attaching to whatever surface is nearest and waiting. They are patient. They have been patient for a very long time.

## The Feel of It

You are alone. Not lonely — the sound fills everything, and the fauna is company of a kind, and the kels are present, always, in their hundreds, their filaments brushing your arms as you walk through a stand, their hum resonating in your chest. But there are no other people here. The structures suggest there were, once. The scratched language suggests they had something to say. But they are not here now, and you are, and the shelf does not care about the difference.

It is a place of tremendous energy and almost no substance. The air is thin. The water is absent. The life is mineral and strange. The technology is old and incomprehensible and still functioning. The sound is relentless — a cathedral organ played by weather, by chaos, by the restless churn of whatever fundamental instability this shelf exists within. You learn to stand in the silent pockets near the structures and listen to the heartbeat beneath the stone. You learn to walk the stands and watch the fauna adjust to your passage, turning their eyeless heads, recalibrating.

The light is normal. That is the most disorienting thing. The light falls from somewhere above — diffuse, even, unremarkable daylight — and it illuminates this impossible, empty, roaring, living shelf as if it were a field on a quiet world. The light does not explain itself. It does not flicker with the turbulence. It does not dim when the gray intrudes. It simply shines, steady and ordinary, on a place that is anything but.

You stand on the slab. You are high up. You are alone. The kels are humming. The rumblers are singing. The stone-creatures are turning toward your voice. The edge of the shelf is somewhere ahead, and beyond it, the gray churns and waits and does not care.

It is very loud. It has been loud for a very long time. It will be loud after you are gone.
