The air is the first thing you notice—a held breath, a permanent damp chill that clings to the inside of your lungs. This is a basin, a great, shallow scoop in the world’s crust, walled by young, raw cliffs of dark basalt and slate. Their faces are sharp, unweathered, still bearing the fracture-lines of their recent violent emergence. Water weeps from every joint and fissure, running in slick, black rivulets down to the floor of the valley.

And the floor is saturated. A marshy, waterlogged plain stretches for miles, a mosaic of deep, peat-black pools, trembling sedge-meadows, and islands of solid ground. The water table is barely a foot below the surface; a boot pressed down will summon a gurgle and a stain of dark moisture. The scale is regional, a whole contained ecosystem, but every inch of it is claimed. There is no open earth, no bare stone on the valley floor. It is a packed, interwoven skin of life.

The vegetation is a lush, cold-climate riot. Great, drooping conifers with bark like iron rust cling to the lower cliffs, their roots prying the new rock apart. On the valley floor, thickets of willow and alder, their branches woven into impenetrable screens, crowd the water’s edge. Moss is the true ruler—thick, emerald velvet swallowing fallen logs, carpeting stones, hanging in heavy swags from every branch, drinking the perpetual condensation from the air. It muffles sound. It softens every edge. The only true noise is the ubiquitous, quiet conversation of water: drip, trickle, the deep *glug* of a submerged root, the sigh of mist settling.

And within this dense green web, a city.

It is not a city of straight lines or cleared ground. It is an urban growth as complex and packed as the forest itself. Structures of dark, water-logged timber and stone, shaped from the valley’s own bones, rise on stilts and terraces above the marsh. They are built in layers, leaning on one another, connected by a web of covered walkways and arching bridges that avoid the wet ground. Roofs are steep, shingled with slabs of shale, designed to shed the constant condensation and sudden, cold drizzles. There is no advanced technology—no glint of metal machinery, no wires or engines. This is a settlement of rope, peg, fitted stone, and carpentry. The infrastructure is organic: narrow, winding stairs carved into rock outcrops; channels and small canals diverted to manage the seepage; raised garden plots on the larger platforms, filled with hardy, leafy greens and root vegetables.

The fauna is abundant and integrated. Clouds of small, silent insects breed in the still pools, their swallows’ primary food source. Birds are everywhere—sooty dippers bobbing on the water, sharp-eyed hawks perched on the high crags, flocks of grey, cooing birds nesting in the eaves of the highest terraces. In the deeper pools and sluggish canals, sleek, dark-furred animals slide, their eyes just above the waterline. Moss-covered, shaggy beasts, something between a goat and a boar, pick their way along the bridges and terraces, domesticated and tended for their wool and milk. The city does not dominate the ecology; it is a new, complex stratum within it. The people move quietly, their voices absorbed by the moss and mist. They are part of the active dynamics: fishing, tending, repairing the perpetual water damage, harvesting the endless moss for insulation and tinder.

Light filters down, normal in quality but muted by the high, enclosing cliffs and the ever-present mist. It gives the basin a perpetual, soft twilight, even at noon. The cold is constant, a dry, penetrating chill that the lush life seems to ignore. The rules of this world are written in water and wood: things that resist rot are precious; footing is always uncertain; stillness is an invitation for moss to claim you. The basin is a young wound in the earth, already teeming, already ancient in its wet, quiet, crowded logic. It is a terrarium, a terraced, living thing, breathing out a cold, green sigh.
