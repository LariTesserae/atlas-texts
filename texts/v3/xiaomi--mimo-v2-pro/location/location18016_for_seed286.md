# The Great Chloric Expanse

---

There is no horizon. There is only green, and then more green, and then the place where green becomes indistinguishable from sky because the mist is so thick with transpired water that the air itself has become something the plants own.

You are standing—if standing is the word for balancing on a living surface that gives slightly underfoot like packed moss—on what the mind wants to call ground but which is actually the roof of an unimaginably deep organism. The vegetation here is not growing *on* anything. The vegetation *is* the thing. Roots fused to roots fused to the slow decay of older roots fused to still-older roots, layer upon geological layer, until somewhere far below—theoretically—there is rock. No one has ever confirmed this. Probes sent downward find only more cellulose, more fungal thread, more waterlogged heartwood from trees so old they predate the species of trees now living on their backs. The system has been here so long that it has become its own stratigraphy. It has settled. It has stopped asking questions of itself.

The elevation is high—perhaps two thousand meters, perhaps more; the air is thin enough that sound behaves strangely, carrying farther than it should, arriving at your ears with a flat, clarified quality—and the water is everywhere. Not rain exactly, though there is rain. Not flooding exactly, though the lower layers of the mat are fully submerged in a dark, tannin-stained water that moves so slowly it barely qualifies as flow. The water is *in* everything. The vegetation drinks it and holds it and releases it and the canopy catches it and it runs down every vertical surface in thin silver threads that never reach the ground because there is no ground, only more vegetation, only more water in vegetation. A continent-sized sponge suspended over its own aquifer, which is also itself.

The coolness is the first thing you feel, and then the sound.

---

The sound is the second thing, but it is not second in importance. It is enormous. It is industrial in the original, unmetaphorical sense: it sounds like *industry*. Like a factory the size of a city block, humming and grinding and thumping, but the factory is alive and the factory is the entire visible world. The source is everywhere. Deep in the mat, the waterlogged lower layers of vegetation are in a state of perpetual slow fermentation—cellulose breaking down under the weight of the layers above, methane and hydrogen sulfide and ethanol belching upward through porous channels in the mat, and the mat itself groaning as gas pockets collapse and reform, collapse and reform. It is a sound like pistons. Like conveyors. Like a diesel engine buried fifty meters down, running on rot.

Above that bass note, the surface is a cacophony of insects. They are abundant in the way that a city is abundant with people—not random but organized, each species occupying its station in the architecture of the mat with a precision that borders on municipal. There are gnats the size of dust motes that swarm in columns so dense they look like smoke rising from the green. There are beetles with carapaces like lacquered copper that chew methodically through outer bark with a sound like hundreds of tiny machines stamping sheet metal. There are wasps that build their nests not from paper but from living plant matter, coaxing fibers to grow into walls, so that the nests themselves are indistinguishable from the vegetation—just another swelling in the green. And there are the pollinators, vast and slow in the cool air, heavy-bodied moths with wingspans that could cover your two hands held together, coated in pollen so thick it looks like they've been dipped in yellow dust. They fly with a deep, thrumming buzz that harmonizes, somehow, with the fermentation below. Everything hums. Everything is a machine that does not know it is a machine.

---

The vegetation. Let us try to describe the vegetation.

It is not one thing. It is an assemblage so ancient and so intertwined that taxonomy has given up. What you see from above, if you could somehow rise above it, is a canopy—but not a canopy of individual trees. It is a canopy of *merging*. Trees that grew together a thousand years ago have fused their trunks into single columns forty meters across. Branches from one organism have grafted themselves to branches of another, and another, and another, until the canopy is less a collection of trees and more a single enormous membrane of wood and leaf held aloft on a forest of fused pillars. Light filters through it in green sheets. Below the canopy, there is the understory—a tangle of ferns and epiphytes and mosses so thick that moving through it is like pushing through wet wool. Below that, the waterlogged heartwood. Below that, the deep dark where nothing has seen light in millennia, and the fermentation engines run without interruption.

At the margins—where the mat thins, where the elevation climbs too high or the water table drops too low—there are sparse stands of something like conifers, though they are pale and longneedled and they grow in spirals rather than upright columns, as if even they have been slowly twisted by the weight of the system they are part of. Between them, bare patches. The density is sparse in these outer reaches. You can see through the trunks to other trunks, and beyond them to mist. There is space. The emptiness between presences is part of the aesthetic—a kind of breathing room in a world otherwise so saturated it threatens to collapse under its own abundance.

But in the interior, in the deep mat, there is no space. There is only more.

---

The water deserves its own accounting. It is not a lake, not a river, not an ocean. It is a *condition*. The entire system exists in a state of waterlogged saturation so thorough that the distinction between aquatic and terrestrial has dissolved. The lower mat is fully submerged. Fish live there—not in the open water, because there is no open water, but in the interstices between roots, threading through gaps in the wood like eels through coral. They are pale. Many are eyeless. They feed on the fermenting cellulose itself, scraping biofilm from waterlogged bark with teeth that have evolved into flat grinding plates. Higher up, in the understory, the "air" is so humid that small aquatic organisms—copepods, water fleas, larval mayflies—drift through it as if it were water, because for their purposes it is water. Rain falls, but it falls *into* the system and is immediately absorbed and held and recycled. Nothing reaches the deep water. Nothing escapes.

The water in the deep zones is dark and warm—not from temperature but from dissolved organic matter, tannins and humic acids leached from centuries of decay. It smells of peat and old wood and something faintly sweet, like ethanol. It is, in its own way, the fuel that drives the fermentation engines below. The water is the medium. The plants are the refinery. The whole system is a biological chemical plant operating at continental scale, converting sunlight and water and dead wood into methane and heat and sound and more plant matter and more death and more fermentation, forever and forever and forever.

---

The weirdness is subtle. It is not that anything is wrong. It is that everything is *too settled*. The system has been in equilibrium so long that it has become something like geological—a landscape that happens to be alive, a biome that has achieved the stasis of a mineral formation. Nothing changes. The canopy has not moved in centuries. The fermentation zones produce the same gases at the same rate, year after year, eon after eon. The insects occupy the same stations. The fish thread the same root networks. If you could photograph this place a thousand years ago, it would be indistinguishable from a photograph taken today. The dynamic parameter is zero. The system is frozen—not in temperature, but in time. It has achieved a perfect loop: growth, death, fermentation, gas, water cycling, growth again, and nothing enters the loop and nothing leaves it. Even the weather is recycled. Even the light, filtered through the canopy, arrives at the surface with the same spectral profile it has always had, because the canopy adjusts—microscopically, imperceptibly—its leaf angles to maintain a constant irradiance at the understory level.

This is the strangeness. Not that the place is alien, but that it is *complete*. It is a closed system. It has no edges in any meaningful sense, because everything at the margin is slowly being incorporated into the interior—new growth creeping outward, roots extending into thin soil, ferns establishing in the sparse reaches, water accumulating as the mat thickens and alters the local hydrology. The continent is growing. Slowly. Infinitely slowly. But growing.

---

The sound never stops. It is there in the light hours—when the insects are active and the moths are thrumming and the ferns are unfurling with tiny creaking sounds you can hear if you press your ear to the mat—and it is there in the dark hours, when the insects quiet somewhat but the deep fermentation groaning intensifies as if the system is working harder in the dark, metabolizing what the light hours produced. You can hear it in your sleep. You dream of machinery. You dream of pistons and conveyor belts and the steady churn of something vast and purposeless and unutterably old.

There is no construction here. There are no artifacts. There is no intelligence, unless you count the system itself, and even that is a stretch—it is not intelligent; it is merely so complex and so old that it *resembles* intelligence the way a coastline resembles a fractal. It is pattern without intent. Process without purpose. An industrial economy of matter and energy that has been running for longer than most geological formations have existed, powered by sunlight filtered through its own canopy, fueling its own decay, feeding its own growth, making its own weather, producing its own sound.

You stand on its surface and you are very small and the mist closes behind you like a green curtain, and the sound is in your chest, and the mat gives slightly under your weight, and somewhere far below you a gas pocket collapses with a sound like a closing fist, and the moths drift past you with their cargo of pollen, and the ferns drink from the air itself, and nothing here has ever needed you, and nothing here will notice when you leave.
