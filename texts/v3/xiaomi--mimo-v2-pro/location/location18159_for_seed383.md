# The Shivering Plain

You stand on a vast, cracked plain of basalt andite, far below what might once have been a surface. The light is not sunlight, but a hard, white, sourceless glare that emanates from the very mineral crust, a faintly phosphorescent quality in the silica-heavy rock. It casts no shadows, only a flat, unwavering illumination that makes the distance shimmer. The air is cool and perfectly still, carrying the dry, electric scent of ozone and old dust.

This is a world at the end of its erosion. The great geological forces have done their work. The plain stretches for leagues, a near-perfect horizontal plane interrupted only by shallow, sinuous depressions—dry riverbeds from an epoch ago—and the occasional, startlingly deep sinkhole. The rock is scoured smooth, polished in some places to a dull obsidian sheen, everywhere etched with a micro-fissure network like ancient pottery. It is a landscape settled into its final form, a primordial stability.

Yet it is not still.

The ground trembles. Not with the violence of an earthquake, but with a constant, deep-seated *shivering*, a tectonic restlessness. This is the turbulence. The entire crust is in slow, viscous motion. Great plates of stone, miles wide, drift and grind against one another at the pace of a sleeping creature's breath. Where they meet, they buckle upwards into low, jagged ridges of shattered rock, or subduct downwards into new, raw-edged trenches that exhale plumes of cold, mineral dust. The landscape is being rewritten, but at a pace that feels ancient and inevitable. Every few hours, a section of plain may founder, collapsing into a new sinkhole with a low, grinding roar that is the loudest sound here, quickly swallowed by the immense quiet.

Upon this restless stage, there is a city.

Or the skeleton of one. It is built from the land itself—blocks of the same basalt, quarried from the sinkholes and trenches, cut with primitive but effective stone and copper tools. The construction is dense, organized, yet profoundly simple. There are no fine carvings, no arches or delicate structures. It is all megalithic: thick walls, corbelled roofs, heavy lintels. The architecture follows the shivering of the ground. The city is not one contiguous mass, but a constellation of *wards*—fortified islets of construction, each a small town of low, interconnected towers and enclosed courtyards. They are built upon the more stable blocks of crust, like stone lily pads on a slow, churning pond.

Between the wards, the ground is in perpetual, glacial motion. The inhabitants have built *roads* that are not fixed paths, but dynamic systems of movable stone causeways—great slabs that teams of people, using levers and rollers, can shift and re-lock into new alignments as the gaps between wards widen or narrow. This is the anthill energy: a constant, low-grade, communal labor of adaptation. You see them now, tiny figures in the bright, shadowless light, moving with deliberate purpose across the shivering stone.

And there is life, abundant but alien. No plants. Instead, the dampness—the one in ten days when condensation gathers in the deepest cracks, or a rare, brief drizzle falls from the featureless white sky—feeds vast, slow-blooming colonies of lithic lichen. These are not soft things; they are crystalline, forming intricate, fractal patterns of quartz and calcite across the dark rock, fluorescing in gentle blues and greens under the ambient light. They are the primary producers in this ecosystem.

The fauna grazes on the lichen. Herds of **Gyroliths**—low-slung, six-legged creatures armored in interlocking plates ofite, their heads broad and shovel-like for scraping the crystal-lichen. They move with a ponderous, rocking gait, their heavy tails leaving smooth grooves in the dust. Smaller **Skitter-jacks**, like armored, many-legged beetles, dart between the Gyroliths' legs, cleaning parasites from their shells. In the air, **Veilstings**—translucent, ray-like beings that float on subtle thermal updrafts from the shifting crust—filter microscopic spores and dust. There is no birdsong, no buzz of insects. Only the scrape of a Gyrolith's jaw on stone, the faint click of Skitter-jacks, and the immense, breathing silence of the world.

The rules of this place are written in stone and movement. Nothing is permanent. Your ward may be a fortress today, but the ground beneath it is restless. The great work of this urban, primitive society is not conquest or art, but *navigation*—a perpetual, collective dance with a world that refuses to be still. The bright, cool light shows it all in stark detail: the ancient smoothness, the fresh fractures, the crystalline life, the patient builders on their floating continent. It is a portrait of civilization clinging to the skin of a slumbering, shivering giant.
