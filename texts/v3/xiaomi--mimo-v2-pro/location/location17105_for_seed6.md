The place is the **Great Cistern**.

It exists at the peak of a mountain that no longer remembers sky. What was once a summit has been hollowed out and overbuilt into a single, vast, enclosed chamber—a cathedral of industry and irrigation, permanently submerged in a meter of warm, still, clear water. The floor is a grid of polished basalt slabs, perfectly fitted, with water pooling in the geometric recesses. This is a high-altitude reservoir, but one that has become its own closed ecosystem.

The water, fed by captured mountain springs and atmospheric condensers hidden in the vaulted ceiling above, is everywhere. It is the primary medium. It reflects the dense, packed canopy that fills the space from the water’s surface to the ceiling some sixty meters above. But this is not a forest. It is a **Hydroponic Mekanosystem**.

Every surface is constructed. The "trees" are towering spars of riveted brass and blackened iron, rising from submerged pedestals. They are not dead, however. They are alive with a vegetation so lush and engineered it has become a new form of nature. Thick, rubbery vines with leaves the size of shields spiral up the metal trunks, their roots drinking directly from the warm basin. Bulbous, fruit-like structures, actually organo-metallic nutrient bladders, hang heavy and drip a steady, syrup-like fluid into the water below. Canopies of broad, waxy leaves, genetically tuned to the constant electric-arc light from the ceiling, weave a solid mat of green so thick it creates a twilight zone in the lower reaches. The air smells of ozone, wet chlorophyll, and warm metal.

There are no animals. Not a single insect, bird, or fish. The rustling is not from wings or paws, but from the slow, hydraulic drip of irrigation systems, the minute creaking of metal expanding in the warmth, and the almost imperceptible gurgle of water circulating through filtration grates in the floor. The silence is profound, a heavy, humid quiet broken only by these soft, mechanical sighs. It is the sound of patient, automated cultivation.

The scale is hamlet-size, but packed to saturation. You cannot move through it without pushing aside heavy leaves or ducking under low-slung conduits that weep condensed water. Pathways exist only as submerged catwalks of iron grating, just beneath the water’s surface, forcing one to wade or use small, silent flat-bottomed skiffs. The walls, where visible, are lined with more growth, but also with the guts of the place: banks of silent turbines, control panels with softly glowing vacuum tubes, and vast, circular pressure gauges whose needles tremble with almost no movement. This is an industrial system that has achieved a state of near-perfect equilibrium, a slow, patient cycle of growth, harvest (though no harvester is present), and regrowth, all automated and timeless.

The light is normal, but filtered. The ceiling is a grid of incandescent bulbs behind thick, grime-furred glass, casting a perpetual, warm, yellowish daylight that saturates the greens and makes the wet metal gleam. Shadows are deep and soft.

It is strange. It feels both like a primordial swamp and a heart of a dead machine. The vegetation is too lush, the metal too wet, the quiet too deep. There is no wind. The water is never disturbed by anything but the drip from above. The ecosystem is total, constructed, and utterly alone on its mountaintop, a bubble of engineered warmth and life, slowly, silently growing in the thin, cold air of the world outside its sealed walls. It is not old; its edges are too sharp, its mechanisms too intact. It is young, and it is waiting for a purpose it seems to have already fulfilled.
