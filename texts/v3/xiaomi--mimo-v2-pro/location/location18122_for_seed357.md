# The Steam Gardens of Vehla-ka

You stand on a ridge of freshly-split basite, its edges still sharp from the recent uplift that birthed this entire highland. The rock is dark, almost black, weeping silicate tears that gather in pools before trickling into the air. But the air is not empty. It breathes.

This is not a landscape of stone and soil, with plants growing upon it. This is a landscape of plant, which has chosen to become a place. The substrate is the vegetation itself—a colossal, interconnected root-mat that has fused with the living basite, digesting it slowly, incorporating its minerals into a continent-scale architecture. Where the rock erupted hot and new, the roots were already waiting, sensing the warmth. They have woven a scaffold of themselves around the newborn cliffs and spires.

The dominant form is the **Pipe-Stalk**, a towering, hollow reed of vegetable chitin, some as slender as a wrist, others wider than ancient trees. They rise in scattered groves, their spacing deliberate, allowing the humid, hot atmosphere to swirl between them. They are the engineers here. Their purpose is condensation. The air is thick, wet, and carries the metallic tang of geothermal steam. As the hot vapors from deep earth vents rise and meet the slightly cooler upper air, they condense on the rough, intricate outer surfaces of the Pipe-Stalks. Water beads, then runs down channels grooved into their sides, feeding into the root-mat below. The sound is constant: a percussive, dripping music, underscored by the hiss of steam vents and the occasional, startling *crack* of a stalk splitting under its own rapid growth.

From this root-mat and the lattice of the Pipe-Stalks springs everything else. There are no leaves in the familiar sense. Instead, there are **films** and **bladders**. Translucent membranes, like oily soap bubbles the size of houses, drift slowly, tethered by sticky vines. They fill with captured rainwater or distilled condensation, creating suspended ponds that shimmer with opalescent light. Other structures are **foam-mounds**, spongy, rapidly-growing accretions of cellular material that can double in size in a day, only to collapse under their own weight into a nutritious slurry for the root-mat to reabsorb. This is the turbulence—the ecosystem is a frantic, silent bazaar of growth and reclamation. Nothing is permanent. A spire of woven vines might tower for a week, serving as a condensation chimney, before becoming too heavy and imploding, its materials to be carried away by root-tendrils for new construction.

The heat is pervasive, a byproduct of the geothermal activity that the plants so cleverfully tap. It makes the air shimmer and accelerates every biological process. Light is normal, filtered through the highland mists and the translucent bladders, casting shifting, watery patterns of gold and green across the organic surfaces.

There is no fauna. No birds, no insects. The only movement is the slow, purposeful growth of the plants, the drift of water-filled bladders, and the chaotic, dynamic collapse and regrowth cycles. The "built" environment is total, but it is a grown environment. The plants are the buildings, the plumbing, and the inhabitants. They have rules: grow toward heat and water, share resources through the root-mat, and never let a structure stand if its materials could serve a newer, more efficient design. The weirdness is in this hyperactive, mindless purposefulness—a city with no citizens, building and destroying itself in a loop, a marsh-edge, a rainforest, and a coral reef all compressed into a high, hot, wet, and utterly solitary place. The sound is the only voice it has: the drip, hiss, crack, and slither of a world forever remaking itself.
