The air itself is a thin, hot varnish, tasting of ozone and powdered stone. You stand at the hinge of a world—a high plateau of fused violet basalt that stretches to a horizon wavering with heat-shimmer, cracked into polygons like ancient mudflats but scaled to cathedrals. There is no water here, not in any form you know. Moisture is a rumor from a deeper, vanished age. What liquid exists is mercury-pale, pooling briefly in the deepest cracks at dusk before sublimating into the glare.

This is a world of mineral and mechanism. The "vegetation" is a sparse, brittle forest of silicon formations—great, jagged spires of smoked quartz and obsidian, grown not from soil but from the slow, patient exhalations of the planet's crust over millennia. They are not alive, yet they are not inert. They *sing*.

The sound is everywhere. A deep, pervasive, industrial hum, the thrum of a world-sized engine whose pistons are the grinding of tectonic plates far below. It resonates in the quartz spires, which act as colossal, natural tuning forks, emitting a chorus of clear, ringing tones that layer into a deafening, harmonic roar. Above this foundation, the sound of *movement* skitters and scrapes: the fauna.

And it is overwhelming. Not in number, but in presence. These are not creatures of flesh, but of the industrial sublime. Vast, slow-moving *tectonic grazers*—hulks of pitted iron and obsidian, their bodies akin to walking factories, treads and geared limbs dragging across the basalt. They feed on mineral seams, their internal furnaces glowing cherry-red in their abdomens, processing rock and ejecting fine, hot dust that plumes behind them like slow-motion smoke. Smaller *skitter-kin* made of brass and ceramic dart between their feet, repairing, scavenging sparks of energy. Winged *signal-beasts* of hammered copper and glass vacuum tubes glide on the thermal updrafts, their wings catching the light in sharp flashes, emitting bursts of radio static and decoded, meaningless machine code into the roaring air. They are all part of an ecology, but not of life. Of function. Of ancient, runaway industry with no guiding hand.

The light is normal, a harsh, white sun in a pale brass sky, but the weirdness warps it. At the edges of your vision, reality *stutters*. A quartz spire might appear to be in two places at once for a heartbeat. The shadow of a grazing engine seems to detach and lag behind it, moving a half-second too slow. Heat distortion becomes literal spatial folding, offering glimpses of *elsewhere*—a flash of a different, darker desert, or the inside of a machine clogged with the same violet basalt. The laws of physics here are tired, frayed by the sheer age and the resonant sound, bending under the weight of the slow, dynamic heat and the weight of the overwhelming, mechanical fauna.

There is no built environment, yet the entire landscape is a product. The engines are not artifacts; they are the fauna. The factories are their bodies. The industry is their life cycle, a patient, 2.4-billion-year-old process of reshaping the crust, slowly, so slowly, in accordance with a pattern long broken and forgotten. The scale is regional, open, a plateau where you can see for hundreds of miles and watch a dozen different herds of engines crawling in parallel, forever, their paths worn into the stone like riverbeds, weathered and in balance with the crushing heat and the ringing, empty air.

This is a world after the water and the green, after the builders and their buildings. It is a monument to a process that outlived its purpose, a desert of hot, singing stone animated by the ghosts of industry, where reality itself is just another resource to be mined.
