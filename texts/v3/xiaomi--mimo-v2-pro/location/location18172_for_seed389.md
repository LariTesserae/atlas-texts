The world is a high-angled city of stone and steel, sweating under a constant, heavy sun.

It rises from a raw, unfinished plain of fractured basalt and sharp-edged shale—a geology in its infancy, still cooling from some ancient cataclysm. The plain is not flat; it heaves in slow, unfinished folds and sudden, deep fissures where the crust has not yet settled. From this chaotic foundation, the city ascends in steep terraces, clinging to the sheer, hot faces of mountains that are more like colossal pillars of cooling mantle than traditional peaks. Elevation is everything here; the lower fissures are hazy with heat shimmer and condensation, while the upper reaches bake in a thin, scalding atmosphere.

The city itself is a dense, layered hive, built directly from the mineral substrate. Structures are not erected so much as they are carved, cast, and fused from the dark basalt, the red-veined granite, and sheets of dull, oxidized metal. There is no wood, no plaster, no living green. The architecture is a brutalist terracing of foundries, smelters, and habitation blocks, connected by enclosed bridges and deep, shaded stairways cut into the rock. Towers are few; the city expands laterally and downward, a labyrinth of subterranean plazas and shaft-lights piercing the gloom of deep pits.

It is an industrial place, but the industry is ancient, patient, and deeply integrated. The sound is a low, ambient hum—the thrum of geothermal taps driving piston-engines deep within the rock, the occasional distant *clang* of a press in a district that never sleeps, the sizzle of condensation on hot metal pipes. Yet these sounds are muffled, absorbed by the sheer density of the stone and the oppressive, hot silence between them. It is a quietness of mass, not of absence.

Water is the secret currency of this vertical desert. It does not flow in rivers. Instead, it appears as a system of engineered condensation. Great, slotted metal fins, high on the sun-facing walls, cool rapidly in the brief twilight, weeping droplets into cisterns. In the lower fissures, where the heat is most intense, steam vents from the earth are captured by vast, turbine-like contraptions, their intake breath a low whistle, their exhaust piped upward to cool and become potable fluid. Pockets of water exist in sealed, industrial reservoirs, their surfaces still and hot. Dampness is everywhere—a slickness on shaded stone, a drip in a forgotten corner, the constant humidity of the lower levels where fungal growths thrive in the eternal, artificial twilight.

Here is the core strangeness: the dominant life is fungal, and it has entwined itself utterly with the machine and mineral world. Not plant-like, but architectural. Thick, leathery shelves of *Coniomyces petrus*—the Stone-Fungus—grow directly on the damp, cooling walls of the smelters, digesting metallic oxides and exhaling a faint, coppery scent. In the deepest cisterns, bioluminescent mats cast a slow, pulsing green light on the water’s surface, their cycles measured in weeks. Rare, blind insectoid fauna, more chitin than flesh, navigate these fungal forests, their life cycles synchronized to the slow drip of condensate and the even slower pulse of the city’s heart.

There is no soil. The fungal networks derive their energy from chemosynthesis, breaking down the minerals and the chemical byproducts of the industry above. They are the ecosystem, the sanitation system, and a low-grade, ever-present nuisance, clogging filters and etching patterns into the metal. They are recognized not as biology in the traditional sense, but as a form of slow, organic corrosion, a partner in the world’s patient erosion.

The light is normal, filtered through a perpetually hazy atmosphere—a mix of fine mineral dust and condensing vapors. It casts everything in a bronze, diffuse glow, without sharp shadows. The heat is a constant, radiating from the rock itself, a residual memory of planetary formation amplified by the city’s own engines.

Time moves in geological and industrial cycles here. The dynamic is slow: a cooling front advances a meter per century; a new terrace is carved from the mountain over generations; a fungal bloom spreads through a ventilation shaft over a decade. It is a dense, hot, quiet, and profoundly patient place, where the boundary between the built and the grown, the ancient and the industrial, has long since fused into a single, sweating, mineral reality.
