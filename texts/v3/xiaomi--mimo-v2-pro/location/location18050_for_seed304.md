The city is called Verath-in-the-Cleft, and it is less built than woven into the throat of the world.

You arrive not by road, for there are none, but by water. A flat-bottomed punt, poled by a silent figure in oilskins, glides through a network of canals that are the streets. The scale is intimate, claustrophobic. The canyon walls rise sheer on either side, not twenty paces apart at the water’s surface, narrowing further as they ascend until they frame a mere ribbon of pale, normal sky. The rock is young, sharp-featured basalt, dark and weeping. Constant condensation beads on every surface, gathering into rivulets that cascade down in glittering threads, feeding the central canal and a dozen smaller channels that chitter and drip from every level. The air is cool, heavy with moisture, and smells of wet stone, woodsmoke, and something akin to crushed mint.

This is a packed place. The density is not horizontal but vertical. From the very lip of the cleft down to the dark, slow-moving water, the settlement is a staggering tapestry of walkways, balconies, rooms, and bridges, all clinging to the cliff faces. It is a city of shelves. The construction is primitive but ingenious: whole trunks of a pale, water-resistant wood are pegged into cracks in the basalt, supporting platforms of woven reed and thatch. Rope bridges, slick with spray, stitch the two canyon walls together at dizzying intervals. There is no metal, only wood, reed, stone, and a black, tarry substance used to seal joints against the perpetual damp. From a distance, it resembles a colossal, intricate swallow’s nest, or the layered sediment of some ancient, flooded seabed.

Vegetation is sparse, but tenacious. Patches of a velvety, black-green moss, called “velvetlung” by the locals, absorb the moisture and faintly luminesce at dusk, providing a soft, eerie glow to the lower levels. Hardy ferns with fronds like dark wire erupt from cracks, and a single, ancient willow, its roots questing down the cliff for hundreds of feet to sip from the canal, is considered a sacred grove.

Fauna is rare, but the place is not without other life. The constant activity is weather and water. The cleft creates its own micro-climate. Fog rolls in along the canyon’s length at dawn, so thick you cannot see the opposite wall, and by midday, sun shafts pierce the gloom, turning the mist to gold and silver. The drip and trickle of water is a constant, percussive soundscape, punctuated by the creak of wood, the distant, rhythmic *thock* of someone splitting a root with a stone adze, and the murmur of voices carried far in the still, wet air. The dynamic energy is in the water’s cycle—seeping, gathering, flowing, evaporating—and in the ceaseless, slow-motion bustle of the inhabitants.

They are a small, quiet people, moving with deliberate care on the slick surfaces. Their tools are bone, polished stone, and dense wood. Their economy is based on the *velvetlung* moss, harvested and dried into a slow-burning, waterproof fuel, and on condensation traps—arrays of woven mats and stone basins placed to catch the weeping from the rock, channeling fresh water into cisterns. Their technology is the technology of the catch, the channel, and the careful weave.

The weirdness is in the relationship between the packed human order and the wild, wet, vertical chaos. There is no boundary between city and cliff. A stalactite might form the centerpost for a lantern. A family’s hearth is built around a natural spring that bubbles up through the floor of their woven chamber. Children play on bridges that sway over the dark canal, their laughter echoing up the cleft, while above, water drips a ceaseless, cool rhythm onto everything that is, and everything that is built. It is a place saturated with presence—stone, water, wood, life—all occupying the same tiny, resonant slice of the world.
