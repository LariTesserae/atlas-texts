You are standing on the **God-Steps**, a terraced megastructure that climbs a mountain so high its peak is lost in the permanent, bright overcast. Everything here is made, but nothing is dead. The place is a fusion of ancient architecture and a engineered, living biome—a total built environment that has fully synthesized with cultivated life.

The mountain itself is gone, replaced by a pyramid of concentric, circular platforms, each a kilometer wide, stacked and set back as they ascend. The material is a kind of ceramicite, warm to the touch, pale grey and weathered by millennia of condensation and slow, rootless growth. It is not inert. Its surface is patterned with capillary networks, bead-like vesicles, and porous ridges, designed to collect, store, and distribute moisture. From a distance, the entire structure looks like a colossal, layered cake left in the rain, sweating and green.

**Water** here is a managed miracle. There is no river, no lake. The dampness is systemic. Every dawn and dusk, a engineered condensation cycle occurs. The warm, humid air from the lower terraces is pumped upward through vast, hidden turbines (the source of the constant, deep industrial **sound**—a resonant, low-frequency hum felt in the sternum). As it cools at higher elevations, water precipitates onto every surface. It runs in glittering rivulets down fluted channels carved into every wall and walkway, pooling in cisterns that double as planters. The air is perpetually thick with moisture, bright and hazy, refracting the **bright** diffuse light into a soft, omnipresent glare.

**Vegetation** is the skin and the flesh of the Steps. Lush, engineered botany carpets every surface in dense, layered profusion. There are no soil beds; instead, plants root directly into the ceramicite’s nutrient-filled vesicles. Low, velvety mosses in electric greens and rust-reds form a base layer. Above them rise thickets of bioluminescent ferns, their fronds pulsing with a slow, cool blue light. Flowering vines with waxy, trumpet-shaped blooms in saffron and violet cascade down the sides of the terraces, their scent heavy and spicy in the **warm** air. Canopy trees with buttress roots that grip the architecture like skeletal hands create dappled shade on the upper terraces, their leaves broad and dark to capture the abundant light.

This botanical density supports an **abundant** and peculiar **fauna**. There are no large creatures. Life is small, quick, and often airborne. Swarms of jewel-beetles with iridescent carapaces click and whirr among the ferns. Hummingbirds with needle-thin beaks and feathers that shift color with the angle of light sip from the trumpet-flowers. In the misty air, translucent, gelatinous "float-jellies"—engineered filter-feeders—drift slowly on air currents, trailing sticky filaments to catch microscopic organisms. Their presence, combined with the flying insects, creates a living, moving haze.

The **scale** is regional, but felt intimately. You can see across to the opposite curve of the mountain, a sweeping wall of terraced greenery and dripping ceramic, but the details are lost in the luminous haze. The **density** is overwhelming. Every cubic meter is occupied by structure, plant, water-channel, or creature. There is no bare space. It is a saturated, three-dimensional tapestry of life and mechanism.

**Light** is bright but indirect, filtered through the eternal cloud layer and the canopy. It creates a shadowless, even illumination that makes colors pop with unnatural vividness. The **temperature** is a steady, humid warmth, like a greenhouse or a jungle floor, maintained by the geothermal vents deep in the structure’s heart and the metabolic heat of its dense ecology.

The **tech** is industrial in scale but organic in execution. The condensation turbines, the hydraulic pumps, the network of nutrient-delivery capillaries—these are the organs of the place. They are visible as great, polished bronze wheels turning slowly behind lattices of living wood, or as broad, pulsating root-like conduits that run along the joins between terraces. The industry is the metabolism of the God-Steps.

**Erosion** here is a deliberate, aesthetic process. The ceramicite is designed to weather. Over centuries, rain and condensation carve new channels, soften sharp edges, and encourage certain mosses to create patterns. The builders—the absent gods—designed for this slow, patient change. The **dynamic** is geological. The Steps are in a state of slow, managed becoming. A new fern species might take a century to dominate a terrace. The great turbines turn at a rate of one revolution per year. Change is measured in lifetimes, not seasons.

The **weirdness** is low because everything, however strange, follows a coherent, if alien, logic. This is not chaos; it is a meticulously crafted, closed-loop ecosystem. It feels ancient, settled, and utterly complete—a living monument to a patient, engineering intelligence that built a mountain to house a world.

The only sounds are the deep, structural hum, the drip and trickle of water, the buzz and click of insects, and the occasional, distant chime of a float-jelly brushing against a ceramic bell-flower. It is loud with life and process, yet profoundly slow. You are not a visitor in a landscape. You are a temporary organism in a vast, breathing, constructed machine-garden.
