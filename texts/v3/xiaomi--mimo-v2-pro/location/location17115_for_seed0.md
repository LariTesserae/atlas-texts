# The Resounding Marsh

---

The first thing that enters you is the sound.

Not gradually. Not as you adjust. It arrives the way pressure does — a physical thing, a wall of layered vibration that finds the resonant frequency of your sternum and stays there. There is no quiet moment. There is no gap between one wave of noise and the next. The marsh *speaks*, continuously, at a volume that makes language impossible.

Beneath everything is the insect chorus — a foundation tone, almost geological in its constancy, composed of billions of tiny stridulating bodies rubbing wing-cases and legs together in a collective pulse. It has a rhythm, though you'd never call it music. More like breathing. The whole biome inhales and exhales on a cycle of roughly nine seconds, swelling and receding by fractions, and overlaid on this are the sharp staccato clicks of beetles the size of your thumb, the glassy whine of midges in spiraling columns, and — every few minutes — the deep, chest-filling thrum of something massive moving through the upper canopy, shaking loose a rain of condensation that has no origin in clouds.

Above the insects: frogs. An impossible density of frogs. They occupy every surface — the trunks of trees, the undersides of broad leaves, the slick black mud, the backs of other frogs. Their calls are not the polite evening choruses you might imagine. They are territorial, agonistic, desperate. A single species somewhere in the mid-story produces a sound like a metal gate being repeatedly slammed; another, ground-dwelling and unseen, issues a bass note so low it registers more as nausea than as heard sound.

Above the frogs: birds, or things that function as birds. You never see them clearly. The canopy is too dense and the light too poor. But their calls come from everywhere — shrieks, trills, mechanical repetitions, and once, from deep in the marsh's interior, a sound so prolonged and sorrowful that the frog nearest you falls silent for the only time you will witness such a thing.

And above all of this: the turbines of insect wings in coordinated flight. Swarms. Not clouds — *architectures*. They move in structures that suggest collective intelligence, columns and sheets and spiraling helices of tiny bodies catching what little light filters down, turning it into something that looks, from a distance, like slow smoke.

## The Light

You must understand the darkness to understand the marsh.

It is not night. You know this because the darkness *breathes* — it shifts in density, pulses, brightens imperceptibly and then dims again, as though the atmosphere itself is alive and passing things through its body. The sun, if there is one, is never visible. The canopy overhead is an interlocking mass of vegetation so thick and so high that it constitutes a second sky — a living ceiling of waxy leaves, aerial root systems, hanging moss-analogues, and epiphytic organisms that drip steadily with condensation. What light reaches the ground arrives in scattered, diffused form: a dim olive glow, sometimes amber, sometimes a faint and sickly violet that suggests the composition of whatever star hangs above this world is not quite right.

But the light that exists is *enough*. Enough for the vegetation, which is so aggressive in its growth that you can watch it — actually watch it, in real time — extending tendrils across open mud, thickening, darkening, reaching. Enough for the animals, whose eyes catch it and reflect it back in pairs: tiny points of reflected amber everywhere, in the mud, in the branches, in the water. You are being watched from every direction. Not with malice. With simple, total, ambient attention.

In places where the canopy has been disrupted — by a fallen tree, by a recent mudslide, by the sheer weight of accumulated epiphytes pulling a host trunk to the ground — a column of relative brightness falls to the forest floor, and the effect is startling. You see the marsh in full for the first time: the colors, which are almost violent in their saturation. Deep greens so dark they read as black. Flowers in reds and oranges that exist not for beauty but as signals, as warnings, as lures. The mud itself is a rich dark umber, still steaming faintly with residual warmth from whatever geothermal process recently pushed this land above the waterline.

These clearings do not last. You can see the advance — a green tide of shoots and runners and climbing stems pouring into the light, racing each other, already interlocking. A gap that opened this morning will be sealed by nightfall, if nightfall comes. It may not. The darkness may be permanent.

## The Ground

The land is young. You feel this constantly underfoot.

The substrate is soft — not quite mud, not quite soil, but a transitional substance that has not yet decided what it wants to be. In places it is still warm to the touch, heated from below by whatever vulcanism or tectonic pressure birthed this landscape from the sea. The terrain is low-lying and only barely above water level: a broad, flat expanse of emergent land, perhaps twenty or thirty kilometers across, still settling, still compacting, still forming. It gives slightly when you walk. It remembers your footprints.

There are no hills, no ridges, no stone outcrops — nothing old enough to have been carved by time. Instead, the land has a *wrinkled* quality, as though it was compressed and folded as it rose: shallow ridges and elongated depressions, running roughly north-south (if north means anything here), creating a series of parallel channels and elevated fingers of land. The channels collect water — not flowing water, not rivers, but standing pools and seeps and slow, dark trickles that ooze between root systems and vanish into the substrate. The water is warm. It smells of sulfur and green things breaking down.

Where the land is slightly higher — a matter of centimeters, sometimes — the vegetation is thickest. Trees (if they are trees; they might be something else, something with the architecture of trees but the growth habits of fungi, or corals) rise in dense stands, their trunks buttressed by flaring root systems that grip the soft ground the way a hand grips a sponge. They are not tall by the standards of old forests — perhaps fifteen meters — but they are *wide*, spreading horizontally, interlocking with their neighbors, fusing in places where branches have met and grown together. The result is a lattice, a network, a living scaffold from which the canopy is suspended.

Between the trees, in the lower strata, the density becomes almost physical. Vines, aerial roots, hanging mosses, broad-leafed understory plants, and epiphytic organisms pack every cubic meter of space with living matter. You cannot see more than a few meters in any direction. The marsh does not allow distance. It presses in. It occupies every niche, fills every void, exploits every photon and every drop of moisture with an efficiency that borders on greed.

## The Fauna

The animals own this place.

You are a visitor. A brief, irrelevant interruption in a system that has been running — furiously, turbulently, deafeningly — for as long as the land has existed. And the land is young, which means the animals have been here from the beginning, which means they got here *fast*, which means whatever process seeded this ecosystem did so with an almost aggressive generosity.

They are everywhere. Not metaphorically. Everywhere. In the canopy, where things with too many limbs and membranous gliding surfaces move between branches with a fluid, unsettling ease. In the mid-story, where the frogs cling and call and watch with their gold-ring eyes. In the understory, where insects of staggering variety — armored, iridescent, winged, legged, mandibled — pursue their tiny wars of predation and reproduction in quantities that make the air itself feel granular. In the mud, where things burrow and writhe and occasionally surface in a slow boil of dark bodies. In the water, where shapes move beneath the surface and occasionally break it with a soft, deliberate roll.

There are no large predators that you can identify. Nothing that announces itself as an apex. But the sheer *volume* of animal life suggests a complex food web of extraordinary depth — hundreds of trophic levels, perhaps, or a web so tangled that the concept of levels dissolves. Small things eat smaller things. Things the size of your hand eat things the size of your fingertip. Something you cannot see eats things you can. The cycle is fast, violent, and continuous. You hear it in the constant crunch and pop and wet, brief cries that punctuate the insect roar.

Some of the larger fauna — and "larger" here means perhaps the size of a small dog — move in groups. Packs. Swarms. Columns. They are not social in any way that implies hierarchy or affection; they are social the way schools of fish are social, each individual responding to its neighbors with split-second reflexes, the group flowing around obstacles and through narrow gaps with a collective intelligence that emerges from nothing more than proximity and fear. They are fast. They are startled by everything. When one moves, they all move, and the sound of their collective passage through the undergrowth is like a sudden gust of wind.

You get the sense, moving through this place, that you are passing through a *system* — not a habitat, not an environment, but an active, processing, self-maintaining engine of life that has no room for you and no interest in accommodating you. The animals do not flee from you (some do, briefly, then forget you). They do not attack you (not yet, not that you've noticed). They simply continue their business around you, through you, occasionally across you, as though you were a rock or a fallen branch — an obstacle to be routed around, a surface to be climbed, a shadow to be ignored.

## The Turbulence

Nothing is stable.

The marsh exists in a state of constant, churning change that makes it impossible to orient yourself — not just physically, but temporally. Things happen in bursts. A tree falls — you hear it, a deep wet crack followed by a slow cascade of snapping branches and a final, ground-shaking impact that sends ripples through the mud and triggers a chain reaction of animal movement that rolls outward from the site like a wave. Within minutes, new shoots are appearing in the gap. Within an hour, the gap is half-sealed. By the next time you think to look, there is no gap.

Fog moves through the marsh in pulses. It arrives without warning — a thickening of the already-damp atmosphere into something visible, something that condenses on your skin and in your lungs and turns the already-limited visibility into a gray, dripping nothing. It is warm fog. It smells of green. It lasts for minutes or hours; you have no way to measure time in this light. When it clears, the marsh has changed. A pool that was there is gone. A cluster of trees that was there is now submerged to its lower branches. A column of insects is passing through the space where you were standing.

Animal populations surge and crash in waves you can actually perceive. A species of beetle — small, bronze, with a faintly metallic sheen — appears suddenly in numbers that turn the ground into a moving carpet. They are everywhere, on every surface, in every crevice. The sound of their movement is a soft, continuous hiss. Then, as suddenly as they came, they are gone — eaten, dispersed, descended into the mud — and in their place, a species of winged thing you haven't seen before fills the air in spiraling columns, drawn by some chemical signal or seasonal cue that has no season, no calendar, no external reference point.

Storms roll through. Not rain — there is not enough organized moisture for rain, not in the conventional sense. But *disturbances*: sudden increases in wind that whip the canopy into a frenzy, sending leaves and small creatures tumbling; pulses of atmospheric pressure that make your ears pop; flashes of dim, diffused light from above the canopy that illuminate the whole marsh in a brief, eerie glow, revealing the full scope of the ecosystem for one frozen instant — trees in every direction, animals clinging to every surface, fog, water, green, green, green — before the darkness closes back in.

And then there are the *events*. Things you cannot explain and try not to think about. A sound from the interior of the marsh that is not an animal, not a tree falling, not wind — something structural, something deep, something that makes the ground tremble in a way that has nothing to do with weight. A shift in the light that is not a storm-flash but something slower, more deliberate, as though a vast eye opened above the canopy and is slowly, carefully scanning the surface. A sudden silence — the only silence you will ever experience here — lasting three or four seconds, during which every animal stops moving and every sound ceases, and the marsh holds its breath, and you hold yours, and then it all resumes at once, louder than before, as though compensating for the pause.

You do not know what these things are. You file them away. You keep walking.

## The Humans

They are here.

You find their traces before you find them: a sharpened stone wedged in the crotch of a tree, its edge still sharp, its purpose unclear. A scrape in the mud that might be a footprint. A small, deliberately cleared space on a slightly elevated finger of land — two meters across, the vegetation cut back to the roots, the soil packed hard by repeated occupation — where a fire was recently burning. The fire is out. The ash is still warm.

When you find them, they are few. A group of perhaps a dozen, moving through the mid-story of the marsh on pathways they have established between the trees — not bridges, not platforms, but *routes*, memorized sequences of branches and trunks and aerial roots that allow them to travel above the ground-level density without touching the mud. They move silently, or as silently as anything can move in this soundscape. They carry tools: stone blades, bone needles, bundles of fiber twisted into cord. They wear little — the warmth makes clothing unnecessary, and the moisture would destroy anything not constantly maintained. Their skin is marked with deliberate scars or pigmentation patterns that might be cultural, might be practical, might be camouflage.

They do not live here, not really. They *pass through*. They harvest — insects, primarily, and small amphibians, and certain plant products that they collect in woven pouches and carry with them. They do not build shelters. The marsh would consume them. They do not plant. The marsh does not need their help. They are tolerated by the ecosystem the way all small, mobile, generalist feeders are tolerated: as one more presence among thousands, no more significant than the beetles or the frogs or the vines.

You watch them for a time. They do not see you, or they do not acknowledge you. They move with a practiced efficiency that suggests generations of knowledge — they know which branches will hold weight, which pools are safe to drink from, which insects are nutritious and which are toxic. They know the fog-patterns, the surge-cycles, the sounds that mean danger and the sounds that mean nothing. They know the marsh. They are *of* it, in a way that you, with your upright posture and your useless hands and your inability to hear a predator over the insect roar, will never be.

They disappear into the green. The sound closes behind them like water filling a wound.

---

## What It Is

This is a young world, or a young corner of an older one — land that was water, or magma, or nothing, until recently, and is now furiously, turbulently, deafeningly alive. The darkness is not a limitation; it is a condition, like the warmth or the moisture or the density of life, and the ecosystem has long since adapted to it, built itself around it, learned to exploit it. The animals see in spectra you cannot. The plants photosynthesize in wavelengths you cannot perceive. The whole system is tuned to a frequency of darkness and sound and heat and green that excludes you by design.

You can stay. You can learn the pathways the humans use, learn the sounds that mean danger, learn to move through the density without disrupting it. You can eat what they eat, sleep where they sleep (standing, wedged between branches, one hand on your blade and the other on the tree). You can adapt.

But the marsh will not adapt to you. It will continue its cycles — the surges, the crashes, the fog, the storms, the silence, the sound — indifferent to your presence, overwhelming in its vitality, turbulent in its constant, churning becoming. It was here before you. It will be here after. It does not need you. It barely notices you.

The sound, if you stay long enough, becomes a kind of silence. The darkness, if you stay long enough, becomes a kind of sight. The density, if you stay long enough, becomes a kind of home.

But you will never be comfortable. You will never be safe. You will never, for one moment, be alone.
