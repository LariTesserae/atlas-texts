The air is the first thing you notice—thick, not with moisture, but with *presence*. It’s cool, carrying a scent of ozone, hot lubricant, and ancient, powdered stone. Light comes not from a sun, but from the diffused, pervasive glow of the walls and floor themselves, a faint phosphorescence trapped in a substance like polished, milky quartz. It casts no shadows, only flattens everything into a uniform, clinical luminescence.

You are in a space no larger than a grand hall, yet it feels infinite, because the walls curve away and the ceiling is lost in a haze of its own light. The ground is a vast, shallow basin of something that was once regolith, now worn to a glassy smoothness by unimaginable ages. It is not soil, but a compacted amalgam of fused silicates, metallic nodules, and the occasional glint of something crystalline. Erosion has done its work here to a final, absolute degree; every edge is rounded, every surface slick. There are no pebbles, only a continuous, undulating plain of petrified dust.

And it is crowded.

Rising from this smooth floor, in dense profusion, are the *structures*. They are not buildings in any familiar sense, but the skeletal remains of an industry so old its purpose is forgotten. Skeletal gantries of corroded bronze alloy twist towards the ceiling, their lattices woven with delicate, frozen webs of mineral deposit. Bulbous, tank-like forms sit half-submerged in the floor, their surfaces blooming with intricate, fractal patterns of rust and verdigris. Conduits, thick as ancient trees and gnarled with metallic burls, snake across the ground and up the walls, pulsing faintly with a residual, rhythmic warmth. This is the "scattered built"—an overwhelming density of decaying machinery, so layered and interconnected it has become the landscape itself.

This is where the fauna lives.

At first, the sound masks them: a deep, resonant hum that vibrates in your teeth, overlaid with the *clink-scrape-tick* of a million tiny impacts. It is the sound of the place breathing. Then you see the movement. It is everywhere. The fauna is not flesh, but function. Creatures the size of your hand skitter on multi-jointed legs of brass and ceramic, their bodies faceted lenses that focus the ambient light into sharp, darting points. They scrape films of mineral crust from the conduits, ingesting it, and excrete tiny, perfectly spherical pellets that roll into crevices. Larger things, like armored centipedes of riveted iron, patrol the gantries, their mandibles snipping at trailing wires that grow like metallic vines. From vents in the larger tanks, gossamer clouds of spore-like machines drift on thermals, settling to infest new crevices.

This is an ecosystem in overdrive, a frantic, silent ballet of maintenance and consumption. The "vegetation" is the rust, the crystal blooms, the mineral accretions that coat every surface—a slow, inorganic growth that the fauna endlessly grazes upon. The "water" is the viscous, coolant-like fluid that occasionally weeps from sealed joints, forming iridescent, slow-moving pools that are immediately swarmed by filter-feeding mechanisms resembling metallic anemones.

The dynamic energy is the cycle itself: the constant, low-level erosion fought by the constant, low-level repair and consumption of the machines. Nothing decays without being reprocessed; nothing grows without being gnawed back into balance. The primordial age of the place is not in its stillness, but in its eternal, settled activity. It has been doing this, in this pocket of existence, for so long that the distinction between machine and organism, between structure and life, has been worn as smooth as the floor beneath your feet. You are not an observer in a landscape. You are an intruder in a single, ancient, living engine, forever humming its quiet, loud song to itself.
