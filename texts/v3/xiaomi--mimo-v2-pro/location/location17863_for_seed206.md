# The Hollow Engine

You find it by following the sound.

Not a roaring — nothing so dramatic. A low, bodily hum, the kind you feel in your sternum before you hear it. It rises from the ground itself, from a fold in the terrain you might otherwise walk past: a shallow depression in the low country, no wider than a large room, cupped between two weathered ridges of blackite stone that have been here long enough to lose their edges to the fog.

The stone is warm to the touch. Everything is warm here. The air sits heavy and close, not oppressive exactly, but *present* — you are aware of it against your skin, of its moisture condensing on your forearms, on the fine hairs of your neck. You breathe it in like broth. Above, the sky is visible in patches through a canopy that is not a canopy in any arboreal sense. There is no single trunk here, no crown, no branch-logic. Instead the vegetation is *thready* — filamentous, sprawled, a loose web of vine and runner and root that stretches across the hollow's open mouth like someone half-heartedly covering a bowl. Light filters through in normal quantity, neither dim nor bright, just the grey-green of a world seen through glass.

But below — below is where the space opens up, and where you begin to understand why the sound has such depth to it.

The hollow descends gently, its floor a spongy composite of living root-mat and decomposed plant matter that gives slightly underfoot, almost imperceptibly, as though breathing. Water is everywhere but nowhere in volume — it beads on surfaces, runs in thin rivulets along channels in the stone, pools briefly in the cups of broad leaf-structures before spilling onward. The rocks weep. You can see it happening: the dark stone slick with a constant seepage, not from any visible spring, but from the atmosphere itself collapsing into liquid on the cool(er)-ish mineral faces. The moisture feeds the root-mat. The root-mat feeds the runners. The runners feed the filaments that climb the walls and stretch across the ceiling and dangle in loose curtains that sway with no wind you can detect.

And threaded through all of it — *all* of it — is the machinery.

You almost miss it at first. That is the thing about this place. The tech is not bolted on. It is not welded or assembled in any way you can identify. It looks less like something built and more like something *grown into a shape that works*. Gear-structures that might be wood but aren't — they're too regular, too precise in their teeth, and when you press your ear close, the hum sharpens into the unmistakable meshing of engineered surfaces. Conduits that could be vine but pulse with a fluid too viscous, too metallic-sheening, moving in a steady direction as though pumped. Plates of something dark and composite, like shell or bark or ceramic, layered in overlapping rows along the hollow's walls, vibrating faintly, constantly, the source of the deeper resonance you felt in your chest from above.

The plants have not overgrown the machines. The machines have not replaced the plants. They are *the same system*. You follow a runner with your finger — green, rough, photosynthesizing, alive — and watch it thickening, darkening, losing its chlorophyll at a node, becoming denser, until at the junction it is indistinguishable from the cast-iron knuckle of a universal joint. There is no seam. There is no graft-point. The transition is as smooth as a gradient in watercolor. The living tissue simply *becomes* structural metal, and the metal continues onward, humming, doing work you cannot fully see.

What is the work? You can't tell. The hollow is perhaps thirty meters across and ten deep at its lowest point. There are no conveyor belts, no furnaces, no output you can identify. The machines do not produce anything you can find. The plants do not grow in any direction that serves the machines. But the hum is constant — twenty-four hours a day, you'd wager, or whatever cycle passes for a day in this low, foggy country — and the water keeps condensing on the stone, and the filaments keep creeping outward along the ridge-edges, and the gears keep turning at a rate so slow you have to watch one for several minutes to confirm it's moving at all. A revolution per hour, maybe. Perhaps less.

The fauna live in the margins. Small things — you hear them more than see them. Chittering, a wet clicking, the occasional thump of something dropping from filament to root-mat. Beetles the color of machine oil. A amphibian shape that sits perfectly still on a vibrating plate and rides the frequency, its throat-sac pulsing in sync with the gear-mesh below. Winged things that navigate by sound, their echolocation bouncing off the metallic surfaces in patterns that must paint a picture richer than sight could offer. They are abundant, though they keep to the spaces between — between the filaments, between the plates, between the gaps in the root-mat where the machinery shows through. The hollow is full of life, but the life is *in the interstices*, occupying whatever space the dominant system leaves open. Which is, paradoxically, quite a lot. The system is dense in material but sparse in occupation. There is room. The hollow breathes.

Sit here long enough and the sound becomes silence. That is the trick of this place, and perhaps its deepest quality. The hum never changes. The drip never quickens or slows. The gears never speed up, never strain, never break rhythm. The water level neither rises nor falls. The filaments grow so slowly that any change is imperceptible on a human timescale — you could leave and return in a year and the hollow would look identical. It is *established*. Not recently formed, not adapting, not reaching toward anything. It has been doing this — whatever this is — for so long that equilibrium has become its only grammar. The warm, wet, vegetal machinery of the place has calcified into a state of perpetual operation that is functionally indistinguishable from stillness.

And yet it is loud. Aggressively, bodily loud. The hum presses against you. The vibration enters your skeleton. You can feel your molars resonating at a frequency just below music. The sound is not changing, but it is *every present moment*, and it fills the sparse, open, weeping hollow with a density of vibration that the physical space does not otherwise possess.

You sit on the root-mat. Water beads on your boots. A beetle navigates the ridge of your knuckle. The gears turn, and turn, and turn. You begin to wonder if the hollow is a machine that grows plants, or a plant that learned to hum, or a sound that decided to become a place. The answer, you suspect, is that the distinction was settled so long ago that it no longer matters. The hollow is itself. It does not remember becoming. It only continues.

You stay for a while. Not because it changes. Because it doesn't.
