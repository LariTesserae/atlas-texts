The sound is the first thing that becomes law.

It is not a noise you hear with ears, but a physical pressure that begins in the bones of your inner ear and expands to fill the skull, then the chest, then the entire fluid-filled cavity of the body. It is the sound of weight. The sound of a world’s entire ocean pressing down, tonne by tonne, into every trench and onto every plain. It is a sub-aqueous hum so deep and so constant it has become the texture of silence itself. To exist here is to vibrate in sympathy with the abyss.

This is a sphere of drowned continents. The water is not blue, but a uniform, light-absorbing black that grades to a faint, particulate grey where your own limbs disturb it. There is no sun, only a diffuse, directionless glow from above—a memory of light filtering through an atmosphere thick with vapor and a planetary ocean miles deep. It provides no warmth. The temperature is a steadfast, gelid cold, just above the freezing point of this strange, dense brine. It seeps into you, not with the sharp bite of ice, but with a profound, numbing totality.

You are in a highland region, but the concept is translated into pressure. You stand, if one can stand here, on what was once a mountain range. The peaks are gone, sheared off and scoured clean by the initial flood. What remains are vast, blunt plateaus of dark basalt and granite, their features young and sharp in the way that only a freshly drowned landscape can be. There are no soft sediments, no gentle slopes of silt. The erosion is clean, hydraulic, leaving sheer cliffs and dramatic, overhanging ledges that will not slump for millennia. The scale is continental. You could walk (or swim) for a lifetime and see only variations on this theme: colossal, submerged massifs, their flanks carved by ancient, catastrophic currents, separated by plains of shattered rock that fall away into permanent, lightless trenches.

And upon this mineral skeleton, there is no life in the way of green or leaf. The world is post-botanical. The 0.2 of vegetation is a scattering of things that blur the line. Crystalline structures, like frost-flowers of silica, grow in fractal patterns on the rock faces, feeding on geothermal whispers from the planet’s dying core. Mats of chemosynthetic bacterial slime, leprous and pale, coat sheltered overhangs in slow, centuries-long pulses. They are not recognized as biology by the old metrics; they are geological processes that metabolize.

But where vegetation is absent, fauna is abundant, if alien. This is a world of swimmers, drifters, and clingers. Schools of eyeless, translucent things, like animate glass filaments, navigate by sensing the planet’s magnetic field. Larger, slower creatures—armored, crab-like entities with multiple, redundant limbs—scuttle across the plateau tops, grazing on the bacterial mats and the occasional mineral bloom. They make no sound beyond the faint click of chitin, utterly lost in the planet’s roar. They are part of the equilibrium, a slow dance of consumption and reproduction that has not changed in ten thousand years. The dynamic is frozen. The currents are steady. The pressure is constant. Nothing evolves, nothing adapts. It simply… is.

Then, the built world intrudes.

Scattered across the plateaus, like toys dropped by a giant, are the structures. They are industrial. They are of a scale that suggests the water was not always here, or that they were built to withstand it. Colossal cylinders of riveted iron, now encrusted with mineral growths and draped in the slow-growing silica crystals. Great, toothed wheels of bronze-green alloy, seized forever at half-rotation. Pipes as wide as tunnels, broken open, their mouths gaping like silent screams into the black water. This was a mechanized world. A world of scaled production. The factories are drowned cathedrals of industry, their assembly lines now home to schools of the glass-filament fish that dart through shattered skylights. The electrification is dead, but the metal bones remain, slowly, imperceptibly corroding.

The weirdness is in the juxtaposition. It is the absolute, deafening sound of the ocean’s weight, broken only by the occasional, distant *groan* of metal settling or a tectonic sigh from the rock below. It is the industrial skeletons, frozen in mid-task, surrounded by the passive, unchanging alien ecology that has colonized them. It is the light, normal in its quality but sourceless, casting no shadows, making every dimension flat and uncertain. It is the emptiness between the plateaus, a void filled with nothing but water and sound, where the vast scale makes the scattered factories and fauna seem like dust motes in a cathedral.

You are an intruder in a system at perfect, deafening rest. The world is full of machinery and life, yet it is profoundly empty. It is a planet-sized engine, stalled and flooded, humming with the pressure of its own inertia, forever on the verge of a motion that will never come. The cold is absolute. The sound is the sound of eternity. Nothing changes here. It has already become what it will always be.
