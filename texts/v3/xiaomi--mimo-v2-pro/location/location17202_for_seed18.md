You do not so much enter this place as become enveloped by it. The first and last thing you know is the sound—a physical, bone-deep thrumming that is less heard than absorbed. It is the sound of a world holding its breath on an industrial scale: the low, constant growl of immense dynamos buried in the mountain's heart, the rhythmic *clank-hiss* of vast pistons cycling somewhere in the shafts below, and overlaying it all, a high, keening whine from turbines spinning in unseen conduits. This is the voice of the Cage.

For that is what this is: a constructed ecosystem within a single, colossal structure built into the caldera of an extinct volcano. The elevation is extreme, but you stand not on rock, but on a latticework of iron-grate walkways and platforms. There is no natural substrate left. The very walls of the volcanic bowl have been faced with riveted plates of dull, oxidized metal, now streaked with perpetual condensation. Water is not scarce here, but it is managed—dripping from condensation-collectors bolted high on the walls, gathering in shallow, tepid pools in the saucer-shaped plates of drainage systems, and misting from leaky valves in the pressurized steam pipes that run alongside every walkway like metallic vines. The air is warm and humid, a byproduct of the machinery and the trapped solar heat.

And from this engineered dampness, life explodes with a fervent, desperate lushness. Mosses and slick, dark-green algae coat every shaded metal surface, thriving in the constant moisture. From cracks in bolt-housing and between corroded plates, great ferns uncurl their fronds, their roots delving into pockets of accumulated silt and organic sludge. Fungi, in shapes of impossible geometry—translucent shelves, pulsating sacs, delicate corals—bloom on the riveted walls, feeding on the chemical leachates and the ceaseless vibration. This is not a forest, but a grotesque garden, a vertical swamp of vegetation clinging to a machine.

The scale is intimate, yet overwhelming. You are in a grove-sized chamber, a single node in the larger Cage. The walkways branch and reconnect, forming small clearings around a major junction box or a towering, silent capacitor bank that hums with contained lightning. The density of major objects is low; the spaces between the main engines and supports feel vast and hollow, amplifying the emptiness. Yet the place teems with smaller life. Birds with iridescent, oily plumage nest in the crotches of steam valves, their songs drowned out, so they communicate in frantic, visual semaphore with their wings. Insects with crystalline wings flit between fungal blooms, navigating by the vibration patterns rather than light. Sleek, six-legged creatures with reflective carapaces—part mammal, part arthropod—skitter along the pipes, their bodies evolved to find purchase on the slick, mossy metal. They are abundant, a necessary part of the machine's slow decay and rebirth, feeding on the growths, being fed upon by the birds, their waste fertilizing the moss.

The light is normal, grey and diffuse, filtering down from the open sky far above, but it is stained and dappled by the constant mist and the canopy of giant, water-catching ferns that have woven themselves across the upper latticework. Shadows are deep and wet.

The strangeness lies in the balance. This is not a ruin. The machinery is old, weathered, but it is in a state of established equilibrium. The erosion is one of use, not abandonment; surfaces are polished by the touch of creatures, pipes are grooved by the drip of water, growth patterns follow the lines of warmth from active conduits. Change happens at a geological pace for the machine: a new fern takes root, a species of lichen evolves to digest a new polymer seal, a pipe finally corrodes through and is slowly encased in a stalactite ofite mineral deposits. The world runs on its own patient, deafening logic.

It is a terrarium. A vivarium. A single, functioning organ of a greater, unseen body, kept alive not for beauty, but for function. What that function is—power generation, computation, some stranger purpose—is irrelevant to the life that thrums within it. Here, in this hollow mountain of iron and chlorophyll, industry has become an ecosystem, and the ecosystem has become a perfect, noisy, thriving machine.
