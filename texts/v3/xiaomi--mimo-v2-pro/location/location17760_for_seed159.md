You descend into the world.

The air is the first thing that claims you—thick, wet-warm, and tasting of mineral salts and ozone. It is not a cave, not in the traditional sense. The scale is too open, the ceiling lost in a dusky vault where faint, ambient light seems to *gather* rather than shine. It emanates from no single source, but seems woven into the very air: a phosphorescent haze, a residual glow from the warm, damp rock itself, perhaps a slow chemical reaction of moisture on ancient mineral deposits. The light is normal, yet it feels *older* than sunlight, a steady, shadowless illumination that reveals everything with clinical, gelid clarity.

This is a subterranean basin, a region-sized hollow in the world's skin. Elevation is low, but the space feels vast, stretching away into hazy distances where formations like petrified storms or the melted ribs of giants crowd the view. The erosion is embryonic; there are no smooth curves, no patient work of water and time. This is raw geology, a first draft. Jagged spires of obsidian and raw basalt thrust upward, their edges still sharp from sudden cooling. Fractures in the ground are fresh, their walls glittering with unweathered crystals. It feels less carved and more *exuded*, a place still hot from its own violent birth.

And it is dense. Every cubic meter is saturated with presence. The ground is not bare stone; it is a crust of sulfurous crystals, a mat of spongy, rust-colored fungal mycelium that crunches underfoot, and everywhere, a fine, gritty dust that hangs in the warm air. Moisture is constant—a slow, heavy condensation beads on every surface, dripping in irregular rhythms from stalactites that look more like melted wax than stone. There are no rivers, but there are seeps, trickles that feed the fungal mats and collect in transient, steaming pools in depressions. The water is not pure; it is a mineral broth, slightly viscous.

But the overwhelming presence, the reason the very air feels crowded, is the fauna.

There is no greenery, no rooted life. The ecology is built on simpler, hungrier things. Swarms of lithic-armored beetles, each the size of a fist, scuttle over the rocks, their mandibles grinding the softer minerals with a sound like grinding gravel. They are not the loudest sound. That belongs to the **Tremor Flies**—massive, slow-beating insects with wings of chitinous glass that create a constant, low-frequency *thrum* that vibrates in your chest. They navigate by sonar, their clicks and chirps layering into a disorienting cacophony. From the fungal mats, pale, eyeless salamander-things emerge, their skin slick with chemosynthetic bacteria that glow in faint pulses.

This is a world of frantic, primitive industry. The scattered built environment is not of human make. Here and there, clustered around the warmest seeps, are mounds. Some are built of chewed mineral paste and fungal resin by colonial insect-analogues, resembling termite castles but made of glittering mica and quartz. Others are simpler: circles of arranged obsidian shards, or crude lean-tos of basalt slabs, suggesting the passing, minimal shelters of something larger, something that uses basic tools. A discarded flint scraper lies half-buried in the dust. A sharpened bone, stained with mineral pigments, leans against a rock.

The turbulence is in the very fabric of the place. The warmth is not steady; it pulses from geothermal veins below. Every few minutes, a tremor shakes dust from the ceiling, sending the Tremor Flies into a frantic, buzzing orbit. A distant, resonant *groan* echoes through the chambers—the sound of rock under immense stress. Steam erupts from a fissure a hundred yards away, lasting thirty seconds before vanishing. The fauna reacts in waves. The beetle swarms surge and retreat in unison, as if following an unseen tide. The salamander-things burrow back into their mats. Creation and destruction are in constant dialogue; a new crystal formation, sharp and brilliant, is already growing where a steam vent collapsed a fungal tower moments before.

It is a mineral stomach, digesting itself. A hot, wet, loud, and impossibly crowded embryonic world, where the only logic is the logic of immediate need: to eat, to shelter, to avoid the sudden hiss of steam or the crack of splitting stone. The light watches, the sound pulses, and the ground itself breathes. You are not an observer here. You are another disruption in a system made of nothing but.
