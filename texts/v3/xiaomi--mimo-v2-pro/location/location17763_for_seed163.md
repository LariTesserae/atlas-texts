The light hits first. It is a high, thin, brilliant light, the kind that scours a landscape rather than illuminating it, pouring from a sky that is more white than blue. The air itself seems to vibrate with it, a palpable, dry-ice shimmer that has nothing to do with heat. It is cold—not the deep cold of true altitudes, but a persistent, wet cold that sinks into the bones. The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a mat, a living thatch of roots and rhizomes and spongy, green-black stems, breathing moisture into the chill air. Where the vegetation thins, the rock beneath is not ancient, weathered stone, but a raw, porous basalt, still sharp-edged, weeping dark, mineral tears from its many pores. This is a landscape still in its first draft.

The sound is a physical weight. It is the sound of a world being *occupied*. A vast, damp, vegetative hum rises from the root-mat, the combined thrum of a billion fast-growing cells pushing water upward. Interlaced with this is a sharp, rhythmic *cracking* and *sucking*—the sound of the ground itself, expanding and contracting as water is drawn up and released from some shallow, subterranean aquifer. But overwhelming it all is a layered, metallic drone. It comes from the scattered giants that stride across the plateau.

They are harvesters, but they are also pilgrims, perhaps. Structures of riveted iron and stained copper, they move with a slow, tectonic patience, each step a grinding, hydraulic sigh. Their long, articulated arms end in shears and vacuums. They are not clearing the land. They are *shepherding* it. They cut swaths through the thick vegetation, not to destroy it, but to shape its growth. The harvested biomass, a wet, fibrous pulp, is pulped and extruded from vents in their sides, forming low, curving walls or piled into rough, porous mounds. These are the "buildings"—not habitations, but geological markers, artificial moraines of processed life. They are already being reclaimed, green tendrils probing the crevices, mosses softening the edges. The machines themselves are becoming terrain; one, long-stilled, is now a hillock, its iron back furred with grass, its empty eye-sockets trailing ferns.

The ecology is monolithic. There are no trees, no flowers in the human sense. The vegetation is a single, sprawling super-organism, a graminoid thicket of tall, reed-like stalks and vast, low-lying lily pads of succulent leaf, all interconnected. It is the substrate, the soil, and the atmosphere-maker. In the few open spaces—scars left by a harvester’s recent pass, or where raw rock is still too new and wet for colonization—you can see the embryonic geology. The stone is vesicular, full of tiny holes that hiss air. Water gathers in these pockets, or runs in thin, dark rivulets that disappear as quickly as they form, drunk by the rhizome-network. Pools are rare, ephemeral, and fringed with a silvery, lichenous crust.

Fauna is a rumor. A flash of iridescent chitin in a shadow—the wing-case of some beetle-like thing that processes fallen vegetation. A high, thin cry that might be a bird, but is just as likely a stress-fracture singing in a cold-stressed boulder. The machines make their own fauna.

The world’s rules are clear: growth is relentless, but its form is dictated. The machines are not invaders; they are the ecosystem’s slow, industrial teeth, the means by which this hyper-growth is maintained in a state of productive adolescence. The land is being built, not eroded. The "deafening" sound is the sound of construction, of life and machine in a feedback loop of generation and shaping. Change is slow because the project is immense—a regional-scale sculpture of living pulp and weeping stone, under a bright, cold, patient sky. It is a landscape of immense vitality, yet sparse in its final forms; a place of immense noise, yet utterly devoid of chatter; a place that feels both brand new and eternally itself.
