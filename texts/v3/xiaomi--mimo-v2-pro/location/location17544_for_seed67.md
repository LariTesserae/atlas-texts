# The Soaking Foundations

The first thing you understand is that the ground is not ground.

It is roof. You are standing on the lid of something.

---

The surface extends in every direction, a horizon so flat and so far that the curvature of the world-hull — for it is a hull, there is no other word — becomes visible only as a faint sense that everything is *tiling*, falling away at the edges into a haze of warm moisture. The material beneath your feet is dark, dense, and faintly warm to the touch: not stone, not metal, but something ceramic-polymer, poured and cured and layered an unknowable number of times. It is smooth where water has run over it for a long time. It is rough and sharp-edged where water has not — where it was recently surfaced, or where sections have been replaced, or where some internal pressure has *bulged* a plate upward by a centimeter or two. These uplifts are everywhere. They form low ridges and corrugations, and they are one of the only features on this otherwise numbingly vast plane.

The water is everywhere.

Not pooled, not flooding — the surface is too carefully graded for that, sloped at angles so subtle that your eye cannot detect them but your ankles can, over hours, as you realize you have been walking slightly downhill for a very long time. The water moves in films. It runs in capillary channels no wider than your thumb, etched into the surface material in branching fractals. It condenses on every cool shadow-face and drips in slow, metronomic sequences. It weeps from the edges of seams — where one poured section meets another — as if the hull is sweating. The air is warm and heavy with it. Not humid in the way of a jungle. Humid in the way of a room with many mouths breathing. The moisture has a taste: mineral, faintly metallic, with an undertone that might be ozone or might be something organic you cannot place.

There are no plants.

Nothing photosynthetic. Nothing rooted. Nothing green or leafy or even vaguely vegetal in any direction you can see, and you can see a very long way. The fractal channels in the surface might, at first glance, resemble root systems or river deltas, but they are too regular, too obviously *milled*, and they carry no sediment, only clear warm water that moves with a pace set by gravity and surface tension and nothing else. Where the channels widen into shallow sumps — low basins the size of swimming pools — the water is still and clear to the bottom, and the bottom is more of the same dark material, with the same channels continuing downward, as if the entire hull is veined with this circulatory geometry, top to bottom, layer to layer, in three dimensions.

The sumps are where you first encounter the fauna.

---

They are not animals in any sense you are comfortable with.

The first ones you see are sessile — attached to the edges of the sumps, gripping the ceramic with structures that are somewhere between a barnacle and a hand. They are small, fist-sized, the color of wet slate, and they open and close in rhythms that do not correspond to any environmental cycle you can detect. Inside each one is a cavity filled with fluid that is not quite water. It is slightly viscous. It catches the light differently. The creatures — if that is what they are — seem to *process* this fluid, cycling it through their cavities in a way that leaves faint iridescent traces on the surface of the sump water. They do not move. They do not flee from you. Some are clustered so tightly that their shells overlap, and where they overlap, the material of the hull beneath them has changed — corroded, or been *dissolved*, or perhaps *eaten* — revealing a darker, softer substrate below.

This is the first suggestion that the hull is not inert. That the water and the creatures and the material are engaged in some slow, vast exchange you are only beginning to perceive.

Move away from the sumps. Walk. The plane continues. It continues for a very long time.

---

You begin to notice the density.

It is not visible from any single point — the surface appears empty, featureless, a dark wet plain under warm diffuse light (the sky is overcast, always, a ceiling of uniform grey cloud that might be the upper surface of the hull, or might be weather, or might be something else entirely; the light comes from everywhere and nowhere; there are no shadows except in the channel-creases). But as you walk, you begin to understand that the hull is *occupied* at every scale.

Beneath the surface, something is always moving.

You feel it as vibration, faint and constant, transmitted through the ceramic into the bones of your feet. Not machinery — or not only machinery. There is a low, sub-bass rhythm to it, a pulse, and then within that pulse, faster oscillations, and within those, faster still, a nested hierarchy of activity that suggests the hull is not a structure but an *organism*, or a machine complex so vast and so deeply integrated that the distinction has lost meaning. Sometimes, in the stillness of a sump, you can see the water tremble with it. Concentric ripples expanding from no visible source.

The creatures in the sumps respond to it. Their opening-and-closing quickens during certain harmonic peaks. During the troughs, they go still.

And there are more creatures than the sessile ones.

They are larger, and they move, and they are difficult to describe because they are difficult to *see*. They live in the channels. In the seams. In the narrow spaces between panels where the water runs fastest and the light barely reaches. You catch glimpses of dark elongated bodies, segmented or jointed or possibly neither — moving against the current, or with it, or standing still in the current and letting the water move around them. They are the size of your forearm, or your leg, or — rarely, and seen only in the deepest channels where the water is dark — the size of something you do not want to stand next to.

They do not come onto the surface.

You do not go into the channels.

This is not a rule you were told. It is a rule the landscape teaches you through geometry: the channels are too narrow, too deep, too branching. You would be lost inside them in minutes. And they are *full*. The density of life inside the hull's circulatory system is, you come to understand, orders of magnitude greater than anything on the surface. The surface is the desert. The channels are the ocean. You are walking on the thinnest possible skin of a world that is almost entirely interior.

---

What is inside?

You have not found a way in. The seams between panels are centimeters wide at most — enough for water, enough for the channel-creatures, not enough for you. There are no doors. No hatches. No maintenance access. The hull was not built for inhabitants to walk on it, and it was not built for anything to enter it from the outside. It was built to *enclose*. What it encloses is below you, around you, beneath every centimeter of the surface, humming and pulsing and cycling water and dissolving and rebuilding and *alive* in a way that makes the word "alive" feel like a translation from a language you don't speak.

The few features that break the surface — the panel-uplifts, the occasional *structure* — only deepen the sense.

The structures are low, wide, dark, and they extrude from the surface as if grown rather than built, though everything here is built. They have the same ceramic-polymer material but in different densities, different finishes: some matte, some glassy, some with a texture like skin. They vent warm air, or warm water, or — in one case — a mist of droplets so fine it hangs in the air for minutes, forming a localized cloud that drifts slowly across the plain before dissipating. They hum at different frequencies. Some of the channel-creatures emerge to cluster near them, pressing their bodies against the warm surfaces, and then retreat.

These structures might be the factories. The industrial heart. But they do not look like factories. They look like organs.

---

The world does not change.

You have been here — you cannot tell how long. The light does not vary. The temperature does not vary. The water does not rise or fall. The creatures in the sumps open and close in the same rhythms. The vibration in the hull is the same vibration. There is no weather except the constant warm overcast. No rain, no wind, no storm. The moisture condenses and drips and films and weeps in exactly the same patterns as when you arrived.

This is the strangest thing. Not the creatures. Not the hull. Not the scale or the density or the absence of anything you would call nature. The strangest thing is the *stasis*. A world this wet, this warm, this dense, this alive — it should be *churning*. There should be growth and decay and competition and cycles and seasons and upheaval. Instead, everything is in equilibrium. The hull is in equilibrium. The creatures are in equilibrium. The water is in equilibrium. It is a vast, warm, wet, populated, fully-constructed world that has settled into a state so stable it might persist forever, and the only question — the question that grows louder with every hour you spend walking across its sweating, humming, irreducibly alien surface — is:

Equilibrium with *what*?

What is this system optimizing for? What is the steady state? What is the hull *doing*?

The water runs. The creatures open and close. The vibration hums.

Nothing changes.

You keep walking.
