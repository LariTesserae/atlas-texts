The city is grown, not built. It rises from the salt pan like a frozen, mineral exhalation, a forest of spires and arches and impossible buttresses that climb toward a dim, pearlescent sky. There is no water here, not in any familiar sense. The ground is a cracked, crystalline plain of fused chlorides and silicates, a vast salt lake evaporated to its final, glittering bone. The air is thin, dry, and carries a faint, metallic tang. It is cool—not with the chill of ice, but with the profound, stable coolness of a deep cavern or a moonlit desert.

This is a place of extreme elevation. The city-cliff towers thousands of meters above the salt flat, its foundations lost in shadow, its highest pinnacles piercing a permanent inversion layer where the thin atmosphere glows with a soft, sourceless luminescence. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight that seems to emanate from the very substance of the sky and the faint phosphorescence of the stone itself. There are no suns, no moons, only this diffuse, silvery gloom.

The architecture is ancient and weathered, yet utterly alien. It is not made of block or beam, but appears to have been *extruded*—grown like a crystal garden or a coral reef, then sculpted by millennia of silent, abrasive winds. The structures are porous, latticed, full of vast, hollow naves and spiraling ramps that lead to nowhere. The material resembles a fusion of basalt, quartz, and something organic, like chitin or bone, all with a dull, matte finish that drinks the light. Erosion has not destroyed it, but refined it. Edges are soft, surfaces are etched with fine, swirling grain patterns, and entire districts have slumped into beautiful, abstract forms, like the worn molars of titans.

It is empty, yet full. The density is shockingly low. For kilometers, one walks through silent plazas and concourses where only the wind—inaudible, felt only as a steady, cool pressure—moves. Between the great spires, distances are vast. The scale is regional, planetary. You can see for hundreds of kilometers from a high balcony: across the salt pan to other, smaller city-mesas on the horizon, themselves great mineral growths, all connected by faint, geometric traces on the plain—ancient roadbeds or aqueducts for substances long forgotten.

And yet, it is not dead. It is profoundly dynamic.

A slow, geological pulse thrums through the stone. In certain districts, over spans of years, new minarets bud from the sides of older towers, smooth and pale. In others, facades silently shear away in great, curved plates, falling to the ground over decades to reveal fresh, intricate interiors. This is the city's metabolism: a cycle of crystalline growth and sublime decay, a weathering that is also a form of creation. The "fauna" are part of this. Rare, ambulatory mineral formations—things like walking stalactites or floating geodes—drift on thermal currents, their silicate bodies tinkling in frequencies below human hearing. They leave no tracks, consume nothing obvious, and seem less like creatures and more like mobile organs of the city itself.

The "weirdness" is the atmosphere itself. Reality here is thin, negotiable. Perception glitches. A spire seen from one angle appears solid; from another, it is a lacework of light. Sounds are impossible: not echoes, but *pre-echoes*—a faint chime might be heard a full second before a distant crystalline growth completes its fracture. Space folds oddly; a corridor might return you to your starting point, though it seemed straight and long. The ground might feel spongy for a step, then diamond-hard. This is not malfunction, but the native state—a place where the rules of geometry and time are suggestions, worn smooth by age.

There is no technology. There are no devices, no wires, no wheels. The entire city is the technology: a vast, silent, growing-and-dying machine of stone and light and broken physics, built by no one, serving no discernible purpose but its own endless, slow transformation. The silence is not an absence, but a presence. It is the sound of stone growing at the rate of continents, of light bending through a crystal lens over centuries, of a world dreaming itself into a new and stranger shape, one imperceptible moment at a time.
