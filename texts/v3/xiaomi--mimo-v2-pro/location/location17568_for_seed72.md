# The Burrow-Town of Khenarath

---

It smells like sulfur and wet iron, and the air is heavy enough to drink.

You descend. The entrance is not dramatic — a collapsed sinkhole in a dry upland, shored up with old timber and corrugated sheet metal that radiates heat like a griddle. Someone has welded a railing from rebar. It is too hot to touch, but there are handprints on it anyway, dark with old oil. The stairwell turns twice, three times, and then opens.

You are inside the earth, and the earth is *alive*.

---

The space is roughly the volume of a cathedral nave laid on its side — perhaps eighty meters long, thirty wide, and fifteen meters to the ceiling, which is not a ceiling so much as the bottom of a rock shelf, seeping. The stone is pale, powdery, half-formed. In places it is still rough with the texture of raw sediment. It has not had time to become hard. Erosion here is embryonic: the rock is still deciding what it is. Fissures run along the walls in bright wet lines, and from these fissures water weeps in slow, beadlike curtains. It collects on the floor in shallow, lukewarm pools no deeper than your ankle, and from these pools rise stands of pale reed — the only vegetation — thin as wire, yellow-white, growing in clumps of three or four. They do not photosynthesize. They feed on the minerals in the seep. They are not abundant. There are perhaps two dozen clusters across the whole chamber, and between them, bare stone and water.

The light is dim. It comes from three sources: chemical lanterns hung on cables that span the ceiling at irregular intervals, each one a glass jar full of slow greenish luminescence; a single industrial floodlight mounted on a wooden pole near the center of the space, flickering and buzzing, powered by a generator you can hear but cannot see; and, behind everything, a faint bioluminescence in the rock itself — just barely there, like the memory of a glow, bluish, most visible in the seams where water runs. None of it is enough. You keep squinting. The edges of the room remain uncertain.

---

The sound is loud, and it is made of overlapping things.

The generator — somewhere behind a partition of stacked stone and scrap metal — produces a constant mechanical drone, a low throb that you feel in your molars. Above it, the water. Dripping, trickling, seeping, plinking into shallow pools in a thousand different rhythms, none of them synchronized. The humidity carries sound well, and the rock walls, unfinished and porous, scatter it in every direction. Everything echoes and nothing resolves. There is no silence here, only gradations of noise.

And beneath the machinery and the water: the fauna.

---

The fauna is *overwhelming*.

This is what Khenarath is, in the end. A town built to coexist with — or perhaps to harvest from — an extraordinary concentration of animal life.

The dominant species are invertebrates. Beetles, primarily: dark-shelled, ranging from the size of a thumbnail to the size of a man's fist, moving in slow, columnar processions along the walls and across the floor. They are everywhere. They crunch underfoot if you are not careful. They climb the reeds. They collect around the weeping fissures, drinking. They do not seem to mind the light. They do not seem to mind you.

In the water: translucent shrimp, eyeless, moving in languid loops. They are edible. The townspeople eat them. You can see children — three or four of them, barefoot, wearing cotton wraps — crouched at the edge of the largest pool, scooping shrimp into clay bowls. They are efficient and unhurried. They have been doing this all their lives.

On the ceiling: roosting things. Bat-like in shape but not bats. Larger. Wing membranes that are not membranes but something more like stiffened sailcloth, ribbed with cartilage. They hang in clusters of twenty or thirty from the rock shelf, and occasionally one shifts and resettles, and a rain of fine pale dust falls to the floor. They are silent during the day. At night — though there is no real night here — they descend to feed on the beetles. This is the cycle. This is what keeps the beetle population in check, and the beetle population is what keeps the reed-beds from being stripped bare, and the reed-beds are what filters the groundwater enough for the shrimp to survive. The town has inserted itself into this loop. It is the reason the town exists.

You see now the infrastructure. Along the far wall, a series of wooden platforms raised on stilts above the shallow water, connected by walkways of planking. On these platforms: drying racks. Beetle carapaces laid out in rows, harvested for chitin. A low building — more of a shed — where someone operates a mechanical press, crunching carapaces into powder. The press is electric. The generator's throb makes sense now. Beside the shed, a tank of standing water with a mesh net draped over it, shrimp being sorted by size. A small forge, cold at the moment, where metal tools are shaped from salvaged material brought down from the surface.

The construction is layered but unambitious. Nothing here is grand. It is all utilitarian, improvisational, built from what was available: stacked stone, timber, corrugated metal, woven reed. The walkways creak. The shed has a tarpaulin roof. A few dwellings are built into niches along the chamber wall — small alcoves widened and smoothed, fitted with cloth doors. One has a shelf of clay pots. Another has a sleeping platform and a hookah, unlit. These are homes. They are small and they are warm and they are damp.

---

The temperature is extraordinary. Nearly thirty degrees underground, and humid enough that sweat does not evaporate. It comes from below — geothermal warmth, bleeding up through the unfinished rock. The floor is warm to the touch. The pools are warm. The beetles thrive in it. The bat-things thrive in it. The people have adapted. They move slowly. They drink constantly from ceramic jugs filled with seep-water that has been strained through reed-fiber. They wear little. Their skin glistens. They are lean and dark-eyed and they do not rush.

The pace of change is slow. The rock is forming around them at a rate they will never perceive. The fissures weep today as they wept ten years ago. The beetle columns follow the same paths. The generator is refueled on a schedule that has not changed in a generation. Children learn the same scooping technique their grandparents used. There is a patience here that feels geological. The town does not grow. It does not shrink. It *endures*, at the same rhythm as the stone that contains it.

---

In one corner, a kind of market. Not much — a table, really, and an old woman sitting behind it. She has jars of chitin powder, bundles of dried shrimp, small sachets of reed-pith. She also has, incongruously, a radio. It is plugged into a portable battery. It plays static, and occasionally, faintly, a voice from the surface — weather reports, grain prices, a frequency no one up there remembers broadcasting on. She does not sell the radio. She does not even acknowledge it. It is simply there, a small rectangle of noise, existing alongside the beetles and the dripping and the drone of the press. It is the most normal thing in the world.

---

You stay for a while. Your eyes adjust. The dimness begins to feel not like deprivation but like a kind of softness — a generosity, almost. Things do not need to be seen clearly here. You hear them instead. You feel the warmth through your boots. A beetle the size of a walnut crosses your hand and you let it. It is dry and light and it tickles.

When you leave — climbing the hot rebar railing, stepping out into the blinding white of the surface — you will carry the smell of sulfur and wet iron in your clothes for days. You will miss, for reasons you cannot fully articulate, the sound of the water.

You will not go back. But you will remember it as a place that was real.
