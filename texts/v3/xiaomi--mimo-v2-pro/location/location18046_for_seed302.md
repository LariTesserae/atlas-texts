The basin is a bowl of salt and steam. It is high—so high the sky is a thinner, deeper blue at the edges, but the basin itself is filled with a relentless, dazzling light. The light does not come from the sun alone; it is amplified, thrown back and forth between the ground and the low, wet clouds until the very air seems saturated with a white, buzzing glare. The ground is a cracked mosaic of mineral flats: vast polygonal plates of white and pale pink salt, crusted over deeper, darker brine. Where the plates meet, they form ridges. From every fissure, from every porous stone, steam breathes.

The water here is not in pools or rivers. It is in the air, on the skin, weeping silently from the rock faces that ring the basin. It condenses on the cold, salt-slick surfaces, gathering into heavy drops that hang for an impossible time before falling with a faint *hiss* onto the scalding crust. The sound is everywhere: a constant, deafening susurration—the collective sigh of a million tiny steam vents, the gurgle of unseen water turning to vapor in subterranean veins, the crackle of salt crystals expanding and contracting in the heat. There is no quiet moment. The sound is the texture of the place, a pressure on the eardrums.

There are no plants. Nothing rooted, nothing green. The only concession to life, if it can be called that, are the occasional mats of extremophilic bacteria—dull orange or greyish films that stain the wettest rocks near the most vigorous steam vents. They are barely noticeable, a discoloration, a slickness under the condensation. Animals are rarer still: a small, blind, heat-loving insect might be seen skittering across a salt plate, moving between patches of shade offered by jagged mineral growths. These growths are the basin’s only structures. They are not built, but *grown*—slow, crystalline formations of selenite and calcite that erupt from the ground like the ribs of some ancient, buried creature. They are packed densely in places, a silent forest of glassy, translucent blades, their facets catching the brilliant light and shattering it into a million cold sparks. They crowd together, leaving only narrow, winding paths between their close-packed forms. The basin feels full, occupied to its very capacity by light, sound, and mineral.

The temperature is cool, a stark contrast to the steaming vents. The high altitude pulls heat from the air, and the constant evaporation creates a clammy chill that clings to the skin beneath the ever-present moisture. The landscape is established, weathered. This is not a new volcanic field; the salt flats are deep, the crystalline spires are ancient, their bases fused into a solid mineral plain. The erosion is in balance—the slow dissolution of salt by condensate is perfectly matched by the constant deposition from mineral-rich steam. Nothing changes. The basin has held this exact state for millennia: steaming, glittering, roaring, frozen in a dynamic equilibrium of water cycling between liquid and vapor, forever trapped in the heart of the mountains. It is a place of pure, alien stasis—a bright, deafening, mineral lung breathing in and out with no one to hear it.
