The air itself is wet. Not in the way of fog or mist, which coalesce into visible droplets, but a saturated, breathable fluid so laden with moisture it feels like you are drinking with every breath. This is a place that cannot dry. The ground is not soil but a densely packed mat of algal felt—velvety, spongy, and a deep, bruised greenish-black. It covers everything, a thick, absorptive skin over the underlying stone, and squelches faintly under any pressure. Beneath it, the bedrock is warm, a low-grade geothermal heat that seeps up, keeping the constant condensation from ever turning cold.

You are in a shallow basin, a sunken caldera no more than a few hundred paces across, its rim formed of weathered, rounded rhyolite outcrops that weep perpetual tears. Water doesn’t flow here; it *emerges*. It beads on every surface—the dark felt, the grey stone, the rust-colored patches of iron-oxide crust—as if the world is sweating. It gathers in shallow, tepid pools that reflect the dim sky not as mirrors, but as dull, oily surfaces. The sound is a ceaseless, soft *hush*: the collective whisper of a million tiny drips, of condensation running in microscopic rivulets, of water squelching through the saturated felt. It is the sound of stillness made audible.

There is no grand vegetation, but the world is far from barren. The algal mat is the dominant life, a monoculture of simple, damp being. In denser, more shaded hollows between the stone outcrops, it is joined by other forms: sprawling crusts of pale, grey-green lichen that look like old paint peeling from the rock; occasional eruptions of a fungal analogue—not mushrooms, but intricate, shelf-like structures of a woody, water-impervious material, their undersides coated in a slimy, spore-producing gel. They are the architects here, slowly dissolving the stone with acidic secretions, creating new, finely pitted textures on the rock.

The structures of intelligence are faint, almost lost. In the exact center of the basin, where the warmth is most pronounced, someone, long ago, had arranged a circle of stones. They are not large, each no taller than a knee, but they are oddly smooth, shaped by hands and tools rather than by water. Their surfaces bear shallow, repetitive scratches—cross-hatching, spirals, patterns that suggest counting or mapping, but the meaning has weathered away. Nearby, half-sunk into the felt, lies a single artifact: a flat, oblong piece of dense, dark wood, its end shaped into a blunt blade. A paddle, a shovel, a weapon? It is the only hint of purpose, and it is slowly being swallowed by the mat, its form softening, its definition lost.

The light is a filtered, grey gloom, as if the sun is forever behind a thick pane of smoked glass. The basin’s low walls and the moisture-saturated air conspire to dim and diffuse all illumination. Shadows have no hard edges; they are soft, deep pools of darkness under the fungal shelves and in the crevices of the weeping rock. Nothing changes. The algal mat grows in a year’s time by a thickness you could measure only with a fine tool. The fungal shelves expand outward by a millimeter a century. The weeping water has traced the same paths down the stone for millennia, carving faint, glossy channels. The circle of stones will not shift. The wooden tool will continue its slow descent. This is a world in perfect, humid equilibrium, a pocket of warmth and moisture sealed against the passage of time, packed to its edges with the quiet, persistent business of mineral and simple life, waiting for nothing at all.
