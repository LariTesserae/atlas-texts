The settlement of **Cairn-tha** does not sprawl. It gathers itself, a patient cluster of stone and timber wedged onto a high, weathered ridge that shoulders aside the clouds. You come upon it not from a distance, but all at once, as the path—more a suggestion of flattened lichen and packed gravel than a road—crests the final rise.

**Terrain & Materials:** The bedrock is a dark, iron-veined granite, scoured smooth by a forgotten glacier and now settled into its final form. It rises in broad, rounded humps and low, flat ledges. The settlement is built directly upon and *into* this stone. Walls are of dry-laid, irregular granite blocks, their joints so old and lichen-patched they seem part of the geological strata. Roofs are heavy slabs of schist layered like fish scales, or thick, sod-like mats of moss and heather that bleed into the surrounding ground. There is no mortar, only the patient, balancing craft of generations.

**Atmosphere & Light:** The light is clear, thin, and carries a perpetual chill. It is normal daylight, but stripped of warmth, making shadows sharp and colors distinct—the grey stone, the deep green of the moss, the rust-red of oxidizing iron stains on the rock. The air is damp, but not wet. Mist is a constant companion, rolling up the slopes from unseen valleys below, condensing on every surface into tiny, clinging beads. It collects in shallow, man-made granite cisterns carved into the ledges, their water dark and still. Occasionally, a soft, quiet rain whispers down, not in storms but as a persistent, gentle veil.

**Soundscape:** The quiet is profound, a low hum of absence. It is punctuated by the *plink* of water dripping from a schist eave into a cistern. The distant, breathy whistle of wind through a natural notch in the ridge. The muffled, rhythmic *scrape-thud* of a stone being shaped somewhere unseen. There are no birdsong choirs, only the rare, solitary croak of a high-altitude raven. Human sounds are muted, domestic: the clunk of a wooden bucket, a low murmur of voices contained by the stone walls.

**The Settlement & Ecology:** This is a hamlet-sized place, perhaps thirty structures in all, defined by its boundaries—the sheer drop-offs on three sides and the rising, barren scree slope on the fourth. The "urban" quality is in its organization. The buildings huddle around a central, open **hearth-court**, a large, flat granite slab where a communal fire burns low, its smoke rising straight up in the cool, dense air. Pathways are paved with flat stones, channeling the occasional runoff. Vegetation is sparse and tenacious: cushions of grey-green moss, tough bell heather with a few late purple blooms, and stunted, wind-twisted rowan trees growing from cracks in the rock, their roots like gnarled fingers. Fauna is rare—just the ravens, perhaps a nimble, grey-coated goat browsing on a roof-garden, and unseen voles in the moss.

**Technology & Rules:** The tools are of worked flint, antler, and simple, smelted bog iron. Construction is a manual craft of leverage and patience. The "urban" layer is in the civic engineering: the careful grading of paths, the communal cistern system, the designated workshops (a weaver's nook with a simple vertical loom, a knapper's ledge). The world's rule here is **conservation**. Every drop of water is saved. Every scrap of fuel (dried peat and root-tangles from below the ridge) is rationed. The balance is ancient, hard-won, and fragile. Change is an alien concept. The settlement is in a state of **frozen equilibrium**, its form dictated by the unyielding stone and the slow, reliable cycles of mist and rain. It feels less built and more *exposed*, as if the stone always held these hollows and rooms, and the people simply moved into the vacancies the world had already prepared.
