The world is called the Ghel Plateau, though there is no one left to name it. It is a high, cold tableland, so vast that the horizon in every direction is a soft blur where grey earth meets grey sky. The air is thin, and carries a chill that bites not with violence, but with a deep, patient certainty. Nothing is in a hurry here. The silence is a physical presence, a low, pressure-filled hush broken only by the occasional, muffled crunch of hooves or chitin on frost-hardened soil, or the distant, breath-like sigh of wind across a stone outcrop.

The ground is a mosaic of fractured basalt and deep, gritty moraine, scoured and settled over millennia. Erosion has done its patient work. There are no sharp young mountains, only the worn-down stumps and long, sweeping tors of ancient ones, their forms soft and rounded like the backs of sleeping giants. This is a landscape in a state of profound balance—what is eroding is being replaced by the slow upthrust of a geology that measures time in ice ages.

Life here is sparse in kind, but overwhelming in presence. The vegetation is limited to two main forms: vast, continent-spanning fields of "spine-lichen," a greyish, thorny crust that grows in slow, circular colonies, each plant a rigid, defensive star; and the "glass-straw," towering, translucent stalks that cluster around the few seeps and damp depressions. They are hollow, and when the rare breeze stirs them, they do not rustle, but emit a faint, eerie chime, like distant crystal.

But the fauna… the fauna owns this place. They are not animals as a more verdant world would know them. They are the Industrial Ecology. The dominant species are the "Rust Kudu," herds of hundreds, even thousands, of creatures whose bodies are a marvel of slow-heat biology and metallic exoskeletal plates. Their broad, flat hooves are perfectly evolved for the terrain, and their digestive systems are living refineries. They graze on the spine-lichen, and their waste, rich in processed minerals, fertilizes the next generation of growth in a closed, efficient loop. They move in immense, silent waves across the plateau, a living, breathing, metal-skinned river of life.

They are not alone. Following the herds are the "Carrion Clocks." These are scavengers, but they are also the janitors and the architects. They are slow, multi-legged creatures resembling giant, intricate beetles or isopods, their carapaces resembling riveted iron and bronze. When a Rust Kudu falls, a Carrion Clock will arrive in days, not minutes. It methodically disassembles the carcass with mandibles that work with the precise, hydraulic sound of machinery. It processes everything—flesh, bone, even the metal plates—recycling it into its own body or depositing it in neat, geometric piles that slowly fossilize into the landscape. The sound of their work is the only sharp noise here: a rhythmic *clink... scrape... clink... scrape* that carries for miles in the still air.

There is another presence. High on the ridges, where the wind’s breath is coldest, are the "Vent-Hives." These are not structures of craft, but of biology. They are great, mound-like structures of secretedite andite, looking like rusted iron factories. Within, a different kind of creature—the "Piston-Wasps"—tend to larvae that generate a low, consistent heat. The hives vent this excess warmth through great, chimney-like tubes, producing plumes of white steam that rise straight into the dim sky before dissipating. They are the engines of the high country, their faint, deep thrumming felt in the bones more than heard by the ears.

The light is forever dim, a perpetual twilight under a thick, high blanket of cloud that diffuses the sun into a featureless glow. Shadows are soft and grey, never sharp. There is no hurry in the light, no sharp angles of dawn or dusk. The dynamic is one of immense, slow cycles—the advance and retreat of the spine-lichen fields over decades, the generational migration routes of the Kudu herds that shift over centuries, the imperceptible rise and fall of the Vent-Hives as one colony dies and another is founded on its mineral-rich remains.

Water is a rare, cherished thing. It exists as the deep, slow aquifer, tapped only by the deepest roots of the glass-straw. It appears as condensation, painting the northern faces of the old tors with a slick, black varnish each long, cold night. And in the lowest hollows, where the air is stillest, there are "fog-lakes"—shallow, frigid pools of clear water that form from condensed mist and persist for weeks before evaporating again under the subtle sun. The Rust Kudu know these places. Their arrival at a fog-lake is a silent pilgrimage, a moment of stillness in their eternal, measured march.

This is a world of industry without invention, of overwhelming life without exuberance, of vast space filled with quiet, deliberate motion. It is a landscape that has forgotten the sound of its own voice, speaking only in the slow scrape of chitin on stone, the distant chime of glass-straw, and the deep, subsonic hum of life living at the pace of stone.
