# The Drowned Engine Halls

## Under the Shelf

There is no horizon. You understand this first, and it takes a while to understand that you understand it. The ice shelf extends in every direction above — not a sky but a lid, a pressure, a pale grey-green membrane the color of a bruise on old fruit. It is perhaps forty feet overhead here, perhaps sixty. Its underside is not smooth. It is scalloped, ridged, pocked with conical divots where water once boiled upward and refroze. Meltwater runs along these contours in thin silver threads that never reach the water below — they freeze mid-drip, mid-fall, and become stalactites of sorts, translucent, each one whispering water into the air that immediately hardens into glass.

Between the lid and the water is the space where you are. And the water is everywhere — not beneath you, necessarily, but *within* everything. You stand on a catwalk. The catwalk is submerged to your ankles. Every surface is wet. The air itself seems to be a lighter form of water, a less committed version, and you breathe it and feel it settle in your lungs like something heavier than air ought to be.

## What Was Built

This was a mining and processing complex, once. The evidence is everywhere but the narrative is hard to assemble because the water has rearranged it — not violently but persistently, the way a tongue moves a loose tooth. Everything is where it was and also somewhere adjacent to where it was.

The catwalk you stand on leads to a series of platforms connected by short stairways, each step slightly concave from decades of boot traffic. The platforms overlook what were once engine halls — tall, narrow spaces, cathedral-scale in height but not in width, more like vertical tunnels with ambitions. The walls are lined with machinery. Turbines. Generators. Belt systems. Cracking columns. Heat exchangers with their fins furred with mineral deposits the color of oxidized copper. Everything is bolted to the walls or to the floor or to frameworks of I-beam steel that have been standing long enough to develop their own sedimentary layers: rust, then calcite, then a thin dark biological film, then ice, then condensation.

Much of this machinery is still running.

Not all of it. Not coherently. But enough of it that the halls are not silent — they are *almost* silent, which is a different and more disturbing thing. A turbine on the third level still turns at maybe eight revolutions per minute, slow enough to count, slow enough to see each individual blade catch the low light. Its bearing is gone; the shaft grinds against its housing in a sound that is less a sound than a vibration you feel in the fillings of your teeth. Somewhere below the waterline, a pump cycles on and off at irregular intervals — a wet, concussive thud, like a heartbeat that hasn't decided on a rhythm. A condenser drips. The drips echo because the halls are tall and narrow and the walls are metal and the water surface is a ceiling of its own down below, so every small sound bounces and bounces and arrives at you layered, softened, translated into something that sounds like speech in a language with no consonants.

But between these mechanical whispers, there is silence. Deep, cold, structural silence. The kind that makes you aware of the blood moving in your own ears.

## The Water

It is salt water. It came from below, from the ocean beneath the ice shelf, pushing up through fissures in the bedrock that the miners had opened and never properly sealed. The flooding was not sudden. Accounts found later in a sealed locker — the paper now pulp, the ink a brown ghost — suggest it took eleven months. Eleven months of the waterline rising a centimeter a day, then two centimeters, then ten. Eleven months of moving equipment to higher platforms. Eleven months of shoring up bulkheads that were never designed to hold back a sea.

The water in the engine halls is not deep — three feet in most places, six or seven in the gaps between platforms where the floor drops away to accommodate larger machinery. But it is *present*. It laps. It moves in long, slow swells driven by something far away — a tidal pulse transmitted through the fissures, maybe, or the thermal churn of the earth itself. The surface is dark, not black but a deep green-grey that swallows light. You cannot see the bottom. You can see perhaps two feet into it, enough to make out the shapes of tools and pipe fittings and the occasional boot, sole-up, preserved by the cold.

The water is very cold. It is near freezing — perhaps a degree above, perhaps a degree below, held liquid by the dissolved salts and by the faint residual heat of the machinery that still turns. Where it meets metal, it has built structures: ice formations that are not quite frozen, mineral deposits that are not quite stone. The interface between water and steel has become its own landscape, a littoral zone of chemical negotiation.

## The Settlement Layer

Above the engine halls, accessible by ladders and a freight elevator that has not moved in decades, are the habitation levels. These are more intimate — rooms, not halls. Low ceilings. Narrow corridors. The doors are dogged shut with wheel-locks, and behind them are quarters: bunks, lockers, a mess hall with tables still bolted to the floor and chairs still bolted to the tables. Everything is arranged with industrial precision, as if the occupants believed that order was a form of defense against the dark and the cold and the water and the grinding of the ice.

The water has not reached most of these levels. The moisture has. The walls weep. Condensation runs down every vertical surface and pools on every horizontal one. The insulation — mineral wool packed behind sheet metal — has absorbed water over the years and sags away from the walls in heavy wet curtains. Some of it has frozen mid-sag, creating formations that look organic, almost fleshy, like the exposed musculature of some enormous organism that the complex was built inside of.

There are personal effects. A photograph in a warped frame — faces indistinct, waterblurred. A calendar on the wall of a small office, stopped at a month and year. A pair of gloves on a shelf, fingers curled inward as if still gripping something. A pair of boots beside a bunk, not placed there by the water but *set* there, by someone, who then left. Who then went somewhere. Who did not come back for them.

The density of objects is high — high enough that the smallness of the spaces makes each room feel overfull, not cluttered but *concentrated*, as if the occupants reduced their world to its smallest functional unit and then filled that unit to capacity. Tools hung on every hook. Supplies stacked to the ceiling. Wiring routed along every wall in neat bundles, each one labeled, each label now illegible.

## The Ecology (Such As It Is)

Life here is not absent but it is *reduced*. It has been edited down to its most persistent drafts.

On the submerged surfaces — the lower walls, the machinery below the waterline, the catwalk rails — there is a thin biofilm. It is dark olive, almost black, and it moves very slowly, growing at a rate measured in millimeters per year. It is not algae exactly. It is a consortium: bacteria, archaea, fragments of diatoms, a community that has found a way to metabolize the minerals leaching from the steel and the trace nutrients carried in by the tidal seep. It is the dominant life form here, and it is not much to look at. A stain. A suggestion. But it is everywhere, and it is alive, and it is doing the slow patient work of converting the installation into something else.

There are small crustaceans — amphipods, mostly, no larger than a grain of rice. They move in the water in tiny flickers, too small and too fast to see clearly. You catch them in your peripheral vision, like floaters in the eye, and for a moment you think the water is full of stars. They feed on the biofilm. Something — not here, somewhere out in the dark water beneath the ice — feeds on them. You do not see it. You see only the occasional disturbance on the water surface: a brief concentric ripple, a momentary swell, a smell that is not the smell of salt water or rust but something older and more animal and then it is gone.

Once, in the deepest engine hall, where the water is seven feet deep and the turbine turns at its slow eight RPM, something broke the surface. A dark shape, brief, the size of a hand or a forearm. It was gone before you could look directly at it. The water settled. The turbine turned. The pump thudded. You decided not to think about it. This was the correct decision.

## The Cold

The temperature is not a number here. It is a presence. It is the organizing principle of the place.

Everything is cold. The metal is cold. The water is cold. The air is cold, not with the biting ferocity of a surface wind but with a deep, settled, bone-level cold that has had decades to work its way into every material. Touch the wall and feel the cold move into your hand like something alive and curious. Breathe and feel the cold settle behind your eyes.

The cold has done work here. It has cracked concrete along hidden fault lines. It has warped the steel framing of the habitation level by fractions of degrees, enough that doors no longer sit in their frames, enough that the corridors have developed a subtle, nauseating curvature, as if the building is slowly turning in place. It has preserved things that should have decayed — the photograph, the gloves, the calendar — and it has accelerated the decay of things that should have lasted — the insulation, the wiring, the seals around every window and door.

The cold is also what keeps the water from winning. The salt and the residual heat keep it liquid; the cold keeps it from rising. There is a balance, and it has held for a long time, but it is not a stable balance. It is the balance of a coin on its edge. The ice above is thinning — you can see this in the meltwater channels, which are wider than they were, which drip faster than they should. The machinery is slowing — the turbines lose a fraction of an RPM each year, the pumps cycle less often, the heat exchangers exhale less heat. The water is very, very gradually warming, or the air is very, very gradually cooling, or both, and the margin between liquid and frozen is narrowing on both sides.

Something will give, eventually. The coin will fall.

## What It Feels Like

To be here is to be inside a machine that is dying very slowly and that does not know it is dying and that, in its dying, has become something more than what it was designed to be. The halls are not empty. They are full — of the sound of the turbines and the pumps and the dripping, of the weight of the ice above, of the density of objects and systems and pipes and wires and the biofilm and the amphipods and the dark ripple beneath the water. They are full the way a lung is full — not with stillness but with a single held breath.

You move through it slowly because the cold demands it and because the water is at your ankles and because the catwalks are narrow and because something about the density of the place makes speed feel wrong, feels like an intrusion, feels like talking loudly in a library or stepping on a grave.

The darkness is not absolute. There are lights — not electric, not anymore, but chemical. The biofilm glows faintly in places, a dim green luminescence that is not bioluminescence exactly but something adjacent, something that happens when certain bacteria oxidize certain minerals and release a photon as an afterthought. It is enough light to see by, barely. It paints the machinery in shades of swamp and foxfire. It makes the water glow from below, so that the surface is not a mirror but a membrane, a skin stretched over something that is breathing.

The installation is strange in the way that any place is strange when it has outlived its purpose and been claimed by processes that do not care about purpose. The pipes run where the pipes run because someone planned them that way, but the water runs where it runs because of gravity and salt and the shape of the fissures, and the biofilm grows where it grows because of iron and oxygen and time, and the amphipods live where they live because of the biofilm, and the thing beneath the water lives where it lives because of the amphipods, and none of these systems were planned, and all of them are running, and the planned systems are also running, still, somehow, against all probability, the turbines turning, the pumps pumping, the condensers condensing, the whole apparatus grinding on in the dark under the ice at the bottom of the world.

It is quiet. It is so quiet that the quiet becomes a sound. It is the sound of cold water against cold steel in a place where nothing has spoken in a very long time.
