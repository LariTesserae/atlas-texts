The sky is a flat, unchanging sheet of brass-colored light—not a sun, but an ambient glow that presses down without shadow. Below it lies the **Rust Basin**, a shallow, continent-wide depression where everything is oxidized, settled, and humming.

The ground is a compacted plain of iron-rich laterite and shattered pumice, rust-red to burnt umber, broken into a mosaic of meter-wide polygons by ancient, dry shrinkage cracks. The air is warm and faintly metallic, carrying a perpetual dampness that doesn’t pool into rivers but condenses each night, coating every surface in a cold, brown dew that evaporates by midday. This is the water: not flowing, but breathing—a slow, geologic respiration of moisture from deep strata, surfacing only as slickness, then gone.

Nothing grows here. There are no plants, not even lichen. The land is purely mineral, yet it is not inert. Across the plains, spaced with an almost geometric regularity, stand the **Spines**. They are not structures; they are emergent features—tall, fluted columns of banded iron and volcanic glass, some slender as obelisks, some broad as ruined towers. Their surfaces are worn smooth by eons of acidic condensation, yet they are not static. Deep within their cores, a geothermal throb persists—a warmth that isn't from the sun but from the planet’s own straining heart. This heat, interacting with the metallic salts and the damp air, generates a constant, low-voltage current.

This is the industry. Not built, but *expressed*. The Spines are the landscape’s engines. They hum. A deep, resonant vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it—a sound like a thousand dynamos buried in bedrock, running forever at idle. It is the sound of equilibrium: chemical potentials being eternally, quietly discharged. The air shivers with it. Sometimes, a louder *groan* rolls across the basin as a Spine’s internal stress fractures and resettles, a process as slow and inevitable as continental drift.

The fauna is the Spines’ attendant ecosystem. Metallic-shelled arthropods, the size of beetles or small dogs, graze on the electrochemical film that slicks the ground at dawn. They are rare, clicking and skittering in the vastness, their carapaces of fool’s gold and tarnished brass. Larger, slower things—armadillo-like rollers of iron-rust composite—root in the polygon cracks for mineral salts, leaving polished trails that glint for an hour before the dew reclaims them. They are the only mobile things in a world defined by immobility.

Weirdness here is not in surreal forms, but in the paradox of a factory that never was, a production with no product. The energy is generated and bled off as heat and sound, forever. The scale is regional, a vista of rust plains and distant, humming Spines fading into the brass haze. It is open, yet the density is low; presences are scattered landmarks in a ocean of oxidized silence beneath the engine-song.

Nothing changes. The polygons have been here for millennia. The Spines’ hum has not altered in pitch. The condensation follows its ancient, invisible clock. This is a world caught in a permanent, warm, industrial twilight—a mechanics of stone, a factory of erosion, an equilibrium so total it has become its own eternity. The loud, deep hum is its heartbeat, and it will never, ever stop.
