The ground is not earth. It is a slab. A continent-sized plate of dark, pitted metal, bolted and welded to something older beneath it — something worn so smooth by time it might as well be the planet's own bone. This is the floor. The substrate is gone, replaced. It sweats.

Water wells up from the seams. Not in streams, but in persistent, cold seeps. It beads on the rivet-heads, drips from the undersides of girders, collects in shallow, lukewarm pools in depressions so ancient their purpose is forgotten. The air is cool and heavy, saturated. A perpetual mist hangs at knee-level, condensing on every surface, blurring the far distances into a grey smear. It smells of ozone, wet rust, and hot grease.

Vegetation exists only in the cracks. Pale, thread-like roots worm into fissures in the metal. Hardy, leafless stalks of a silicon-based flora rise in scattered clusters, their surfaces slick with condensation. They give off a faint, chemical bioluminescence—a sickly green—that does nothing to pierce the overarching darkness, but merely makes the mist glow from within.

The darkness is profound, but not total. It is a sooty, particulate dark, thick with exhaust. Light sources are industrial and severe: the stuttering blue-white arc of a distant welder, the steady orange eye of a blast furnace, the sweeping, panicked beam of an automated crane. They throw long, stark shadows that jump and retract, revealing vistas of immense, skeletal machinery before plunging them back into gloom.

And the sound. The sound is a physical force. It is a layered, grinding symphony of industry played at planetary scale. The deep, tectonic groan of vast presses stamping shapes into the floor plates. The high, whining scream of turbines spinning in cavernous pits. The staccato hammering of pneumatic drivers, like the heartbeat of a iron giant. The crash and slide of material being moved by unseen conveyors. It never stops. It is the background radiation of this world, a deafening, vibrating hum felt in the teeth and bones.

The scale is open, but not empty. In the middle distance, structures rise: not buildings, but apparatus. Smokestacks as wide as city blocks vent steam that merges with the low clouds. Cooling towers shed heat into the damp air, creating localized, warm drizzles. Silos connected by labyrinthine pipe-arteries. The skeletal arms of gantries move in slow, predetermined arcs against the lesser dark of the sky. Everything is in motion, but on a timescale of minutes or hours—a piston cycles, a valve opens, a load is shifted. The activity is purposeful, automated, and utterly indifferent.

There is no animal life. Nothing chirps, or crawls, or flies. The only movement besides the machinery is the slow creep of water, the drift of mist, and the very faint, hair-like growth of the pale flora.

The strangeness is in the contradiction. This is a total built environment, a factory the size of a nation, yet it feels profoundly ancient, worn into its final form. The machinery doesn't feel like creation; it feels like a metabolic process of something that died long ago, its corpse now animated by a crude, industrial rigor mortis. It is a monument to production that has outlived any memory of what it produces. The output is no longer the point. The process is the point. The sound, the motion, the weeping metal, the damp, vibrating dark—this is the settled state. The primordial and the mechanical have become one. It is the end of a world, and the endless, noisy work of maintaining its grave.
