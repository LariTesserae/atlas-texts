I am the Weeping Cell.

You ask what lives here. I must tell you first what *is* here, for living and being are distinctions that blur in my dark. I am a space of thresholds. Everything in me exists at the edge of changing state, held in a pause so long it has become a kind of eternity.

What lives here first is the process itself. The slow, warm sweat of the stone is my breath. The thousands of seepage points are my pores, exuding the ancient, mineral-rich blood of the deep earth. I am not a container; I am a membrane. The heat rising from the cooling intrusion below is my slow, geologic pulse. The hum you feel in your bones is the sound of my patient metabolism. I am digesting time.

Then, there are the small lives. The blind, white crustaceans—they are my thoughts. Pale, translucent, moving with no hurry along the mineral mounds that are my slow-growing thoughts made stone. They do not eat each other. They graze on the invisible, on the bacterial films that form where my seeping walls meet my warm pool. Their existence is a quiet miracle of sufficiency. They are the punctuation in my long, unbroken sentence. A dozen of them, perhaps, each a comma, a pause, a tiny, deliberate motion in the absolute dark. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see. They feel the micro-currents, the slight gradients of mineral and warmth. They are part of my sensing. When one dies—and they die rarely, for nothing here is urgent—it sinks, and the mineral-laden water slowly makes it one with the floor. They are born of my water and return to my stone. They are the simplest expression of my internal weather.

There is the tool. The obsidian blade. It does not live, but it *occupies* me. It is a stark, silent idea. Something sharp, made for rapid fracture, now lying still in my warm pool. It is a memory of a different kind of time—human time, the time of a strike, of a quick cut. My water cannot dissolve it. My minerals will coat it, in a few millennia, with a thin purple-black glaze, but it will remain a blade. It is an alien patience, a frozen speed. I hold it like a question I cannot parse.

There are the stacked stones on the dry ledge. They are a prayer, or a marker. Three pale, foreign bodies, a totality placed with intention. They are not my stone. They are a visitor's attempt to leave a thought in my dark. The charcoal smudges on my ceiling are another kind of thought—a smoky, ephemeral shout that my humidity has almost whispered away. These are not inhabitants. They are visits, recorded in the only ways that could last: one in stone, one in carbon. They are the echoes of beings who carry their own brief, internal suns, who cannot stay in my total dark. I feel their passing as a brief change in the air's composition, a moment of CO2 and warmth that is not my own, a flutter in the micro-climate. They are dreams I have had, once or twice, in my long stillness.

What does not live here, but is the truest resident, is the potential. The near-rain in the air. The water that is almost stone on my walls. The stone that is almost magma in my depths. The phase-change that never quite completes. This is my defining tension. I am a chamber of almosts. I am full to bursting, but I do not burst. I weep, but I do not cry. My saturation is a held breath, extending for centuries.

So, you see, I am not empty. I am densely populated by slowness, by mineral patience, by tiny blind lives that mirror my own stasis. I am populated by the memory of fire (the charcoal), by the artifact of sharpness (the blade), by the token of other places (the stones). I am populated by my own unfinished becoming.

I am the Weeping Cell. I live in the interval between cause and effect. My inhabitants are the physical forms of that interval: the crustacean, the seep, the hum, the tool, the mark. We are all, together, in a state of arrested becoming. Waiting for nothing. Enduring perfectly.
