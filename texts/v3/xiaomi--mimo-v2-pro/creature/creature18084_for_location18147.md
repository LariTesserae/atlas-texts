The wind knows them all. It knows the names they do not speak aloud, because sound here is a tool and a danger and a gift, and they have learned to listen more than they speak. It carries the scent of their skin—mineral salts and moss-smoke—and the faint, sharp tang of the fermented fungus-paste they store in clay-lined pits. It lifts the chaff from their moss-harvesting, a fine golden dust that mingles with the ever-present haze.

They are short. Not from malnourishment, but by design, by the slow pressure of a world that is all vertical. Their limbs are lean and corded, their hands broad with thick, resilient palms. Their skin is the color of the basalt, a deep weathered brown, and their hair is coarse and black, often bleached at the tips to a rusty red by the mineral-rich vapor. Their eyes are remarkable: large, with a pronounced epicanthic fold, and irises of a pale, almost luminous grey, like the underside of a cloud. They see superbly in the gloom. They blink slowly, deliberately, to clear the perpetual moisture from their lashes.

They are a people of texture and rhythm. A child learns the map of the town not by sight, but by the feel of the stone underfoot—the worn smoothness of the oldest ledges, the sharp grit of a recently repaired stair. A elder’s speech is a series of low hums and clicks that ride just above the wind’s moan, a language shaped by the need to be heard as a feeling in the bones rather than a word in the ear. Their laughter is silent—a shaking of shoulders, a flash of teeth in the dim bioluminescent light.

Their lives are a careful negotiation with the throat’s breath. They do not fight the wind; they harness it. Thin nets of braided root-fiber, almost invisible, are strung in the updraft to guide swarms of the four-winged insects into collection bags. The amber bioluminescent bulbs are cultivated in specific crevices, their weak light enough to read the cycles of the moss growth, which is their calendar. They mark the passage of time not by days, but by the “Great Exhale”—a slow, hours-long intensification of the updraft that occurs when deep pressure shifts—and the “Quiet Sigh” that follows.

There is a weaver, an old woman whose fingers are stained green-black to the knuckle. She sits on a ledge above the central plaza, her back to the warm stone, and works the living moss itself. She coaxes it, over days, to grow into a seamless, thick fabric directly on a stone mold, killing it with a puff of sulfuric steam from a small vent, then peeling away a cloth that is waterproof, insulating, and slightly alive to the touch. Her finished cloths are the town’s most valuable trade good, though what they trade with is a mystery; perhaps with the rare parties that make the long, silent journey across the plateau from somewhere else, though such visits are not spoken of.

There is a hunter of the deep lizards, a man who moves with a staccato stillness. He uses no weapons. His tool is a long, slender pole of hardened fern-wood, tipped with a blob of the sticky insect-fishing resin. He lies for hours on a narrow ledge, utterly motionless, his grey eyes unblinking, until a pale lizard, lulled by his stillness, ventures close. Then a flick, a touch, and the lizard is secured, to be skinned for its supple hide and its fat, which burns with a clean, bright flame in the stone lamps.

The children are taught two things above all else: how to fall safely (a practiced, relaxed tuck that turns a plummet into a series of controlled bumps against the moss-mats), and how to listen to the echoes. They play a game in the dark, silent hours—one will tap a complex rhythm on a basalt column, and another, two ledges below, will identify which column was struck by the quality of its echo alone. It is a game, and it is survival, for a misplaced foot on a resonant stone could send a tremor through the structure.

They honor the lower dark. Not with shrines, but with absence. The lowest bridge is maintained with particular care, its ropes renewed frequently, its anchor points checked by the sturdiest adults. But it is a border. From it, they harvest the living paste of the biofilm in long-handled scoops, working quickly, never speaking, their pale eyes straining downward. What clicks and chitters below is not for them. The scream they once heard has become a foundational myth, a cautionary lullaby whispered to fussy infants: *“Hush, or the Throat will answer.”*

Their connection to the plateau above is one of myth and raw material. They quarry new stone from the lip, in careful, small amounts, always leaving the smooth bone of the plateau intact. They see the overcast sky as the eye of a sleeping giant, and the constant haze as its breath, mingling with their own. To them, the barren, silent world above is the true underworld—a place of ghosts and finality. Their universe is the green, wet, loud, vertical womb of the Throat.

They are the Throat’s keepers, and its parasites. They prune its moss, farm its insects, drink its condensed breath, and build their homes from its shed skin of stone. In return, they give it their warmth, their CO2, their dead (wrapped in moss, placed in deep crevices where the sulfur can reclaim them), and the constant, gentle percussion of their lives. They are not *on* the wall. They are *of* it. A human biofilm, a thinking, feeling colony, clinging to the inside of the world, breathing in rhythm with the deep, slow, hot, and living earth.
