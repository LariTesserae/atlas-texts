# Who Lives Here

Almost no one. And that is the answer, but it isn't the whole answer.

---

## The Holdfast Mites

You will not see them at first. You may never see them. But if you crouch near a fumarole on a cold morning and hold very still — not breathing, not shifting your weight — you may eventually become aware of them: a congregation of **tiny, pale forms** moving with an unhurried, mechanical patience across the wet, warm surface of the spore-layer where it borders a vent.

They are **holdfast mites**, though they are not mites in any taxonomic sense. They are small — the largest barely two millimeters across — and nearly colorless, their bodies so translucent you can see the slow movement of fluid through their interiors. They have six legs, or eight, or sometimes seven; they are not consistent. Their carapaces are soft, unchitinous, more cartilaginous than anything, and they deform slightly with each step as though walking on a surface barely firm enough to support them. They leave no tracks. The surface they walk on is too wet, too absorptive, too alive. Their passage is erased behind them as they go.

They feed on the **surface secretions** of the flesh-layer — the thin, amber, enzymatic films that bead and pool in warm zones — and on the **condensation-algae**, the barely-there films of photosynthetic microorganisms that coat damp surfaces near light-exposed openings. The algae themselves are hardly visible: a faint greenish-grey greasiness, a discoloration so subtle you might mistake it for mineral staining. But it is there. And where it is, the mites follow.

They do not interact with each other. They do not compete. They do not seem to be aware of each other's existence. Watch two of them converge on the same droplet of secretion and they will feed side by side, their mouthparts working the surface in parallel, neither yielding nor contesting. There is no territoriality. There is no aggression. There is no courtship that anyone has observed. They reproduce — they must, or they would not persist — but how, and when, and in what fashion, remains unknown. Occasionally an observer has noted clusters of minute, gelatinous nodules adhering to surfaces near fumaroles, nodules that were not present the day before, but no one has witnessed their deposition.

They are, in their way, **part of the system**. Not independent of the Growth. Not parasitic upon it. More like — commensal in the oldest, most incidental sense. They live in the margins. They eat what the Growth produces without meaning to produce it. They are the mites that live in your eyebrows: present, invisible, inconsequential to the host. The Growth does not notice them. They do not notice the Growth. They are barely there at all.

## The Threadworms

In the deeper warm zones — where fumarole clusters create pockets of sustained moisture and heat within the upper flesh-layer — there are **threadworms**. They are exactly what the name suggests: threads. Pale, nearly white, often less than a centimeter long, often thinner than a human hair. They move through the thin films of water that persist in the grooves and channels of the surface, undulating slowly, feeding on dissolved organics and microbial films.

They are less visible than the mites. You will not see them unless you bring a lens. You will not see them unless you look. Even then, they resemble nothing so much as stray fibers, detritus, the trailing filaments of condensed moisture. They are the kind of organism that disappears into the texture of its environment. They are part of the wallpaper.

Their biology is almost entirely uncharacterized. They are not nematodes, though they superficially resemble them. They are not annelids. They are something else — something that has adapted so completely to the specific chemical environment of the Growth's surface that they can survive nowhere else. Transfer them to a petri dish with plain water and they die within minutes. Transfer them to soil, to leaf litter, to any "normal" substrate, and they coil, stiffen, dissolve. They are **specialists** to an extraordinary degree — locked into a symbiosis not with the Growth itself but with its *chemistry*, the specific cocktail of enzymes, minerals, and metabolic byproducts that the flesh-layer secretes.

There may be other organisms. There almost certainly are — bacteria, certainly, and archaea in the deep flesh-layer where temperatures and pressures and chemical gradients create niches that nothing else on Earth could occupy. Microscopic things that feed on the sulfides. Things that eat the crystalline deposits. Things that live in the condensation pools and are gone when the pools are gone, reconstituting from cysts or spores when water returns.

But these are not visible. These are not *encounterable*. They exist at a scale below experience.

## What Does Not Live Here

This is, perhaps, more important.

There are **no birds**. The silence of the Basin is absolute at altitude — no calls, no wingbeats, no territorial songs. Birds occasionally fly over. They do not stop. Something about the place repels them, or fails to attract them. There is nothing to eat. There is nowhere to nest. The surface is too uniform, too vertical, too *soft* to offer purchase. A bird that landed here would find no branch, no ledge, no dry ground. It would be standing on warm, damp, yielding tissue. It would leave.

There are **no insects** in any conventional sense. No beetles, no flies, no moths, no ants. The chemical atmosphere — the yeasty, acetone-heavy fumarole gases, the volatile organics, the sheer *concentration* of metabolic output — may be repellent. Or may simply be incompatible. Or may not be the reason at all. It is possible that insects once came here and were outcompeted, absorbed, dissolved into the flesh-layer and digested. The Growth is not hostile. It is not predatory. But it is not *permeable*. It does not share.

There are **no plants**. Not one. Not a moss. Not a lichen. Not a fern in a crevice. The bone-layer surfaces — the oldest, most mineralized, most stone-like strata — might theoretically support lithophytic growth. They are exposed to light. They collect moisture. They have the texture and porosity of weathered rock. But nothing grows on them except the Growth itself, which colonizes its own dead tissue as readily as it colonizes basalt. Any spore of moss or lichen that fell here would find no gap, no purchase, no moment of stillness in which to establish. The surface is too active. It rearranges itself too quickly. The flesh-layer would grow over it, encyst it, digest it, absorb it before it could anchor.

There are **no vertebrates**. None. No rodents. No reptiles. No amphibians. Nothing with a skeleton, a nervous system of any complexity, a body large enough to see. The Basin is hostile to size — not through any active defense, but through its nature. There is no food chain. There is no food web. There is only the Growth and the microscopic margin of life that clings to its surfaces. A vertebrate here would starve. It would also, perhaps, be swallowed. The flesh-layer closes over depressions. The floor gives way. The walls shift. A large body in the Basin is a large body in a landscape that is continuously, unpredictably *rearranging itself*. You walk carefully here not because the terrain is steep or rugged but because it is *unstable* — because the ground you stand on is not ground at all but the living surface of something that will not hold still.

---

## The Growth Itself

And so we come to it. The thing that lives here. The only thing that truly *lives* here.

It is not a creature. It is not a plant. It is not a forest or a reef or a city. It is a **system** — a self-sustaining, self-reproducing, self-organizing biological architecture that has been growing in this basin for longer than anyone can determine. Radiocarbon dating of the deepest bone-layer samples returns ages that push against the method's limits — twenty, thirty thousand years — and even those are not the bottom. The bedrock beneath the deepest strata shows no sign of a lower boundary. The Growth may extend into fissures in the basalt itself, threading through stone, mineralizing, becoming indistinguishable from the rock it consumes.

It is a single organism in the way that a coral reef is a single organism — and also not. It is genetically diverse, or it is not. No one has successfully sequenced its genome, because no one can identify where one individual ends and another begins. Samples taken from opposite sides of the Basin show *some* genetic variation, but also vast stretches of shared code. It may be one lineage that has diverged over millennia into regional varieties. It may be a consortium — dozens, hundreds of species so thoroughly entangled that they function as one. It may be something for which the concept of "species" simply does not apply.

It does not think. It has no nervous system, no centralization, no organ that could be called a brain. Its responses to stimuli are local and slow — a hydrostatic event here, a fruiting upheaval there — mediated by chemical gradients and hydraulic pressures rather than nerve impulses. But the *totality* of its responses — the way the entire Basin breathes and shifts and adjusts — has a coherence that feels intentional. Walk through it and you cannot shake the feeling that it *knows where you are*. That the floor softens under your weight not by coincidence but by design. That the walls narrow around you not because of random growth but because of *something else*.

It is not true. The Growth does not know you are there. The Growth does not know anything. It is a chemical system running on chemistry, executing programs written in enzyme gradients and moisture pressures and mineral crystallization sequences, operating at a scale and on a timescale that makes individual human awareness irrelevant. You are a warm spot on its surface. You are a pressure differential. You are a brief, insignificant event in a history measured in tens of millennia.

But it *feels* like something is watching. And that feeling — that sense of vast, inhuman, indifferent attention — is perhaps the only thing in the Basin that is truly alive in the way you understand the word. It is your own mind, projecting intention onto a system too large and too slow and too alien to have any. It is the vertigo of being *inside* something alive and realizing it does not care.

---

## So: Who Lives Here?

No one you could have a conversation with. No one you could trade with, or fight, or flee from. No one who would mark your passing or remember your name.

Only the Growth: ancient, silent, busy, and alone. Manufacturing its strange chemistry in the dark. Growing over its own dead. Erupting and collapsing and erupting again. Feeding on stone. Producing water. Holding the basin together and filling it and *being* it, completely and without remainder.

And in the margins, in the warmth, in the cracks between its processes: a few translucent things the size of commas, moving slowly across a wet surface, eating without hunger, living without purpose, invisible to the only system that could sustain them.

That is what lives here. That is all that lives here.

It is enough.
