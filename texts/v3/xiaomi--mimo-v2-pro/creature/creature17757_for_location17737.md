# Who Lives in the Green Dark

---

They are here. You just can't see them yet.

Stand still long enough and the citadel stops treating you like weather. The stalk-climbers are the first to come close. You saw one earlier as a skittering blur at the edge of your vision, and you registered it as *something with too many legs.* That was accurate. They are segmented, flattened, jointed things—imagine a woodlouse that decided to become a centipede, then kept going. Twelve, sixteen, twenty-four legs depending on species. Their carapaces are the same jade-green as the stalks they live on, polished to a wet gleam. They are invisible against the walls the way a moth is invisible against bark. But they are *everywhere*. When you pressed your palm to the stalk and felt that slow thrum of sap—there were three of them on the other side of that same segment, their mouthparts rasping gently at a patch of fungal bloat. They are gardeners. They graze the parasitic growths that try to take hold on the stalk surfaces, and their droppings are nutrient paste the plant reabsorbs through its skin. They have been doing this for longer than the citadel has had chambers. The relationship predates the city. The city grew because the relationship existed.

---

## The Drifters

Up. Look up between the stalks, into the mid-canopy darkness where the walkway-tendrils web across the void.

The gelatinous things are there. They drift.

They are shaped like jellyfish that never had a sea, repurposed for an atmosphere. Their bodies are translucent bladders of captured gas—hydrogen, metabolically extracted from the ceaseless damp—and their undersides trail curtains of fine, sticky filament. They float between the stalks at various heights, bobbing in the convection currents of warm respiration rising from the root-wells below. They are filter-feeders, and what they filter is the air itself: spores, micro-fungi, the invisible rain of organic particles that the citadel constantly exhales. You can see through them. When a pulse of bioluminescence travels through the fungal shelves nearby, the light passes through their bodies and they glow from within like lanterns, casting pale shadows of their own internal organs onto the mist around them. They have no brains. They have nerve nets, distributed, democratic, every filament a vote. When one of them collides gently against a stalk, the filaments on that side retract and the ones on the far side extend, and the creature turns, slowly, as if changing its mind.

They are the reason the air is so clean. They are the citadel's lung cilia.

Some of them are very old. The largest ones—big enough that their filaments trail thirty meters down toward the root-wells—have been drifting since before whatever you might call the citadel's "founding." They predate the architecture. They were here when the first stalk was a green shoot, and they will be here when the last one softens back into the fungal mat.

---

## The Chamber-Dwellers

Deeper. Into the hollowed internodes.

The chambers vary in size from the intimate—a space you could curl up in, the walls glistening and slightly warm—to the vast, where entire ecosystems play out inside a single node of bamboo-like stalk the width of a concert hall. The inhabitants of these spaces are what you registered as *shadows with too many eyes.*

They are. They do have too many eyes. Or rather, they have exactly the right number of eyes for a world that has no sun, where light comes in pulses and gradients and color-shifts, and the only reliable information is contrast. Their eyes are arranged in irregular clusters around the head—four here, six there, a lone one on a stalk above—each tuned to a slightly different spectrum. Some see only the blue of the fungal glow. Some see heat. At least one pair in each cluster is vestigial, cloudy, adapted from an ancestor that once lived in something brighter.

They are larger than the stalk-climbers. Solitary or paired. Their bodies are soft, mottled grey-violet, shaped like something between a salamander and a deep-sea octopus—limber, boneless, capable of squeezing through gaps you would not think a body that size could fit through. They move through the chambers with a liquid, rolling gait, their multiple eyes swiveling independently. They do not speak. They communicate through vibration—pressing their ventral surfaces to the floor and sending low pulses through the stalk's own tissues. The plant carries their messages. The citadel is their telephone.

What do they *do*?

Watch one. It has found a section of wall where the living tissue has been damaged—some fungal rot, a bruised segment where a stalk-climber colony overgrazed. The chamber-dweller extends its forelimbs and presses them to the wound. Its skin flushes a darker shade. Something chemical is happening. An enzyme exchange. After a long, still hour, the wound looks different—cleaner, the edges already knitting. The chamber-dweller pulls away and moves on. It is not clear whether this is deliberate medicine or reflexive symbiosis. It is not clear there is a difference here.

They sleep in the deepest chambers, the ones farthest from any walkway, where the fungal glow dims to near-black. They curl into spirals, their eyes closing one by one from the outermost pair inward, like a city shutting off its lights.

## The Tendril-Weavers

Small. Quick. Almost cute, if you can apply that word to something with a radula and no skeleton.

They look like star-nosed moles redesigned by a marine biologist. Their forelimbs end in clusters of sensitive, finger-like projections—dozens of them, each one tasting the air and the surface of the plant simultaneously. They live on the bridges and walkways, and in a sense, they *are* the bridges and walkways—or at least, their architects.

When the citadel needs a new connection between two stalks—a tendril reaching across a void, a new walkway spiraling up the inside of a hollowed chamber—the tendril-weavers know first. They feel it in the vibration of the plant's tissues, the chemical gradient of a growth surge beginning. They gather at the site, sometimes dozens of them, and they do something extraordinary: they coax. They lick the tender shoot of new growth with their radulae, depositing trace minerals and pheromone markers. They press their bodies against the tendril and *lean*, their weight a gentle suggestion of direction. Over days—sometimes hours—the tendril grows where they have pointed. It reaches the target stalk. It connects. The weavers carpet it with moss they carry in cheek-pouches from older growths.

They are the citadel's engineers. They don't build. They persuade.

Their nests are in the moss itself, little tunnels in the rubbery carpet of the walkways. If you step carefully, you will not crush them. If you do not step carefully, you will feel a small, startled vibration beneath your foot and a flash of violet-white bioluminescence—a distress signal—and then nothing. They are quick to forgive and quick to rebuild.

---

## The Root-Singers

You have not gone down far enough.

Beneath the spongy floor, beneath the root-laced mat of decomposing material, there are the aquifers—shallow, mineral-rich, and warm with geothermal faintness. And in the water, tangled with the roots themselves, are clusters of bioluminescent nodes. Each is the size of a fist. They glow in slow, rhythmic pulses: blue, pause, blue-green, pause, blue. They pulse in waves that travel through the root network, up through the stalks, across the bridges. One pulse every forty-five seconds. The heartbeat of the citadel.

They are not separate organisms. Or they are, but that might be the wrong frame. They are organs. Nerve ganglia. The citadel's distributed nervous system given flesh. Each node connects to its neighbors by threads so fine they are invisible unless the light catches them just right—mycelial filaments, electrical conduits, carrying information about water levels and mineral balance and the integrity of the stalk above.

But there is something else down here. Something the chamber-dwellers will not approach. Something that occasionally sends a ripple across the surface of a cistern-pool—slow, wide, purposeful—when nothing visible has fallen in.

---

## The Cistern-Dwellers

You do not want to fall into the internal cisterns.

Not because the water is dangerous. It is clean, mineral-rich, cold. You do not want to fall in because of what lives in the dark below the surface.

They are eyeless. They have never needed eyes. The cisterns are absolute darkness, and the creatures that inhabit them navigate entirely by pressure, by the displacement of water around their bodies, by the vibrations that travel through the root-tissue walls. They are large. How large is difficult to say because no one has ever seen one whole—only fragments, suggestions, a pale limb briefly surfacing before sliding back under. The chamber-dwellers describe them in their vibration-language as something like *the slow ones,* or *the deep heartbeat.* The tendril-weavers simply avoid the cistern-access roots entirely.

They may be the oldest things in the citadel. They may have been here before the stalks, existing in some simpler form in the aquifers, and the plant grew up around them. They are filter-feeders, perhaps. They may eat the root-singers' filaments, or the dead material that drifts down from above, or nothing visible at all. The water in the cisterns is unnervingly still. If you leaned over the edge of one and dropped a light—a bioluminescent fruit, say—you would watch it sink and sink and the glow would shrink to a point and then it would vanish, not because the pool has a bottom but because something between the light and the bottom absorbed it.

---

## The Spore-Dancers

These are almost not creatures at all.

When a spore-laden fruit drops from the canopy—you heard the *plop*—something happens. Within seconds, a cloud appears. Not smoke. Not mist. A cloud of living organisms, each one microscopic, that rise from the moss and fungal mats in a coordinated rush. They converge on the fallen fruit. They envelop it. For a few minutes, the cloud *moves*—billowing, reshaping, catching the faint bioluminescent light and scattering it in shimmers of blue and green. It looks like a thought. It looks like something the citadel is briefly thinking and then forgetting.

The spore-dancers are colonial micro-organisms, each one a single cell, but they move together as if coordinated by a mind. They consume the fruit's flesh. They release the spores into the air in a controlled burst—timing it, somehow, to coincide with a convection updraft from the root-wells, so the spores travel higher and farther than they would by gravity alone. Then the cloud disperses. The cells sink back into the moss. They become invisible again.

You will see several of these events during your time in the citadel. Each one will feel like watching a sentence being spoken in a language you almost understand.

---

## The Hum

You mentioned the hum. The low-frequency vibration, felt in the bones.

That is not an organism. That is *all* of them. The hum is the citadel itself—the collective metabolic process of the plant, the stalk-climbers, the root-singers, the drifters filtering air, the cistern-dwellers displacing water, the tendril-weavers coaxing growth, the chamber-dwellers healing wounds, the spore-dancers releasing spores. It is the sound of a city breathing. It is the frequency at which the entire system resonates when all its components are working.

When you are very still and the hum is all you can feel, you will have the disquieting sensation that the citadel knows you are here. Not as an individual. Not with intention. But the way a body knows it has an itch. You are a foreign signal in the system. The stalk-climbers have already begun to investigate your footprints, tasting the salts you left in the spongy floor. The chamber-dwellers in the nearest node have increased their vibration-chatter by 12%. A tendril near your left foot has changed direction by three degrees.

The citadel is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *processing* you. You are an input. Given time—weeks, months—it would incorporate you. Your moisture, your minerals, your heat. A walkway would grow over you. A chamber-dweller would tend the small fungal colony that would bloom from your remains. A spore-dancer cloud would rise when the last of your soft tissue was consumed, and it would shimmer in the dark, and then it would settle, and the citadel would continue its slow dreaming growth as if you had never been there at all.

The quiet presses against your ears. A drop of condensation falls. Somewhere in the dark, many eyes blink, and then close, and then open again.
