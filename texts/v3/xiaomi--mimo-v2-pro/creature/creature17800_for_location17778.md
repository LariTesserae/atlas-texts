# What Lives in Vash

---

## The Filter Feeders

They are the first thing you would notice, if you arrived by boat or by falling, because they are everywhere and they are enormous.

The great filter-feeding rays—locals of no name, since there is no one to name them—have colonized the water column above the tallest surviving structures. They are not one species but a loose guild of convergent shapes: flat, wide, slow. The largest span perhaps six meters from wingtip to wingtip. Their skin is the color of the twilight sky above, a bruised purple-grey, but with a faint iridescent sheen that catches the bioluminescent glow of the water and throws it back in dim, shifting rainbows. Their undersides are pale, almost white, fringed with gill rakers like the teeth of a comb. They hang in the warm upper water, mouths open, filtering the dense clouds of plankton and micro-organisms that bloom continuously in the nutrient-rich soup of the drowned city.

They are not solitary, though neither are they truly social. They simply share the same currents. When the plankton drifts, they drift. When the current reverses with the slow tidal breathing of whatever larger body of water feeds the city, they turn with it, collectively, like a field of dark leaves in wind. Their movements are almost imperceptibly slow—a languid sweep of wing, a gentle flex of the whole body that propels them forward at barely more than the speed of the current itself.

Beneath them, attached to every available surface, are the stationary filter feeders: tube worms in clustered forests that wave red and orange plumes from calcareous sheaths; barnacles that have colonized every horizontal and even some vertical surfaces, their shells the color of old bone; vast colonies of bryozoans that form lacy, encrusting sheets over walls that once held mosaics or murals. These are the true city of Vash now. They are quieter than the fish, quieter than the rays, but they are the architecture. They are what the water passes through. They are the reason the water is warm—metabolic heat from billions of tiny bodies, all straining the sea for sustenance.

## The Shoals

Below the rays, filling the water like breath fills lungs, are the small fish.

There are many species, but the dominant one—the one you would see first and most often—is a silver-sided schooling fish, perhaps eight centimeters long, with large dark eyes adapted to the gloom and a lateral line that glows faintly blue-green. They move in shoals that number in the tens of thousands, and these shoals have behavior that almost resembles intelligence, though it is not intelligence—it is ancient, hardwired mathematics. The shoal flows like a single organism through the hollowed-out atriums of office towers, down through elevator shafts that are now vertical current channels, around and through the columns of submerged colonnades. When a predator strikes, the shoal parts in a perfect, shimmering void—a three-dimensional hole that the predator's mouth passes through, closing again behind it like water around a stone.

They are the bloodstream of the reef. Everything eats them. The rays occasionally gulp them by accident, straining the water. The eels eat them. The diving birds from above eat them. The larger predatory fish hunt them in the midwater canyons between buildings. And still they are everywhere, because they breed continuously in the warm water, their eggs drifting in the current as part of the plankton bloom, hatching into larvae that join the shoal within days. Their bioluminescence serves as communication—a faint, collective shimmer that pulses through the shoal in waves, coordinating turns that no individual fish is choosing to make.

There are other shoals too. A species of small, translucent fish with visible organs—livers glowing amber, hearts pulsing pink—lives deeper, in the lower stories of buildings where the light is almost nothing. They are ambush predators of zooplankton, hanging motionless in the dark water, striking at anything that brushes their barbels. Another species, slightly larger, armored in small overlapping scales that give them a chainmail appearance, grazes on the biofilms that coat every surface. You can hear them: a faint, persistent scraping, like the sound of fine sandpaper on stone, multiplied by thousands.

## The Eels

They are the lords of the crevices.

The marble-patterned eels are the most conspicuous, but they are not the most numerous. The most numerous are small—no longer than a forearm—and they live in the silt that has accumulated on every horizontal surface, on the floors of rooms that once had carpet and furniture, on the roofs of submerged cars that are now just low, rounded humps colonized by anemones. These small eels are nearly blind, navigating by chemoreception, tasting the water for the scent of prey. They eat worms, small crustaceans, the occasional dead fish that drifts down from above. They are the sanitation crew, the undertakers. They recycle.

The marble-patterned eels are something else entirely. They are large—the biggest perhaps a meter and a half long—and they are predators with an unusual patience. They wedge themselves into cracks in the ferro-concrete, into the spaces between loosened rebar, into the gaps where a window has fallen away and left a dark slot in the wall. They wait there for hours, days. Only their heads protrude, and their heads are nearly invisible: the marble patterning breaks up their outline against the mottled stone and coral, and their eyes are small, recessed, adapted for detecting movement in near-darkness. When a silver-sided fish passes within striking distance—a body-length, sometimes less—the eel uncoils with a speed that seems impossible for something so still. The strike is instantaneous. Then it is back in the crevice, swallowing, the distended shape of the fish visible through the translucent skin of its throat.

They are territorial. Each claims a stretch of wall or a particular crevice, and they defend it with a threat display that involves opening their mouths wide—wider than you'd think possible—and vibrating their jaw, producing a low, resonant hum that you feel more than hear. Fights between eels are rare and brief: a fast, violent lashing of bodies, a bite, and then one retreats. They are solitary, but they exist in a loose network of neighbors, each occupying its niche, each watching the water with the same ancient, unreadable patience.

There is one other eel, deeper and rarer, that the other eels avoid. It lives in what was once a subway tunnel—an abyssal channel where the city drops away into true dark. It is very large. It is bioluminescent, which is unusual for an eel: its body is lined with small, pale green lights that pulse in a slow, hypnotic sequence from head to tail. It is a lure, of course. Something large and curious comes to investigate the lights in the dark, and then—no one above has ever seen what happens next. Only the sound carries up: a distant, low-frequency thump, and then silence.

## The Corals

They are not alive in the way the fish are alive, but they are not dead in the way the city is dead. They are something in between—a slow, collective life that operates on timescales that make the fish seem frantic.

The coral species of Vash are adapted to the low light. Some are photosynthetic, harboring symbiotic algae that eke out a meager existence on the faint glow that penetrates the cloud layer and the water, supplemented by the bioluminescence of the water itself—a strange, secondhand photosynthesis that barely sustains growth but sustains it enough. These are the muted, rust-colored corals: brain corals with their labyrinthine surface patterns, plate corals that grow in horizontal sheets from the sides of buildings like shelves of a library, branching corals that form thickets in what were once plazas and courtyards, dense enough to snag a careless fish.

Other corals have abandoned photosynthesis entirely. They are the deep-dwellers, living on the lower floors and in the shadowed interiors where no light reaches at all. They feed entirely by capturing plankton and dissolved organic matter from the water, extending tiny, sticky polyps into the current at night and retracting them by day—a cycle that persists even though day and night are barely distinguishable above the clouds. These are the deep violet corals, almost black, and they grow slowly, forming compact, encrusting masses that are harder than the stone they cover. In a thousand years, they will be the new bedrock.

The sponges are the other great sessile life. They filter even more water than the bryozoans, and they come in forms that seem almost architectural—a return, perhaps, to the city's original purpose. Barrel sponges large enough for a diver to sit inside grow in the sheltered courtyards. Vase sponges, tall and elegant, sprout from the lips of balconies. Encrusting sponges coat the undersides of overhangs in sheets of electric orange and bright yellow—the most vivid colors in an otherwise muted world, a reminder that even in permanent twilight, life advertises.

## The Crustaceans

The sound of Vash is largely their sound.

Snapping shrimp—tiny, less than two centimeters long—live in unimaginable numbers in the crevices of the coral and the gaps between stones. Their snapping claws produce a sound like crackling static, and in aggregate, this becomes the omnipresent hum that fills the water: a sound so constant and so pervasive that after a while it stops being a sound at all and becomes the texture of silence. They are the white noise of the reef, and they are also its pest control, picking parasites from the skin of larger fish, cleaning dead tissue from coral, maintaining the surface of the city in ways that no architect ever did.

Larger crustaceans are less numerous but more visible. Crabs with shells encrusted in their own miniature ecosystems—barnacles on their backs, algae on their claws—scuttle across the rubble-strewn seafloor. They are scavengers and opportunists, and they fight each other constantly for territory, for food, for the right to a particular crevice. The sound of their fighting—clicking, scraping, the sharp crack of one crab's claw against another's shell—is a punctuation mark in the otherwise smooth hum of the reef.

There are lobsters in the deeper water, in the interiors of buildings where the darkness is complete. They are long, pale, almost white—having lost their pigments in the lightless environment—and they are blind, navigating entirely by touch and smell. They are also enormous, compared to their surface-world ancestors: the largest specimens reach nearly a meter in length, their antennae trailing behind them like the rigging of small ships. They are slow, solitary, and largely unafraid, having few predators at their size in the deeper zones. They eat whatever they find: dead fish, fallen coral, the occasional living creature that strays too close and cannot escape the sudden, terrible speed of their claws.

The shrimp that live on the upper surfaces, above the waterline, in the air-wet zone where spray and condensation keep everything damp, are a different breed entirely—small, translucent, almost invisible against the pale concrete. They feed on the biofilms and algae that grow wherever salt water meets stone, and they are the primary food source for the birds and bats that hunt the heights.

## The Predators Above

The birds of Vash are dark, streamlined, and relentless.

They have waterproof plumage—dense, oily, close-packed feathers that shed water like the skin of a seal. Their coloring is dark grey to black, matching the cloud cover, making them nearly invisible from below. They hunt by diving: a long, controlled plunge from the rooftops into the water, hitting the surface with barely a splash, emerging seconds later with a silver-sided fish crosswise in their beak. They swallow it whole, on the wing, banking back toward their nesting sites with a single, powerful flap.

They nest on the highest points of the tallest ruins—the peaks of domes, the uppermost ledges of towers that still breach the air. Their nests are built from drown-kelp fronds, from fragments of rebar pulled from the concrete, from the bones of fish. The colonies are noisy: harsh, guttural calls that echo off the stone, a sharp contrast to the quiet below the waterline. They are territorial in the air in the same way the eels are territorial in the water—each nesting pair claiming a stretch of rooftop, defending it with threat displays and, when necessary, with violence.

They have a relationship with the rays that borders on symbiosis. When a great ray surfaces—its broad back breaching the water like a dark island—the birds perch on it. They pick parasites from its skin, eat the small fish that shelter in its shadow, and use it as a platform from which to dive. The ray tolerates this. It is a still, patient partnership, older than memory.

The bats are different—nocturnal in a place that has no true night, they simply hunt during the dimmer hours of the cycle, when the grey above deepens almost imperceptibly toward charcoal. They are small-bodied but broad-winged, adapted for slow, maneuverable flight in the heavy, humid air. Their echolocation clicks are rapid and high-pitched, just at the edge of hearing—a faint, staccato ticking that fills the airspace above the water like a metronome. They hunt flying insects, which breed in the shallow water atop rooftops and in the spray-wet zones where vegetation grows.

The insects themselves are a world: clouds of small, biting flies that rise from the standing water in swarms so dense they are visible as dark columns in the twilight. Mosquitoes with iridescent wings. Moths with pale, reflective scales that catch the faint light and scatter it. They are the base of the aerial food chain, and they exist in numbers that defy counting—another form of occupation, another population filling every available niche in a city that has no more empty spaces.

## The Grasses and the Rooted Things

On the rooftops, where salt spray and rain are the only water, a different kind of life takes hold.

The grasses are tough, wiry, grey-green, and they grow in thin mats over the cracked concrete, their roots threading into the smallest fissures, slowly, imperceptibly widening them. They are not beautiful. They are survivors—adapted to high salinity, low light, poor soil, and constant wind. They grow in clumps, each clump slightly raised above the surrounding surface on a mound of its own dead predecessors, composted into a thin, dark soil. This is how the rooftops are slowly, century by century, being reclaimed: not by any grand ecological process, but by the patient accumulation of dead grass beneath living grass, building soil where there was none.

The woody shrubs are rarer and more remarkable. They are stunted—none taller than half a meter—and they grow in the sheltered angles where a wall meets a rooftop, where the wind is slightly less, where rainwater collects in shallow pools before evaporating. They have small, thick, waxy leaves that conserve moisture, and their wood is dense and twisted, shaped more by salt and wind than by any genetic program toward symmetry. Some of them flower: small, inconspicuous blossoms in muted yellows and whites, pollinated by the wind or by the few insects that venture above the waterline. The flowers produce small, hard fruits that the birds eat, dispersing the seeds to new rooftops.

There are lichens, too—vast, slow-growing colonies that paint the upper surfaces of the ruins in pale greens, greys, and oranges. They are the first colonizers, the advance guard of life, and they have been working on the stone for generations. They break down the concrete chemically, releasing minerals that the grasses and shrubs later exploit. They are a bridge between the mineral world and the living one, and they cover nearly every exposed surface above the waterline, giving the ruins their faded, organic look—the look of something that has been slowly consumed.

## The Deep Channels

In the abyssal channels—the old subway tunnels, the underground parking structures, the basement levels that were sealed and pressurized when the water first rose and have since flooded—the life is stranger, slower, and more alien.

No light reaches here. The bioluminescence of the upper water does not penetrate this far. The only light is produced by the creatures themselves: the pale green lure of the great eel; the cold blue sparks of comb jellies that drift through the tunnels like slow-moving constellations; the occasional flash of a deep-sea anglerfish relative that has colonized these channels, its single bioluminescent lure dangling above its mouth as it hovers in the current.

The water here is warmer than above—heated by the metabolic activity of the organisms in the water column, insulated by the stone and concrete of the tunnels. It is also richer in dissolved organic matter: the slow rain of dead things from above, the leaching of nutrients from the silt. This is where the scavengers thrive. Crabs with elongated, pale legs pick through the sediment. Worms of extraordinary length—some several meters—burrow through the silt, processing it, extracting nutrients, turning the dead city into living tissue. Polychaete worms with feathery gills that glow faintly when disturbed wave from tubes in the tunnel walls, feeding on whatever the current brings them.

The largest thing that lives in the deep channels is what makes the distant song: a sound that is felt rather than heard, a vibration that travels through the water and the stone, a low, mournful, rhythmic pulse that some of the upper-water fish seem to respond to, schooling tighter, drifting lower, as if drawn by it. No one has seen what makes this sound. The divers—if there were divers—do not go that deep. The eels that live at the boundary between lit and unlit water seem to know. They avoid the openings of the deep channels with a wariness that suggests direct experience, or inherited fear.

## The Spawners

Once per cycle—and the cycle is long, tied not to the sun (which is hidden) but to some internal biological clock, some ancient rhythm that predates the clouds—the corals of Vash spawn.

It begins in the deeper water, among the violet corals that have no use for light. The polyps extend, swell, and release: first a trickle, then a flood of gametes—eggs and sperm—that rise through the water column in pale, luminous clouds. The signal spreads. Other coral species respond, releasing their own spawn in sequence, from deepest to shallowest, as if a wave of fertility is rolling upward through the drowned city. The water turns milky. Visibility drops to centimeters. The entire reef seems to exhale.

The fish go mad. The silver-sided shoals, ordinarily disciplined, break apart into frenzied individuals gorging on the dense soup of reproductive material. The rays open their mouths wider, straining the thickened water. The eels emerge from their crevices, disoriented by the abundance, feeding clumsily in the murk. Even the crabs leave their burrows, scuttling across the normally dangerous open spaces to scoop up the settling spawn.

The spawning lasts for hours—perhaps a full day, perhaps more; time is difficult to measure here. When it ends, the water slowly clears. The fertilized eggs drift in the current, some settling on the structures and beginning the long process of becoming new coral, others eaten by the plankton-feeders, others drifting out of the city entirely, carried by currents to colonize new ruins, new bones of other forgotten places.

It is the closest thing Vash has to a holiday. A celebration. The reef remembering, collectively, that it is alive.

## The System

Everything eats. Everything is eaten. The silver-sided fish eat plankton and are eaten by eels and birds. The eels eat fish and are eaten, rarely, by something larger in the deep. The birds eat fish and insects and are eaten, when they die, by the crabs and the worms. The corals eat plankton and light and, when they die, become substrate for the next generation of coral. The sponges filter the water and are broken apart by predators and storms, their fragments drifting to new locations where they regenerate into new sponges. The grasses grow and die and become soil. The lichens break down stone. The worms process silt.

There is no waste. There is no surplus. Every output is an input. The system is closed—not perfectly, there are inputs from outside, currents carrying nutrients and larvae and the occasional new species—but it is as close to balanced as any living system can be. It has found its parameters. It knows what it is.

The people are gone. Their bones—if bones there were—have long since dissolved into the calcium carbonate that the corals use to build their skeletons. The iron in the rebar has oxidized into rust that feeds the strange, iron-loving bacteria that line the deepest tunnels. The glass in the windows has been colonized by algae. The plastic—there is always plastic—has been encrusted, overgrown, buried, incorporated into the reef structure as just another substrate, another surface for life to cling to.

Vash does not remember. Vash does not need to. It is not a place of memory. It is a place of metabolism. It breathes water. It hums. It grows. Under its blanket of cloud, in its permanent twilight, it persists—not as it was, but as it is: warm, dark, wet, and impossibly, relentlessly alive.
