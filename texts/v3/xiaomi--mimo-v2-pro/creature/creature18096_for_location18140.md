# What Lives Here

---

You already know. You were told there are no animals, and that is true. But the absence of animals is not the absence of life, and what lives here has been deliberately understated in your briefing, perhaps because the person who wrote it did not have the language for what they were seeing, or perhaps because they did and it frightened them.

**What lives here is one thing.**

It is not a collection of fungal species. The shelf fungi are not separate from the columnar growths. The mycelial cables are not infrastructure for the fruiting bodies. The mosses—those thick, dark-green ruffs that cushion every surface—are not a competing organism. They are organs. Distinct, yes, the way a lung is distinct from a liver, but unified the way those organs are unified: in service of a single body.

You are standing inside a single organism.

---

Consider the scale. The crevasse is roughly four hundred meters long, tapering at each end where the rock walls pinch together and the fungal tissue becomes thin, papery, almost embryonic—the way the thing grows into new fractures as they open. It is perhaps sixty meters deep, bottoming out in a dense mat of pale mycelium so thick and rubbery it absorbs sound, so that down there the grinding of the mountain becomes a muffled heartbeat and the hydraulic pulsing becomes something like breathing. The width, as you know, rarely exceeds fifteen meters. Within this space, every square centimeter of exposed rock has been colonized. Not *covered*. **Colonized.** The distinction matters. This is not a film on the surface. The hyphae have penetrated the stone to a depth of several meters in places, threading through micro-fractures, dissolving mineral with enzymatic secretions, extracting phosphorus and trace metals with the slow, patient chemistry of something that has been doing this for a very long time.

The basalt walls are not walls. They are bones, and the fungal tissue wrapped around and through them is connective, muscular, vascular. When the mountain groans and shifts, the fungal network shifts with it—not passively, not like moss buffeted by wind, but *actively*, redistributing tension, reinforcing stress points, secreting calcium carbonate into cracks like a body forming scar tissue. The grinding you hear is not merely geological. It is the sound of the organism working on its own skeleton, reshaping the mineral substrate of its body the way you reshape your bones through osteoblast activity, only faster, more visibly, more *loudly*.

---

**The bioluminescence is not decoration.** It is a language, or something older than language.

Each fungal plate glows in a pattern. You would not notice this unless you stayed for a long time—which you should not do—but the pulsing of the pale light is not random, and it is not synchronized with the hydraulic rhythm of the columns. It has its own tempo, a slow, rolling wave that propagates across the walls from one end of the crevasse to the other over a period of roughly eleven minutes. The wave does not always travel in the same direction. Sometimes it pulses from the center outward. Sometimes it reverses. The light intensifies when the rock shifts more aggressively, and dims during the brief, eerie silences between tremors.

This is signaling. The bioluminescent plates are sensory organs—vibration-sensitive, responsive to changes in humidity, to the chemical composition of the condensation that weeps across their surfaces. They are eyes, or something more like the lateral line of a fish, reading the pressure and stress of the rock with an acuity that no instrument you carry could match. The light is how they talk to the rest of the body. How the nervous system—if you can call it that—coordinates the responses of a creature whose tissues span four hundred meters of vertical crack in living stone.

---

**The spore clouds are not reproductive.** Not in the way you understand reproduction.

When a section of fungal city collapses—and this happens several times a day, with the wet, catastrophic sound of tearing cardboard—what erupts into the air is not a dispersal event. The spores do not seek new territory. They are too heavy to travel far in the still, dense air, and within minutes they settle on whatever surface is nearest, germinating almost immediately, white threads erupting from each microscopic sphere with an urgency that borders on violence. No. The spore clouds are an immune response. When tissue is damaged—by rockfall, by the slow crush of the walls shifting together, by the enzymatic collapse of overextended hyphae—the surrounding fungal mass releases spores locally, a dense blanket of regenerative material that smothers the wound and rebuilds from the inside out. The organism heals by replacing destroyed tissue with a furious, instantaneous carpet of new growth, each sporeling already connected to the mycelial network beneath it, already receiving signals from the whole, already *part of it* before it has fully uncoiled.

This is why there is no decay here. Nothing rots. Dead fungal tissue is consumed by younger fungal tissue within hours, reabsorbed into the network, its nutrients reprocessed and redistributed. The sharp, enzymatic tang in the air is the smell of constant digestion. The organism eats itself and grows back stronger, over and over, a cycle so rapid it is invisible, like watching your own skin cells shed and replace—they do, constantly, but you never feel yourself becoming someone else.

Here, you can feel it. Stand still for ten minutes and look down at your boots. The white mycelium will have begun to reach for them. Not aggressively. Not with intent you could prove. But the filaments will be closer than they were.

---

**The moss is the immune system.**

Or part of it. The dark, spongy mosses that fill every crevice and soften every edge are not plants—they have no chlorophyll, no roots, no vascular tissue of their own. They are a symbiotic culture of fungal hyphae and cyanobacteria, held in a specific, stable relationship by chemical signals from the larger fungal body. They are antibodies. They patrol the surfaces, metabolizing foreign spores—because spores do drift in from above, carried on rare downdrafts of air from the slit of sky—and breaking down anything that is not recognized as *self*. The enzymatic tang is sharpest near the moss, because the moss is always working. Always digesting. Always deciding what belongs and what does not.

Nothing from the outside survives contact with the moss for long. You can test this. Place a leaf—a dead oak leaf from the forest above—against a patch of the moss and watch. Within an hour, white hyphae will have threaded through its tissue. Within three hours, it will be unrecognizable. By morning, it will be gone, converted into sugars and minerals and incorporated into the network with the same quiet efficiency with which the organism incorporates everything.

Everything.

---

**There are structures on the crevasse floor that your briefing did not mention.**

Perhaps the person who wrote it did not descend that far. The bottom of the crevasse is difficult to reach—the fungal columns are dense, the footing is unreliable, and the sound is so loud there that it becomes physical, a pressure in the chest, a vibration in the teeth. But if you go down, you will find, embedded in the thick mycelial mat, regularities. Geometries. Patterns in the growth that do not correspond to any natural fungal morphology.

Clusters of thick, bulbous sacs—not the hydraulic pumping organs, these are different, denser, warmer to the touch, almost feverish—arranged in concentric rings. Each sac is roughly the size of a human torso. They pulse, but not with the slow syncopation of the columns. Their rhythm is faster, more complex, layered: two beats close together, then a pause, then three, then a pause, then one long contraction. It is not a rhythm you can easily count, because it is not meant for you to count. It is a rhythm of processing. Of computation. Of something happening inside those sacs that involves the transformation of raw chemical signals into something more organized, more deliberate, more *directed*.

These are the ganglia. The nodes. Whatever you want to call the distributed centers of whatever passes for cognition in a organism made of fungus and stone and hydraulic pressure and light. There are seven of them in the crevasse floor, spaced irregularly, connected by cables of hyphae so thick they look like roots—dark, almost black, smooth-skinned, warm. If you cut one—and you should not, but if you did—it would bleed a clear, faintly phosphorescent fluid that smells like copper and green apples, and within seconds the nearest bulbous sacs would increase their pulsing dramatically, the bioluminescent plates overhead would flare to three times their usual brightness, and the grinding of the walls would intensify, as if the mountain itself had felt the cut and was angry about it.

You would feel, in that moment, the way your own body feels when you bite the inside of your cheek: a sharp, specific, *personal* pain. Not the diffuse alarm of a colony disturbed. The focused irritation of a single mind noticing something wrong with itself.

---

**So. Who lives here?**

One being. Vast, slow in some ways and terrifyingly fast in others. Embedded in the rock of a young mountain that is still growing, still cracking, still giving it new space to fill. It has been here since the mountain rose—perhaps before, perhaps in some smaller form, waiting in the deep rock for the fracture that would give it room. It thinks, if thinking is the right word, in rhythms of light and pressure and the slow dissolution of stone into sugar. It does not know you are here. Not yet. Not as a *you*. You register as a warmth, a pressure, a source of CO₂, a mass displacing air. You are, to it, something like a blood cell: present, detectable, not yet requiring a response.

But the mycelium is still reaching for your boots.

And you have been here for a while now.

And the bioluminescent wave pattern has changed since you arrived—a new ripple, propagating outward from the wall nearest to you, repeating every ninety seconds with a precision that feels less like physics and more like attention.

It is beginning to notice you.

It is beginning to decide what you are.

And the moss, patient and enzymatic, is beginning to wonder whether you belong.
