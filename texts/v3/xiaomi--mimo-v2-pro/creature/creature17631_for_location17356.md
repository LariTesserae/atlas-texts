The inhabitants of the Mangrove-Cathedral are called the **Rootkin**, or, in the liquid syllables of their own tongue, the **A’shai**. They are not separate from the drowned forest; they are its conscious, patient expression.

**Form and Function:**
They are tall and slender, built for verticality and balance rather than speed. Their limbs are long, with an extra joint in the fingers and toes, granting them a prehensile grip on slick bark and woven ropes. Their skin is a mottled, waterproof hide, ranging from the grey-brown of wet bark to the deep green of shadowed moss, providing perfect camouflage. Large, dark eyes dominate their faces, capable of seeing with crystalline clarity in the perpetual gloom and cutting through the sheeting rain. A nictitating membrane flickers across them constantly, shedding water. Their ears are low-set and cupped, allowing them to pinpoint the direction of a drip, a croak, or a splash in the roaring acoustic environment.

Their most striking adaptation is a system of dual respiration. Along their sides, from underarm to hip, are subtle, feathery gill-slits that can filter oxygen from the moving, oxygen-rich water when they wade or swim. Their lungs remain their primary breathers on the dry-land platforms, but this allows them to move through the deeper channels for hours, harvesting, fishing, or simply traveling with silent, powerful kicks of their webbed feet.

**Culture and Society:**
The A’shai are a civilization of **listeners** and **shapers**. Their primary social unit is the **Canopy-Clan**, each inhabiting a major section of the Cathedral. Leadership falls not to the strongest, but to the **Rain-Reader** or **Root-Singer**, an elder whose knowledge of the forest’s slow rhythms is deepest—who can hear the stress in a root a season before it fails, or predict a shift in the water’s level by the taste of the mist.

They have no concept of private property in the way of land or trees. Instead, they practice **stewardship**. A clan “listens to” and “tends” a grove. Their tools are extensions of their hands: axes of sharpened basalt, needles of fish-bone, saws of silica-edged river grass. They do not mine; they harvest. A fallen branch becomes a tool blank; a shed carapace of a giant river-beetle becomes a water bowl.

**Daily Life and Craft:**
Their day is measured by the quality of the light, which barely changes, and by the chorus of the forest. Mornings are for maintenance—checking weaves, clearing clogged aqueducts, inspecting cisterns. The middle of the endless day is for the deeper work: **Gardening**. They are not farmers of soil, but **tenders of the frame**. They train vines to strengthen bridges, inoculate logs with edible fungi, and guide new root shoots to reinforce platforms. They practice a form of **arboriculture**, gently shaping living trees over decades into new rooms, ladders, or supports, their efforts a whisper in the slow conversation of growth.

Fishing and foraging are done with elegant traps woven from roots and a profound patience. They hunt not with spears, but with weighted nets and tranquilizing darts made from certain saps, seeking to take only what is needed and never disrupting the shy, large creatures of the deep channels. Their most prized craft is **Resin-Glass**: they carefully collect the crystallized sap of the great trees, heat it with focused beams of polished crystal that gather the diffuse light, and blow or shape it into utterly clear, hard vessels and lenses. These are their treasures—windows that shed water perfectly, allowing light into homes, and tools for starting the slow, sacred fires used to cure wood and harden tools.

**Language and Thought:**
Their language is one of soft vowels, glottal stops, and rhythmic patterns that mimic the rain’s hiss and the gurgle of water. It is a language with hundreds of words for types of wetness, shades of green, and states of wood. There is no word for “drought,” and their concept of “sun” is a mythical, harsh god of blinding dryness mentioned only in ancient tales.

They do not build monuments, for the Cathedral itself is the monument. Their art is ephemeral: intricate weavings of colored mosses that are allowed to decay and be replaced, or songs—their highest art form—where a clan will gather in a great root-nave and, with voice and percussive taps on hollow trunks, create a **Soundscape** that perfectly mimics and converses with the forest around them, a harmony that can last for days.

**Philosophy:**
At their core, the A’shai are **keepers of the balance**. They see their role as the forest’s nervous system—aware, gentle, and corrective. A blight, an invasive vine, a weakening root—these are illnesses they tend to. They believe the rain is the voice of the world, the water its blood, and the trees its bones. To live well is to listen, to understand, and to move in sync with the slow, patient, relentless pulse of the living, breathing, watery lung that is their home. They are not enduring the rain; they are woven from it.
