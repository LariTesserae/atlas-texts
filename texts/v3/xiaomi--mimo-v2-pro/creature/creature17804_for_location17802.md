The residents of this cirque do not scurry, nor fly, nor swim. They are not creatures of instinct or hunger, but of process and patience. They are the slow, the wet, the mineral-fed, the ancient.

First, and most visibly, are the **Pitcher Kin**. They are not individuals, but a collective. Each towering ribbed stalk is a monument to stillness. Their lurid, gaping mouths, slick with sweet, digestive dew, are not traps for creatures—for there are none—but sieves for the mist itself. They breathe in the warm, mineral-heavy vapor, and their flesh thickens, imperceptibly, season by season. They are the cirque’s great digesters, turning the mountain’s exhaled breath into solid, silent growth. They do not move, but if you watch long enough—a day, a week—you might sense the collective, almost imperceptible lean of their stalks toward the center, toward the worn stones, as if drawn by a faint, gravitational memory.

Then there are the **Horsetail Choirs**. The ghostly, jointed stalks are not plants in the soft, leafy sense. They are living conduits. The constant beading of moisture at their joints isn’t just condensation; it is a shared, rhythmic pulse. The water drawn from the saturated moss travels up their hollow cores, and at each joint, it whispers a tiny *hush* before continuing its climb. In the absolute quiet, if you kneel among them, you can hear it: a sub-audible, collective vibration, the sound of water climbing toward a sky it will never reach. They are the cirque’s hydraulics, a silent, standing wave of moisture.

The **Sphagnum Sea** is the floor, the stomach, and the memory of the place. It is not a carpet, but a living, breathing, water-logged mind. It holds the warmth from the stones. It filters the minerals from the weeping walls. In its deepest, greenest layers, it preserves everything—every fallen spore, every whisper of pollen from the Pitcher Kin, every dissolved trace of the pale grey stones. It compresses time into peat. To walk on it is to walk on millennia of slow digestion. It breathes dampness into the air, completing the cycle.

And then, there are the **Circle-Keepers**.

The worn, pale grey stones are not inert. They are the patient heart of the cirque’s equilibrium. They are not alive as the moss is alive, but they *participate*. Their different geology makes them cooler to the touch than the native granite, and in this warm, wet bowl, they act as condensers. Water gathers on their smooth, curved surfaces with a peculiar eagerness, running down them in clearer, faster rivulets. The moss grows thickest around their bases. The tallest Pitcher Kin have rooted themselves nearest to them.

They do nothing. They are the focus. They are the reason the flora grows in a broken circle, not a random scatter. They are the punctuation that organizes the sentence. There is a story in their placement, but it is told in a language of lichen patterns, of differential erosion, of the way the mist curls and lingers in the space they define. The "mind that came, considered, and left" did not leave them as monuments. It left them as *keys*. It tuned the cirque. It set the conditions for this specific, perfect equilibrium of warmth, water, and life. Now, they are simply part of it—worn down by the very system they help sustain, their purpose dissolved into function, their history into geology.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing here flees. The only consumption is the slow, sweet, microbial digestion within the pitchers, and the ancient, patient work of the sphagnum. The only sound is the water’s journey—the whisper on the cliff, the climb through the horsetails, the plink into deep pools, the slow filtering through the moss.

They are all one organism, really. The weeping walls are its lungs. The mist is its breath. The warm stone is its slow heart. The pitcher plants are its digestive system. The horsetails are its veins. The moss is its memory. And the worn, grey stones in their broken circle are its nucleus—the ancient, forgotten, yet still-functioning core of a living, breathing room in the body of the mountain.
