The inhabitants of the Foundry Ranges are a study in cohabitation with the incomplete.

**The Koralathi.** They are the anchored people. Their physiognomy is shaped by the altitude—broad chests, efficient lungs, a slight bluish tint to their nailbeds that they consider a mark of place. But their defining trait is their vigilance. They are watchers. Their eyes move slowly across the cityscape, not in fear, but in a state of continuous, low-grade assessment. They measure the drip-rate of condensation from a specific beam. They note the particular pitch of the hum in their floor. They remember which cracks are new and which are old friends.

They are builders, but their building has a peculiar, reactive quality. An expansion is often less a new project and more a response to a change in the rock's posture—a new buttress because the mountain shrugged. Their engineering is robust, empirical, and haunted by a polite superstition. They speak in technical terms about load-bearing capacities and alloy compositions, but they will also tell you, with equal seriousness, that you must oil the hinges on a door that appeared overnight, whether it leads anywhere or not. Their society is structured around the **Survey**, which functions as both a scientific body and a quiet priesthood. Surveyors are listened to with a gravity that goes beyond their data. They interpret the gradients.

They have a culture of patience. Their art is etching—slowly incising patterns into metal or stone that will be consumed by rust or lichen within a generation. Their stories are not epics of conquest, but sagas of observation: *The Year the Stream Began to Flow Backward, The Season of the Silent Bats.* Their greatest fear is not catastrophe, but *unnoticed change*. They build not for permanence, but for legibility; every rivet and weld is meant to be seen, so that any alteration to the structure will be starkly, obviously *there*.

**The Adapted.** The ibex are not normal ibex. Their hooves have hardened to grip iron grating as well as rock. They chew on mineral-rich lichen that grows on smokestacks, and their digestive systems have adapted to process trace metals. They are not tame; they are *indifferent*. They regard humans as another geological feature—unpredictable, but rooted. The bats in the smokestacks have evolved a form of echolocation that works through the industrial din, a rapid, staccato clicking that harmonizes, eerily, with the rhythm of certain stamp-mills. They are vital to the ecosystem, controlling the vast, slow insects whose segmented bodies are almost geological in their patience and whose shed carapaces, when collected and boiled down, yield a resin of incredible adhesive strength.

The rats in the lower levels are a open secret. They are large, yes, but also *organized*. They navigate the labyrinth of fault-clefts and service tunnels with a systemic intelligence. The Koralathi do not exterminate them; they trade with them. A piece of scrap metal left in a specific junction will be gone by morning, replaced by a perfectly machined gear or a bundle of high-grade wiring harvested from somewhere deep in the unfinished rock. No one speaks of where the rats learned this.

**The Compositional.** Then there are the others. They are not animals. They are *manifestations*.

There is the **Clatterjack**, a thing that moves through the mid-level scaffolding with the disjointed gait of a broken marionette, its limbs producing the exact sound of a wrench dropped on steel. It is not malicious. It seems to be *mimicking* the city's sounds, practicing them. It leaves no tracks.

In the perpetual twilight of the northern plazas, **Vein-Runners** can sometimes be seen at dusk. They look like foxes, but their fur is the exact color and texture of wet granite, and when they move, they seem to flow along the existing lines of stress in the stone, as if they are not running over the ground, but emerging *from* it. They are gone if you blink.

The **Loom-Rats** of the upper fog are the strangest. They appear as smoky, indistinct shapes in the fog itself, weaving between the arc lights. They have the vague outline of enormous rodents, but they are composed of the fog, the buzzing light, and the murmur of the ringing. They are harmless, but their presence causes the arc lights to dim and the fog to swirl in patterns that, if you stare too long, almost resolve into schematic diagrams. The Survey has a standing order: do not interfere with the Loom-Rats. They are considered part of the monitoring system.

**The Mountains.** Ultimately, the primary inhabitants are not the Koralathi or the fauna, but the mountains themselves. They are not alive in a biological sense, but they are *active* in a geologic one that borders on the intentional. The Koralathi feel them as a presence—the deep hum is its breath, the slow rise is its growth, the sulfurous exhalations are its speech. The "weirdness"—the extra catwalk, the uphill stream—is the mountain's dreaming seeping into the world. The unfinished state is a form of consciousness, and all who live here are living within that slow, vast thought.

The relationship is not symbiotic; it is occupant and host. The Koralathi are explorers who have set up camp inside a mind that is still forming its first ideas. They are careful not to startle it. They listen to its hum. They build their heavy, temporary things upon its skin. And they wait, with the same patient, unspoken anticipation as the stone itself, for whatever it is that is finally, slowly, beginning to take shape.
