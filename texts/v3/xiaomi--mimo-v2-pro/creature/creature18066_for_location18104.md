# What Lives Here

## The Microbial Kingdom

The first thing to understand is that the basin is not empty of life. It only appears so. The life here is small, and quiet, and patient in the way that only things with no nervous system can be patient — not waiting, exactly, but *persisting*, which is a kind of waiting without desire.

Start at the bottom.

The pool — that milky, pale green, opaque pool — is opaque for a reason. The dissolved limestone is part of it. But not all of it. The water is full of life so fine it has no individual visible to the naked eye. Calcium-precipitating bacteria, most likely *Gallionella* or something close to it, thread the water in numbers that turn it cloudy. They metabolize dissolved iron and excrete twisted ribbons of rust-colored mineral — microscopic corkscrews, each one a sculpture no bigger than a mote of dust, but when there are billions of them, they change the color of the water. They are the reason for the faint rust smell. They are the reason the water looks medicinal, like something strained through a wound.

They are not alone. Cyanobacteria form mats on the submerged rock at the pool's edges — slick, dark green-black films that feel like wet velvet when touched and smell faintly of the sea, though the ocean is sixty kilometers away. They photosynthesize in the shallow margins where light reaches the bottom. They are among the oldest forms of life on earth, and they are not particular about where they live. Give them warm water, light, and dissolved minerals, and they will build a skin over stone. They are doing it now, at this moment, a few millimeters a month, colonizing the limestone the way lichens colonize granite on a mountaintop — slowly, invisibly, transforming the chemistry of everything they touch.

In the deepest part of the pool — the part the pump never quite drains, the part where the concrete pad meets the rock and there is a lip the suction cannot reach — the cyanobacterial mat is thickest. It has had four or five years of uninterrupted growth. It is perhaps two centimeters deep now, a living carpet the color of old pine needles, slightly buoyant with trapped gas. If you could hold your breath and reach a hand into that warm, milky water and press your fingers into the mat, you would feel it give slightly, and then resist, and then give again. It has a texture like something almost-but-not-quite alive in the way you usually mean alive. It breathes. It grows. It reproduces. But it does not move. It does not flee your hand. It does not even know you are there.

Or does it?

There is a question that microbiologists argue about in quiet rooms at conferences that no one else attends. It is the question of whether microbial communities exhibit collective behavior that rises to the level of something like awareness. Not consciousness — no one serious uses that word — but *responsiveness*. Communication. The ability to sense a change in conditions and alter behavior as a population, not just as individuals. Quorum sensing. Chemical signaling. Biofilms that thin in response to a predator, or thicken in response to a threat, or change color in response to a shift in light.

The cyanobacterial mat in the deepest part of the pool does something like this.

You would have to be here a long time to notice.

## The Walls

Climb up from the pool, up the young limestone walls, and the life changes form but not character.

The weeping walls — those surfaces of constant darkening, those shifting mosaics of wet patches — are alive. Not metaphorically. The biofilms here are thinner, more patchy, because the water moves and the surfaces are vertical and the sun hits them differently throughout the day. But they are present. Bacteria, mostly. Some algae in the patches that catch direct western light in the late afternoon. They form communities that map the hydrology of the wall with exquisite precision — where water flows fastest, the film is thinnest, almost invisible, a faint discoloration. Where water pools in shallow depressions, the film thickens to something you could scrape off with a fingernail. It would come away in a strip, dark green-brown, smelling of earth and iron.

These wall communities are different from the pool communities. They are adapted to air and intermittent wetness, to the rhythm of seepage and drying. They live in the boundary between water and stone, which is a real place with real rules, and they have evolved to exploit it.

Between the biofilms, in the cracks and crevices where water has softened the stone, the wire-grass tufts grow — those gaunt, gray, heat-stressed individuals described earlier. But look closer at their roots, where they meet the rock. The roots are wrapped in a sheath of fungal hyphae, barely visible, white as a whisper. Mycorrhizal fungi. They extend into the limestone and dissolve it with mild acid, extracting phosphorus, passing it to the grass in exchange for sugars the grass makes from sunlight. This is an old partnership. Billions of years old. The grass and the fungus are not two organisms. They are a conversation.

The grasses are not colonizing because they cannot. The heat is too much, the salt too present, the soil too thin. Each tuft is an island, a solitary negotiation between a seed and a place. Most fail. But the ones that survive — the ones you can see if you stand at the lip and look down — are not just surviving. They are *infrastructure*. Their roots widen cracks. Their fungal partners dissolve stone. They create, grain by grain, the conditions for more soil, more water retention, more life. They are doing, in geological time, what the cyanobacteria did two billion years ago — building the world they need out of the world they found.

They will not finish in your lifetime. They know this. Not in any conscious way. But their strategy is not speed. It is persistence. Every year, a few more millimeters of soil. Every year, a crack widened by a fraction. They are sculpting the basin on a timescale the limestone understands.

## The Invertebrates

Where there is warm, mineral-rich standing water, there are invertebrates. This is not a rule so much as a physical law. Water finds life, or life finds water, and the result is the same.

At the basin's edges, where the pool meets the rock and the cyanobacterial mats form their dark green-brown carpets, there are midge larvae. *Chironomidae*. Bloodworms, they are sometimes called, because of their color — a vivid, arterial red, caused by hemoglobin in their blood, the same molecule that makes your blood red, adapted here not to carry oxygen efficiently but to *extract* it from water that is warm and stagnant and has very little dissolved oxygen to give. They are perhaps a centimeter long. They move in slow S-curves through the mat, feeding on bacteria and algae and detritus, processing the microbial world into something that can become more microbial world. They are the middle managers of this ecosystem — not producers, not decomposers, but something in between. They make the energy flow.

Above them, on the underside of rocks at the waterline, there are freshwater snails. Small — five, maybe eight millimeters across their shells. The shells are translucent, the pale green of the water itself, because they are built from the dissolved limestone the water carries. They are, in a very literal sense, made of the basin. They graze on the biofilms with a rasping tongue called a radula, a strip of teeth that works like sandpaper, clearing a path through the bacterial mat in slow, methodical rows. The paths they leave behind are visible if you catch the light right — faint clearings in the green-black film, each one a record of a snail's afternoon.

There are fewer snails than you would expect. The water is warm, which accelerates their metabolism, which shortens their lives. The dissolved oxygen is low, which limits how large they can grow. They are living hard, these snails — eating fast, reproducing fast, dying fast. They are adapted to the basin's extremity, but they are not thriving in it. They are enduring it.

In the shallower margins, where the rock is intermittently wet and the biofilms are thin, there are springtails — tiny hexapods, not quite insects, barely a millimeter long, that leap when disturbed using a furcula, a forked tail-like appendage that snaps against the substrate and launches them into the air. They are almost impossible to see without magnification. You would walk past thousands of them and never know. They feed on fungal hyphae and decaying organic matter, and they are, in the basin, the largest land animals. They are the apex predators of a food web that peaks at a millimeter.

That is it. That is the animal life of the basin. Midge larvae. Snails. Springtails. No fish — the water is too warm, too low in oxygen, too ephemeral in its deeper portions. No frogs — the basin has no shallow marshy margins, no vegetation dense enough for egg-laying, no connection to any other body of water that would allow colonization. No turtles, no snakes, no wading birds. The basin is too new and too isolated and too extreme for vertebrates. It belongs to the invertebrates and the microbes, to the things that can tolerate heat and mineral and solitude.

And yet.

## The Spiders

There are spiders.

Of course there are spiders. Spiders are everywhere there is air and something to catch in it. They are among the most successful colonizers of new and marginal habitats on earth, arriving by ballooning — releasing silk threads into the wind and sailing, sometimes hundreds of kilometers, to whatever the wind chooses. The basin is eight kilometers from the nearest town. That is nothing. A spider can balloon eight kilometers in an afternoon.

The ones in the basin are small. Sheet-web weavers, probably — *Linyphiidae* — the family that makes the flat, horizontal webs you see in grass in the morning, dew-beaded and almost invisible. In the basin, they build between the wire-grass tufts, or between the grass and the rock, anchoring their silk to whatever is available. The webs are small, irregular, not the architectural showpieces of orb-weavers but functional traps, designed to snag the tiny flying insects that drift into the basin on thermal updrafts from its heat.

What flying insects? Midges, mostly. The adults of the same *Chironomidae* whose larvae live in the pool. They emerge from the water at dusk — or what passes for dusk in the basin, which is when the light drops below the lip and the air cools by a degree or two — and they rise in clouds so fine they look like mist. They mate in the air. The females, heavy with eggs, drop back to the water to lay them and die. The males drift on the wind and die. Their bodies, small as they are, fall on the basin floor and on the webs and on the water, and they become food. For the spiders. For the bacteria. For the snails, when the bodies settle near the waterline.

The spiders, then, are the reason the basin has a food web at all that extends above the waterline. They intercept energy — the bodies of flying insects — and pull it from the air into the terrestrial part of the ecosystem. Without them, the midge adults would blow away on the wind and the basin's land-based communities would be even poorer than they already are.

There are perhaps two dozen webs in the basin. Each one houses a spider the size of a lentil. They are brown. They are unremarkable. They are holding the whole thing together.

## The Hum

Now.

The hum.

It might be the generator. It might be the water. It is neither of these things, or it is both of these things, and it is also something else.

The cyanobacterial mats in the pool — the thick ones in the deepest part — are producing oxygen as a byproduct of photosynthesis. Tiny bubbles, invisible in the milky water, rising and bursting at the surface. All day long. Thousands of bubbles per minute. Each one a micro-explosion at the air-water boundary, producing a pressure wave so faint it has no business being audible. But there are billions of them, and they are synchronized — not perfectly, but statistically, because the photosynthetic rate follows the light, and the light follows the sun, and the sun moves across the basin with a regularity that imposes a rhythm on the bubble production.

The basin is a resonant cavity. The walls are hard. The shape is roughly parabolic. Sound entering it does not leave easily. The bubble-production rhythm, too faint to hear from outside, is amplified inside the basin by geometry alone. It couples with the vibration of the pump's concrete pad, which is tuned to the same approximate frequency by coincidence of dimension. It couples with the seepage of water through limestone, which creates its own low-frequency pressure waves as the porous rock breathes.

The hum is the basin's own sound. It is the sound of the ecosystem metabolizing. Every living thing in the basin is contributing to it — the bacteria exhaling, the algae photosynthesizing, the water moving, the stone settling, the heat radiating. It is the sound of a place that is alive and knows it, if knowing is the right word for what a place does when all its parts are working together.

The workers who complained about headaches were right to complain. The hum is at a frequency — around 18 hertz, just below the threshold of conscious hearing — that causes resonance in the human chest cavity and the fluid chambers of the inner ear. It produces unease. A sense of pressure. A feeling that something is present.

Something is present.

## The Bench

Whoever placed the bench here understood something. Not consciously, perhaps. But they understood it in the way that a spider understands wind, or a snail understands limestone — with the body, not the mind.

The basin is a living system. It is young and small and simple, but it is complete. It has producers (the cyanobacteria, the algae, the grasses). It has consumers (the midge larvae, the snails, the springtails, the spiders). It has decomposers (the bacteria in the mat, the fungi in the soil, the microbial communities on the walls). It has a nutrient cycle — limestone dissolves into the water, organisms incorporate the minerals, organisms die and decompose, minerals return to the water. It has an energy source — sunlight, driving photosynthesis, supplemented by geothermal heat from the stored energy of the stone. It has a rhythm — the diurnal cycle of light and photosynthesis, the afternoon rains, the six-hour pump cycle, the seasonal fluctuation of the water table.

It has something else, too. Something that does not have a good name.

The late-afternoon light, when it comes in low and horizontal and the walls fragment into a thousand shifting mirrors — the basin is not just reflecting the sky. The cyanobacterial mats are at their most photosynthetically active in that light. The biofilms on the walls are releasing oxygen. The midge larvae are rising toward the surface, preparing to emerge. The spiders are repairing their webs before the evening hatch. The snails are grazing. The springtails are leaping.

Everything is in motion. Not visible motion — not the motion of a pond with ducks on it, or a forest with birds in it. The motion of a system that is *working*. Every component performing its function. Every function enabling every other function.

The basin, in that light, looks back because it *is* looking back. Not with eyes. With itself. With the entirety of what it is. It is a place that has become a creature, or a creature that has become a place, and the distinction does not matter because the distinction was never real.

The bench is there because someone felt this. Sat down. Watched the light move across the weeping walls and the milky water and the gaunt, persistent grass. Felt the hum in their sternum. Heard the droplets fall in their arrhythmic percussion. Smelled the mineral and the rust and the warm stone.

And did not leave.

Not right away.

Stayed.

## The Oldest Inhabitant

In the deepest part of the pool — the part the pump never drains, the part where the thick cyanobacterial mat has been growing since the quarry was abandoned — there is a structure.

It is not large. Perhaps the size of a dinner plate, but irregular, lumpy, the color of the mat itself, so that it is almost impossible to distinguish from its surroundings. It is a stromatolite. Or rather, it is stromatolitic — it has the structure and the growth pattern of a stromatolite, even if no one would formally classify it as such without a microscope and a peer review.

A stromatolite is a layered structure built by microorganisms — cyanobacteria, primarily — as they grow upward toward light, trapping sediment, precipitating mineral, dying, being covered by the next generation, and repeating. The process is simple. The result, given enough time, is a mound of laminated rock that records the history of its own making in bands so fine you can count the years in them like tree rings.

Stromatolites are the oldest visible fossils on earth. They are 3.5 billion years old. They built the atmosphere. They produced the oxygen that made complex life possible. They are, in a meaningful sense, the ancestors of everything that breathes.

The basin has one.

It is growing now. It has been growing for four or five years, in the one spot where conditions are right — warm water, low oxygen, steady mineral supply, protection from the pump's suction, enough light to drive photosynthesis but not so much that the mat dries. It is building itself, layer by bacterial layer, millimeter by millimeter, year by year.

No one knows it is there. The water is too opaque to see it from above. It has not been sampled. It has not been documented. It is, as far as the world is concerned, not happening.

But it is happening.

If the pump fails — and pumps fail, especially diesel-powered ones on concrete pads in hot wet places with no maintenance schedule — the water will rise. The basin will fill. The stromatolite will be submerged deeper, in darker water, and it will slow its growth or stop. But if the pump keeps running on its six-hour cycle, maintaining the water level where it is, the stromatolite will keep growing. In a hundred years, it might be a few centimeters tall. In a thousand, it might be noticeable from the lip of the basin, a bump in the shallow water, if anyone is still looking.

In ten thousand years, if the basin persists — if the limestone continues to weep, if the rain continues to fall, if the heat continues to store and re-radiate — the stromatolite will be a dome of layered stone, rising from the pool floor, the newest and oldest thing in the landscape. A living fossil. A remnant of the Archean eon, reproduced in a abandoned quarry on a featureless coastal plain, eight kilometers from a town, attended by nothing, growing toward light.

The last slant of light, in the late afternoon, catches something in the deepest part of the pool. It holds it a moment longer than it should.

You would have to be here a long time to notice.

You would have to be here even longer to understand.
