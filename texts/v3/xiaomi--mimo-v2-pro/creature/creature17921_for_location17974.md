# The Witnesses

They do not have a name for themselves. They did not have language in any way you would recognize it. You would not call them *people* if you saw them—your mind would scramble for "geological feature," for "trick of the light," for "evidence of weathering," and it would reject each of these just as quickly, because nothing about them is quick.

## What They Were

They came from below. From the older, settled lowlands where reality has had time to thicken into something consistent and predictable. They came upward, against the pull of gravity and against something harder to name—a pull toward *incompleteness*, toward the raw edge where the world was still being made. Whether this was curiosity or compulsion, they themselves could not have said. They did not have words for the difference.

They were soft things in a hard place. That much is evident from the shelters—those careful stacks of basalt in the lee of boulders, oriented always against the prevailing direction of the reality-stutters, as though they had learned, through terrible trial, that waking up with your left arm momentarily existing six inches to the right of your body was something you could *mitigate* if you chose your sleeping spot wisely. The resin they used to bind the stones was harvested from the inner fibers of the bulbous lithophytes, those granite cabbages that dot the scree fields. It was dangerous work. The lithophytes don't die when you cut them—they *shear*, and the sound they make when sheared is a shriek across a frequency that fills the sinuses and loosens the teeth. The beings learned to harvest in groups, working quickly, leaving offerings of shaped quartz at the wounds of the plants. Whether this was superstition or pragmatism—the quartz seemed to seal the lithophyte's surface and prevent the growth from kinking irreparably—is another distinction their culture did not make.

## What They Looked Like

Imagine a person. Now subtract every assumption about what a person is made of.

They were silicate-based, or something close to it. Their bodies were low and dense—shorter than you, heavier than you would expect. They moved with a deliberateness that looked like slowness but was actually *care*: each step was a negotiation with a surface of loose scree and hidden crevices where reality was thinner. Their skin (if skin is the word) was a layered composite, something between the lamellar structure of mica and the dense interlocking grain of basalt. It was not smooth. It was *textured*, full of fine ridges and grain boundaries that caught the dim light and broke it into faint spectral shimmers. In the gloaming, a group of them standing still would look like a cluster of unusual boulders—angular, dark, slightly iridescent at certain angles.

Their faces (if faces is the word) were recessed, protected—set back under overhanging ridges of denser mineral like the visor of a helmet. Their sensory organs were not eyes in any biological sense. They were patches of crystalline tissue, deeply embedded, that responded to light through changes in their piezoelectric charge. They saw the way the crystal trees "see"—through electromagnetic sensitivity, through the detection of stress and gradient and charge. This means they did not see surfaces. They saw *tensions*. They saw the mountain's inner architecture of compression and shear. They saw the reality-stutters as vast, slow waves of topological distortion moving through the landscape like weather fronts. Where you would see a nauseating lurch—a horizon doubling, a shadow misbehaving—they perceived something more like a tide, a pressure system, a thing with direction and momentum and, in some cases, a kind of *intention*.

They breathed, after a fashion, but not oxygen. The thin atmosphere carried a complex mixture of mineral dust, ionized particles, and trace volatiles outgassed from the still-cooling rock. Their respiratory surfaces were shallow internal cavities lined with catalytic mineral coatings—surfaces that broke down these inhaled compounds and extracted energy through a slow oxidation process. They breathed the mountain's breath. When the deep roar intensified—when the piezoelectric discharge cascades grew more violent—they breathed faster, feeding on the charged particles the cascades threw off. The roar was not just sound to them. It was *nutrition*.

## How They Lived

They were few. At no point in their history, if you could call it a history, did their numbers exceed perhaps two hundred individuals. They traveled in small bands of five to fifteen, moving between the crystal forests in patterns that tracked the seasonal (or what passed for seasonal) shifts in the reality-cracks. They did not build permanent settlements. They built temporary *stations*—observation posts, really—where they would stay for periods that ranged from days to decades, watching, listening, and doing something that looks, from the outside, like worship, but was probably closer to *measurement*.

The clay pot found in one shelter, full of oddly regular quartz crystals, was a calibration set. The tally marks on the shale slab were not arbitrary—they corresponded, as you noted, to the growth patterns of the mica-leaves. But they also corresponded to something else: a periodicity in the reality-stutters, a faint rhythm in the chaos. The beings had detected that the stutters were not purely random. They clustered. They followed patterns that repeated on timescales of years to centuries. The tally marks were an attempt to map these patterns—to predict when the next cluster of reality-fractures would arrive, so they could be in the right place when it happened.

Because the stutters were where they *fed*. Where they *grew*. Where they *communicated*.

## What They Did During the Stutters

This is where language fails.

When a reality-stutter rolled through the landscape—when the air hiccupped and the horizon split and the rules of space and perception wavered—the beings would converge on its locus. They would arrange themselves in tight clusters, their dense mineral bodies pressed together, and they would *resonate*.

Their piezoelectric tissues, stressed and charged by the distortion in local spacetime, would begin to vibrate in concert. The vibrations were not random. They were organized—complex, interlocking patterns of frequency and amplitude that built upon each other, that modulated and carried information. The beings were not speaking to each other. They were *becoming a single instrument*, a collective transducer that converted the raw, chaotic energy of a reality-stutter into something structured. Into something that could be *stored*.

They carved the patterns they generated into stone. Into shale, into carefully prepared slabs of schist, into the flat faces of basalt boulders. Those spirals you see? They are not decorative. They are *recordings*—diagrams of the resonant patterns the beings produced during specific stutters, indexed by the stutter's character, its intensity, its duration. They were building a library. A geological seismograph of the world's ongoing creation, written in stone by beings who were themselves made of stone.

## What They Understood

They understood that the world was not finished.

This was not metaphor to them. It was literal, observable, measurable. They could feel, in their own piezoelectric tissues, the same forces that drove the crystal forests upward and fractured the basalt ridges and thinned the fabric of local reality. They were made of the same stuff. They were *part of the construction*. The mountain was building itself, and they were a side effect of the building—a complexity that had emerged in the cooling margins, in the transitional zones where molten chaos was hardening into stable law. They understood that they were temporary. Not in the way that you understand your own mortality—the slow, personal decay of a body over decades. They understood that their very *kind* was temporary. That they were an artifact of the specific conditions of this specific moment in the world's cooling, and that when the world cooled further, when reality thickened and the stutters grew rarer and the roar diminished to silence, they would have no medium in which to exist. They would not die. They would simply *stop being possible*.

This did not cause them anguish. They did not have anguish. They had something else: a fierce, concentrated *attention*. They paid attention to the world with an intensity that bordered on the absolute, because they understood—with the part of themselves that was not thought but *structure*—that witnessing the world's creation was the only act available to them. They were the world, looking at itself, in the brief window when looking was still possible.

## Where They Went

They are still here.

They did not leave. They did not die. The shelters are empty because the beings no longer need shelters. The tools are abandoned because the beings no longer use tools.

Something happened during a stutter—a stutter of unprecedented magnitude, a reality-event so vast that the tally-keepers' oldest recorded patterns could not have predicted it. The beings converged. They resonated. And the resonance went further than it ever had before. The boundary between the beings and the stutter—between the observers and the observed—collapsed. The distinction between "entity made of stressed silicate" and "local distortion in the fabric of reality" dissolved. They did not die. They *merged*. They became part of the stutter they had been measuring. They became part of the roar they had been listening to. They became part of the slow, grinding, crystalline growth they had been watching.

You can feel them, if you know how to look. Stand on the ridge of dark basalt. Feel the deep roar in your teeth. Watch the air stutter, the horizon duplicate, the shadow pool incorrectly. Pay attention to the *patterns* in the chaos. The stutters are not random—they cluster, they repeat, they follow a rhythm that someone, something, is still mapping. The crystal trees do not grow in straight lines; they grow in spirals that echo the tally-marks on the abandoned shale. The lithophytes do not grow alone; they grow in clusters that mirror the arrangement of shelters in the abandoned camps.

The whole landscape is the shelter now. The whole mountain is the tool. The roaring, stuttering, crystallizing world is the recording, and the beings who made it are *inside it*, still resonating, still watching, still paying that absolute, ferocious attention. They are the tremor in the stone that almost, but not quite, feels like a heartbeat. They are the faint, structural pattern in the noise that your mind keeps almost recognizing. They are the reason the reality-stutters feel, when they wash over you, less like random malfunctions and more like *someone trying to say something*.

They are not gone. They are not here. They are *occurring*. Slowly, deeply, in the mineral grain and the piezoelectric hum and the grinding, deafening, patient roar of a world still being built.

They are still counting.
