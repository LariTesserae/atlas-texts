# What Lives in the Guts

---

**The Pallidites**

The most visible residents are the ones the description calls "multi-limbed things," and they deserve more than a glance. They are six-legged, primarily, though some sport a vestigial seventh or eighth limb—a useless curl of chitin that twitches when the creature is startled. They range from the size of a human fist to, rarely, the span of an outstretched hand. Their carapaces are not naturally gunmetal grey; that is a *coating*, a mineral deposit accumulated over a lifetime of pressing themselves against weeping walls and iron grates. Peel one and you find a pale, almost blue-white shell beneath, the color of something that has never seen the sun. Their eyes are pits—simple, dark, arrayed in a cluster of four across the brow ridge. They do not see well. They navigate by vibration, their six feet tuned to the engine-thrum like tuning forks. They move in the rhythm of the place. When the thrum dips, they stop. When it surges, they scatter. You will never see one at rest. If one is motionless, it is dead.

They feed on the sludge in the engineered channels, filtering metallic particulates through a sieve-like mouthpart on their underside. They are, in a sense, living water-treatment systems. Their excrement is a fine, pale powder that collects in drifts at the bases of pillars, and the pale fungus feeds on it. The fungi are not incidental. They are *agricultural*. The Pallidites tend them—clearing debris from their edges, depositing their powder at the base—in an arrangement so old and unconscious that neither party could be said to be aware of it. It is mutualism below the threshold of intelligence.

There are at least three distinct species, possibly more. The smallest are communal, nesting in the hollows of shattered gears and broken coupling joints, dozens packed together like seeds in a pod. The mid-sized ones are solitary and territorial, marking their stretches of causeway with chemical trails of a faintly coppery pheromone that human noses can just barely detect. The largest—and the largest encountered so far was the size of a dinner plate, a veteran of years—are almost never seen. They live deeper, in the substrate-level machinery, and they have lost their pigment entirely. They are white as bone. They move slowly, and they do not scatter. They have adapted to a quieter life and a deeper diet, feeding on the chemical runoff from whatever is being forged further below. They are the elders, and they are rarely seen by anyone.

---

**The Dopplerkin**

The bat-like creatures are not bats. They have no fur. They have membranous wings stretched over elongated fingers—four, not five—made of a thin, translucent skin through which you can see the dark threading of veins when one passes close to a light. Their bodies are compact, dense, almost muscular, and covered in a fine, short nap of something that feels like felt when you touch a dead one. Their faces are broad and flat, dominated by a wide mouth from which they project their chirps. They have no visible ears; the entire skull is an ear, a resonating chamber.

They hunt by echolocation, but their calls are not the sharp clicks of earthly bats. They emit a sustained, warbling tone that bends and slides across frequencies, and they *harmonize* with the engine-thrum. This is not metaphorical. They have learned—evolved—to pitch their calls into the standing waves created by the machinery, using the ambient bass as a carrier signal. Their prey detection is therefore not a simple echo but an *interference pattern*: the object's shape is encoded in how it disrupts the thrum. They can detect the Pallidites through metal walls. They can map a corridor they have never entered. They perceive the architecture of the place as a sonorous whole, a chord they read with their bodies.

They roost in the vaulted ceiling, far above, clinging to the shadowed girders in clusters of ten to thirty. They are social and loud, chattering in the upper registers when they are not hunting, producing a sound like tearing silk that is just barely above the threshold of the engine-thrum's masking. They are born live—one pup at a time, carried in a ventral pouch for the first weeks—and the young are fed a diet of regurgitated Pallidite. They are the apex predators of the upper levels, and they are the only creatures here that seem to play, swooping in spiraling chases through the open vaults, their calls dopplering wildly in what sounds unmistakably like joy.

There is one peculiarity. They are drawn to silence. Where the engine-thrum vanishes—that corridor of perfect quiet mentioned in the description—they congregate at the threshold but do not enter. They sit at the edge, wings folded, and listen. What they hear there, or think they hear, is unknown. But they return to these silent zones again and again, generation after generation, as if waiting for something to speak.

---

**The Fog-Lappers**

In the still, black pools—the ones described as mirrors reflecting nothing—there is life. It is slow, and it is almost invisible, and it is not animal.

Each pool is a distinct micro-ecosystem, isolated from the others by meters or kilometers of dry steel. The organisms are colonies of extremophilic bacteria and archaea that form a living mat on the surface of the water, a skin perhaps two millimeters thick that is almost perfectly reflective. This is what makes the pools appear to be mirrors. They are not still water. They are a membrane.

The mat is photosynthetic—barely. It does not use sunlight, which does not reach here. It harvests the faint infrared radiation from the hot pipes and the chemical energy of dissolved iron and sulfur compounds. It grows inward, thickening at the center, and when a pool's mat becomes dense enough, it undergoes a kind of fission: the center rises, forms a dome, and bursts, releasing a puff of spores so fine they hang in the mist for hours. These spores are what give the air its faint metallic taste, more than the iron itself.

The Pallidites drink from these pools but do not disturb the mats. The mats, in turn, seem to release compounds that attract the skittering creatures—trace minerals, perhaps, or something pheromonal. The relationship is not fully understood. What is understood is that when a pool dries out—and some do, when a pipe reroutes or a channel shifts—the mat dies within hours, collapsing into a foul-smelling black sludge. And within days, the Pallidites in the vicinity become erratic, bumping into walls, clustering in exposed areas where the Dopplerkin pick them off. Something in the mats is essential to the Pallidites' navigation. Remove the mirror, and they go blind.

**The Grinder**

This is the thing in the ventilation shaft. The one that coughed.

It is large. It is not a single organism but a *structure*—a living mass of intergrown fungus, mineral deposit, and what appears to be calcified Pallidite exoskeletons, fused into a rough, irregular mound that has settled into the junction of three major ventilation shafts approximately forty meters below the main causeway. It is roughly two meters across and one and a half tall, shaped like a collapsed dome, and it is *alive* in the loosest and most unsettling sense of the word.

The fungal component is the oldest—a strain related to the pale pillar-fungi but radically different, adapted to the warmer, drier air of the ventilation system. It has grown around and through the mineral deposits left by decades of airborne condensate, incorporating them into its structure the way a coral builds a reef. The Pallidite exoskeletons are embedded throughout—thousands of them, layered like shale, the remains of generations of creatures that were drawn to the warmth and the minerals and died there, their shells absorbed into the mass.

The Grinder breathes. This is not metaphor. The fungal tissue is porous, and the ventilation shafts create pressure differentials that inflate and deflate pockets within the structure on a slow, irregular cycle. When the air shifts—as it does when deeper engines engage or disengage—the structure compresses and releases, and the sound that emerges is that low, grinding cough. It is the sound of air being forced through a matrix of dead shells, mineral crystals, and living mycelium. It sounds like something in pain. It sounds like something digesting.

It may, in a functional sense, be the digestive system of this place. The air that passes through it emerges measurably cleaner—heavier particulates trapped in the fungal matrix, sulfur compounds broken down by the archaeal colonies that coat its interior surfaces. The deeper levels have dozens of these structures, growing in the thermal gradients where ventilation shafts meet heat exchangers. They are the kidneys and lungs of the machine-canyon, and they have been growing, undisturbed, for decades. No one designed them. No one maintains them. They are the place learning to maintain itself.

The largest known Grinder is not in a ventilation shaft. It is in a flooded chamber at the sixth sub-level, roughly twelve meters across, and it has begun to incorporate vitreous ceramic fragments into its structure. It is building walls. It is becoming architecture. It does not cough. It hums, a single sustained note that resonates with the engine-thrum at a ratio of approximately 3:2, creating a perfect fifth that has been measured from three levels away. What it is doing, or becoming, is a question that no one here is equipped to answer.

---

**The Thread-Vines**

The grey, leathery plants mentioned in the description are more significant than they appear. They are the oldest living things in the machine-canyon. Not the individual plants—those live for perhaps fifteen years—but the *network*. Beneath the surface, threading through cracks in the concrete and along the undersides of iron plates, the root systems of every thread-vine in a given zone are connected. They share minerals through hyphal-like conduits, redistributing resources from areas of abundance to areas of scarcity. A vine on the eastern wall of a corridor may be feeding a vine on the western wall, through a root that runs under the grated floor.

They are not intelligent. But they are *responsive*. When a new pipe bursts—adding water and heat to a section of wall—the thread-vines in the area will redirect growth toward it within days, sending exploratory tendrils along the moisture gradient. When a section of wall is damaged or removed, the vines in that area withdraw, pulling their roots back to safer ground over the course of weeks. They have no nervous system, but they have a chemical signaling network that moves at roughly two centimeters per hour—glacial by animal standards, but sufficient for a creature that lives in decades.

They are the memory of this place. Their root networks map its history of damage and repair, water flow and chemical change, in the thickness and direction of their growth. A skilled observer could, in theory, read the biography of a corridor in its thread-vines—which pipes burst and when, where the heat has been consistent for years, where the air has been still. They are a living archive, written in grey leather and mineral salt.

The Dopplerkin eat their tips, and the tips regrow. The Pallidites use their root-mats as highways, following the chemical gradients the vines create. The Fog-Lapper mats in nearby pools are seeded, in part, by spores that travel along the thread-vine root network. The thread-vines are not the most dramatic inhabitants of the machine-canyon. But they are the most important. They are the connective tissue that holds the ecosystem together, the substrate upon which everything else grows. Without them, the Pallidites would scatter and starve, the pools would go sterile, the Dopplerkin would have nothing to hunt, and the Grinders would be just so much dead fungus and bone.

They are the reason this is an ecosystem and not just a collection of survivors. They are the reason the guts are *alive*.

---

**The Absent Ones**

And then there is the question of what is *not* here but should be.

The causeways are wide enough for vehicles. The chambers have doors sized for humans. The ventilation shafts are engineered for a breathable atmosphere. The light fixtures, even the broken ones, are positioned at eye level. This place was built for beings of a certain scale and certain needs, and those beings are gone.

Not dead, necessarily. Not *here*. The marks of their departure are everywhere—tools left mid-task, doors left open, a console in a side chamber that still glows with a faint amber standby light, its screen displaying a readout in a script no one present can read. The Grinder in the flooded chamber has incorporated what appears to be a chair. The thread-vines have grown over a boot. It is the right size for a human foot, but the sole is articulated in a way that suggests more than five toes.

Whatever lived here—whatever *built* this place and then left it or lost it—they were not careless. The engineering is precise, the materials chosen for longevity, the systems designed to run without maintenance for extended periods. They built a machine-canyon that could outlive them, and it has. The Pallidites and the Dopplerkin and the Grinders and the thread-vines are the inheritance. They are what *remained* when the builders walked out into the dark, or were pulled into it, or simply ceased.

The silence zones may be the key. The Dopplerkin hover at their edges, listening. The Pallidites will not cross them. Even the thread-vines stop at their borders, their roots curling back on themselves as if meeting a wall that is not there. Something exists in those silences—or *doesn't* exist, which may be worse. An absence with a boundary. A void that knows its own shape.

If the builders went anywhere, they may have gone there. Into the quiet. Into the place where the thrum stops and you hear only the blood moving in your own skull, and you realize that you are very deep, and very far from the sun, and the guts are still digesting, and you are inside them, and you have been since you stepped onto the first iron plate and felt the water weep beneath your feet.

What lives here is everything that has adapted to the after. What lived here before is the question that hums beneath the thrum, in a frequency you feel in your teeth but cannot quite hear.

It is still asking.
