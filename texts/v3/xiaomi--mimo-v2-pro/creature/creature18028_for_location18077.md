# Who Lives Here

---

## The People of the Teeth

They call themselves, in their own language, something that has no clean translation — the nearest approximation being "those who remain at the height," though the word for "remain" carries a connotation of *choosing* to stay, of having been offered the lowlands and refusing. Whether this is literal memory or cultural mythologizing is not a question they ask. They are here. They have always been here. The mountain holds them and they hold the mountain, and that is sufficient.

There are perhaps eight hundred of them. Maybe fewer. No one counts. Census is a concept from warmer, flatter places where people accumulate like silt; here, you know every face, every gait, every family's particular way of stacking their hearth-stones. Children are rare — not desperately so, not in crisis, but rare enough that each one is known to the entire city before they can walk. A birth is announced not with fanfare but with a quiet redistribution: someone leaves a bundle of the softest hide at the family's door, someone else brings a pot of the richest, most fat-layered milk, someone carves a small stone rattle and leaves it on the threshold without knocking. The child belongs to the parents in name and to the city in practice. They are raised in courtyards, in the animal-level warmth, passed from lap to lap while adults work hides or chip stone or sort the larvae-harvest from the condensation basins.

They do not have a leader in the way outsiders would recognize. There is no throne, no council chamber, no title passed by blood. What they have is *weight* — a social gravity that accrues to those who have proven, over decades, that their judgment is sound and their hands are skilled. The oldest stone-workers are consulted before a new terrace is begun. The best herders decide when the herds move. The woman who knows the lichen pharmacology — there is always one, it is always a woman, and the knowledge passes from grandmother to granddaughter in a line so old it predates the city's oldest standing walls — she decides when someone is sick enough to warrant the precious salves and poultices. These people are not rulers. They are load-bearing walls. Remove them and the structure wobbles, so everyone works to keep them in place, and they work to be worthy of the holding.

They are, by the standards of any lowland culture, extraordinarily patient people. But this is not passivity. It is the patience of the stone-worker who spends a morning placing a single block, tapping it into alignment with a basalt hammer, feeling the grain, adjusting by fractions of a millimeter, because a block placed wrong in a load-bearing wall will, in a generation, crack, and in two generations, shift, and in three, bring down the room and everyone in it. They know this because they have found the evidence — old collapse-zones in the lower city, bones in the rubble, the ghosts of mistakes made centuries ago by people who rushed. The mountain remembers. The mountain does not forgive errors of haste.

Their emotional register is narrow by outside standards but deep. They do not shout joy or keen grief with the volume of temperate peoples. But in the communal halls, in the evening gatherings, you see it — the way an old man will sit beside a younger one who has lost a child, and say nothing for an hour, and then place a hand on the back of the younger man's neck and hold it there, and that is enough. That is the entire ceremony. Grief is a cold thing; you share body heat. Joy is a quiet thing; you share food. The only loud expression is music, and even that is loud only in relative terms — the singing that carries through the corridors at night is not performed for an audience. It is performed for the stone. The stone resonates. The stone amplifies. A single voice in the right corridor becomes a choir, and the singer stands in the dark and feels the mountain sing back.

They are not ignorant of the world beyond the plateau. Trade exists — the particular dry wood for fire-starting comes from below, carried up by a route so old and so treacherous that the traders who make it are a separate people with their own customs and their own language, and the relationship between the two groups is one of deep mutual dependence and deep mutual incomprehension. The lowland traders bring wood, seeds (which mostly fail in the thin soil), salt, and occasionally metal goods — a knife, a pot — that the upland people regard with the polite interest one shows toward a curiosity. The upland people send down hides, sinew-rope, obsidian blades of extraordinary sharpness, and a thick paste made from fermented animal milk that keeps almost indefinitely and is prized at lower elevations as both food and medicine. The trade happens twice a year, at the equinoxes, at a flat place on the plateau's edge called the Shelf, and it takes three days, and neither group fully trusts the other, and neither group can survive without the other, and this has been the arrangement for longer than either group's oral histories reach.

They age well here, or perhaps they simply age *differently*. The old are not frail. They are *weathered* — their skin takes on the texture of the stone they have worked all their lives, their joints stiffen but do not fail, their eyesight remains sharp because the landscape demands it. A woman of seventy can read the sky for weather with more accuracy than any instrument. A man of eighty still chips obsidian, his hands slower but no less precise, his blades still the best in the city. They die, when they die, mostly in their sleep, in the coldest months, when the body simply decides it has had enough of the altitude and the cold and the thin air, and it lets go. There is a place for this — a ledge on the cliff-face, open to the wind, where the dead are laid on stone slabs and left to the elements and the birds. The bones are collected after a year by the family and incorporated into the walls of the home. Literally. A femur here, a clavicle there, set into the mortarless stone between two basalt blocks. The dead become load-bearing. The dead hold up the city. This is not morbid to them. This is obvious. Of course you would use what is strongest.

## The Herd-Beasts

The people call them *udh* — a word that is also, depending on tone, the word for "wealth," "foundation," and "the thing we cannot do without." They are the center of everything.

They are massive. A full-grown female stands as high as a person's chest and weighs more than two people combined. Their bodies are dense — not fat, though they carry a thick layer of insulating blubber beneath the hide, but *compressed*, as if the mountain's pressure has squeezed them into a more efficient shape. Their hooves are broad, splayed, keratinous plates that grip stone the way a climber's boot grips — with surface area and slight flex. They can ascend and descend the switchback stairways of the city itself, and they do, in long patient processions, guided by herders who walk beside them with a hand on the flank and a voice that is more vibration than sound.

Their fur is extraordinary. It is double-layered: an outer coat of coarse, oily guard-hairs that shed water and resist the wind, and an undercoat so fine and dense that it traps a layer of warm air against the skin like a second atmosphere. When a udh stands still in the wind, you can see the outer coat rippling and the undercoat beneath it perfectly still, a private climate maintained against the world. This undercoat is harvested once a year, in the spring-equinox trade-cycle, by a process the people call "combing" — though it is closer to peeling, the loose undercoat releasing in thick sheets that are rolled into bundles and woven into the heaviest, warmest cloth imaginable. A single garment of udh-undercoat, properly made, will last a lifetime and outlive its wearer.

They are not intelligent in the way that dogs or horses are intelligent. They do not learn tricks or respond to complex commands. But they are *knowing* — they understand the routes of their migration, the rhythms of the condensation, the locations of every spring and every sheltered overhang on the plateau. They move in herds of forty to sixty, and within each herd there is a hierarchy so subtle it took the people generations to read: the lead animal is not the strongest but the one with the best memory for water-sources, and the herd trusts this animal with a totality that borders on reverence. If the lead udh turns, the herd turns. If it stops, they stop. The herders learned long ago not to fight this — they work *with* the lead animal, nudging its choices rather than overriding them, and the best herders can read a udh's intention in the set of its ears a full minute before it moves.

They give birth once every two years, in the deepest cold of winter, in the lowest and most sheltered animal-levels of the city. The calves are born heavy and covered in a dark, wooly coat that lightens as they age. They can stand within an hour and walk within two, because the plateau does not allow weakness a margin. The mortality rate among calves is perhaps one in five — a number the people accept with the same steady pragmatism they bring to everything. The dead calves are not wasted. Their hides are the softest and most prized. Their bones are carved into the finest needles. Nothing is discarded. The mountain gives nothing extra; you use everything.

Their dung is the city's primary fuel. This is not as grim as it sounds — or rather, it stopped being grim generations ago, when the people figured out the optimal drying and compression techniques. The udh's diet of lichen, moss, and algal film produces a dung that, when dried in the wind on the upper-tier racks, burns slow and hot and clean, with a faintly sweet smell that is, to the people of the Teeth, the smell of home. Dung-fuel is stacked in careful pyramids near every hearth, and the management of fuel-supply is one of the city's quiet, continuous, essential occupations — children are set to dung-collecting on the plateau as soon as they can walk the talus safely, and there is no shame in it, no hierarchy of labor. Everyone has carried dung. The elders who now sit by the fire and shape obsidian spent their childhood years with dung-baskets on their backs. It is as fundamental as breathing.

## The Pale Predators

The people call them *skei*, which means, roughly, "the ones who also have a claim." This is not affection. It is not hostility. It is a statement of ecological fact, delivered with the same flat pragmatism that governs everything here: the skei eat udh, and this is a cost, and the cost is managed, and the skei are not going away.

They are long — longer than a person, nose to tail — and low-slung, built for the broken terrain the way a river is built for its bed, flowing over and around obstacles rather than vaulting them. Their fur is pale, almost white, with a faint blue-grey undertone that makes them nearly invisible against the basalt in certain lights. Their limbs are disproportionately long for their body, with flexible spines and wide paw-pads that grip rock. They hunt in packs of three to seven, and their strategy is not pursuit but *positioning* — they find a defile, a narrow passage between terrace-walls, and they wait. The udh herds know the dangerous passages. The skei know the herds know. The whole thing is a chess game played across generations, with each side adapting to the other's last move, and the people watch it with the respect one gives to a process that has been going on longer than they have.

They den in cliff-caves — low, deep openings in the talus that are too cramped for humans to enter comfortably, which is the point. The skei chose their dens for inaccessibility. A litter of three to five cubs is born in the spring, in the dark, and the cubs stay in the den for two months before they emerge, already long and already pale, blinking in the thin light like creatures from another world.

The people do not hunt the skei. There is an old prohibition against it — not a religious taboo, but a practical one, rooted in observation: places where the skei were driven out saw an explosion of udh populations followed by a collapse, as the herds overgrazed and then starved. The skei are a necessary death. They take the weak, the slow, the old, the sick. They keep the herd strong. The people know this the way they know how to read stone: with centuries of accumulated evidence, passed down not as dogma but as data.

Sometimes, on the open plateau, a herder will see a hunt. The skei will have found their passage, and the udh will have sensed it, and the herd will mille in a tightening circle with the calves in the center, and the skei will wait, and the standoff will last hours — a negotiation conducted in silence across a distance of stone. Eventually, always, something breaks: an old udh, a sick one, separates from the group and walks toward the pass, and the skei take it, and the herd moves on, and the herder watches and says nothing, because there is nothing to say. This is the arrangement. This is the cost of the height.

## The Cliff-Birds

*Tekh* — a word that is also the sound they make, a hard percussive syllable that the people can imitate perfectly and do, to call the birds to the drying-racks where insects swarm. The tekh are not domesticated. They are *adjacent*. They have learned that humans generate dung, and dung generates flies, and flies are food, and so the tekh live in the talus near the city's lower levels in flocks of several hundred, and they come when called, and they eat the insects on the drying-racks and in the courtyards, and in exchange the people do not eat them, though they could — the birds are dense-bodied, muscular, heavy enough that when they land on a roof-block you hear the thud. Their feathers are short and stiff, more like scales than plumage, an adaptation to a place where feathers that caught the wind would be a liability. They can fly, but they prefer not to — they hop, scramble, climb, using their beaks as a third grip on the stone. Watching a tekh navigate a vertical wall is watching something that has solved the problem of gravity through sheer bloody-mindedness.

They nest in the crevices of the talus, in scrapes lined with udh-hair and moss, and they lay four eggs, and they guard them with a ferocity that has taught every child in the city to respect the talus slopes during nesting season. The eggs are never taken. This is not prohibition; it simply does not occur to anyone. The eggs become birds. The birds eat the flies. The flies come from the dung. The dung comes from the udh. The udh need the city. The city needs the birds. Break any link and the chain fails.

## The Basin-Flies and Their Larvae

You would not notice them unless you were told. They are small — smaller than the flies that swarm the dung-heaps — and they live their entire adult lives in the microclimate above the condensation-basins, hovering in a thin cloud just above the surface of the collected water, mating, laying eggs, dying. Their eggs sink. Their larvae hatch in the water and grow there, feeding on the algal film that forms on the basin-stones, and after three weeks they are fat, pale, thumb-sized grubs that the people harvest with small scoops of woven sinew.

The larvae are food. Not a delicacy — a staple. They are high in fat and protein and they require no cooking, though they are sometimes heated in pottery bowls with a little udh-milk and a scraping of dried lichen, which produces a porridge of sorts, grey-green and sour and deeply sustaining. Children eat them raw by the handful, popping them between their teeth with the casualness of eating berries. Adults eat them as part of every meal — a handful of larvae, a cup of thick milk, a strip of dried meat, and that is dinner, that has always been dinner, and no one wishes for more because wishing for more is a kind of madness in a place where *enough* is the highest praise you can give a meal.

The flies themselves serve a secondary purpose: they are the tekh's primary food source, and their presence above the basins is what draws the birds into the city, and so the entire system — condensation, algae, larvae, flies, birds — is a single continuous chain of nourishment that begins with the cold stone and the air and nothing else. It is, in its quiet way, a miracle of adaptation, though the people of the Teeth would not use that word. They would say: *this is what works*.

## The Burrowers

In the lower terraces, where the fog is thickest and the thin soil between the stones is deepest, there are colonies of small animals — the people call them *mikh*, a word that means "the ones underground" — that have carved networks of tunnels through the packed earth. They are rodent-like, but no outside zoologist would recognize the lineage: hairless, pink-grey, with oversized forepaws shaped like spades and tiny eyes that are all but vestigial. They navigate by smell and by the vibration of the stone around them. They eat the roots of tundra-grass and the underground portions of moss, and they are, gram for gram, the most calorie-dense creature on the plateau — a product of the constant cold, which forces them to burn energy at a furious rate to maintain body temperature, which makes their flesh rich and hot and dense.

Children catch them. This is one of the first tasks given to young people — not because it is easy (it is not; the mikh are fast underground and their tunnel-networks are labyrinthine) but because it teaches patience, reading of terrain, and the handling of snares. The snares are simple: a loop of sinew on a stiff bone toggle, lowered into a burrow-mouth and held by a long stick. The child waits, sometimes for an hour, feeling through the stick for the vibration of an approaching body, and when the vibration is right they pull, and the toggle catches, and they draw up a wriggling, warm, blind creature that goes into the sack and then into the pot. The best mikh-hunters are always children between eight and twelve — old enough to be patient, young enough to be small enough to lie flat on the stone for long periods without discomfort. By adolescence, most people have moved on to other tasks, but they remember the skill, and in lean years — when the herds are thinned by a skei-surge, when the larvae-harvest is poor — the adults go back to the burrow-mouths with their snares and wait.

## The Lichen-Keeper

She is old. She is the oldest person in the city, though no one knows exactly how old, and she does not know either, because the people of the Teeth do not count years in the way lowlanders do. They count seasons — the movement of the herds, the coming of the trade-goods, the freezing and thawing of the spring-channels — and by this reckoning she has seen more than two hundred cycles, which is impossible by any standard medical understanding, and yet she is here, and she moves through the city with the slow certainty of water finding its way downhill.

She is the keeper of the pharmacology. She knows which lichens, scraped from which facing of which wall, mixed with which udh-fat and which basin-water, will soothe a burn, or break a fever, or ease the coughing sickness that comes in the deep cold. She knows which moss, dried and powdered and packed into a wound, will stop the bleeding and which will draw out infection. She knows the preparation of the larva-poultice — a thick paste of crushed larvae and lichen-ash that, when spread on a bruise, draws the pain out like heat from a stone. None of this is written. None of it has ever been written. It lives in her hands, in her nose, in the memory of textures and smells that she has been accumulating since she was a child learning from her grandmother, who learned from her grandmother, who learned from hers, in a chain so long it has no discernible beginning.

She is not revered in the way a priest is revered. She is relied upon, the way one relies on a wall. She sits in a small room on the mid-levels, surrounded by bundles of dried lichen sorted by species and growing-surface, by pottery jars of rendered fat, by basins of spring-water at various mineral concentrations, and people come to her, and she looks at them, and she smells them — literally, she smells their breath, their skin, their urine if necessary — and she mixes something, and she gives it to them, and mostly they get better. When they don't, she says: *the mountain has decided*. And that is accepted. No one argues with the mountain.

She has no apprentice. This is the quiet, unspoken anxiety of the city. She has tried — she has taken on young women, granddaughters of women she trusted, and taught them the basics — but the full depth of her knowledge requires a lifetime of attention, and she has not found anyone who can hold it all. She is still looking. In the meantime, she works, and the city depends on her, and she knows this, and it is the one weight she carries that she finds heavy.

## The Unnamed Presence

There is something else. The people do not talk about it in the way they do not talk about the wind — it is too constant, too fundamental, too much a part of the texture of existence to require naming or discussion. But it is there.

In the deepest levels of the city — below the animal housing, below the storage, below the old collapse-zones where bones protrude from rubble — there are passages that no one uses. They are not sealed. They are simply... avoided. The stone in these passages is different. It is smoother, as if worn by something larger than a person, and it is warm — noticeably, measurably warmer than the surrounding rock. The condensation does not form here. The stone is dry. In a city defined by moisture, the dry places are the uncanny ones.

Sometimes, in the deep night, when the wind drops and the animals are still and the city holds its breath, there is a sound from below. Not the tectonic groan of the mountain — that is familiar, almost comforting, the mountain clearing its throat. This is different. It is rhythmic. It is slow. It is the sound of something *breathing*, if breathing were done by stone, if stone had lungs the size of terraces, if it inhaled the cold and exhaled the heat that keeps the deep passages dry.

The people hear it. The children ask about it. The adults say: *the mountain breathes*. And the children accept this, because they live on a mountain, and the mountain has weather and animals and condensation and breath, and all of these are facts. The elders, the ones who have been here longest, the ones who have the most seasons in their memory — they know more. They know that the warmth in the deep passages follows a pattern. They know that the pattern matches the rhythm of the condensation above — when the surface weeps most heavily, the deep stone is warmest, and when the surface dries, the deep stone cools. Something below is drawing the moisture down. Something below is exchanging cold for heat, wet for dry, in a cycle so vast and so slow that it encompasses the entire city, the entire plateau, the entire range.

No one goes to look. Not because they are forbidden, but because they understand, in the way they understand everything here — through observation, through patience, through the accumulated wisdom of generations who watched and waited and drew conclusions — that the thing below is not a threat. It is infrastructure. It is the deepest foundation. The city is built on stone that breathes, and the stone breathes because something within it is alive, and the something is what makes the condensation possible, and the condensation is what makes the city possible, and so the chain goes all the way down, past the bones of the dead and the dung of the animals and the larvae in the basins and the lichens on the walls, all the way down to the warm dry dark where something ancient and slow and unnameable breathes the mountain into being, one exhalation at a time.

The people of the Teeth do not worship this thing. They do not pray to it or offer it sacrifice. They simply live above it, carefully, patiently, with the same attention they bring to everything — and they listen, sometimes, in the deep night, for the sound of its breathing, and when they hear it they are reassured, because it means the mountain is still alive, and the condensation will come in the morning, and the basins will fill, and the larvae will grow, and the birds will come, and the udh will graze, and the city will continue, one block, one breath, one slow patient season at a time.
