# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that the creatures here do not *occupy* the caldera. They are not residents who have moved in. They are the caldera, expressed as motion and hunger.

## The Foundation

Start at the bottom, because everything else depends on what churns in the peat. The black, saturated ground is alive in a way that surface soil rarely is. It is warm—barely, but perceptibly so—fed by the deep geothermal memory of the mountain's old throat. Beneath the topmost layer, which slurps and yields underfoot, there is a stratum of fungal mycelium so dense it forms a continuous, pallid membrane, woven through the peat like a subterranean skin. This is the true ground. The peat merely clings to it.

Worms live here. Not the simple, familiar earthworms of garden soil, but something adapted to an eternity of darkness and saturation. They are large—some as long as a forearm—and they move through the peat in slow, hydraulic undulations, eating the mycelial membrane from one end and redepositing it, altered and enmeshed with their own castings, from the other. They are pale, not white but a translucent pinkish-grey, and if you held one to your face you could see the dim outline of its gut, processing. They have no eyes, no real need for sensation beyond chemoreception and pressure. They feel the peat compress around them and know where the most concentrated nutrients lie. They are the slow pistons of this place, turning the soil over on a timescale that makes seasons meaningless. A single worm might move three inches in a day. A generation might take decades. They are the patient, blind architects of everything that grows above them.

Nematodes are everywhere in the saturated layers—too small to see individually but so numerous that the peat is, in some sense, more nematode than soil. They graze on the hyphae, on each other, on the gelatinous coatings that bacteria lay over every surface of every particle. In a place where predation is the default state of being, even the water itself is threaded with tiny predators.

And above the worms, in the waterlogged humus, live the salamanders.

## The Salamanders

They are the largest animals in the caldera, and they are, in their way, the lords of it—though they would not recognize the concept.

There are three species, though "species" is a clumsy word for what they are. They are more like three tendencies, three solutions to the same problem of living in permanent darkness, permanent wet, permanent cold. The smallest are the ones you see first: eyeless, slick, no longer than a finger, pale as the flesh of a mushroom. They live in the topmost centimeters of peat and emerge when the humidity climbs high enough that the boundary between air and water becomes meaningless. They do not swim so much as *ooze* across the surface of the moss, following chemical gradients toward fungal fruiting bodies or the tiny invertebrates that graze on them. Their mouths are small, round, and surprisingly strong—a ring of muscle that creates a gentle, continuous suction, vacuuming mites and springtails from damp surfaces. They eat constantly. Their metabolisms are so slow that they cannot afford to miss a meal, and so they simply do not stop eating, moving through the moss in a continuous, gliding graze. If you watched one for an hour, you might see it cover six inches. If you watched it for a week, you might see it cover six feet. But it would never leave the patch of moss it was born on. It has no need to.

The second species is larger—a hand's length—and darker, a dusky charcoal that absorbs the faint light completely. These are the ones who hunt. They live in the hollows between root buttresses and in the cups of water that collect where branches meet trunks. They are ambush predators, patient to the point of catatonia, and they feed on the first species of salamander, on the larger insects, on anything that comes close enough to register on the sensory pits along their jaw. These pits detect heat—barely, the faintest metabolic warmth of a moving body against the cool background of stone and water. When something passes within range, the salamander strikes with a lunge that is startlingly fast, a cobra's quickness in a body that had been utterly still for hours. The strike is silent. The consumption is slow. It can take twenty minutes for a large salamander to work a smaller one down its throat, and during this process both animals remain still, one dying and one eating, and the moss continues to grow around them.

The third species is the rarest and the strangest. It is the largest—as long as a man's forearm, thick as a wrist—and it lives exclusively inside the hollow trunks of the dead conifers, in the absolute dark where not even the phosphorescent fungi glow. It is the color of wet wood. It has vestigial eyes beneath a membrane of smooth skin, and its sensory pits are so highly developed that it can detect the vibrations of a spider walking on the outside of its trunk. It feeds on everything that enters its domain: beetles, larvae, the first and second salamander species if they wander too deep into a hollow. But it also does something that the others do not. It cultivates. In the warm, still air of its hollow, it maintains a garden of fungi on the dead wood, licking the growing surfaces with a broad, flat tongue that carries the spores of beneficial species and suppresses those that would compete. It does this unconsciously, or perhaps with a kind of purpose so alien that consciousness is the wrong word. Its tongue is a tool, and its hollow is a farm, and it has been doing this for longer than any individual's lifetime. The behavior is genetic. It is written in the marrow.

## The Arthropods

The skittering.

This is the sound of the caldera's true population—the arthropods, which are not one thing but an endless, particulate multitude. They fill every niche that the salamanders do not, and many that the salamanders could not reach.

The **harvestmen** are the most visible. They are long-legged, dark-bodied, and they move in aggregations of dozens across the moss and fungal shelves, their legs a blur of coordinated motion that makes the group look like a single, many-limbed organism sweeping across the substrate. They are detritivores and opportunists, eating dead insects, fungal tissue, the eggs of smaller arthropods. They do not spin webs. They do not need to. In a place this crowded, food arrives by proximity alone.

The **beetles** are diverse and specialized to a degree that would delight and unsettle an entomologist in equal measure. There are beetles that eat only the spores of one species of fungus. There are beetles whose larvae live exclusively inside the fiddleheads of ferns, consuming the tightly coiled tissue from within and emerging, when the frond unfurls, into a world they did not know existed. There are predatory beetles with elongated mandibles that function as forceps, plucking springtails from the air mid-jump. There are beetles that have lost the ability to fly and even to walk quickly—they simply sit on a fungal shelf and let the food come to them, feeding on the microfauna that teems in the moisture film on every surface. They are small, brown, and shapeless, and they could be mistaken for droppings or fungal nodules. That is the point.

The **spiders** are here, of course. You cannot have this many insects without spiders. But they are not the orb-weavers or the jumping spiders of sunlit places. They are sheet-weavers and tunnel-builders, constructing dense, mat-like webs in the spaces between moss clumps and beneath fungal shelves. Their webs are not sticky in the usual sense—they are saturated with moisture, and the adhesion comes from surface tension. An insect that walks onto the web becomes trapped not by glue but by the water itself, its tiny legs puncturing the film and the surrounding moisture closing over them like a hand. The spiders sense the vibrations through the wet silk and come to feed. Some of the larger sheet-webs span the gaps between trunks, invisible in the dim light, and harvestmen occasionally blunder into them. The spiders that build these larger webs are themselves large—legspans of two inches, dark-bodied, with a single reflective point on each of their eight eyes. They sit at the edge of the web, motionless, and when a harvestman breaks through, the spider does not rush. It approaches with a slow, deliberate grace, testing each thread, reading the web's story through its feet. Then it bites, once, and waits.

There is one spider in the caldera that does not build webs at all. It lives on the third salamander, in the hollow trunk, and it feeds on the mites and beetles that infest the salamander's skin. It is tiny—no larger than a grain of rice—and it has evolved to tolerate the salamander's mucus, to walk across the slick surface of its host without slipping, to find its way by touch through the corrugations of the salamander's sensory pits. The salamander tolerates it, perhaps benefits from it. The spider cleans what the tongue cannot reach. It is a mutualism so old that neither party recognizes it as cooperation. The spider thinks of the salamander as geography. The salamander thinks of the spider as nothing at all.

## The Invertebrate Tide

The **springtails**—the "tiny, hopping things with reflective eyes"—deserve more than a passing mention, because they are the caldera's circulatory system. There are more of them than anything else. They are the size of pinheads to sesame seeds, and they move through the moss and leaf litter in vast, grazing waves, consuming fungal hyphae and decomposing plant matter with a ceaseless, mechanical efficiency. Their reflective eyes are not true eyes but simple ocelli that detect changes in light intensity, and in this perpetual twilight they serve mainly to distinguish shadow from deeper shadow, predator from substrate. Their hopping—which gives them their name—is an escape mechanism, a sudden coiling and release of a forked appendage beneath the abdomen that launches them into the air in a random direction. In the caldera, where the air is thick and still, this escape works often enough to sustain their numbers but not so often that the predators who hunt them go hungry. They are the middle of the food web, the connective tissue between the fungal foundation and everything that moves with purpose. A single square meter of moss in this caldera might contain fifty thousand springtails. They are not individuals. They are weather.

The **mites** are their constant companions and competitors—different in body plan, similar in function. Some are grazers. Some are predators of other mites. Some are parasitic, attaching to the salamanders, to the spiders, to the harvestmen, drawing tiny amounts of fluid through the exoskeleton or skin. They are so small and so numerous that they constitute a layer of the ecosystem in themselves, a living dust that coats every surface and enters every crevice. The caldera's air, thick with moisture, is also thick with mites—drifting on the slow, almost imperceptible convection currents that move through the basin. They are the plankton of this dark, wet ocean.

And there are the **diplurans**—elongate, eyeless, white, looking like something between a silverfish and a tiny centipede. They live deeper in the peat than the springtails, in the zone where fungal hyphae are densest, and they feed on the hyphae and on each other. They are ancient, these creatures, belonging to an order that predates most of what crawls and hops above them. They have been here, or somewhere like here, since before the conifers, since before the ferns, since the mountain itself was young and hot and the caldera was a fresh wound in the rock. They have outlasted epochs by requiring nothing that epochs cannot provide: darkness, moisture, decay.

## The Fungal Kingdom

They are not fauna, but they *act*—and in this place, the line between animal and fungus is thinner than you might think.

The **phosphorescent fungi**—the ones that cast that startling blue glow on the wet surfaces—are not a single species but a guild, a loose coalition of genera that have converged on bioluminescence for reasons that are not entirely clear. The light is faint—a human eye, adjusted to the dark, can perceive it as a soft, blue-white radiance on the surfaces of decaying wood and on the caps of fruiting bodies that emerge from the moss. It is cold light, produced by a luciferin reaction that generates almost no heat. The function is debated even in sunlit forests; here, in the absolute dark, it may serve to attract the insects that disperse spores, or it may be an incidental metabolic byproduct, a chemical inevitability that evolution has neither selected for nor against. Whatever its purpose, the effect is ethereal. In the deepest hollows, where the third salamander lives and no light has touched for centuries, the fungi glow in patches and streaks, mapping the decay of dead wood in blue-white constellations. A beetle that wanders into this light is following a trail it did not know existed, drawn toward the fungal surface where it will become coated in spores and carry them, unwittingly, to the next dead trunk.

The **cordyceps** are here too. They have to be. In a population this dense, this stable, parasitic fungi are inevitable. There are species that infect the beetles, species that infect the spiders, species that infect the springtails with such specificity that a given cordyceps can only colonize one genus of one family. The infected arthropod does not climb, as in warmer forests—it has no height to climb to. Instead, it burrows. The fungus drives its host downward, into the peat, into the warmest and most humid layer, where the conditions for sporulation are optimal. There the arthropod dies, and the fungus erupts from its body in a pale stalk that pushes up through the peat and into the moss, releasing its spores into the still air. The caldera floor is littered with these stalks—thin, white, rising from the moss like the fingers of something buried. They are everywhere. They are a harvest.

There is a fungal network here—a mycorrhizal web—that connects the roots of every tree, every fern, every moss colony in a single, vast, underground communication system. It carries nutrients from decaying matter to living roots. It carries chemical signals between plants, warnings of beetle infestations or fungal infection. It may, in some sense that we do not yet understand, coordinate the growth patterns of the entire caldera, ensuring that no single species outcompetes the others, that the equilibrium is maintained. If the caldera is a single organism—and in many ways it is—then this mycorrhizal network is its nervous system, and the fungi are its neurons, and the slow chemical pulses that travel through the mycelial web are its thoughts. They are slow thoughts. They think in seasons. They have been thinking for millennia.

## The Birds

There are none.

No, that is not quite right. There are no *adult* birds. But once, and only once within living memory—which in this place means the lifespan of the oldest conifer, perhaps eight hundred years—a bird entered the caldera. It was a storm-petrel, blown far off course by a cyclonic system that scraped the upper atmosphere. It fell into the caldera through the cloud layer, exhausted, its feathers soaked, its salt-gland working overtime in this fresh, mineral-poor water. It landed on the peat and could not take off again. There is not enough room in the caldera for a bird to build the airspeed needed to escape its gravity. The storm-petrel walked, and then crawled, and then sat still, and the mites found it, and then the harvestmen, and then the larger beetles, and then the second salamander, which had never tasted anything so rich in protein. Within a week, the bird was a skeleton clothed in fungal hyphae, and within a year, it was a stain in the peat, a concentration of phosphorus that caused a small bloom of phosphorescent fungi on the surface above it.

The caldera does not reject intruders. It *processes* them.

## The Microfauna

Beneath and between and within all of this are the rotifers, the tardigrades, the protozoans—life so small that it inhabits not the caldera but the *films of water* that coat every surface. Each droplet on each leaf, each bead of condensation on each spider's web, is a world. The rotifers spin their corona of cilia and draw in bacteria and algae. The tardigrades—those indestructible, eight-legged water bears—crawl through the moisture film on moss stems, eating whatever they encounter, capable of entering cryptobiosis if the moisture ever recedes, which it never does. The protozoans pulse and divide and consume, an invisible ferment that processes the organic molecules shed by every larger organism into forms that bacteria can then break down further into forms that fungi can use to feed the mycorrhizal web that feeds the ferns that feed the beetles that feed the salamanders that die and feed the fungi.

Everything in the caldera is feeding on everything else, or being fed upon, or being fed *by*—the distinction collapses under the weight of interconnection. Remove any one species and the others would adjust, because the niches are so tightly packed that any vacancy is filled within a generation. But nothing has been removed. Nothing has been added. The caldera is a closed loop, a circulatory system with no heart, pumping itself through itself, forever.

## The Absence

What does *not* live here is as telling as what does.

There are no mammals. The altitude is too high, the caldera too enclosed, the food web too specialized for the metabolic demands of warm-blooded life. A mouse, introduced here, would starve—not for want of food, but because the food is spread too thin, too slow, too deeply embedded in a web that has no room for a quick, warm, inefficient body. The caldera would process the mouse. It would become peat.

There are no amphibians other than the salamanders—no frogs, no toads, whose calls would shatter the silence and whose tadpoles would need open water, of which there is none. The water here is never pooled, never still in a way that a frog would recognize. It is always moving, always dripping, always condensing, always being absorbed. It is less a body of water than a condition of water, a state of being wet that pervades everything.

There are no reptiles. It is too cold, even with the faint geothermal warmth. A snake here would slow to a stop within hours, its muscles seizing in the chill, and the beetles would find it and begin.

There are no social structures, no hierarchies, no territories in any sense that implies defense. The salamanders do not fight for mates or food. They simply encounter each other, or they do not. The harvestmen aggregate not for cooperation but because the resources are clustered, and where resources cluster, so do the things that eat them. The spiders defend their webs, but the webs are rebuilt within hours of destruction, and the defense is reflexive, not strategic. There is no planning here, no foresight, no memory that extends beyond the chemical signals left in the moisture film. The caldera does not remember. It does not need to. It is not going anywhere.

## The Equilibrium

You stand in this place, and the moisture condenses on your skin, and the mites begin to explore the foreign landscape of your body, and the temperature of your breath adds a faint warmth to the air that the caldera absorbs without comment. You are not an intruder, not exactly. You are a perturbation, a local anomaly, and the caldera will correct for you. If you stood here long enough—if you stood here for years—the peat would rise around your ankles, and the moss would climb your legs, and the fungi would explore the dead cells of your skin, and the beetles would find the moisture in the folds of your clothing, and the salamanders would approach your cooling feet with their sensory pits, tasting the strange, warm chemistry of you. You would be, eventually, incorporated. Not consumed—consumption implies urgency—but *integrated*, slowly, into the web.

This is what lives here. Not creatures, not plants, not fungi, but a *process*—a single, self-sustaining act of being alive that has found a form so stable, so perfectly adapted to its tiny, dark, wet, high room, that it has essentially stopped evolving. There is nothing to adapt to. There is no change to respond to. The caldera reached equilibrium centuries ago, or millennia, or perhaps it has been in equilibrium since the first spore settled in the first crack of the cooled basalt, and everything since has been elaboration, decoration, the slow unfolding of a solution that was complete from the start.

It breathes. It eats. It reproduces. It dies. It does not think. It does not need to. It is enough.
