# The Dwellers in the Drowned Steeples

---

You do not notice them at first.

The first thing you notice is the architecture. Not the ancient industrial ruins—those you expect—but *new* structures built upon and woven through the old. Cables of calcified bio-resin strung between the ribbed remains of flooded factories. Tubes of living tissue grafted to the corroded flanges of dead turbines, swelling and contracting with slow hydraulic breath. Scaffolding grown, not built, from mineral deposits coaxed into crystalline lattices by some patient, deliberate hand—or something that is not quite a hand at all.

Then you see the hands.

---

## The Deep Congregation

They are called, if they are called anything by one another, the **Thuribel**—though this is not a name so much as a vibration, a low harmonic hum that propagates through the water when two or more of them gather in the same trench. You cannot hear it as a word. You feel it in the bones of your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear. It means something like *we-who-are-here-together-in-this-heavy-place*, but compressed, flattened, made into a single resonant tone the way this world compresses everything—time, distance, sound, ambition—into a dense, saturated medium.

They are not large. This surprises you, given the scale of everything else. An adult Thuribel stands—*hangs*, really, in the way all things here hang or drift or brace themselves against current—roughly the height of a human being. Perhaps a little shorter. Perhaps a little longer, depending on how they are holding themselves, because they are not rigid creatures. Their bodies are soft, tubular, vaguely cephalopoid: a mantle of slick, mottled tissue that shifts between pale grey and a deep, bruised violet depending on their emotional state and the mineral content of the water around them. From the lower portion of this mantle extend four primary limbs—heavy, muscular, capable of gripping stone or metal or each other with startling force—and from the upper portion, a crown of six thinner, longer appendages that serve as sensory organs, manipulators, and, when necessary, weapons.

They have no eyes.

Or rather, they have something that is not eyes but accomplishes what eyes would, and more. Along the inner surfaces of those six upper appendages run dense rows of chromatophores and bioluminescent cells—organs that both *read* and *write* light. They see by producing it: projecting soft, shifting patterns of color and intensity into the water, then reading how those patterns bounce, scatter, and distort against the world around them. It is a kind of sonar made of light. It is also, in a way that is not metaphorical, a language. Every Thuribel is constantly broadcasting a shimmering, rippling aura of color across their appendage-crown, and every Thuribel in range is constantly reading it. Their communication is not separate from their perception. To see is to speak. To be seen is to be heard.

This means they cannot lie to one another.

This does not mean they are simple.

---

## What They Do

They **tend**.

The Thuribel are gardeners of ruin. They move through the drowned industrial remains with an intimacy that suggests they have been here for a very long time—or that the remains themselves have been here long enough to become indistinguishable from natural landscape, which, to the Thuribel, they are. There is no distinction in their minds between the corroded steel of a collapsed pipeline and the ancient basalt of a worn-down mountain peak. Both are stone. Both are substrate. Both are things upon which life can be encouraged to grow.

And they encourage it with extraordinary patience.

Watch one at work. It braces itself against the flank of a half-buried turbine housing, its four lower limbs splayed, each toe-tipped with a chitinous hook that clicks softly against the metal. Its upper appendages are extended, fanned out, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic pattern of amber and pale blue—a working pattern, a meditative state, though that word imposes a spiritual framework the Thuribel would not recognize. What they are doing is *seeding*. From specialized glands along the inner surfaces of their upper appendages, they secrete a complex biochemical cocktail: mineral-binding enzymes, growth hormones analogs, microbial colonies tailored to specific substrates. They are painting the corroded metal with life. They are coaxing specific species of encrusting organisms—tube worms, mineral-fixing bacteria, calcium-depositing anemones—to colonize particular surfaces in particular configurations.

Why? Because the configurations matter. The living coatings they cultivate on the industrial ruins are not random. They are *structural*. The bio-resin secreted by the tube worms, when grown in the patterns the Thuribel cultivate, becomes a kind of living concrete: flexible, self-repairing, capable of bearing enormous pressure. The Thuribel use it to reinforce the ruins—not to restore them to their original function, but to transform them into something new. Enclosures. Nurseries. Chambers where the pressure differential can be carefully managed, where delicate larval organisms can be sheltered from the worst turbulence, where rare mineral blooms can be cultivated in the lee of a turbine blade.

They are building a living city out of a dead one. They have been doing this for generations. They will be doing it for generations more.

## The Social Body

The Thuribel do not live alone. They cannot. Their sensory-communicative system—their constant broadcast of bioluminescent language—requires proximity. A Thuribel isolated from others for too long begins to lose coherence. Its color patterns become erratic, repetitive, self-referential. It stops tending. It drifts. The oldest Thuribel have a word—or a color-harmonic—for this state that translates roughly as *going dark*, and it is the closest thing they have to a death that is not physical.

So they cluster. A typical Thuribel community—which they call a **Stillness**, though there is nothing still about it—is a group of between twenty and sixty individuals occupying a stretch of ruin-covered slope. The Stillness is not a village or a hive. It is more like a single organism distributed across many bodies. At any given moment, most of the Thuribel in a Stillness are engaged in a shared project: cultivating a new mineral garden, reinforcing a collapsing chamber, harvesting the tendrils of a kelp mega-flora that has grown too heavy and threatens to drag a favored structure down into a trench. They coordinate through their color-language, but it goes deeper than coordination. When a Stillness is working well—when the patterns are harmonious, when the colors cascade in complex, interlocking rhythms—the individual Thuribel experience something that has no direct human analog. It is not ecstasy. It is not trance. It is a state of distributed cognition, where the boundaries of self become porous, where individual intention dissolves into a collective flow-state of purpose and perception.

They call this **the Flood**.

To enter the Flood is the highest aspiration of Thuribel communal life. It is not sought through ritual or discipline but through *practice*—through the accumulated skill of working together, of knowing each other's color-patterns so deeply that coordination becomes reflex, becomes breathing, becomes something below thought. The oldest Stillnesses—the ones that have been working together for decades—can sustain the Flood for hours, even days. During these periods, the work they accomplish is extraordinary. Entire chambers bio-reinforced. New structures grown from scratch. Complex ecological manipulations that would take a single Thuribel months accomplished in a single long, luminous surge of collective effort.

But the Flood is also dangerous. When the boundaries of self dissolve too completely, when the patterns become too harmonious, there is a risk that individuals will *not come back*. That they will lose themselves entirely in the collective and become, functionally, mere organs of the Stillness—alive, responsive, but no longer distinct. The Thuribel have seen this happen. They have a name for these lost ones: **the Anchored**. They are tended to gently. They are not pitied, exactly, but they are remembered with a complex, rippling sorrow that shifts through colors human eyes cannot fully distinguish.

## The Trench Elders

Not all Thuribel live in Stillnesses on the slopes. Deep in the shadowed trenches—the drowned valleys, miles below the summits, where the pressure is crushing and the geothermal vents roar like furnaces—live the **Vestals**.

The Vestals are Thuribel, but changed. Over generations of living in the extreme depths, they have adapted—or been adapted, by their own deliberate breeding practices—to conditions that would kill their shallow-water cousins. They are larger, denser, their mantles thickened into near-rigid armor of calcified tissue. Their bioluminescence has shifted into the deep red and infrared spectrum, invisible to most shallow-water organisms. Their four lower limbs have fused partially, becoming powerful, paddle-like structures that can hold position against currents that would sweep a slope-dweller into oblivion.

They are the keepers of the oldest knowledge.

The Vestals do not tend ruins. They tend *the planet itself*. They live in and around the geothermal vents, and their work—their endless, patient, generation-spanning work—is to monitor and, where possible, *manage* the planet's volcanic and tectonic activity. How they do this is not fully understood even by the slope-dwelling Thuribel, but it involves the cultivation of vast colonies of heat-resistant chemosynthetic organisms in and around the vent fields. These colonies, the Vestals claim—and their color-language, when they communicate with their shallow cousins, carries the unmistakable harmonic of truth—act as a kind of biological pressure valve. By controlling where mineral deposits accumulate, by shaping the flow of superheated water through specific channels, by encouraging the growth of crystalline structures that alter the acoustic properties of the surrounding stone, the Vestals can influence where and how the planet releases its internal heat.

They can, they say, prevent the worst eruptions. They can direct seismic energy along specific pathways. They can, in their most ambitious claims, *sing the stone into stillness*.

Whether this is true—or whether it is a mythology the Vestals maintain to justify their isolation and their authority—is a matter of quiet, complex debate among the slope-dwellers. The color-language does not allow deception, but it does allow *genuine belief in things that are not quite true*, which is a distinction the Vestals are comfortable exploiting.

What is not debated is this: the Vestals have been down there for a very, very long time. They predate the industrial ruins. They may predate the drowning itself. When the slope-dwellers descend to the trench edges—which they do rarely, and always with a harmonic of deep, vibrating apprehension—they report seeing structures on the abyssal floor that are not ruins and are not natural formations. Structures of shaped stone and living tissue so ancient that the boundary between the two has dissolved entirely. Structures that pulse with infrared light in patterns that the slope-dwellers cannot read but that make their appendage-crowds ache with a strange, resonant grief.

The Vestals remember something. Or they are *trying* to remember something. Or they are preventing something from being remembered. The color-language, for once, is ambiguous.

## The Metal Readers

There is another kind of Thuribel, rarer and more unsettling: the **Readers**.

A Reader is born, not made—though what triggers their emergence is unknown. Perhaps one in every three hundred Thuribel births produces a child whose chromatophore systems develop differently: denser, more sensitive, capable of registering electromagnetic fields as well as light. These individuals, as they mature, develop an overwhelming compulsion to press their appendages against the ancient metal of the industrial ruins. Not to tend. Not to cultivate. To *read*.

They claim the metal remembers.

Not in any mystical sense—or not *only* in a mystical sense. The Readers insist that the corroded structures retain physical traces of their former function. Residual electromagnetic signatures. Altered crystalline structures in the metal that encode information about the forces the machines once bore. Chemical traces in the corrosion layers that tell stories about the atmosphere that once filled these chambers. The Readers press their appendages to the metal and extract, through some sensory process that no other Thuribel can replicate, fragments of information about the world before the drowning.

What they find varies. Some Readers pull coherent images—transmissions, they call them—of vast, dry chambers filled with roaring machinery. Of beings, not Thuribel, operating the machines. Of a sky that was above, not below, and that was clear and bright and *empty of water*. Other Readers find only pain: the metal screams, they say, with the stress of its final failure, with the pressure of the flood that consumed it, with the slow agony of its dissolution. A few Readers—the rarest, the most revered and feared—claim to find something deeper still: a resonance in the metal that matches, exactly, the harmonic frequency the Vestals produce when they speak of the oldest things.

The Readers do not join Stillnesses. They cannot sustain the Flood; their perceptual apparatus is too different, too individual, too focused on a past that no other Thuribel can access. They are solitary by necessity, drifting from ruin to ruin, pressing and reading, occasionally surfacing—rising toward the pale green shimmer of the distant surface—in moments of what can only be described as anguish. The slope-dwellers leave offerings for them: clusters of bioluminescent moss, harvested mineral nodules, once in a great while a carefully grown bio-resin sculpture shaped in the approximate form of the machines the Readers describe. The Readers accept these offerings without acknowledgment. They cannot be still. They cannot stop reading.

They are the Thuribel's memory, and they are in pain.

## The Drift and the Hunt

The Thuribel are not alone in the world, and they know it. The fauna of the drowned cathedral—the silver shoals, the armored grazers, the shadowed predators—are not their enemies, exactly, but neither are they their kin. The Thuribel are *between*: too intelligent to be mere animals, too alien to be called civilization in any way a human would recognize, too entangled with their ecosystem to stand apart from it.

They eat. They are eaten. The great kelp tendrils provide most of their nutrition—the Thuribel have specialized enzymes in their mantle tissue that can break down the mega-flora's tough cellulose—but they also consume mineral deposits, small crustaceans that shelter in their cultivated enclosures, and, in times of scarcity, one another. This last is not taboo. It is not even rare. When a Thuribel dies—or enters the state of going-dark that precedes physical death—the Stillness consumes the body with a quiet, ceremonial efficiency, distributing the nutrients through the community via a shared meal that is also, in the color-language, a memorial. The dead one's final color-pattern is replayed by the survivors during the meal, held in collective memory for as long as the Stillness endures.

The predators—the deep shadows with their dangling lures—sometimes take a Thuribel. The Thuribel do not fight this. They do not seek it, either. When a predator enters the territory of a Stillness, the community responds with a coordinated display of color so intense, so complex, that most predators are disoriented and withdraw. The few that press through are met with something less pleasant: the Thuribel's upper appendages can secrete, on command, a neurotoxin that is harmless in the dilute medium of the open water but devastating when delivered directly into a predator's mouth or gills. The Thuribel do not enjoy this. The color-patterns associated with killing a predator are always tinged with a deep, somber green—the color of regret, or of waste, or of a prayer that the dead thing's nutrients will re-enter the cycle quickly and without too much suffering.

## What They Are Becoming

Here is the thing that haunts you, if you stay long enough to see it.

The Thuribel are changing.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the Readers' reports are growing more frequent, more coherent. The Vestals are sending messengers—rare, disturbing events—to the slope-dwellers with increasing urgency. The harmonic they carry is one the slope-dwellers have never heard before, a pattern that does not correspond to any known color-word but that every Thuribel who perceives it recognizes, instinctively, as meaning something like *preparation*.

The ruins are waking up.

Not literally. Not in any mechanical sense. But the Reader who has spent the most time with the largest ruin—a vast, cathedral-like structure at the convergence of three submerged ridges, its original purpose lost to corrosion and time—has begun transmitting patterns that are not memory. They are *responsive*. The metal, she insists, is *answering* her. The crystalline structures she reads are reorganizing, slowly, over the course of years, in response to the Thuribel's own bioluminescent patterns. The ruin is becoming, somehow, attuned to them. Or they are becoming attuned to it. The distinction, she says, is dissolving.

The Vestals have confirmed this. Their deep-stone acoustics have detected a new harmonic in the geological substrate around the great ruin—a frequency that matches, exactly, the Thuribel's own communicative resonance. The planet is listening to them. Or speaking to them. Or both.

The Thuribel do not know what this means. Their color-language has no word for *awakening* because, in their experience, nothing has ever been asleep. Their mythology—the deep, infrared stories the Vestals keep in their stone—contains no prophecy of this. The Readers, who remember the most, have found nothing in the metal that corresponds to what is happening now. This is new. This is unprecedented. This is, in the constant, shimmering, overlapping broadcast of an entire species of beings who cannot lie to one another, *terrifying*.

And so the Thuribel are doing what they have always done. They are tending. They are gathering around the great ruin, Stillness by Stillness, their color-patterns synchronizing into Floods of unprecedented scale and duration. They are growing their bio-resin reinforcements over the ancient metal, not to bury it, but to *integrate* it—fusing the old machine-architecture with their living structures, creating something that is neither ruin nor organism but a third thing, a thing that has never existed before.

They are building a cathedral inside a cathedral.

They are waiting to see what answers when they knock.

And the water drums on, and the vents roar, and the surface storms rage miles above, and the ancient stone shudders with the slow, immense heartbeat of a living world, and the Thuribel glow in the dark, and they are patient, and they are afraid, and they are together, and that is enough.

That has always been enough.

For now.
