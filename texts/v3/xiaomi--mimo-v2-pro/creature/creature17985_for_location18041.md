They are the Damp-Keepers, the Condensation-Handlers, the Plateau’s Own. They do not have a name for themselves that translates, but if forced, they might point to the slow drip of water from a conifer needle and make a sound that is half sigh, half hiss—the sound of steam meeting cold stone. That is what they are.

They are not tall, but they are long. Their limbs have the same stretched, jointed quality as the gaunt predators that move between the pines, though they are softer, wrapped in layers of oilskin and felted moss-fiber that bead with moisture. Their skin is the color of wet slate, and their eyes are large, dark, and lidless, filmed with a nictitating membrane that wipes clear with a soft click, like a camera shutter. They see primarily in gradients of grey, green, and the dull red of geothermal heat. The sun is a forgotten myth; their sky is the fog.

They live in the cliff-faces, in the cave-mouths glowing with amber lantern-light. Their homes are humid, warm hollows where the air tastes of metal and hot stone. The walls sweat, and they do not mind. Furniture is minimal—low platforms of smooth, water-worn rock, shelves carved into the living stone. Their tools *are* grown: handles of a pale, dense fungus cultivated in the dark, heads of sharpened flint or bizarrely shaped brass pieces salvaged and re-smelted a thousand times. They fit the hand not because they were designed to, but because the hand, over generations, has learned to grow around them.

Their life is the hum and the clang. From birth, they are apprenticed to a **Vent**. It is not merely a source of power; it is a living, capricious relative. Some Vents are steady and true, hissing a consistent plume. Others are temperamental, gushing violently or falling silent for days. The Damp-Keepers read the sounds of the pipes, the temperature of the stone, the taste of the escaping gases. They are **Engineers of Sighs**, **Listeners-to-the-Deep**. The great hammer-press is their cathedral bell, its rhythm the tempo of their collective breath. They don’t speak much; a gesture toward a valve, a low hum that harmonizes with a particular gear’s grind, communicates complex instructions.

Their primary harvest is not metal or gem, but **Distillate**. The vast cisterns are their fields. Here, the **Harvesters** work, the most silent of all. They are often the gaunt, long-limbed ones seen flitting through the trees, but now you see them up close. They move with preternatural stillness, their long fingers skimming the surface of the dark water, feeling for the subtle vibrations of the blind, translucent eels. The eels are not eaten, but cultivated. They filter the captured condensate, and their shed skins, when processed in alchemical steam-vats, yield a iridescent, oil-slick fluid that waterproofs everything and has a faint, pleasing narcotic effect when inhaled. The Harvesters are often seen with small, porous clay pots at their belts, breathing in the fumes, their large eyes half-closed in a state of serene alertness.

The **Weavers-of-the-Green** tend the moss. They are not farmers, but curators. They coax it to grow over new structures, to swallow unsightly scars in the cliff. They know the recipes for the luminous varieties, blending phosphorescent minerals into the substrate. At night, they are the ones who trace slow, geometric patterns on the ground—a map, a prayer, a tuning of the land’s energy? It is unclear. The ghost-pale lichen garlands are their ceremonial vestments, worn only during the deep-night hours when the plateau’s hum changes key.

The strangeness is in their synchronicity. They do not startle at the groan of shifting rock; they sway with it, a micro-adjustment in their posture. They can hold their breath for a unnervingly long time, a trait useful when a steam vent gushes unpredictably. Children play a silent game of tag, moving between the phosphorescent moss-lines with the same fluid, jointed grace as the predatory creatures, who are neither pets nor pests, but simply other expressions of the plateau’s quiet, relentless life.

They are not mining the plateau. They are its circulatory system, its nervous system. They are the conscious, willful part of the ancient, weeping land, tending the engines that keep its slow, geothermal heart beating, forever harvesting the moisture of its breath, and living in the cool, damp, resonant quiet of its vast and settled mind.
