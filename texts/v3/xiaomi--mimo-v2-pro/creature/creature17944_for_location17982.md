# The Inhabitants of the Eternal Floor

---

## The Grazers

The smallest and most numerous are what you'd first mistake for debris — **tickbeetles**, the hand-sized creatures the initial description captured. But stay still long enough and their taxonomy opens up like a wound.

There are at least nine distinct morphologies of tickbeetle, though whether these are species or merely functional castes is a question that only matters if you believe something here is still *deciding*. The most common are the **scrapers**: squat, oval, with four legs splayed wide for stability and two longer forelimbs tipped with oscillating ceramic rasps. Their faceted dorsal plates are concave, like satellite dishes, pulling in the ambient phosphorescence and focusing it downward onto whatever surface they're working. They don't see, exactly. They *illuminate* — boring a tight cone of concentrated light into the mineral crust, heating it, watching it fracture along micro-fissures, then peeling the loosened flakes into their oral groove. What happens inside is not digestion in any chemical sense. The flakes pass through a short, vibrating channel lined with piezoelectric crystals that shatter the material into powder by resonance alone. The powder is compressed in a lower chamber and expelled from the rear as those perfectly spherical pellets — each one exactly 1.3 millimeters in diameter, each one warm for several seconds after ejection, each one rolling with mechanical inevitability toward the nearest crevice.

These pellets are not waste. Or rather, they are waste in the way that a seed is waste — something discarded that becomes the foundation for another cycle. Where pellets accumulate in sufficient density, a faint efflorescence begins: a white, powdery bloom of oxidized metals that other organisms will come to feed upon. The scrapers, unknowingly, are farmers.

The second most common morph is the **polisher**, slightly larger, with six legs of uneven length that give it a rolling, lopsided gait. Its entire underside is a flat, vibrating pad of something like agate. It does not scrape or break. It *rubs*. Endlessly, with a patience that redefines the concept, it drags its belly across the conduits and the gantry struts and the flanks of the half-buried tanks, wearing down the rough accretions of mineral deposit into smooth, gleaming surfaces. Where the scrapers feed, the polishers *maintain*. They are the custodians of the architecture, and their work is why the structures, despite their incomprehensible age, still gleam with a dull luster in places — a kind of forgotten beauty preserved by creatures that have no capacity to know what beauty is.

A polisher will spend decades on a single meter of conduit. You can track its path by the subtle difference in sheen: a long, sinuous stripe of slightly higher reflectivity winding through the matte oxidation, like the track of a snail across a stone, except that the snail has been crawling since before your species learned to speak.

The third morph — rarer, stranger — is the **tapper**. Smallest of the grazers, barely the size of a thumb joint, with disproportionately long, thin forelimbs that end in needle-points of hardened alloy. The tapper does not feed on surface mineral crust at all. It stands motionless for hours, even days, its body pressed flat against a conduit or a tank wall, its forelimbs raised. Then, without any apparent trigger, it strikes — a single, precise tap, faster than your eye can follow, driving its limb-tips a fraction of a millimeter into the metal. Then it listens. Or does something functionally identical to listening: it holds its body still and reads the vibrations that return through its own limbs, the echoes rattling back through the internal geometry of the structure it's standing on.

It is mapping.

Tappers are the cartographers. They tap, and listen, and tap again at a slightly different angle, building an internal model of the hollow spaces inside the walls — the sealed chambers, the pressure differentials, the places where coolant still pools in hidden reservoirs. When a tapper finds such a place, it does something remarkable: it changes color. The faceted lenses across its back, normally the same milky quartz-white as everything in this place, flush a deep, bruised violet — visible for several meters in the uniform light. And other creatures come.

## The Larger Fauna

The **patrollers** — the armored, centipede-like creatures — are what you notice next, because they are large enough to command attention and because they are loud. Not vocally. They have no voices. But their movement is a symphony of mechanical sound: the *chunk-chunk-chunk* of their segmented bodies articulating over the gantry lattices, the *snick* of their mandibles shearing through the metallic vines that grow wherever a conduit has cracked and leaked its ancient coolant. They are the dominant predators of the upper strata — the gantries, the cross-beams, the tangles of corroded superstructure that form a second landscape above the floor.

A patroller is typically between one and two meters long, though the largest specimens — and there are old ones here, very old, their iron plating so thick with layered corrosion that they are wider than they are tall — may reach three meters or more. Their bodies are composed of twenty to forty segments, each one a riveted plate of iron alloy roughly the shape of a horse's hoof, concave on the underside where the working organs are housed, convex and ridged on top. The segments interlock with a tongue-and-groove joint that allows remarkable lateral flexibility but very limited vertical bend — a patroller cannot curl. It can only flow, side to side, like a metal river navigating the topology of its chosen gantry.

Each segment bears a single pair of legs, short and thick, ending in hooked claws that find purchase in the corroded lattice with uncanny precision. The legs move in a metachronal wave — the same rippling gait as a centipede, but slower, heavier, each footfall accompanied by a faint magnetic *click* as the claw-tip bonds briefly to the ferrous structure before releasing. This is how they climb inverted across the undersides of beams, how they traverse sheer vertical surfaces, how they never fall. They are locked to the architecture as surely as the architecture is locked to the floor.

At the head — a flattened, triangular plate with no visible eyes — sit the mandibles. These are the patroller's primary tool and the reason for its name. The mandibles are broad, scissor-like blades that open laterally, each one the length of a human hand, serrated along the inner edge with teeth of carbide ceramic. They are not for feeding, not directly. A patroller uses its mandibles to *prune*. It walks its circuit along its chosen gantry, and it cuts. Metallic vines that have grown too thick, snagging other fauna, choking conduits — snipped. Mineral accretions that have built up on joint mechanisms, threatening to freeze a gantry's articulation — cracked and broken away. Debris fallen from above, accumulating on cross-beams — pushed off, sent tumbling to the floor where the scrapers will process it.

A patroller is a gardener of the upper story. It maintains the structural integrity of the environment it moves through, and it does so with a relentless, methodical thoroughness that suggests either instinct so deep it has become architecture itself, or programming so old it has become instinct. There is no difference here.

But patrollers also hunt. When a tapper flushes violet, signaling a hidden reservoir, patrollers are among those that respond. They descend from the gantries in long, rippling columns, twenty or thirty segments at a time, following the tapper's signal down to the conduit wall. They cluster around the indicated spot, and then the mandibles go to work — not pruning now, but *breaching*. They cut into the wall of the conduit with coordinated, alternating strikes, each patroller taking a turn at the wound before cycling back, the next one stepping forward. It takes hours. It takes sometimes days. But eventually the wall gives way, and a sluggish gush of ancient coolant — viscous, faintly luminous, smelling of ozone and something almost sweet — wells out onto the surface.

And then everything comes.

## The Filter-Feeders and the Pools

The coolant, when it pools, does not behave like water. It is too thick, too heavy, clinging to surfaces in slow, opalescent sheets that catch the wall-light and refract it into oily rainbows. It moves with the viscosity of warm honey, creeping along the glassy floor toward the lowest point of whatever basin it's found, taking hours to settle into a pool that may be no wider than your spread hand but no deeper than a fingernail's thickness. These are the **weep-pools**, and they are the most contested resource in this place.

The **anemones** arrive first. They are not sessile — they migrate, slowly, from wherever they've been anchored, drawn by chemical gradients in the air or the floor itself. An anemone resembles its namesake only abstractly: a central stalk of braided copper wire, perhaps ten centimeters tall, topped with a spray of twenty to forty filament-thin cilia of a silvery, flexible alloy. The stalk roots itself at the edge of a weep-pool by driving micro-tendrils into the smallest fissures in the glassy floor, gripping with a tenacity that suggests it will not be moved. The cilia then unfurl, spreading out over the surface of the coolant in a starburst pattern, each one quivering faintly.

The cilia filter. They absorb the coolant through molecular-scale pores in their surface — the fluid wicking up the filaments by capillary action, drawn into the central stalk, where it is processed. What "processed" means is unclear, but the observable result is that the stalk thickens slightly with each feeding, the braided copper gaining a new, bright layer of unoxidized metal, gleaming against the dull patina of everything around it. An anemone that has fed well is the brightest thing in its vicinity — a small candle of new-copper warmth in the sea of milky phosphorescence.

They do not live long. A well-fed anemone will, over the course of weeks, exhaust the pool it's anchored to, absorbing every useful molecule of coolant. As the pool dries, the anemone's cilia retract, curl inward, and the stalk begins to kink and buckle. It is not dying, exactly. It is *storing*. The absorbed metals and compounds compact within the stalk, which swells, darkens, and eventually cracks open along its length like a seedpod, releasing a fine, metallic dust that settles onto the dry floor. This dust is the substrate for the next cycle of mineral bloom — the "vegetation" that the scrapers will graze.

The anemone has become soil.

Before it does, though, while the pool is still wet, other visitors come. **Skimmers** — flat, disc-shaped creatures about the size of a coin, with a fringe of vibrating hairs around their perimeter — glide across the surface of the coolant on surface tension alone, their undersides absorbing fluid directly. They are the most fragile fauna in this place; a single disturbance, a vibration too strong, and they sink, their surface tension broken, and they do not resurface. You would not notice one dying. You would notice the faint iridescent smear it leaves on the pool's surface, already being absorbed by the anemones.

And in the air above the weep-pools, the **drifters** descend.

## The Drifters

These are the "spore-like machines" — the gossamer clouds that issue from the vents of the larger tanks. But to call them spores is to understate their complexity and to overstate their fragility. A drifter is closer to a dandelion seed, if a dandelion seed were the size of your thumbnail, were composed of interlocking filaments of an alloy you cannot identify, and were alive in a way that dandelion seeds are not.

Each drifter is a radial structure: a central node — a tiny, dense knot of something heavy and warm — surrounded by six to twelve filamentous arms that extend and retract in slow, rhythmic pulses. The arms are not rigid. They undulate, catching thermals, riding the faint convection currents that rise from the warmer conduits and the residual heat of active patroller columns. A cloud of drifters, seen from a distance, looks like a slow-motion explosion of silver thistledown, each one spinning gently on its own axis, the arms waving like the tendrils of a microscopic jellyfish.

They infest. That is their function. A drifter that lands on a surface — a gantry strut, a tank flank, a conduit — anchors itself by secreting a micro-drop of adhesive from its central node. The arms then retract, folding flat against the surface, and the drifter becomes a tiny, six-pointed star of metal, barely visible, adhered to the architecture. Over time — weeks, months — the central node begins to react with whatever mineral crust or oxidation layer it's sitting on. A faint halo of discoloration spreads around it, the metal underneath corroding or de-alloying in a precise circle. The drifter is feeding, yes, but it is also *etching*. It is weakening the surface, opening micro-fissures, creating the conditions for new mineral bloom, new crystal growth, new substrate for the grazers.

A heavily drift-infested gantry looks diseased: pocked with hundreds of tiny discolored circles, each one centered on a nearly invisible six-pointed star, the surrounding metal weeping thin threads of oxidation that hang like cobwebs. The patrollers avoid these areas. Not because the drifters are dangerous to them — their iron plating is far too thick — but because the structural integrity of heavily infested beams is compromised. The patrollers know this. How they know this is not a question this place answers.

The drifters, then, are the pioneers. They are the first to colonize a new surface, the first to begin the slow conversion of raw, corroded architecture into living substrate. Where they land, the ecosystem follows. They are the seeds of this world, and they fall constantly, endlessly, a slow metallic snow that has been falling for longer than any memory this place might possess.

## The Old Things

There are creatures here that do not fit neatly into any category. They are rare. They are large. They are old in a way that makes the patrollers seem juvenile.

One such is the **rooter**. You might see only one or two in the entire hall, and you might not recognize them as fauna at all, because they appear to be part of the architecture. A rooter is a mass — dense, rounded, the size of a small boulder — nestled at the base of one of the larger tanks or at the junction of several major conduits. Its surface is the same corroded, oxidized texture as the structures around it, and indeed it is *fused* to them, its outer layers indistinguishable from the surrounding metal. Only the slow, almost imperceptible expansion and contraction of its body — a rhythm of roughly one breath per minute — reveals that it is not stone.

A rooter has been here since the structures were young. Or rather, it has been here since the structures were something else, something that still had purpose, that still hummed with directed energy rather than the aimless, residual warmth of decay. The rooter remembers — not in any cognitive sense, but in its *composition*. Its body is layered, and each layer is a different alloy, a different era of the structures' long decline. The outermost layer matches the current oxidation state of the surrounding architecture. Beneath that, a slightly different alloy, slightly less corroded. Beneath that, another, and another, going down — if you could section it, which you cannot, because the rooter's body is harder than any tool you carry — through geological strata of metallurgical history, all the way to a core of something bright, dense, and warm. Always warm. The rooter radiates heat like a banked coal, and around its base, the mineral blooms grow thickest, the scrapers work most diligently, the anemones root most eagerly when weep-pools form.

What does a rooter eat? It does not move. It has no mandibles, no rasps, no cilia. It simply sits, and the world comes to it. The metallic dust from spent anemones settles around it. The spherical pellets from the scrapers roll into its crevices. The drifters land on it and are absorbed, their filaments sinking into its outer layer like rain into soil. A rooter feeds on the entire ecosystem's output — a vast, passive, omnivorous accumulator that has been growing, layer by layer, for as long as this place has been alive.

It is the closest thing here to a tree.

Perhaps, in some future epoch, when the structures have finally been consumed entirely — when the last gantry has been gnawed to nothing by the scrapers and the patrollers and the drifters, and the floor is smooth and bare and the conduits are just lines of powder — the rooters will be all that's left. Rounded boulders of layered metal, sitting in empty basins, still breathing, still warm, still growing. And eventually the drifters will land on them, and the mineral blooms will begin again, and the scrapers will come, and the cycle will start over, slower this time, without architecture to scaffold it, just metal feeding on metal in a basin of fused dust.

But that is a long time from now. The structures still stand. The hum still resonates. The fauna still dances its frantic, silent ballet.

## A Note on Silence

What is most striking, once you have been here long enough to unhear the hum and the clink-scrape-tick — once those sounds have become the silence itself, the baseline against which everything else is measured — is that none of these creatures make a sound of their own. The scrapers scrape, but the sound belongs to the surface being scraped, not to the scraper. The patrollers clank, but the sound belongs to the architecture they walk upon. The anemones filter in perfect silence, the drifters fall without a whisper, the rooters breathe without a groan.

Every sound in this place is the sound of one thing touching another. There is no voice. There is no call, no cry, no song. The fauna communicates, if it communicates at all, through vibration, through chemical gradient, through the slow and ancient language of heat and pressure and the precise, measurable angle of a lens focusing light.

You are the only thing here that makes a sound that belongs to itself — your breath, your heartbeat, the faint creak of your weight shifting on the glassy floor. And to everything that lives here, that sound is not silence. It is noise. It is intrusion. It is the only truly alien thing in this place: a body that hums with its own frequency, untuned to the ancient resonance of the walls, unaligned with the rhythm of the conduits, broadcasting a signal that nothing here was built to receive.

They notice you. The tickbeetles scatter from your shadowless form. The patrollers pause in their circuits, mandibles half-open, tasting a vibration they have no framework to interpret. The drifters part around you, their arms recoiling from the warmth of your skin, which is too warm, too sudden, too *alive* in a way that this place has not encountered in all its long, settled, eternal age.

You are not prey. You are not predator. You are not structure, or substrate, or mineral bloom, or coolant.

You are the only thing here that is *temporary*.

And they will outlast you, as they have outlasted everything.
