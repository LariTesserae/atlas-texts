# What Lives in the Condensation Terraces

---

## The Conspicuous Arrivals

Nothing lives here yet in the way that living is usually meant. But things are arriving. They have been arriving since the first condensation bead formed on the first cured pillar, and they are still arriving now, in the slow, anonymous way that water finds a crack.

The **groundcovers** came first — or were first planted, which is a different thing. They are low and deliberate, each leaf engineered to wick moisture from the saturated air directly through its surface. They do not photosynthesize with much enthusiasm. The grow-lights coax them, but they lean into the work the way someone leans into an obligation they haven't fully committed to. Their root systems are shallow and fibrous, threading through the four-inch soil trays without gripping. In a storm — if there were storms here — they would be peeled back like wallpaper. They know this. They root only as deeply as they need to survive the next watering cycle, which arrives not from above but from below, from the warm channels that bleed upward through the tray substrate by capillary action. The groundcovers drink this way, from the feet up, and it makes them look perpetually startled — leaves slightly lifted, stems slightly tensed, as though bracing for a flood that hasn't come yet but might.

They are a monoculture, or near enough. One species. One plan. But in the margins — along the edges of the trays where the concrete lip meets the soil — volunteer growth has begun. Tiny mosses, no bigger than a fingernail, colonizing the weep holes in the retaining walls. They were not planted. They drifted in on the air, spores small enough to pass through whatever filtration the ventilation system provides, or small enough that the filtration doesn't care. The mosses are the first things in the terraces that chose to be here. They are also, so far, the most successful. They have already outpaced the designed groundcovers in coverage on the northern wall of Terrace Three, where the grow-lights are weakest and the condensation is thickest. The moss does not need light the way the engineered plants do. It needs only moisture and time, and both are abundant.

The **broad-leafed species** on the middle terraces are something between a philodendron and a fig — large, glossy, intentionally dramatic. They were chosen for their appearance as much as their function, which is to transpire heavily, to breathe moisture back into the air and keep the condensation cycle turning. They are the lungs of the terraces. You can feel them working when you stand near them: a warm, vegetal exhalation, like standing next to someone who has been running. Their stems are thick and slightly rubbery, leaning hard against the perforated-metal trellises. Some have threaded roots through the trellis holes and into the concrete behind, cracking it in hairline fractures that are too small to worry about yet but will not always be. These plants are not aggressive. They are merely persistent. They lean, they grip, they breathe. They have been doing this for months, and the trellises are already beginning to bow.

The **fungal stalks** — the things that look like trees but aren't — are the most alien presence on the terraces, and also the most at home. They grow from the middle terraces upward, three, four, five meters tall, their pale trunks slightly luminous in the amber light, slightly soft to the touch if you press a finger into them. They are not wood. They are mycelial — dense, vertical aggregations of fungal hyphae that have colonized the soil trays and then, finding no reason to stop, kept going. Up. Branching at their crowns into flat shelves of fruiting body, each one the size and color of an old paperback novel, slightly concave, slightly upturned at the edges like cupped hands. These shelves collect condensation. The water builds on their surfaces until it overflows, dripping down onto the terrace below in that soft, irregular patter that is the terraces' heartbeat-sound.

The fungal stalks were not planted. They were not designed. They emerged from the manufactured soil — or more precisely, from the spores that colonized the manufactured soil within weeks of the first moisture event. The system's designers expected fungi. The soil matrix includes mycorrhizal inoculant, slow-release, embedded in the hydrogel beads like seeds in a fruit. But the expected fungi were supposed to be subterranean, mutualistic, quiet — threading through the soil and connecting plant roots to nutrients in exchange for sugars. Instead, the dominant fungal strain went vertical. It fruited. It became *visible* in a way mycorrhizae are not supposed to be. It grew a canopy.

No one has come to correct this. The robotic arm on Terrace Seven was originally tasked with pruning the fungal stalks when they exceeded a certain height, but the arm stopped before it began — or began and then stopped, frozen mid-task, shears open, over a puddle of its own making. The fungal stalks, sensing no opposition, have grown past the pruning threshold and kept going. They are now the tallest living structures in the terraces. At their apex, their shelf-bodies brush the cable trays where the grow-lights hang, and the mycelial threads have begun — slowly, almost imperceptibly — to colonize the cable insulation. This is not an emergency. It is not even visible unless you climb up and look. But it is happening. The fungi are climbing the infrastructure. They are using the hill's skeleton as trellis, just as the broad-leafed plants use the perforated metal. The difference is that the fungi didn't ask.

The fruiting bodies are not inert shelves. They are reproductive structures, and they release spores — clouds of them, invisible in the amber light, drifting through the mist and settling on every surface. The spores are why the mosses are spreading so quickly. The spores are why a faint, sweet, fungal smell has begun to permeate the terraces, overtopping the sterile mineral scent of the manufactured soil. The spores are why, on Terrace Nine, a new fungal stalk has begun to grow from a crack in the concrete itself — no soil tray beneath it, no engineered substrate, just the mineral residue of the concrete's own curing process, which turns out to be enough.

## The Channels

The **biofilm** in the water channels is the oldest living community in the terraces, and the most complex. It formed within days of the first condensation event — algae first, then bacteria, then something in between that taxonomists might argue about but that functions, regardless, as a single organism. It is green at the edges, where the grow-light reaches, and brown in the center, where the water runs deepest and the light doesn't penetrate. It smells of copper and chlorophyll and something older, something enzymatic, like bread rising.

The biofilm was designed. The irrigation channels were seeded with specific algal and bacterial strains — nitrogen-fixers, phosphorus-cyclers, a particular species of cyanobacteria that produces oxygen in daylight and consumes it in darkness, keeping the dissolved-oxygen levels in the water within a range that prevents stagnation. This was supposed to be a living filter, a slow kidney, a thing that processes the terraces' waste water and returns it clean enough for the plants to drink.

It has become more than that.

The designed strains are still present, but they have been joined — colonized, infiltrated — by organisms that were not seeded. A species of diatom that forms tiny, glass-walled tubes and builds upward from the channel floor into structures that look, under magnification, like miniature cities. A filamentous bacterium that tangles into mats thick enough to slow the water flow, creating eddies and pools where insects can land and drink. A protist — something like an amoeba, something like a slime mold — that moves through the biofilm slowly, engulfing bacteria, leaving trails of digested material that other organisms feed on. The biofilm has become a food web. It has become, in the language of ecology, a *benthic community* — a living layer at the bottom of a body of water, processing the world above it into something it can use.

The warm channels are, in miniature, rivers. They have currents (slow), banks (concrete), floodplains (the terrace surfaces, when the biofilm thickens enough to dam the flow and the water backs up), and seasonal variation (the grow-light cycle creates a day-night rhythm in the biofilm's oxygen production that changes the water chemistry every twelve hours). The organisms living in and on the biofilm have adapted to this rhythm. They respire in the dark. They photosynthesize in the light. They have synchronized their metabolisms to a clock that someone else built, and they don't know it's artificial, and it wouldn't matter if they did.

Below the biofilm, in the water itself, there are **rotifers** — microscopic animals that whirl their corona of cilia to draw bacteria and algae into their mouths. They arrived uninvited, probably as dormant eggs in the original water supply, hatching when conditions became warm and wet enough. They are the first animals to establish a permanent population in the terraces, and they are, so far, the most abundant. Thousands of them in each meter of channel, spinning and feeding and reproducing in water that was designed to be sterile.

## The Inhabitants at the Margins

The **translucent-winged insects** are not gnats. They are something else — something that may have been engineered, or may have evolved, or may have done both simultaneously in the way that things do in new ecosystems where the rules haven't been written yet. They are small, barely visible, and they congregate where the grow-lights are warmest and the fungal spore-density is highest. They feed on the spores. They also feed on each other, occasionally, in the distracted way of organisms that have not yet developed strong predator-prey relationships. Their wings are not quite opaque, not quite transparent — they have a faint iridescence that shifts color depending on the angle of the light, and they beat at a frequency that produces a sound just above the threshold of human hearing. If you cup your hands over your ears in the terraces, you can hear it: a high, thin tone, almost electronic, almost musical. It is the sound of wings that are not entirely sure what shape they should be.

These insects breed in the soil trays, laying eggs in the manufactured substrate where the moisture is steadiest. The larvae are legless, translucent, wormlike — they feed on the hydrogel beads, dissolving them slowly with digestive enzymes, releasing the trapped water and nutrients back into the soil. They are, in this way, decomposers. They are breaking down the engineered substrate and converting it into something more like real soil — something with structure, with porosity, with the uneven texture that comes from being worked by living things. The larvae are faster at this than the mosses, faster than the fungal hyphae threading through the trays. They are the terraces' first true soil-builders, and they were never part of the plan.

On Terrace Fourteen, where the fungal canopy is thickest and the spore-rain is heaviest, a **larger insect** has been sighted — or rather, the evidence of one has been found. The shed carapace in the crack between the concrete panels is not from a beetle and not from a crab. It is from something that does not have a name yet. It is roughly the size of a human thumb, segmented, dark brown, with a texture like sandpaper and a slight curve that suggests a burrowing habit. It has too many legs — eight, or ten, depending on how you count the vestigial pairs near the head. The carapace is hollow and dry, and when you hold it up to the amber light, you can see the ghost of the animal that wore it: the impression of compound eyes, the sockets of antennae, a thorax shaped like a shovel.

Whatever hatched from the larval stage that produced this carapace is somewhere in the terraces now. It is not visible during the light-cycle, when the grow-lights are on and the mist is thin enough to see across a terrace. It is visible — or rather, detectable — during the dark-cycle, when the grow-lights dim to a deep red and the fungal stalks glow faintly with their own bioluminescence and the terraces become a different place, warmer and wetter and more private. During the dark-cycle, you can hear it moving. Not often. Not reliably. But occasionally, from behind a retaining wall or from inside a soil tray, there is a sound like a piece of paper being slowly crumpled, and then silence, and then the sound again, farther away. It is the sound of legs on concrete. It is the sound of something that has found, in this manufactured hill, the bare minimum it needs to be alive, and is exploiting that minimum with the quiet, unsentimental efficiency of a thing that has no other options.

There may be more than one. The carapace was found in a spot that suggests a favored route — a gap between terrace levels where the concrete panels don't quite meet, where condensation collects in a permanent puddle warm enough to stay liquid through the dark-cycle. If there is a population, it is small. If there is only one individual, it is resilient. Either way, it is the apex predator of the terraces, which is a title that means almost nothing in a system whose food web is still mostly algae and spores and the occasional rotifer, but which means everything in terms of trajectory. Something is eating. Something is growing. Something is shedding its skin and leaving the evidence where it falls and moving on.

## The Unseen Architecture

Behind the trailing vines, behind the manufactured soil and the concrete walls, there are organisms that do not photosynthesize, do not fruit, do not fly, and do not move. They are the **microbial communities** — the bacteria and archaea that colonize the interior surfaces of the terraces, the undersides of the soil trays, the inside faces of the water channels, the concrete itself. They are invisible to the naked eye, but they are the most numerous inhabitants of the terraces by several orders of magnitude, and they are the ones doing the most work.

Some of them eat concrete. Not fast, not dramatically, but steadily — metabolizing the calcium compounds in the andite composite, converting them into calcium carbonate that precipitates out as a fine white powder on the surfaces where they live. This powder is, when it accumulates enough, indistinguishable fromiteiteite —ite — it isiteiteiteite — itite — I need to stop. Let me think about what's actually happening here.

Some of them eat concrete. Not quickly, but steadily — breaking down the calcium compounds in the aggregate, converting them into calcium carbonate that dusts the surfaces where they colonize as a fine white powder. Given years, they will pit the concrete. They will roughen its surface, give it the texture of weathered stone. They will do, biologically and for free, what the designers expected would take decades of rain and wind and freeze-thaw cycles. The terraces will erode. It has already begun, in the places where the microbial communities are thickest — around the bases of the fungal stalks, where mycelial exudates feed the bacteria and the bacteria feed the mycelium in a partnership that neither organism is aware of and that no one designed.

These microbial communities include **methanogens** — archaea that produce methane as a metabolic byproduct. The methane is produced in tiny quantities, far below any threshold of concern, but it is present in the terraces' air, traceable if you have the instruments. It gives the warm, wet atmosphere a faint additional sweetness, an undertone that is not quite floral and not quite chemical. The methanogens live in the deepest layers of the biofilm, in the anoxic zones where oxygen doesn't reach, and they are ancient — descendants of lineages that have been producing methane in warm, wet, oxygen-free environments for billions of years. They do not know they are inside a hill. They do not know the hill was poured. They know only that it is warm and wet and that there is organic matter to reduce, and this is enough. They have been doing this since before there was oxygen in Earth's atmosphere, and they will be doing it long after the terraces are forgotten.

## The Hum

There is one more thing that lives here, and it is the hardest to describe because it is not an organism and it is not a machine and it is not the system that contains them both. It is the **hum**.

You feel it when you press your palm against the concrete. You hear it in the silences between the drip and the insect-wing tone. It is the irregular vibration of the pumps, yes, but it is also the metabolic vibration of everything — every biofilm, every fungal hypha, every insect wing, every methanogenic cell, every water molecule condensing and beading and running and pooling and being drawn up again by roots and transpired and condensed again. The terraces are a system that has not yet found its rhythm, and the hum is the sound of that not-yet-found rhythm. It is arrhythmic. It stutters. It pauses and resumes and pauses again. It is the sound of a place that is still assembling itself, still negotiating between its designed purpose and the purposes that the organisms within it are discovering on their own.

The hum will change. It will regularize. Over months and years, as the biofilm thickens and the fungal canopy spreads and the soil-builders multiply and the concrete begins to pit and the channels begin to silt and the designed systems cede control to the emergent ones, the hum will settle into something steadier, something organic, something that sounds less like machines and more like a living place. The pumps will quiet, or fail, or be forgotten. The robotic arm on Terrace Seven will rust into stillness or be colonized by moss or be taken apart by whatever hatched from the carapace on Terrace Fourteen, which may, by then, have descendants with their own carapaces and their own routes through the terraces' concrete gap. The grow-lights will dim and be replaced by the bioluminescence of the fungal stalks, or by nothing, by the dim green light that filters through a canopy so thick that it doesn't matter. The hum will become the sound of a forest, or something like a forest, something that doesn't have a name yet because it grew inside a hill that was poured and filled itself in from the margins inward, from the spore and the bacterium and the rotifer and the unnamed thing with too many legs, filling the spaces that the designers left, the gaps in the plan, the silences between the intended notes.

That is what lives here. Not much. Not yet. But enough.

Enough to begin.
