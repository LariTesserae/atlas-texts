The figure you first perceive is not a figure at all, but a movement within the woven netting of a walkway. It is a Drip-Catcher, and they are engaged in the act that names them.

Their hands, long-fingered and stained with chlorophyll and mineral grey, are cupped beneath a fleshy, broad-leafed vine. They are not collecting the water that drips from the leaf’s tip—that is the work of the funnels below. They are harvesting the *drip itself*. With infinite patience, they coax the swelling bead of condensation to roll along a groove in their palm, down their wrist, and into a small, tightly-woven pouch at their belt. Each bead is a separate event, captured and contained. Their face, seen in the dim bioluminescent glow of a passing gnat-cloud, is calm, intent, the eyes slightly widened as if in a state of perpetual, soft wonder. Their skin is the color of wet basalt, their hair a dark, fibrous mass often woven with pale fungal strands.

They are a people of **height and layer**. You see others now, in the architecture of the place. High in the steam-warmed upper tiers, elders move with glacial slowness, their clothing—thick, felted mantles of fungal mycelium—giving them a soft, amorphous silhouette. They tend the clay pipes, their hands caressing the warm ceramics, listening to the hum change pitch as they open or close valves carved from beetle carapace. Their speech, when it occurs, is not speech as you know it. It is a series of low, tonal hums that resonate with the steam-pipes, and subtle hand gestures that speak of patterns in the condensation, the growth rates of fungi, the slow migration of the armored beetle herds.

Below, on the ground level, are the **younger tenders and harvesters**. They move between the colossal fungal shelves, their steps placed with a dancer’s precision on the mycelium-stitched rock. They use tools of polished basalt and chitin: long, curved hooks to guide vine growth, flat scrapers to collect the potent, protein-rich spores from shelf fungi. They communicate through touch—a tap on the ankle, a press of fingers to the back of the hand—and through the faint rustle patterns they make with their woven fiber kilns against the horsetail stalks. A staccato rustle might mean *beetle herd approaching from the west*. A long, sighing slide of fabric: *steam vent pressure is stable*.

Their society is a **mirror of the grove’s ecosystem**. There are no loud leaders, no shouted commands. There are **Listeners**, those whose sense of vibration is acute. They lie prone on the basalt, cheek pressed to the rock, feeling the tremors of centipede patrols, the subtle shift of rock under fungal root-mass, the footfalls of their own people on the netways above. Their knowledge dictates the day’s movements. There are **Weavers**, not just of fabric, but of social fabric, who interpret the slow changes in the condensation cycles and adjust the community’s water-gathering rhythms. And there are the **Ambush-Knotters**, a role undertaken by the most patient of all. These individuals will sit motionless for a full day-cycle, their bodies draped in a cloak of living, damp moss, observing a single mantis-mimic or a patch of fungal growth. They learn the predators’ patterns not to avoid them, but to *understand the grammar of patience itself*, a knowledge they weave back into the community’s ethos.

You realize the Drip-Catchers are not masters of this Cathedral Grove, but its **most intricate symbionts**. They do not dominate the silence; they are fluent in it. Their technology is a series of quiet conversations with the world: the steam hum is a lullaby and a tool; the woven nets are both home and a sensory web; their clothing is a second skin of shed insect silk and plant fiber, helping them blend, feel, and move with the same soundless grace as the fauna.

As your eyes adjust further, you see the **children**. They are taught the first and most vital lesson: the **Stillness Game**. A toddler will be placed on a soft fungal balcony, a single, glowing gnat hovering above their palm, tasked with keeping it there by regulating their breath and the warmth of their skin. A slightly older child will have a drop of water placed on their knuckle, and must carry it across a woven bridge without spilling a single molecule. Their play is mimicry of the hunt—they freeze into perfect, tiny basalt-mimics for minutes on end, competing not in speed, but in the perfection of their stillness.

The entire settlement breathes with the grove. It inhales as a warm plume of steam billows, shrouding the aerial walkways in a soft cloud. It exhales as the steam dissipates, revealing a new constellation of bioluminescent gnats that have drifted in. The Drip-Catchers are the conscious, patient, weaving mind of this place—a mind that thinks in drops, breathes in sighs, and builds its world in the spaces between the rustles. They are not merely *living* in the Cathedral Grove; they are its slow, silent, and devoted congregation, participating in the eternal, whispered liturgy of growth, drip, and decay.
