# What Lives Here

**The Crustaceans**

They have no name among themselves. They do not need one. If they did, it would be something like *the swarming*, or *the surface-dwellers*, or simply *the small pulse of movement*—because that is all they are to anything else that lives here, and it is everything to them.

They are born in the silt. This is important. Every one of them begins as a clutch of micro-eggs deposited in the grey mud where upwelling water meets bacterial mat, where the temperature is exactly right, where the dissolved iron is at the precise concentration that nourishes the thin, calcium-poor carapace they will grow. They hatch as near-microscopic larvae, translucent as the mist they live in, and they spend their first hours clinging to the underside of the bacterial mats, feeding on the microbial film. Most are eaten immediately—by each other, by the mat itself, by the slow chemical quiet of the water. The survivors climb upward through the porous lattice of the colony and emerge onto the surface as pinpricks of movement, already formed, already swarming.

They live approximately three weeks. In that time they will never be larger than your thumbnail. Their carapaces never fully calcify; they remain soft, translucent, tinted faintly by whatever mineral deposit they have most recently fed upon. Some carry a copper-green sheen. Others, feeding on the iron-rich surfaces near the drip-lines of the walls, are the color of old blood. They see—if it can be called seeing—with simple pigment-patches on either side of their heads, clusters of cells sensitive to the differences in luminescence between mist and water, between bacterial mat and open silt. They cannot distinguish shapes. They navigate by taste, by the chemical gradients of the water, by the faint currents created by upwelling springs. They know the basin the way a tongue knows a mouth.

Their swarms are not coordinated in the way of bees or birds. There is no leader, no signal. They move in dense, crackling shoals because wherever the microbial film is richest, many will gather, and their gathering draws more, and the surface tension of the water between them creates tiny, audible clicks as their legs break through—hence the constant, crackling rustle that fills the basin. Stop and listen and you realize the sound has a texture. It is the sound of a thousand tiny mouths.

When they die, they sink. The bacterial mats digest them. The calcium of their carapaces is too thin to fossilize, too thin to persist as nodule. They are recycled completely within days. The basin does not waste them. The basin does not remember them. They are, in the truest sense, a medium—energy passing through a form briefly before returning to the system that made them.

**The Millipedes**

They are not millipedes. This is a convenience of description. They have no legs, or rather, their legs are vestigial—tiny, paired nubs running the length of their bodies, visible only under magnification, useless for locomotion. They move by peristalsis, a muscular wave that flows along their segmented bodies, making them pour over and between the stones of the basin floor like something between a worm and a stream of ink.

They are glossy black, and this is their armor. Their exoskeleton is layered with manganese oxides and iron compounds absorbed from the water, fused into a lacquer so dark and reflective that in the sourceless grey light they appear as moving absences—tongues of shadow stretching between rocks, curling under ledges, disappearing into crevices. A single specimen may be fifteen centimeters long, though they can compress themselves flat enough to slide through cracks less than two millimeters wide. They are solitary. They do not swarm. Each one maintains a territory—a loose circuit of favored stones, feeding grounds, and sheltered crevices—that it patrols with the slow, inexorable patience of water finding its way downhill.

They feed on the iron-oxidizing bacteria, but selectively. They scrape the mat with a radula-like structure on their underside—not a mouth, exactly, but a textured band of hardened tissue that grinds the bacterial colony and harvests the iron-rich precipitates within. They are, in a sense, miners. They extract. The iron they absorb becomes part of their armor, which means the older and larger they are, the darker and harder they become, until the oldest specimens are nearly indestructible by the standards of this ecosystem—armored tubes of living mineral, moving through a world of soft water and spongy bacterial growth.

They can live for years. Perhaps many years. It is difficult to tell. They show no signs of aging in the way other creatures do; they simply grow, slowly, darkening, thickening, until eventually they reach a size and rigidity that makes movement impossible. At that point they settle into a crevice, cease to move, and begin to mineralize completely. Their bodies become the stones of the basin—the dark, smooth nodules embedded in the silt, the hard fragments between the bacterial mats. You cannot distinguish an old millipede from an old stone. They are, by the end, the same thing.

There may be several hundred in the basin at any given time. You will never see more than one at a stretch, and only if you look carefully at the spaces between stones, at the places where shadow seems to have length and direction.

**The Eel-Like Things**

They are the largest creatures here, and they know it. This knowledge manifests not as aggression but as a profound, settled indifference. They move through the deeper pools with a laziness that borders on contemptuous, their muscular bodies contracting in long, slow waves, their feathery gills fanning the water with the unhurried rhythm of someone turning pages in a book they have read before.

They are blind. Completely. Not vestigially blind, not reduced-eye blind—*never-had-eyes* blind. Their heads are smooth, rounded, featureless except for the mouth: a wide, lipless opening lined with soft, backward-pointing papillae that function as both sensory organs and feeding apparatus. They taste the water constantly, reading the microbial ooze for chemical signatures of food, of territory, of each other. Their gills are external, feathery plumes of tissue that extend from either side of their heads, each filament coated in a fine, mucous film that traps dissolved oxygen from the water. In the dim light, the gills glow faintly—not bioluminescence, exactly, but a trick of refraction, the mucous catching the ghostly luminescence of the mist and fracturing it into tiny, shifting rainbows. In life, they are the only color in the basin. In death, the gills collapse almost immediately, and the creatures become dull, grey, unremarkable things.

They are predators, but only incidentally. They eat the microbial ooze—bacteria, decomposing matter, dead crustaceans, the occasional millipede that has grown too slow—and they eat it by swimming through it with their mouths open, filtering. Occasionally one will encounter a particularly dense patch of ooze and will pause, hovering in the water column, its gills flaring wide, its body perfectly still, and will simply *absorb*. These feeding pauses can last for hours.

They are territorial, but their territories are vertical, not horizontal. Each claims a column of water—a specific depth range in a specific pool—and they defend it not through confrontation but through presence. Two eel-things encountering each other will simply redirect, one sinking and one rising, each returning to its own stratum. There is no conflict because there is no overlap. The basin is large enough, and the population small enough—perhaps eight or ten individuals—that each can maintain its own unchallenged slice of the water column.

They reproduce rarely. When they do, the female—for there are two sexes, though they are externally identical—deposits a single egg mass in the deepest part of her pool, among the silt, and guards it by hovering above it, her body creating a gentle current that keeps the eggs oxygenated. The eggs hatch in weeks. The young are born as tiny, writhing things with oversized gills, and they immediately sink to the deepest water they can find, as far from the surface as possible. Those that survive to reach the bottom—where the microbial ooze is thickest, richest, safest—will establish their own vertical territory and begin the slow, lazy business of growing.

The largest of them may be forty centimeters long. They have no natural predators in the basin. Nothing here eats them. They will, eventually, die of age or disease, and their bodies will settle to the bottom of their pool, and the ooze will close over them, and the basin will take them back.

**The Bacterial Mats**

They are not alive in the way the other creatures are alive. They are alive in a deeper, older, more fundamental way—a way that predates the concept of *creature*, that belongs to the first billions of years when life was not yet something that moved or thought or consumed, but simply *persisted*.

The mats are vast. They cover the floor of the basin in a continuous, spongy plain, interrupted only by the deeper pools, the upwelling springs, and the ruined structures of the old machines. They are composed of billions upon billions of individual bacterial cells—iron-oxidizers, primarily, species that derive their energy from converting dissolved ferrous iron to insoluble ferric iron, precipitating it as a rust-colored solid that accumulates around and within the colony. The mat is not one organism but a consortium: layers of different species, stacked atop one another, each occupying a different niche in the chemical gradient from water to air, from iron-rich to iron-poor, from aerobic to anaerobic. Together they form a structure so complex and so finely tuned that it functions, in every meaningful ecological sense, as a single entity.

The upper surface of the mat—the part that meets the mist—is a living skin. It is here that the crustaceans swarm, here that the millipedes graze, here that the constant drip of mineral-laden water feeds the colony. The surface is slick, sticky, faintly warm with metabolic heat—not warm enough to feel, but warm enough to register on sensitive instruments, warm enough to create the faint updrafts that keep the mist in slow, perpetual circulation. Touch the surface and you leave a handprint that fills in within minutes, the bacteria covering the impression, healing the wound.

Beneath the surface, the mat is a layered archive. Each stratum is a record of a particular period in the basin's history—a record written in iron and calcium and manganese, in the ratios of isotopes and the sizes of mineral crystals. The deepest layers, meters below the surface, have been compressed and chemically altered until they are stone: dense, laminated rock indistinguishable from the walls of the basin itself. In this way the basin is not just a container for the bacterial mat—the basin *is* the bacterial mat, or rather, the basin is what the bacterial mat becomes when it has had enough time. The walls you lean against, the slick, weeping surfaces etched with vertical channels, are the fossilized remains of billions of years of bacterial growth. The basin built itself.

The mat breathes. This is not metaphor. As the iron-oxidizing bacteria consume dissolved iron from the upwelling water, they release hydrogen ions, which acidify the local environment, which dissolves more iron from the surrounding rock, which feeds the bacteria, which release more hydrogen ions. It is a loop, a self-sustaining engine of chemistry that drives the entire hydrology of the basin. The water seeps up because the bacteria create the conditions that draw it up. The bacteria thrive because the water delivers the iron they need. Neither could exist without the other. Neither came first. They emerged together, from the same ancient impulse toward persistence.

The mat is aware of nothing. It does not know the crustaceans that live on its surface, or the millipedes that mine its flesh, or the eel-things that swim above it. But it responds to them. Where crustaceans swarm, the mat thickens, feeding on their waste. Where millipedes graze, it grows back, harder, denser, resistant. Where the eel-things' waste settles, it blooms. The mat is a body, and the other creatures are its microbiome, its skin fauna, its parasites and symbiotes. The basin is not an ecosystem in the conventional sense—it is an organism, and the bacterial mat is its metabolism, and everything else is just what lives on and within that metabolism, inseparable from it, dependent on it, made of it.

**The Sound in the Rock**

Something else lives here. Something you cannot see.

The *chunk-grind-shush* that echoes through the basin at intervals—regular but not rhythmic, like a heartbeat that has forgotten how to beat steadily—comes from deep within the rock. From below the bacterial mats, below the silt, below the deepest pool, in a place where the laminated stone gives way to something denser and older: a cavern, or a chamber, or a shaft. The sound is mechanical. It is the sound of a pump.

Not the ruined pump house visible on the surface—that skeleton of riveted plates and frozen flywheels. That was a secondary installation, an outpost, a place where someone once monitored and maintained. The real engine is below. It has always been below. It was built into the basin before the basin was a basin, before the bacterial mats had begun to colonize the floor, before the first crustaceans evolved from whatever larval ancestors colonized this dripping, iron-rich world. It was here at the beginning, or close enough to the beginning that the distinction is academic.

It is a pump. It moves water. The rhythmic *chunk* is the compression stroke, the *grind* is the valve mechanism shifting, the *shush* is the water being forced through a conduit. It has been doing this for millennia. Its original purpose—whether it was built to drain the basin, to circulate water for some industrial process, to supply a facility that no longer exists above—is unknowable and irrelevant. Its current purpose is the basin itself. The pump moves water from the deep aquifer up through the fissures in the rock, feeding the springs that feed the bacterial mats that feed the crustaceans that feed the millipedes that feed the ooze that feeds the eel-things. It is the heart of the system. The basin's hydrology depends on it. Without the pump, the water would still seep—the geology allows for passive upwelling—but slowly, too slowly, and the bacterial mats would thin, and the crustaceans would starve, and the eel-things would suffocate in water that no longer carries enough dissolved oxygen. The pump keeps the basin alive. The pump is part of the basin's life.

It is maintained by nothing. No hands have touched it in living memory—or in unliving memory, for that matter. It has corroded, mineralized, been colonized by bacterial growth and mineral deposits, but it still functions because it was built to be simple, because its tolerances were generous, because the materials chosen were iron and steel and copper, and in this environment iron and steel and copper do not rust away entirely—they oxidize and stabilize, forming protective layers that actually *preserve* the underlying structure, the same way the millipedes' manganese armor protects their soft bodies. The pump has become a mineral formation that happens to move. It is barely distinguishable from the rock around it. It breathes. It grinds. It persists.

Does it know what it does? Does it know that its heartbeat sustains a world? This is a question that assumes the pump is separate from the basin, separate from the bacterial mats and the crustaceans and the eel-things. It is not. The pump is as much a part of this ecosystem as the mats, as the mist, as the iron in the water. It has been integrated. It has been metabolized. The basin has made the pump part of itself, and the pump has made the basin part of itself, and the distinction between machine and organism, between the built and the grown, has dissolved entirely, the way the millipedes dissolve into the stone, the way the bacterial mats dissolve into the walls, the way everything here eventually dissolves into everything else.

**What Does Not Live Here**

Nothing with eyes. Nothing with lungs. Nothing that has ever seen the sun.

Nothing that remembers the surface world, or the people who built the pump house, or the purpose of the corroded pipes. That knowledge is gone—not destroyed, but simply not present, the way a fish does not know about fire. The creatures of the basin know the basin. They know iron and water and the slow grind of the deep pump. They know the taste of bacterial ooze and the texture of silt and the chemical gradients that define their world. They know nothing else because nothing else is real to them. The basin is not a closed system in the sense of being trapped. It is a closed system in the sense of being complete. Nothing is missing. Nothing is needed. The world here is full.

You, standing in the basin, feeling the mist bead on your skin, hearing the drip and the rustle and the deep, rhythmic grind—you are the anomaly. You are the thing that does not belong. You carry the memory of sun and sky and surface, and that memory makes you a foreign body in this system, a grain of salt in a freshwater pool, a contamination. The bacteria do not know what to do with you. The crustaceans will investigate your boots, tasting the unfamiliar compounds of leather and adhesive, and will find nothing useful and move on. The eel-things, if you wade into their pools, will feel the disturbance in their water column and retreat, offended, to deeper strata. The basin tolerates you the way a body tolerates a splinter—not with hostility, but with a quiet, chemical patience, waiting for you to leave or to be incorporated.

Stay long enough and you would be. The iron in the water would begin to stain your skin. The bacterial film would coat your clothes. The mist would penetrate your lungs, depositing mineral crystals in your alveoli. You would become part of the basin's chemistry, another input, another source of carbon and calcium and trace metals. The crustaceans would swarm you. The mats would grow over your footprints. The pump would continue its grinding, indifferent to your presence or your absence, its heartbeat steady and slow and older than any memory you carry.

The basin does not want you. The basin does not not-want you. The basin is not capable of wanting. It is capable only of *being*—of persisting, of grinding, of weeping, of growing, of taking in and breaking down and rebuilding and growing again. It is a mouth that never closes. It is an engine that never stops. It is a world that never needed the sun, and does not need you, and will continue long after you are gone, its mats thickening, its walls building themselves layer by layer from the slow rain of iron and calcium, its eel-things pulsing in their dark pools, its millipedes flowing between its stones, its crustaceans crackling across its surfaces, its deep pump grinding in the dark, steady as a heart, patient as stone, alive as only the very old things can be alive—not with urgency, not with purpose, but with the simple, immense, irresistible fact of *continuing*.
