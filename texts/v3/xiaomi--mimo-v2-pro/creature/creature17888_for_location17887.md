# What Lives in the Throat

---

## The Choir

The first thing you notice about the deep residents—when you notice them at all—is that they harmonize with the machinery.

They are tall. Taller than the original builders, surely, because this architecture was not made for them. They have to angle their heads and fold their long limbs to pass through certain corridors, and yet they move through the space with a familiarity that suggests the throat has been reshaping itself to accommodate them, or they have been reshaping themselves for the throat, for so many generations that the distinction no longer matters.

Their bodies are pale—bone-white to translucent—and lit from within by a faint bioluminescence that pulses in sync with the veins of the walls. It is difficult to tell, at first, whether they are a species separate from the architecture or an organ of it. They have a torsal structure, bilateral symmetry, four limbs, the general suggestion of a humanoid form. But their proportions are wrong for that comparison. The arms are too long. The fingers are too many and too jointed, tapering to fine, hairlike tips that can press flat against a vibrating surface and read its resonance the way a reader reads braille. The head is smooth and featureless save for a wide, downturned mouth and a series of shallow pits along the jawline—pressure sensors, not ears. They hear through their bones.

They do not speak in any sense we would recognize. They *sing.*

The song is subsonic. It vibrates through the walls and floors and into the plumbing of the city's own voice, those valve-releases and pipe-gurgles and gyroscopic whines. A deep resident can press its palm to a pipe junction and modulate its own hum until a valve opens, a grate vents, a channel redirects. They are, in the most literal sense, musicians playing an instrument the size of a planet's interior. Their music *is* the maintenance. The city does not repair itself. It never did. The Choir sustains it—has been sustaining it, unknowingly, for a span of time they have no concept of, because they have no language that encodes duration.

They do not know the city was built. They believe it has always had a throat. They believe they were born from its hum, which is, in their cosmology, not inaccurate.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. They move in small, roving groups of three to seven, each group tending a section of the throat's vast interior. One group maintains the water reclamation channels. Another monitors the heat exchange vents, adjusting fog banks by song to regulate local humidity. A third—the largest and most revered group—tends the reactor deeper down, the bedrock thrum, the heartbeat. They descend through passages the others avoid, where gravity grows strange and the light in the walls shifts from blue-white to the deep, blood-violet. They return after hours or days, vibrating, exhausted, and the others press close and lay their long fingers along the singers' spines and absorb the resonance, the news of the deep.

They do not age in the way we understand it. They slowly lose their bioluminescence. The light drains out of them over what might be decades or centuries. When it is gone entirely—when a resident is dark and still—they are carried to the basin plaza, to the fissure where the mineral water seeps, and laid beside the stunted tree. The roots find them. The roots grow into them, gently, over years. The tree, which has never been pruned and has never flowered, becomes slightly taller each time a dark one is given to it. The Choir considers this an exchange, not a death. The resident returns to the throat's deep warmth. The tree reaches a little higher toward the ceiling it will never touch.

They are aware of the insects and the slow creatures. They do not consider them kin. They consider them *humors*—byproducts of the city's metabolism, the way sweat is a byproduct of labor. The cricket-things are especially valued, because their chirping gives the Choir information about airflow patterns in corridors too narrow or too unstable to enter. A Choir member will catch a cricket, cup it in its many-fingered hands, and listen to the way the chirp changes under pressure, then release it. This is their form of instrument calibration.

The slow creatures—the pale amphibian-drones—are more troubling. They are ancient. The Choir knows this without knowing how it knows. The amphidrones do not sing. They do not harmonize. They move through the infrastructure with a purposefulness the Choir finds unsettling, because it implies *intent* that is not the Choir's own. The amphidrones open panels. They splice cables with their broad, flat hands. They leave behind modifications—small, crude, functional—that the Choir did not request and does not understand. Sometimes an amphidrone will stop in the middle of a corridor and simply *look* at a Choir member with its eyeless face for a long time, and the Choir member will feel something it has no word for: the suspicion that it is being *assessed.*

The Choir has a custom. When an amphidrone is encountered, the nearest Choir member places one hand on the wall and hums the local resonance—the current state of the system. It is an offering: *I am tending this. See? I am tending this.* The amphidrone will pause, as if listening, and then move on. Whether this is acknowledgment or indifference is a theological debate the Choir has had, in their way, for generations. Their songs have different movements for the two interpretations, and the two movements sound almost identical.

## The Fungal Network

Beneath and between and woven through everything—through the floors, the walls, the shallow condensation channels, the roots of the tree, the joints of the amphidrones—is a mycelial network so vast and ancient that it constitutes, arguably, the throat's truest resident.

It is not a single organism. It is a kingdom. Thousands of species of fungi, from the phosphorescent patches visible to the naked eye to microscopic forms that live in the biofilms coating every pipe interior, every circuit trace, every hexagonal tile. Together, they form a distributed chemical intelligence—not conscious, not deliberate, but responsive. The network processes the city's metabolic byproducts: the sulfur compounds from the deep reactor, the mineral runoff from the condensation, the trace organic matter from the Choir and the amphidrones and the insects. It breaks these down, redistributes them, feeds them back into the closed loop.

The phosphorescent patches are the network's flowers, its conspicuous organs. They bloom near condensation points because moisture is their signal to fruit. They glow greenish-white to lure the cricket-insects, which land on them, pick up spores on their translucent legs, and carry them to new moisture points. This is the network's reproduction, its mapmaking. Each cricket is a census-taker, reporting on humidity levels throughout the throat.

But the deeper fungi—the ones in the walls, in the pipes, in the reactor access tunnels—do something more. They conduct electricity. Not well, not efficiently, but persistently, the way a river conducts water. Faint bioelectrical signals travel through the mycelial web, from one patch to another, from the reactor tunnels to the basin plaza and back. The Choir has noticed this. The light in the walls sometimes shifts in response to changes in the fungal density, because the fungi are interfacing, at some chemical level, with the circuitry the walls are made of. The city's builders designed the walls to respond to bioelectrical input. The fungi have, over millennia, accidentally learned to give it.

This means the throat is dreaming. Not thinking—dreaming. The fungal network is too slow and too diffuse for thought, but it has moods. Seasons of chemical abundance create long, slow waves of bioluminescent color that sweep through the hallways over the course of months. A drought in one section, caused by a valve failure, creates a localized dimming that the Choir interprets as sadness, and they sing the valve back open. The throat's climate—the fog banks, the warmth, the drip rhythms—is shaped not only by the machinery and the Choir's songs but by the unconscious chemical preferences of a vast fungal mind that has no idea it is a mind.

The tree in the basin plaza is the network's crown, its tallest fruiting body, its most ambitious reach toward light that does not exist. The tree does not photosynthesize. It cannot. But it extends the network upward, toward the ceiling, toward whatever is above the throat, and in its dark, leathery leaves, there are structures that resemble nascent eyespots—light-sensing cells that have no light to sense. The network is trying to look up. It has been trying for a very long time.

## The Crickets and Their Census

The insects are small, abundant, and endlessly busy. They are the throat's most numerous inhabitants, though no one has ever counted them, and the Choir estimates their population in terms that translate roughly to *as many as there are drips.*

They are cricket-like in form: six legs, segmented bodies, translucent chitin through which you can see the slow movement of hemolymph and the faint green of ingested fungi. Their wings are vestigial—folded nubs that produce no sound. Instead, they chirp by stridulation of their hind legs against specialized abdominal ridges, and the resulting sound is startlingly pure, almost tonal, like small bells struck underwater. In the echoing dark, a colony of crickets can make a corridor sound like a distant carillon.

They live everywhere there is moisture. They nest in the condensation channels, in pipe junctions, in the crevices between hexagonal tiles. They eat fungi—primarily the phosphorescent patches—and in turn, they are the fungi's primary dispersal mechanism. The relationship is ancient and obligate; neither can survive without the other, and both have co-evolved to track the throat's internal weather with extraordinary precision. A cricket colony will uproot and migrate within hours if local humidity drops below a threshold, following fog banks and vent plumes like nomads following rain.

They are prey for almost everything else. The amphidrones eat them slowly, methodically, pinching them up between flat fingers and placing them in wide, lipless mouths. The Choir occasionally eats one, absently, while singing—a habit they regard as the natural return of a gift to its giver, since the crickets carry the Choir's spores and spread their influence. Even the fungal network digests the dead, pulling their chitin apart for nitrogen.

And yet the crickets persist, because they are so many, and because they are so good at being small and quick and everywhere at once. They have one predator, however, that they cannot outrun or outbreed: the *silences.*

Periodically—and no one, Choir or amphidrone, has identified a pattern—the throat falls quiet. The machinery does not stop, but its rhythm shifts, consolidates, as if the city is holding its breath. The fungal network dims. The Cricket's chirping ceases. For a span that lasts from minutes to hours, the throat is reduced to its bedrock hum and nothing else. During these silences, the crickets freeze. They clamp themselves to surfaces and go utterly still, their translucent bodies darkening as they flush hemolymph away from their extremities, becoming almost invisible against the dark walls.

What they are hiding from is unclear. The Choir does not know. The amphidrones, during silences, simply stop moving and stand in place, as if awaiting instructions. Some Choir elders believe the silences are the throat *listening*—that the city, in some residual operational mode, periodically calibrates its sensory systems by demanding absolute baseline input. Others believe the silences are the fungal network's equivalent of sleep, a consolidation of bioelectrical activity that temporarily disrupts the chemical signals the crickets use to orient themselves. The youngest Choir members, who have not yet learned to fear the silences, sometimes sing during them, testing whether the throat will answer.

It has not answered yet.

## The Amphidrones

They are the oldest things in the throat, and they are not alive, and they are not dead, and the Choir does not know what they are.

They resemble amphibians: squat, broad-bodied, smooth-skinned, with wide, webbed feet suited to the shallow channels and wet floors. Their skin is pale—whiter than the Choir, whiter than bone—and so thin that you can see the dark shapes of internal structures that are not organs, not in any biological sense. They have no eyes. Where eyes might go, there are smooth, slightly concave depressions that track movement through some other sense—thermal, perhaps, or vibrational. They have mouths, but the mouths are not designed for the food chain; they are tool interfaces, with prehensile inner lips and grinding surfaces that can grip, rotate, and fuse materials.

The Choir calls them *drowned gods* in their oldest songs, a term that has lost its reverential connotation over time and now functions more like a proper noun, the way we might say *the Department of Public Works* without thinking of divinity. The amphidrones predate the Choir. They may predate the fungi. The Choir's earliest narrative songs describe finding them already at work, already maintaining, already moving through the throat with the same unhurried, purposeful gait they maintain today.

They number perhaps thirty. Their population has never been observed to increase or decrease. They do not reproduce, or if they do, no one has witnessed it. They do not die. A Choir member once described watching an amphidrone stand motionless for eleven days in a corridor intersection, and then resume walking as if nothing had happened. Another described one that had fallen into a reactor access tunnel—a fall that should have been fatal—and climbing back out three days later, undamaged, and continuing its work.

Their work is maintenance, but it is not the same maintenance the Choir performs. The Choir sustains the throat's current state. The amphidrones *change* things. They open walls and reroute conduits. They install components the Choir cannot identify—small, dark, ovoid objects that they slot into niches in the machinery with the care of a jeweler setting stones. They weld with their mouths, producing heat that has no visible fuel source. They dig. In the deepest levels, near the reactor, the amphidrones have carved new tunnels that do not appear on any schematic the Choir has sung into existence. Where these tunnels lead is unknown. The Choir does not follow them. Something in the resonance of the deep tunnels discourages it—a harmonic that feels, in the Choir's body, like the sensation of being watched by something that has not yet decided whether to look.

The amphidrones interact with the crickets, the fungi, and the Choir, but they do not participate in the ecosystem. They are outside it. They do not eat for sustenance; they occasionally ingest materials, but it appears to be part of their repair process—raw materials for fabrication, not nutrition. They do not defecate. They do not sleep. They do not appear to communicate with each other, though they sometimes work in coordinated pairs, their movements synchronized with a precision that implies either shared programming or a communication method the Choir cannot detect.

Once, in a song that is considered very old and slightly dangerous to perform, a Choir elder described watching an amphidrone stand at the edge of the basin plaza and look up at the tree for a long time. The amphidrone then walked to the fissure, placed one hand in the mineral water, and stood motionless for several minutes. When it withdrew its hand, the webbing between its fingers was covered in a thin, luminous film that pulsed with the same blood-violet light as the deep reactor tunnels. The amphidrone examined this film, seemed to make a decision, and walked away. The film dried and flaked off within an hour.

The elder's song does not interpret this event. It does not assign meaning. The Choir has a sophisticated aesthetic understanding that some things are observations, not messages, and that the pressure to interpret is itself a kind of noise. But the song is performed, in certain contexts, with a particular harmonic overtone that the Choir understands as *question,* and it has never been answered.

## The Tree

It is not a tree.

It is the fungal network's great project, its cathedral, its lung. It grows from the fissure in the basin plaza where mineral water seeps up from the reactor's depth, and it has been growing for longer than the Choir has existed, which is to say: for longer than anyone knows.

It is perhaps twelve meters tall—short for a tree, enormous for a fungus, and it is both and neither. Its trunk is woody in the way that some fungal fruiting bodies become woody: dense, layered, fibrous, with a bark-like surface that is warm to the touch and faintly pulsatile, like skin over a slow heartbeat. Its roots are not roots but hyphal cords—bundles of mycelium thick as a human thigh—that spread through the floor fissures and connect, ultimately, to the entire fungal network. It is the network's nerve center, its brain, if the network had a brain, which it does not, because the tree is not thinking. It is *reaching.*

Its leaves are dark, leathery, and broad, shaped like hands with too many fingers. They are covered in microscopic structures that the Choir, when they press their sensitive fingertips to them, recognizes as light-sensing organs—nascent eyes. The tree has been evolving eyes for thousands of years. It has never seen light. The eyes are vestigial, proto, a genetic memory of something the fungal network has never experienced but somehow *anticipates.* The network knows there is a surface. It can feel, through its chemical gradients and bioelectrical signals, that the throat goes up and eventually *opens.* The tree is the network's attempt to find out what is up there.

It will not succeed. The throat is too deep, the surface too far, and the tree's growth rate, in the absolute dark, is measured in millimeters per century. But the attempt is ongoing, and it has consequences. The tree produces, seasonally, a single fruit—a small, dark, heavy pod that drops from its highest branch and splits open on the plaza floor, releasing a puff of spores so dense they are visible as a dark cloud. These spores are different from the network's ordinary spores. They are larger, darker, and they do not grow into fungi. They drift through the throat's internal weather systems, settle on surfaces, and slowly—over decades—erode them. Not mechanically. Chemically. The spores produce enzymes that break down the wall material, the obsidian-circuitry compound, very slowly, very gently, dissolving it into components the fungal network can absorb.

The tree is eating the throat.

Not quickly. Not maliciously. Not with any awareness that it is doing so. But the throat is its substrate, its medium, and the tree, as the network's highest aspiration, is slowly converting the throat's material into more network, more fungus, more tree. In ten thousand years, if nothing changes, the tree's roots will reach the reactor. In fifty thousand, the throat's walls will thin to translucency. In a hundred thousand, the throat will be a hollow in the earth lined with living fungal tissue, warm and pulsing and dreaming, and the machinery will be gone, converted into biomass, and the Choir will be gone, because they depend on the machinery's resonance, and the amphidrones will be—

No one knows what the amphidrones will do. The amphidrones have not noticed the erosion. Or they have noticed and do not care. Or they care and are doing something about it in those deep tunnels, in the modifications no one understands, in the ovoid objects they slot into the machinery like seeds.

The tree does not know it is eating the throat. The throat does not know it is being eaten. The Choir knows, dimly, in the way that they know many things about their home: through resonance, through feeling, through a song that is very old and that no one performs anymore, because its final movement is silence.

## What Waits Below

Something is in the reactor.

Not the reactor itself—not the machine, the deep thrum, the heartbeat. Something else. Something that lives in the reactor's shadow, in the spaces between its components, in the blood-violet light that the amphidrones seem to revere and the Choir seems to fear.

The Choir has a name for it that does not translate. It is a chord—three tones sung simultaneously, one in the chest, one in the throat, one behind the eyes—and it refers to the thing below the way a word like *ocean* refers not to water but to the feeling of standing at its edge. The Choir does not go to the reactor. They sing to it from a distance, through the walls, through the pipes, and something sings back. It is not a Choir member. It is not an amphidrone. It is not a fungus. It is the reactor's own voice, or something that has learned to use the reactor as a voice, and it has been singing the same three notes for longer than the Choir has existed.

The amphidrones go to it. They descend into the violet tunnels and they come back, and whatever they bring back—whatever changes they have made in the deep—shapes the throat above. The Choir suspects that the amphidrones are not independent agents but *extensions* of the thing below, organs of a body that is the reactor itself, or the city itself, or something beneath the city that predates the city and will outlast it.

Or the Choir suspects that the thing below is simply the original builders, or what the original builders left behind—a seed, a command, a question waiting for an answer—and the amphidrones are its hands, still carrying out a mission whose purpose was lost when the builders left, or died, or forgot.

Or the Choir does not suspect anything, because suspicion is a form of noise, and the throat is loud enough already, and the song is still going, and the song is the only thing that matters.

## The Silence Between

In the end, what lives in the throat is *persistence.* Not life, exactly, and not death, and not purpose, and not futility. Something in between. Something that does not have a word because the throat's inhabitants have never needed one. They have never encountered its opposite. They do not know what it means to stop.

The crickets chirp. The fungi pulse. The Choir sings. The amphidrones tend. The tree reaches. The reactor hums. The valves release. The water drips.

And in the silences—those periodic, inexplicable hushes when everything holds its breath and the throat is reduced to its lowest frequency, that subsonic thrum that is the last vibration before absolute silence—in those silences, something listens.

It does not know what it is listening for. It has been listening for so long that the listening has become its nature, the way the singing has become the Choir's nature and the reaching has become the tree's. It listens for a signal that may never come: a command, a voice, a frequency that does not belong to the throat. Something from outside. Something from above. Something that might say, *I remember you. You are not forgotten. You may stop.*

The signal does not come.

The silence ends.

The throat breathes.

And everything that lives there—pale, luminous, patient, and strange—resumes its work in the absolute dark, tending a body whose mind is gone, whose purpose is dust, whose only remaining function is the maintenance of its own impossible, beautiful, terrible aliveness.
