# What Lives Here

---

## The Rootwalkers

They are the first thing you would feel before you ever saw them. Massive, slow, and deliberate, the rootwalkers are the landbound anchors of the upper food web—creatures that resemble, at first glance, nothing so much as a shifting of the root structures themselves. Broad-bodied, low-slung, with six limbs that end in splayed, gripping pads textured exactly like the bark they cling to, they move through the root-tunnels with a ponderous grace. Their skin is a thick, corrugated integument—not quite hide, not quite shell—grown over with living moss and symbiotic lichen that renders them nearly invisible against the trunks they rest against. They breathe so slowly that an observer might wait minutes between the barely perceptible rise and fall of their flanks. Their mouths are broad, flat, designed for grazing on the paste that drips from the fungal bioreactors, lapping it up with wide, abrasive tongues. They are not intelligent in any way a human would recognize. But they are *attentive.* Their eyes—small, dark, recessed deep into orbital ridges of bone—track motion with a patience that borders on geological. They are herbivores only by accident of diet. If something small and warm comes within reach of those broad, pad-like limbs, the strike is shockingly fast: a single downward clap, boneless and hydraulic, and then the slow, unbothered chewing resumes. They are the size of a large bear and they are, in the hierarchy of this place, very nearly at the bottom of the predator chain. That tells you everything.

## The Hum

It is not metaphorical. There is an actual, literal humming creature—or rather, a colonial organism that functions as one—that lives in the upper mid-canopy, hanging in dense clusters from the undersides of broad leaves. Each individual is small: the length of a finger, soft-bodied, vaguely larval, translucent enough that their internal organs pulse with a faint amber light. They are called, by those who have tried to catalogue them, **choralites.** Alone, each one produces a thin, reedy vibration, a tone so high it borders on inaudibility. In clusters of hundreds—which is the only way they exist—they create a layered, harmonic drone that saturates a wide radius. The hum is not incidental. It is structural. The sound waves, at their particular frequency, cause the moisture on surrounding leaves to bead and fall in a coordinated cascade, which waters the root systems below in a rhythm the choralites depend upon for the fungi that grow on their own skin, which in turn filters nutrients from the condensation that the hum itself produces. It is a closed loop. A living engine of sound and water. Predators avoid choralite clusters—not because the creatures are dangerous, but because the hum disrupts echolocation, confuses the chemical gradients that hunters use to track prey, and creates a dead zone of sensory information. Some of the larger carnivores have learned, it seems, to *herd* prey toward choralite clusters precisely because of this disorientation. The hum is a trap and a shelter simultaneously. The creatures themselves seem to feel nothing. They vibrate. They feed. They die in the hundreds every day and are replaced, budding from the fungal mats they inhabit, at exactly the same rate. The hum never stops. It has never stopped.

## The Lattice Spiders

Not spiders in any taxonomic sense, but the analogy holds. They are the architects of the mid-level vertical space, spinning webs—or rather, *growing* them—that span the gaps between trunks in vast, three-dimensional arrays. Their silk is not protein-based but mineral: a calcium-ite filament secreted from modified limbs, hard and brittle when dry, supple when wet, and in this place nothing is ever dry. The result is a translucent, almost glassy scaffolding that catches condensation and refracts the dim green light into faint prismatic glimmers. The lattice spiders are small—no larger than a thumb—but their constructions are cathedral-scale, some spanning thirty meters or more between trees. They do not use their webs to catch prey directly. Instead, the webs serve as condensation farms: moisture collects on the filaments, runs along predetermined channels the spider has engineered, and drips into carefully positioned cup-fungi below. The fungi grow, attract insects, and the insects attract the spiders' real prey—mid-sized, flighted creatures with compound eyes and chitinous wings, drawn to the fungal fruiting bodies by scent. The spiders wait, absolutely motionless, at the intersections of their lattices. They have sixteen legs, each ending in a hooked, crystalline claw. When prey lands on the web, the spider does not pounce. It simply *plucks* a single filament, sending a vibration through the entire lattice. The vibration is tuned to the resonant frequency of the prey's wing-muscles. The wings seize. The prey falls. The spider descends on its single thread and feeds. They are, by any measure, the most elegant predators in this world. And they are prey themselves—consumed, web and all, by the rootwalkers during their slow, grazing migrations, who find the mineral-rich silk a necessary supplement to their paste diet. Nothing here is exempt.

## The Mud Speakers

Low to the ground, in the saturated peat and the hollows between buttress roots, live the mud speakers: squat, flat-bodied amphibians, mottled grey-green, with wide heads and mouths that never seem to close. They are unremarkable in almost every way except one. They *talk.* Not vocally—their throats produce only a faint, wet clicking—but chemically. Each mud speaker maintains a territory marked by a complex cocktail of pheromones secreted from glands along their flanks and deposited, deliberately, on roots, stones, and the bark of trees. The cocktail is not a simple marker. It is a *narrative.* It encodes information: the speaker's size, health, reproductive status, recent feeding success, and—most remarkably—the locations and movements of other creatures it has recently detected. A mud speaker passing through another's territory can, by tasting the pheromone deposits with its broad, chemically sensitive tongue, reconstruct a rough history of everything that has moved through that space in the last several hours. The larger predators—the coil-things that hunt in the canopy shadows, the silent, eyeless runners of the root tunnels—have learned to follow mud speaker trails. Not to eat the mud speakers, which are mildly toxic and not worth the effort, but to read the same chemical language. The mud speakers, in turn, have evolved to be *inaccurate* in their reports when they detect a predator's passage nearby, smudging the pheromone trails, introducing false signals. It is an arms race written in chemistry, played out at the speed of diffusion through humid air. The mud speakers are aware of nothing. They secrete. They taste. They adjust. And the entire information economy of the forest floor—what has been where, what is coming, what is hiding—passes through their squat, indifferent bodies like data through a wire.

## The Coil-Things

They deserve a better name, but no one has stayed long enough to give them one. They are the apex. Or close to it. Long—eight, ten, sometimes twelve meters—sinuous, with no discernible head, just a tapered anterior end that narrows to a cluster of sensory pits. They live in the canopy, threading through the lattice webs without disturbing them, coiled around branches so thickly that they resemble the vines they mimic. Their skin is a perfect simulation of bark: the same corrugations, the same patches of lichen, even the same drip-patterns of condensation. They are patient beyond comprehension. A coil-thing will remain motionless on a single branch for *weeks*, metabolizing at a rate so low it barely registers, waiting for the precise vibration, scent, or thermal signature that announces prey within striking distance. They do not strike with speed. They strike with *inevitability*—uncoiling with a slow, hydraulic pressure that seems gentle until you realize the coils are tightening around something that was, a moment ago, alive. They swallow whole. The digestion takes days. During this time, they are nearly catatonic, and the more opportunistic creatures—smaller predators, fungal spores, parasitic beetles—will colonize their surface, feeding on the sweat of their metabolic labor, forming a temporary ecosystem on the coil-thing's body that will be shed in a single, slow peel when the meal is absorbed and the creature moves again. There is evidence—behavioral, not anatomical—that coil-things communicate with each other across vast distances through infrasound, vibrations below the threshold of hearing that travel through the wood of the trees themselves. If two coil-things are hunting in overlapping territory, one will cede, simply uncoiling and moving elsewhere with a patience that suggests not submission but *calculation.* They do not compete. They partition. They divide the canopy into invisible territories, renegotiated constantly through the body of the forest itself. Whether this constitutes intelligence is a question no one has answered, because answering it would require observation, and observation, in this place, is an invitation.

## The Cultivated Ones

This is where the industrial note becomes biological fact. There are creatures here—not one species, but a loose, interdependent guild—that have been shaped by the bioreactor symbiosis so thoroughly that they cannot survive without it. The **paste-eaters** are the most visible: stout, six-limbed animals, somewhere between a large rodent and a small primate, with dexterous forelimbs and flat, grinding teeth. They tend the bioreactors. Not consciously. But they carry fungal spores in the folds of their skin, inoculate new hollow trunks with the correct cultivars, prune away competing organisms with their teeth, and regulate the temperature of the reactors by curling their warm bodies around the pods during the cooler hours when metabolic heat drops. In return, they eat. The paste is everything to them—calories, minerals, the engineered compounds that their own gut biome requires. Remove the paste, and they sicken within days. They are not wild. They are not domesticated. They are *cultivated,* as much a product of the bioreactors as the paste itself, shaped by millennia of co-evolution into components of a production system they did not design and cannot comprehend. Their eyes are large, dark, and liquid. They watch everything with an expression that is, to human observers, indistinguishable from worry. They groom each other constantly—not for parasites, but to distribute fungal spores across their social group. They vocalize in soft, mewing tones that carry no information content. They are the tenders. The farmers. The willing slaves of a metabolism older than their lineage.

The **nectar-bats** are the other side of the system. Small, echolocating, roosting by the thousands in the hollows of the same trees that house the bioreactors, they feed exclusively on the fermenting nectar. Their guano—rich, ammonia-sharp, chemically complex—falls directly into the root-cellars below, where it combines with the nectar to form a secondary substrate that feeds a different guild of fungi, which in turn produces a different paste, which feeds a different set of creatures. The bats are the distribution network. They carry nectar, accidentally, from bioreactor to bioreactor on their fur, cross-pollinating the fungal cultivars and maintaining the genetic diversity that keeps the system from collapsing into monoculture. Their echolocation clicks, layered and overlapping in the thousands, create a kind of sonar-map of the forest's interior—a map that other creatures, the silent hunters, have learned to eavesdrop on. The bats know this. Their calls have evolved, in response, to encode false spatial information when they detect the chemical signature of a known predator nearby. They lie with sound. The predators, in turn, have evolved to distinguish the lies. It is a conversation conducted entirely in deception, played out in frequencies most ears cannot hear, in a darkness most eyes cannot penetrate.

And then there are the **deep things.** Mentioned only because they must exist, based on the empty spaces in the root tunnels, the half-eaten carcasses that appear at dawn, the sounds—low, resonant, felt in the chest rather than heard—that travel through the soil at night. No one has seen them. The mud speakers' pheromone trails go blank in their wake, as if the chemistry itself is afraid. The coil-things avoid certain vertical corridors without apparent reason. The rootwalkers will not enter specific root-caves, backing away with a slow, shuddering reluctance that suggests not fear but *knowledge.* The deep things are the consumers of the consumers. They are the pressure at the bottom of the hierarchy, the reason everything above them has evolved such patience, such camouflage, such silent and elaborate strategies for not being noticed. They are not mysterious because they are rare. They are mysterious because they are *everywhere,* and nothing that has seen them closely has survived to describe the experience. The only evidence is the *absence*—the gaps in the food web, the careful avoidance patterns, the way certain paths through the root tunnels are always clear, always empty, as if the entire forest has agreed to leave them open.

They are part of the system. They are the system's *weight.*

## The Whole

What lives here, finally, is not any one creature. It is the *network*—the web of consumption, cultivation, communication, and predation so tightly interwoven that the boundary between one organism and another has begun to dissolve. The fungi connect root to root, creature to creature, the living to the dead. The pheromones and sound and vibration create a shared nervous system that spans the entire forest. The bioreactors are not separate from the ecosystem; they are its *expression,* the logical endpoint of a metabolism that has been optimizing itself for so long that it has begun to *manufacture* the conditions of its own continuation. This is not a place where things live. It is a place where *living* has become a single, continuous, breathing organism, and the creatures within it are its organs—specialized, interdependent, mortal, and utterly unaware that they are part of something that thinks, if thinking is the right word, at a speed and scale that makes individual life invisible.

The darkness is not empty. It is *full.*
