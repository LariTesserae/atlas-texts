# What Lives in the Dry Gullet

## The Rooting

The ground itself moves first, before you see anything.

It begins as a faint tickle in the soles of your boots—a subsonic shudder that climbs into the bones of your legs and settles there. The earth is humming. Not with water, not with wind, but with *them*. Press your palm flat against the shale and you can feel it: the tiny, incessant percussion of ten thousand chitinous legs hammering through soil in the dark beneath you, a vibration so constant it becomes a kind of silence, the way a single note held long enough becomes indistinguishable from absence.

They are always beneath you. Always.

## The Beetles

They do not have a name. Nothing here has been named. But the closest word for what dominates the Gullet—what gives it its pulse, its voice, its terrible vitality—is beetle, though that word is too small for what they are.

They exist in staggering, unthinkable numbers. Not thousands. Not millions. The Gullet holds a quantity of beetles that has no useful comparison, a living substrate so dense and layered that the ground in certain troughs is more beetle than stone. They carpet the lower ravines in shifting, oil-black sheets, and they stream along the cliff faces in purposeful rivers, their carapaces catching the bruised light in slicks of deep iridescence—greens so dark they are nearly black, purples that flash and vanish, the occasional smear of rust-red where the iron in the clay has been incorporated into the shell itself.

Their size varies. The smallest are no larger than a grain of rice—almost invisible, almost inaudible, a grit that moves. These form the basal layer, the foundation. They live in the gravel beds where the tea-colored water drains away, feeding on the mineral films that coat each stone, perpetually mining the very ground with their mouths. You do not see them individually. You see the gravel *shimmer*, a slow, silvery undulation across a puddle's bed, and you realize the stones are alive.

Larger ones—the thumb-sized majority—dominate the cliff faces. They move in streams, as water would move, as gravity would move, pouring down the creases of the ravines in dark, glittering currents. But unlike water, they climb. They flow upward just as readily, their legs finding purchase in the finest crack, their bodies pressing close to the stone in a behavior that is not quite swarming and not quite marching but something between—an endless, restless redistribution. They are constantly moving toward moisture, toward food, toward each other, but they never pile up, never dam their own flow. The streams thin at the ridges and thicken at the seeps, and the whole vast organism of colony adjusts without signal or leader, a murmuration of insects that somehow maintains perfect hydraulic logic.

The largest—the forearm-length ones—are rarer. Perhaps one in every five hundred. They move more slowly, more deliberately, and they move alone. Their carapaces are thicker, roughened, studded with mineral deposits that make them look like moving stones. They are the burrowers, the ones whose vibrations you feel through the ground. They excavate the deeper channels, the networks that run far beneath the ravine floors, and they are responsible for the drainage systems that keep the Gullet from filling with water entirely. Their mandibles are broad and flat, adapted not for biting but for gouging—scraping through clay and shale with a sound like a file drawn slowly across bone. You hear them working long before you see one. The sound comes from below, from inside the earth, and it is the sound of the land being shaped from within.

The beetles are not social in the way ants are social. There is no queen, no caste, no architectural ambition. They do not build. They simply *occupy*, with a thoroughness that is almost geological. Every surface, every crack, every crevice holds some number of them in various states of motion, feeding, mating, dying. Their interactions are brief and purposeful: two beetles meet, antennae touch, and they either continue on their separate paths or they couple, right there, in the middle of the stream, other beetles flowing around and over them without pause. Eggs are laid in the damp clay walls, hundreds at a time, in clusters that look like tiny pearls pressed into mud. The larvae that emerge are white, soft, legless—they wriggle downward into the gravel beds and do not reappear for weeks, during which they grow their legs and harden their shells and emerge, one day, into the stream as fully formed adults, indistinguishable from the others, immediately swept into the collective current.

They eat the lichen, the fungal stalks, the dead of their own kind, the mineral films, the root fragments of the scrub, each other's shed skins, the condensation itself when it carries enough dissolved organics. They are not picky. They are thorough. A fungal stalk that took three weeks to grow is stripped to nothing in hours by a passing stream. A dead beetle—one of the forearm-length ones, a massive corpse by Gullet standards—is first claimed by a cluster of smaller beetles that bore into the soft tissue beneath the carapace, and then, as the weeks pass and the body slowly dries and collapses, the scavenging moves inward, smaller and smaller species taking smaller and smaller pieces, until what remains is an empty shell filled with frass and fine dust, and even that is eventually consumed. Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is mourned.

The shimmering quality of their massed movement—the oil-slick iridescence—is not decoration. It is structural. The surface of each carapace is microscopically ridged, a pattern of nanoscale grooves that diffracts light into shifting colors. This is not unique to these beetles; it is a common feature of the family. But in these numbers, in this density, the effect is magnified into something almost hallucinatory. A stream of beetles on a cliff face does not look like a collection of individuals. It looks like a single, slow-moving liquid—a dark, prismatic fluid seeping across the stone, catching the dim light in waves of green and purple and black. Stand still and watch long enough, and the boundary between the beetles and the rock dissolves. The cliff face seems to breathe.

## The Clickers

But the beetles are the base. The foundation. The ground tone of the Gullet's great chord. Above them, layered in the sound, are others.

The staccato clicking that gives the Dry Gullet its voice—its most characteristic sound, the one you hear before you see anything, the one that stays in your ears for hours after you leave—comes from a creature that shares the beetles' general form but diverges sharply in behavior.

They are larger than the common beetles but smaller than the great burrowers, perhaps the length of a hand, and they are built for speed. Where the beetles are broad, flat, suited to pressing against stone and flowing with gravity, the Clickers are elongated, their legs longer and set higher, their bodies held above the ground in a posture that is almost arachnid. They move in quick, darting rushes across the open surfaces of the ravine floors, pausing on stones with their forelegs raised, their antennae sweeping the air.

The clicking comes from their mouths. Or rather, from the structures adjacent to their mouths—heavily sclerotized plates that they strike together with extraordinary speed and precision. Each click is sharp, loud, and carries far in the still air. They click in bursts of three to seven, with precise intervals between, and they click *at* each other.

This is communication. Not complex, not nuanced, but functional. The Clickers are the scouts of the Gullet, the ones that range across the open ground between the beetle streams, and their clicks serve as proximity signals. *I am here. I am moving. You are close. Move.* The result is a kind of acoustic mesh that covers the ravine floors, a web of sound that allows the Clickers to avoid collisions and maintain spacing even in the dim light. It is not intelligent. It is not even particularly interesting. It is simply *effective*, in the way that everything here is effective—no more, no less, exactly sufficient.

The Clickers feed on the beetles. Not the large burrowers—the Clickers are not strong enough for that—but the smaller cliff-dwelling beetles, which they pluck from the streams in quick, precise strikes. They eat constantly, and they are themselves eaten by the bats. They are the middle of the food chain, and they seem to know it. They never linger. They never rest in the open for more than a few seconds. Their whole existence is motion and sound, dart and click, dart and click.

## The Whistlers

The piercing whistles that cut through the Gullet's chorus come from something you will almost certainly never see.

They live in the deepest fissures—the narrow, vertical clefts in the shale where the rock has split along its natural grain, creating slots barely wide enough to admit a hand. Far down in these slots, in perpetual near-darkness, colonies of slender, pale-bodied creatures cling to the walls. They are legless, or nearly so—elongated, smooth-skinned, vaguely reminiscent of the salamanders that haunt the pools, but smaller, thinner, adapted to a vertical existence in a space where horizontal movement is impossible.

They whistle by forcing air through a narrow slit in their bodies—a modified spiracle, a breathing structure that has been repurposed into an instrument. The sound is startlingly loud, a high, clear note that resonates up through the fissure and out into the open air like a pipe organ playing a single, sustained tone. Each individual produces only one pitch, but the colonies contain dozens of individuals at different depths, and the combined effect is a chord—thin, atonal, but unmistakable.

What the whistles mean is unclear. They may be territorial. They may be mating calls. They may simply be a byproduct of breathing in a confined space with specific acoustic properties. But their effect on the rest of the Gullet's fauna is clear: the beetles avoid the fissures where the whistlers sing. The streams part around these silent vertical wounds in the rock, flowing on either side, leaving the narrow slots empty of all but the pale, singing bodies within. The whistlers have carved out, not through aggression but through sheer, sustained *presence*, a territory that no other creature challenges.

## The Pool-Dwellers

In the lowest depressions, where the tea-colored water lingers longest before draining through the gravel, the salamanders wait.

They are pale—so pale they are almost translucent, their skins the color of milk glass, with a faint tracery of dark veins visible beneath. Their eyes are absent or vestigial, covered by a thin membrane of skin. They are adapted to a world without light, a world of touch and chemistry and the faint electrical fields generated by living things moving through water.

They are patient beyond what seems possible for a living creature. A salamander will lie motionless in a puddle for days, its body pressed flat against the gravel, its skin absorbing oxygen directly from the water. It does not hunt in any active sense. It waits. When a beetle—one of the smaller ones—falls into the pool, drawn by the moisture, the salamander's body contracts in a single, sudden convulsion, its jaws closing around the prey with a speed that seems impossible for something so inert. Then it is still again. Motionless. Waiting.

The largest pools hold the largest salamanders, some as long as a man's forearm, their bodies thick and muscular beneath the translucent skin. These feed on the larger beetles, even occasionally on one of the Clickers that misjudges the edge of a puddle. They are the apex predators of the water-adjacent zones, and they are utterly silent. They make no sound. They contribute nothing to the chorus. They are absences in the sound-map of the Gullet, quiet pockets where the drone of the beetles thins and the drip of condensation becomes the loudest thing.

The salamanders breed in the deepest pools, the ones that never fully drain. Their eggs are gelatinous, transparent, attached to the underside of stones. The larvae that hatch are almost invisible—tiny, clear, wriggling things that feed on the mineral films and the smallest of the rice-grain beetles until they grow large enough to take their place at the edge of the puddle, still and silent and waiting.

## The Shadows

The bats are the only creatures here that move through the air, and they are the closest thing the Gullet has to a ghost.

They roost in the upper fissures, the cracks and collapsed cavities near the rim of the basin where the shattered remnants of the old cavern roofs still overhang. They are large for bats—wingspans of a foot or more—and their ears are immense, nearly as long as their bodies, veined and delicate, shaped like funnel-shaped leaves. Their bodies, by contrast, are small and compact, dark-furred, almost rat-like. They are built for the ears. The ears are everything.

They hunt by sound alone. The Gullet's darkness is not absolute—the diffuse light from the overcast sky filters even into the deepest ravines—but for a creature navigating at speed through a maze of sharp stone, it is dark enough. The bats echolocate, emitting rapid bursts of sound too high for most creatures to hear, and they read the returning echoes with a precision that borders on the uncanny. They can detect a single Clicker standing on a stone at thirty yards. They can map the beetle streams and choose the gaps between bodies. They can navigate the vertical fissures without touching the walls.

Their flight is silent. Not quiet—*silent*. Their wing membranes have a microstructure that breaks up turbulent airflow, eliminating the whir and rustle that characterizes most bat flight. They pass overhead like something imagined—a flicker of movement in the peripheral vision, a displacement of the dim air, gone before you turn your head. You know they have been there only by the sudden absence of clicking from a section of ravine floor, the way the beetle streams briefly scatter and reform.

They roost in clusters of twenty to fifty, hanging upside down in the dark of the upper fissures, their great ears folded back against their bodies like crumpled silk. They are warm-blooded, and in the chill of the Gullet, they huddle close, sharing body heat in tight, murmuring groups. Their social interactions are brief and vocal—a repertoire of quiet chirps and trills that serve to maintain spacing, establish roosting position, and coordinate the departure of hunting parties at irregular intervals through the dim cycle that passes for day and night here.

They are the only creatures in the Gullet that sleep in any meaningful sense. The beetles do not sleep; they simply slow, entering a state of reduced activity that is more metabolic pause than rest. The Clickers never stop moving. The salamanders are always waiting. But the bats sleep, deeply and in shifts, half the colony hanging motionless while the other quarter hunts and the other quarter returns, gorged on beetles and Clickers, to take their place at the warm center of the cluster.

Their guano accumulates in the upper fissures, a thin, dark deposit that feeds colonies of phosphorescent fungi—some of the only organisms in the Gullet that produce visible light. These fungi glow faintly, a dim blue-green, and their light illuminates the roosting bats in a soft, spectral radiance that makes the upper fissures look, from a distance, like they are breathing with a cold fire. It is the only light in the Gullet that is not borrowed from the sky.

## The Fungus That Listens

There is one more thing in the Dry Gullet that deserves mention, because it occupies a niche that defies easy categorization.

Near the base of the north-facing walls, where the seepage is steadiest and the black moss grows thickest, there are clusters of pale, stalked fungi that grow in dense, orderly rows. They look like any other fungal growth—tender, pale, fragile—but they behave differently. When a beetle stream passes near them, the stalks lean. Slightly, almost imperceptibly, they tilt toward the movement, tracking the mass of bodies with a slow, vegetative attention that should not be possible for something without muscles or nerves.

The mechanism is hydraulic. The stalks are hollow, filled with water absorbed from the seepage, and the beetle streams create minute vibrations in the ground that resonate through the moisture in the soil. The fungi's stalks respond to these vibrations by differential growth—cells on one side elongating slightly faster than cells on the other, a bending that takes minutes to manifest but is unmistakable when watched over time. They are, in a sense, listening. Not consciously. Not with intent. But with a sensitivity to vibration that is extraordinary, and a response that is functional: by leaning toward the beetle streams, they position their spore-releasing surfaces—the caps, which open and close in response to humidity changes—to catch the maximum number of passing bodies. Beetles carry fungal spores on their carapaces, transporting them to new locations, new moisture sources, new nutrient films. The fungi have evolved, over the vast and unchanging eons of the Gullet's stability, to *hitchhike*, and they have done so by developing a kind of crude, vegetable sense of hearing.

They are not intelligent. They are not even particularly interesting, looked at one at a time. But they are representative of the Gullet's deepest truth: that everything here has found its place, and having found it, has stopped changing. The fungus listens. The beetles flow. The salamanders wait. The bats hunt and sleep. The Clickers click. The Whistlers sing their single notes into stone.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is old. Everything here is exactly what it needs to be, and nothing more.

## The Silence Between

If you stand in the Dry Gullet long enough—and you will, because the terrain is confusing and the light never changes and the landmarks are all identical and you will become disoriented, and in your disorientation you will stop, and in your stopping you will listen—you will begin to hear the spaces between the sounds.

Between the deep thrum and the clicking there is a gap, a moment of near-silence that lasts perhaps a quarter of a second. In that gap, the Gullet holds its breath. The beetles pause mid-stride. The Whistlers' notes hang in the air, decaying. The condensation drips once.

And then the chorus resumes, filling the silence so completely that it is as if it never existed.

But it did. And in that silence, if you are listening with the kind of attention the Gullet itself demands, you can hear what the place truly is: not alive, not dead, but *occupied*. Filled to the brim and overrunning with creatures that have no word for themselves, no memory of change, no anticipation of anything other than the next moment, which will be identical to this one.

You are a disturbance here. Your breath, your weight, your heat, your smell—they ripple outward through the beetle streams, which part around your boots and reform behind you. The Clickers fall silent in your vicinity and resume their chatter once you have passed. A bat, overhead, adjusts its flight path by a degree to account for the large warm shape in the ravine below.

And when you leave—if you leave—the Gullet does not notice. The beetles flow into the impressions your boots left in the clay. The condensation fills the hollows where your fingers touched the stone. The chorus does not change pitch or tempo. The silence between sounds remains exactly a quarter of a second.

The Dry Gullet is not waiting for anything. It is not growing toward anything. It is simply *continuing*, with the immense, indifferent patience of a system that has achieved perfection and will defend it—not with aggression, but with inertia. With the sheer, crushing weight of ten billion small lives, each one meaningless, each one essential, each one identical to the last and the next.

The ground hums. The beetles flow. The water seeps.

Nothing changes. Nothing has ever changed. Nothing will.
