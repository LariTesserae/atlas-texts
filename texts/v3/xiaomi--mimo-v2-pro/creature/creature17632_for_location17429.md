The Millpond Quarter breathes with a humid, insectile life. It is a world of the small, the numerous, and the patient.

**The Insects and Arachnids are the true populace.** They are the hum and the drone woven into the water's rush and the mill's groan. Mosquitoes and midges breed in the still pockets of the channels, forming shimmering clouds in the filtered green light. Water striders dimple the surfaces of calmer basins. On the mossy walls and vine-bark, beetles with iridescent carapaces navigate a landscape of furrows and dew. Spiders are everywhere—long-legged harvestmen drifting through the air on gossamer threads, orb-weavers stretching vast nets between reed stems that catch the light and moisture like necklaces of diamonds, and small, quick hunters that stalk the undersides of broad leaves. Their work is constant, a silent counterpoint to the louder, slower labor of the mill and the channels.

**In the water, the life is tiny and translucent.** The "fish" are more like slivers of glass, shoals of minnows or loaches so adapted to the tannin-stained, oxygen-poor water that they seem made of the water itself. They dart between the dangling roots of water hyacinth, feeding on algae and the larvae of the insects that fall from above. Frogs are more felt than seen—a vibrating croak here, a sudden *plop* there—their calls syncopated against the water's percussion. In the deepest, darkest mud of the saturated ground, unseen things writhe: the larval stages of flies and beetles, the blind, pale threads of nematodes, all part of the great decomposition that is the quarter's other engine.

**Above, in the gloom of the low canopy and the mill's upper works, are the bats.** They are the only real flyers, the only creatures that breach the boundary between the quarter's interior and the wider world. They roost in the skeletal spaces between the turning gears and the sagging roof, a colony of a few dozen, their presence marked by the faint, sharp scent of their guano and the sudden, silent flutter at dusk. They leave through holes in the eaves, pouring out into the evening to hunt beyond the quarter's rim, and return before the first hint of diffused dawn, carrying with them the faint, alien scent of higher, drier ground. They are the quarter's ambassadors to the outside, and they bring nothing back but hunger satisfied.

**And then there are the people.** They are not separate from this ecology; they are its oldest, slowest-moving component. They are warm-blooded moss, thinking vines. Their children learn to identify the high, thin whine of a specific gnat that precedes a clog in a feeder channel. They know the spider that builds funnel-webs in the corners of the mill-house is harmless and feasts on the beetles that would bore into the main shaft. They have house-frogs—specific frogs that take up residence in the catch-basin of a particular cistern and are left undisturbed, their appetite for mosquitoes a welcome trade for their croaking.

Their rituals are biological. The "Channel Clearing" is less a chore and more a seasonal migration, as they follow the advance of the hyacinth mats upstream, their cutting tools parting the green flesh to let the water breathe. The "Board Replacing" is a study in rot, as they test the spring of a plank with a foot, judging how much of the original timber remains and how much has been surrendered to fungal threads, now a composite of wood and mycelium. They burn vine-heart not just for heat, but because its smoke, they say, discourages the mold that would otherwise bloom on their sleeping mats overnight.

They have names for the sounds that are not human: the *Mill-Groan*, the *Channel-Song*, the *Drip-Hiss*. A change in the *Drip-Hiss* from a hiss to a patter means a major condensation point has formed on a ceiling beam, and a bucket must be placed. They speak in a patois that has as many words for types of wetness and decay as the Inuit have for snow. They do not own the quarter. They are its custodians, its nervous system, the conscious memory that recalls where the water used to flow before the last great mat of reeds diverted it. When they die, they are buried in the soft earth at the quarter's lip, and the moss claims the mound within a season, and the vines grow over it, and they become, finally and completely, a part of the place they tended. They are the keepers of the slow clock, and their lives are measured not in years, but in the turning of the great wheel and the inches the green canopy lowers each decade. They are the last, most complex, and most fragile form of life in the quarter, and they know it. They stay because to leave would be to un-become themselves. They are the Millpond Quarter, thinking.
