They are the Still. Not a name they speak, for speaking is rare and soft, but a state of being that has seeped into their bones over generations under the glass. They are the human heart of this preserved, packed world, and they have become as much a part of its ecology as the weeping stone or the pale, waxen flowers.

Their bodies are slender, almost fragile-looking beneath their layers of felted moss-wool and the pelts of the blind, invertebrate things that live in the deep, still cisterns. Their skin is pale, like the inner flesh of a root, and cool to the touch, holding the grotto’s perpetual chill not as a discomfort, but as a baseline. They move with a liquid economy, their muscles trained for slow, sustained tension rather than bursts of force. To run here would be to risk a fall on slick stone, to disrupt the drip, to startle the clicking birds. Their labor is a meditation: the careful pruning of a fern tendril threatening to block a mica pane, the patient re-weaving of a bridge-rope frayed by damp, the harvesting of a pulsing fungal shelf for fuel with hands that are never hurried.

Their eyes are large, adapted to the bright, diffuse, shadowless glare. They see the world in exquisite detail—the bead of water forming on a stalactite, the slow unfurling of a new fiddlehead, the faint luminescence tracing a path through a fungal network—but they have no need for long-distance vision. The outside world, the dry, sun-stricken, windy expanse beyond the clouded glass, is a myth to them, a ghost story told in the faint, sweet scent of the water. Their universe is this grotto, its dimensions fully known, its ceiling their sky.

They are a civilization of maintainers. Their ingenuity is poured not into expansion, but into equilibrium. Their simple tools—stone wedges, wooden levers, ceramic pots with a vitreous glaze made from crushed, silicate-rich shells—are designed for repair, not creation. Their social structure is a mirror of the hive-like city: roles are fluid, defined by need. One who patches a roof pane today might tomorrow sit for hours, listening to the drip-pattern in a new section of stone, diagnosing a shift in the water’s path. Leadership is an absence of command; it is the one who sees the imbalance first, the slight tilt in a wall, the change in the fern’s color, and whose quiet movement to correct it is followed by others without a word.

But they are not alone in their stewardship. The pale, blind salamanders are their cousins of the deep. The Still do not keep them as pets, nor do they hunt them. They exist in parallel. The salamanders maintain the water, stirring sediment, consuming the microscopic films that would cloud the basins. The Still, in turn, ensure the basins are kept filled by the ceaseless condensation, and they sometimes guide a wandering salamander back to a main channel with a gentle hand. The long-legged insects are their scouts; a sudden, coordinated skitter of a colony across a pool signals a change in air-current, perhaps a crack in a high pane, long before the Still would feel the subtle draft on their skin.

The clicking birds in the misty rafters are the memory-keepers. Their echolocation clicks, mapping the constantly changing topography of hanging ferns and woven bridges, create a sonic tapestry the Still have learned to interpret in a subliminal way. A change in the birds’ flight pattern, a new echo off a recently harvested fungal shelf, is a message read in the periphery of awareness.

Their *weirdness* is their wisdom. They do not fear the fungi’s pulse; they understand it as communication, the slow, chemical language of the mycelial network that threads every stone and timber, breaking down the old to feed the new. They accept that shadows fall inward, toward the dense core of their city, because in this closed system, all energy, all growth, all gravity, draws toward the center of their packed, living stone. They see the curl of a fern frond and know it is not merely growing, but *listening*, following the faint traces of moisture and mineral in the air. They are a people who have traded wonder for understanding. The miraculous is their daily bread: the fact that the glass holds, that the condensation falls, that the pale flowers bloom from air alone.

They live in a state of grace, a covenant with their terrarium. To disturb the balance is not a sin; it is an unthinkable dissonance, a break in the sacred, metronomic drip. Their stories are not of heroes or conquests, but of perfect harvests, of generations where no pane cracked, of seasons (though there are no seasons) where the moss grew in the exact, right shade of green. They are the ultimate expression of their environment: preserved, patient, damp, and quietly, profoundly alive in the bright, cold, dark heart of their glass-roofed world. They are the grotto, and the grotto is them, a single, breathing organism dreaming of stillness under a roof of clouded light.
