In the Steam Gardens of Vehla-ka, nothing lives *upon* the land. The land itself is the life—a single, sprawling organism thinking in gradients of heat and moisture. Its inhabitants are its own organs, its thoughts the slow seep of water and the snap of rapid growth. What lives here is a democracy of root and chitin, a society of structures that are both citizens and infrastructure.

**The Pipe-Stalks** are the ruling class, the engineers and the clergy. They commune with the deep earth’s breath, their hollow bodies singing with the rise of steam. They are not individuals, but functions. Each one is a thesis on condensation, a living column dedicated to harvesting the sky’s tears from the hot mist. Their growth is a competitive sermon; they strain upward, not for light, but for access to the primeval vapors. When one cracks under its own success, it is not a death, but a planned obsolescence. Its fall is a sermon in humility, its materials a tithe to the common good.

Below them, in the humid, dripping twilight between the stalks, the **Bladder-Minds** drift. These great, shimmering sacs are the libraries and the reservoirs. Their opalescent skins, stretched taut with distilled water and captured minerals, hold the ecosystem’s memory in suspension. They are passive, yet central. A bladder’s slow drift across the lattice of vines is a migration of purpose, its path dictated by the subtle pull of thirsty root-tendrils. To burst is not a tragedy, but a dissemination—a sudden release of knowledge and resource that the root-mat eagerly drinks.

The **Foam-Mounds** are the laborers, the ephemeral guilds. They erupt from the mat in a frenzy of activity, cellular factories blooming on a diet of steam and released nutrients. Their purpose is to be temporary. They build themselves up with frantic, mindless industry, only to reach a critical mass of futility and collapse into a rich paste. This is not death, but task-completion. They are the ecosystem’s sprinters, existing to convert raw potential into a form the patient roots can utilize.

And everywhere, the **Root-Mat** is the silent populace, the commons itself. It is the thinking substrate, the connective tissue of memory and exchange. It is both the map and the citizenry. Through it, resources flow not by conscious decision, but by chemical imperative—the pull of deficiency, the push of abundance. A tendril extending to digest a fallen stalk is a thought. A network of hyphae rushing nutrients to a new spire is a collective decision. It feels the warmth of a new vent and *answers* with a slow, architectural sigh of growth.

There are no mouths here, no eyes. Communication is in the drip of water following a preferred channel, in the subtle tensing of a vine as a bladder settles against it, in the sudden redistribution of sugars through the mat when a Foam-Mound collapses. The rules are embedded in the chemistry: grow toward heat, share through the root, sacrifice form for function.

What lives here is a **self-inhabiting landscape**. The Pipe-Stalks are its towers, its workers, and its windpipes. The Bladder-Minds are its cisterns and its archives. The Foam-Mounds are its builders and its compost. And the Root-Mat is its nervous system, its circulatory system, and its collective unconscious, all at once. It is a city that is its own sole resident, building and dissolving its own body in a cycle as rhythmic and mindless as breathing. The only true inhabitant is the Cycle itself—the relentless, humid, silent loop of growth, function, and reclamation.
