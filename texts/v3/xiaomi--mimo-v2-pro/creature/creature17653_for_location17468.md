The first to be seen are the **Chitin-Singers**.

They move in the middle layer, where the vascular vines are thickest and the light is a green, watery gloom. They are not tall, but their proportions are long—long arms, long fingers, long prehensile toes that grip the woven paths with a gecko’s certainty. Their skin is not bare; it is a cultivated landscape. Patches of the springy, pale moss cling to their shoulders and scalps, not as clothing but as integrated camouflage. In the damp, it glows with a faint, silver-blue luminescence. Their own hair, where it escapes the moss, is a dark, wet tangle, often woven with shed insect wings or thin, fibrous vines.

They are the weavers, the pruners, the milky sap-tappers. Their tools are extensions of the Marrow: needles of hardened bone for stitching living vines into bridges, crescent blades of chitin for harvesting fungal shelves, hollow reeds for sipping the nutrient-rich sap that oozes from the trees’ vascular systems. They communicate in a language of soft clicks and whistles that perfectly mirrors the chatter of the featherless birds and the skittering of beetles. A sharp, rising trill is a warning—a section of walkway is about to be reclaimed by a swelling trunk. A low, repetitive click is an invitation to a new bloom, a feast of sudden, sweet decay.

Their settlements are ephemeral. A "home" might be a hollow in a spongy trunk, lined with shed moss and warmed by the metabolic heat of a cultivated fungal mat. But they will abandon it in a week if the wood begins to weep too fiercely or a colony of corrosive beetles moves in. They do not fight the Marrow’s hunger; they negotiate with it, yielding ground to gain another niche elsewhere. Their greatest skill is reading the turbulence: they know by the increased hum in the soil when a root-system is about to shift, and by the change in the mist’s taste when a new fruiting body is preparing to erupt.

Deeper, in the spongy substrate where the air is thick and the water pools in the cupped leaves of giant rhubarb-like flora, live the **Filterers**.

These are a second, quieter people. They are stockier, their movements slow and deliberate to avoid stirring the silty, organic mush. Their skin has a slick, amphibious sheen, and their fingers and toes are webbed with a translucent, vascular membrane that filters nutrients directly from the water. They tend to the great water-purifying plants and to the fungal blooms, encouraging some, dissuading others with careful applications of mineral-rich clay they gather from the stone bones far below. They speak little, even in clicks; their language is largely gestural, a slow ballet of hand movements in the gloom that communicates patterns of flow and growth.

The two peoples are symbiotic, much like the lichen and the tree. The Chitin-Singers bring down tough chitin from the beetle-sheds and arm-sized sections of vine-cable. The Filterers provide purified water in gourds and potent, condensed fertilizer-balls of processed fungal matter. They meet at twilight-equivalent gatherings on the central platforms, where the soundscape swells to a deafening, rhythmic roar as the Marrow’s nocturnal cycle begins—a shift change in the bazaar of consumption.

A third presence is rumoured, rarely seen: the **Spore-Walkers**. They are said to be those who stayed still too long. Individuals who fell into a deep depression, or meditated for days on end, allowing the Marrow to fully claim them. It is not death, but integration. Their bodies are said to become the architecture—a figure of fused wood and fungal tissue, a face half-veiled by hanging moss, a hand forever raised to tap a sap-vein, now made of the vein itself. They are not worshipped, but they are respected as a cautionary tale and a source of strange wisdom. Occasionally, a Chitin-Singer will consult a particularly ancient-looking Spore-Walker, interpreting the slow drip of water from its "hand" or the pattern of bioluminescent pulses across its "chest" as a omen for the settlement’s next move.

What lives here, then, is not a collection of individuals, but a distributed consciousness of maintenance and adaptation. The inhabitants are neurons in the Marrow’s vast, vegetal brain, firing messages of decay and renewal. Their survival is a constant, gentle war of negotiation against a world that loves them only in the way a stomach loves what it digests: totally, and without distinction between host and meal. To watch them is to watch the art of living inside a process, not upon a land.
