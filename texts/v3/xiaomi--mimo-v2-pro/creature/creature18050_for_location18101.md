The people of the basin move like the water that shapes their world: with a quiet, persistent purpose, their presence a soft, integrated pressure upon the landscape. They are not tall, and they are never dry. Their skin has the cool, damp-smooth feel of river stones, and their hair, often dark and thick, is perpetually beaded with condensation or braided tightly against the mist. They are born to the chill; you never see them shiver, only a certain drawing-in of the shoulders when the wind sighs over the cliffs, a instinctive huddle.

Their clothing is a second skin of adaptation. Tunics and close-fitting trousers are woven from the wool of the shaggy, moss-eating beasts—water-repellent, dense, and smelling faintly of lanolin and wet earth. Over this, they wear layered cloaks of layered, felted moss-fiber, an astonishing textile that is both insulator and camouflage. In the low light, a person standing still becomes a mossy lump, a part of the architecture. Their footgear is most distinctive: boots of supple, oiled hide, sealed with pine pitch, reaching to the knee, with broad, slightly concave soles designed to distribute weight on the soft ground and grip the slick, timbered walkways.

They are a people of the hand and the eye. Their tools are elegant in their simplicity: axes and adzes of dark, hard basalt, honed to a fearsome edge; knives of slate and bone; ropes of incredible complexity and strength, woven from willow-withes and the long, tough fibers of certain marsh reeds. They are master carpenters and water-engineers. One sees it in the way a bridge-piling is shaped to deflect the current of a diverted stream, or in the lattice of a wall that allows air to pass while breaking the wind. Their technology is a deep, embodied knowledge of material—where a root will swell with moisture, which stone will hold an edge, how a joint will settle over years of constant damp.

They do not farm so much as *curate*. The raised garden plots are not tilled in any familiar sense; they are composted layers of moss, fish offal, and silt, constantly renewed. The people move through them, pinching back leaves, collecting snails, their touch gentle and knowing. The sleek, dark swimmers in the pools are not caught with hooks, but with intricately woven nets and traps that are checked in a slow, daily ritual. The moss-covered beasts are led along the bridges, their clicking hooves a familiar percussion, their heavy, docile presence a central fact of life. Their milk is rich and smoky; their wool is shorn in spring, felted, and woven in communal sessions where the air is thick with lanolin and low song.

Their society is layered, like their city. Family units occupy distinct terraces, connected by obligation and a web of intermarriage. There is no ruler in a grand sense, but a council of *Tenders*—those whose expertise lies in reading the water-table, predicting a slide on the cliffs, or diagnosing rot in a foundational beam. Their authority is practical, not divine. Decision-making is slow, consensus-based, like the percolation of water through peat. Voices are rarely raised; a sharp word is shocking, absorbed quickly by the moss. Conflict is addressed through ritualized mediation, often held on the swaying bridges, where the precarious footing reminds all parties of the need for balance.

Their art is ephemeral and integrated. It is in the precise, swirling patterns carved into a lintel to mimic water currents; in the subtle dyeing of moss-wool using lichens and berries to create patterns of shadow and grey; in the low, wordless humming that accompanies work, melodies that weave around the sound of dripping water. They tell stories not by firelight—the air is too damp for open flame, save in carefully vented hearths—but by the light of pale, glowing fungi cultivated in clay pots, their tales speaking of the basin’s violent birth, of the first moss, of the clever swimmers who taught the people how to weave a net.

They are, in essence, the basin’s conscious mind. They observe the way a new fissure weeps, and they build a channel. They see where the silt builds, and they plant a willow. They feel the subtle shift in the beast’s gait and check its hooves for rot. Their every action is a conversation with the environment, a slow, endless negotiation with the damp and the chill and the relentless, soft, green growth. They are not conquerors of this place. They are its most devoted, intricate, and quietly persistent inhabitants, breathing with it, decaying with it, building with it, forever woven into the wet, crowded, and deeply living logic of the basin.
