# Who Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here lives alone. Every creature is entangled with every other—chemically, spatially, temporally—in ways that make the word "individual" almost meaningless. This basin is not so much a place *where* things live as a single, noisy, wet, iron-breathing organism with many mouths.

---

**The Consort.** The most visible, the most architecturally dominant life-form, is not one thing but a collaboration: the **algae mats**. They are the skin of this world. Black and emerald rubber sheets, sometimes an inch thick, they choke the warmer pools and creep across any surface where geothermal water meets light. Within their wet, spongy layers, entire civilizations exist—rotifers turning in their ciliated crowns, bacteria metabolizing sulfur into that faint egg-stench that mingles with the mineral vents. The mats are not passive. They are predatory in their own slow way, trapping sediment, smothering competitors, sealing heat into the pools beneath them. When they die—and parts of them are always dying—they darken to a leathery brown-black, becoming substrate for the next generation. They smell faintly of iron and decomposition. They are the foundation economy of the basin, and everything else has learned to live either on them, under them, or despite them.

**The Rust Eaters.** Growing directly on the corroded machines—on the flaking orange pipe joints, on the iron wheels pocked with pitting, on any metal surface where moisture lingers—are clusters of **pale, fleshy fungi**. They look like swollen trumpets, like something from the throat of a deep-sea creature, their surfaces slightly moist, their color a sickly cream-white or pale yellow. Some are no larger than a thumbnail; others have grown into overlapping shelves as broad as a hand. They are doing something remarkable and patient: they are digesting the iron oxide, breaking down the rust itself, leaching metals into the soil with their filaments. In doing so, they are slowly dismantling the machines that anchor this place's identity. They are the recyclers, the quiet undertakers of industry. Around their bases, the mud is stained in concentric rings of orange and black, chemical signatures of their feeding. If you listen near them—and you must listen, because they are silent—you can sometimes hear the faintest *tick* of metal yielding to fungal acid. A sound almost lost in the larger noise of the basin, but constant. Always constant.

**The Cushions.** On every stable surface—the tops of half-buried stones, the ledges of broken rock walls, the flat faces of dormant machinery—the **dark-leafed mosses** have established themselves. They are tough, almost rubbery to the touch, so deeply green they appear black in the basin's dim light. Each cushion is a world. Turn one over—gently, because it will have taken years to grow—and you will find it teeming. Tiny translucent snails with shells no bigger than pinheads cling to the undersides, scraping algae and fungal spores. Iridescent beetles, their carapaces shifting between green and copper depending on the angle of light, pick their way through the mossy labyrinth. Springtails—barely visible, white as salt—leap in frantic arcs when disturbed. Nematodes thread through the damp interior. The cushions are fortresses, nurseries, and supermarkets all at once. They hold moisture for hours after the surface around them has dried, and in this basin of perpetual damp, that redundancy is the difference between survival and desiccation when a vent temporarily shifts and an area dries for a day or a week. The mosses do not move, but they are among the most active residents, constantly growing, constantly hosting, constantly colonizing new ground as rock and rust yield beneath them.

**The Dancers.** Above everything, filling the grey air with their dark geometry, are the **swarms of black midges**. They are small—not gnats, not quite mosquitoes, something in between—and they are *everywhere*. They dance in columns and clouds, rising from the algae mats and the mud, mating on the wing, laying eggs in the mineral-rich waters. Their larvae are aquatic, feeding on the algae and on each other, and they are themselves food for nearly everything else. The midges are the connective tissue of the basin's food web. Without them, the salamanders would starve; without the algae mat filth they feed on, the midge larvae would perish. They land on your skin, on your eyelashes, in the moisture on your lips. They are a nuisance to anything large enough to notice them, and a lifeline to everything small. In the basin's low light, their swarms look like smoke, like dark eddies in the mist, and their collective hum is a high, thin thread woven through the deeper industrial moan.

**The Stalkers.** In the mud, under the edges of algae mats, in the hollows between stones, live the **salamanders**. They are mottled—brown, grey, black, with faint lighter speckles—and their skin is slick and loose, as though it doesn't quite fit. They are not large; the biggest might stretch to six or seven inches, but most are smaller. Their eyes are dark, lidless, watchful. They sit motionless for extraordinary lengths of time, nearly invisible against the basalt and mud, and then strike with a suddenness that startles even when you're watching for it. Their prey is midges, beetles, small crustaceans—whatever comes within range of their quick, blunt heads. They are amphibians in the oldest sense: creatures of the boundary between water and stone, breathing through their skin as much as through their lungs, absorbing the basin's mineral-rich waters directly into their bodies. In the warmer pools, you will not find them—they retreat from the heat of the vents. But in the cold mud margins, in the shallow trickles where warm and cold water meet, they wait. One was observed catching a midge in mid-air, lunging from a half-submerged position with its whole body, tail thrashing. Then stillness again. They are patient in a way that feels geological.

**The Blind.** In the warmest, most acidic rivulets—the ones that emerge directly from vents, too hot and too mineral-laden for the salamanders, too turbulent for the algae—live the **eyeless crustaceans**. They are white. Not pale, not faded—*white*, the white of bone, of cave fish, of things that have never had reason to produce pigment. They are small, amphipod-like, perhaps a centimeter long, and they move with a quick, jerking grace along the mineral-crusted channel floors. They have no eyes. The sockets are simply absent, their heads smooth where eyes might be, as though they were designed from the start without them. They navigate by chemoreception, by touch, by sensing the temperature gradients in the water. They feed on bacterial films that grow around the vent orifices, and on each other when opportunity arises. They are perhaps the most alien-looking creatures here—translucent enough that you can see their digestive tracts working, white enough to glow faintly in the dim light. When the steam shifts and a thicker plume rolls across the rivulet, they vanish into it completely, invisible even to those who know where to look. They are the creatures most perfectly adapted to this place's harshest margins, and therefore the ones least likely to survive anywhere else.

**The Hum.** And then there is something harder to categorize. In the hollows where water collects in shallow, still films—not quite pools, not quite puddles—the surface sometimes *moves*. Not from wind. Not from any creature you can see. A faint, iridescent sheen shifts and contracts, and beneath it, if you look very closely, there are **microscopic colonies of bacteria and archaea** that are not merely surviving the heat and acidity but *thriving* on it. Thermophilic communities, chemolithoautotrophs—organisms that eat rock and drink steam and produce their own energy from chemical reactions that would kill almost anything else. They are the oldest kind of life on Earth, or something like it, and they form the invisible foundation beneath every other organism here. The algae consume their byproducts. The crustaceans graze their films. Even the fungi, in their patient dismantling of iron, are participating in a chemical conversation that began with these tiny, ancient beings. You cannot see them without a lens. But you can sense them in the basin's feverish productivity, in the way life here seems to *erupt* from the very substance of the place, as though the rock itself were dreaming organisms into existence.

---

**What is absent** is as telling as what is present. There are no birds here—the walls are too sheer, the air too thick, the space too enclosed for anything with wings large enough to see. There are no mammals, no reptiles beyond the amphibians' distant kinship with ancestral forms. No trees, no flowers, no grasses. No large predators. The scale of life is relentlessly, almost aggressively small. Everything here operates at a level of miniature urgency, a world where the largest drama might be a salamander lunging at a midge, where the most consequential event of a given hour might be a fungal filament breaching a new chamber in a corroded pipe.

And yet the basin *feels* inhabited in a way that larger, quieter places do not. It is loud with living. The grind of wind through broken valves, the hiss of steam, the drip of condensation, the hum of insects, the scuttle of crustaceans, the plop of salamanders—it is a chorus, chaotic but whole, and every voice in it depends on every other. Remove the fungi and the rust resists decay; the machines hold their shape longer; the mineral soil changes; the mosses find less substrate. Remove the midges and the salamanders starve, the algae mats grow unchecked, the pools fill with dead organic matter that chokes the warm seeps. Remove the thermophilic bacteria and the entire chemical foundation collapses, and the basin becomes just a damp, cold hole in the ground with some old iron in it.

Nothing here is decorative. Nothing is redundant. Everything is load-bearing.

---

If you stay long enough—and it doesn't take long, because the damp cold seeps into you quickly, and the noise wraps around you like a fist—you begin to feel that you are inside something. Not a place, exactly. A process. A slow, wet, iron-scented act of becoming, in which rock and water and rust and heat and the stubborn, tiny refusal of life to stop have all been arguing for decades, and have finally, noisily, reached some kind of arrangement. Not peace. An arrangement. The machines will continue to corrode. The fungi will continue to consume them. The steam will continue to sing through the broken pipes. The blind crustaceans will continue their white, eyeless patrol. The salamanders will wait, and strike, and wait.

The basin breathes. It grinds. It hisses.

It lives, and it lives *here*, in this one small wound in the earth, busy and profound and entirely its own.
