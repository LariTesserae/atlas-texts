# What Lives in the Gullet

They are sixty, more or less, and the more-or-less is the first thing you must understand about them.

They do not count each other the way surface people do — cleanly, precisely, each body a discrete integer in a sum. Here, someone who has gone deep into the Coils is alive until they are not, and "not alive" is hard to distinguish from "participating in the root-network at a level we no longer understand." People vanish into passages that close behind them. Weeks later a new tap appears in a chamber that didn't exist before, and the water tastes faintly of the vanished person's particular scent — their skin oil, their sweat, the mineral signature of a body that spent decades drinking root-water. The plumbers notice. The plumbers say nothing, or they say *she found her work* and that is enough, and whether it means she is dead or merely *incorporated* is a question that would not have a useful answer.

---

## The First Named Resident

The oldest person in the Throat is a woman the others call Substrate. She may be sixty. She may be eighty. She arrived — or so the oldest stories say — from outside, from the Dry, as a child small enough to be carried through the Gullet in someone's arms. If this is true, she is the only person alive who has seen the sky, though she has never said so and no one asks. Her skin is the color of wet heartwood. Her hair is white and grows in the same direction as the rhizomorphs in her sleeping crevice, as though she has been lying in one place long enough for them to confuse her with themselves.

She does not work. She does not plumb. She does not carve or tend or prune. She sits in the warmest part of the Throat, where three rhizomorph mats converge into a thick, breathing cushion of fungal tissue, and she hums. The humming is low — below the pitch of most human voices — and it resonates with the root-walls. When she hums, nearby pith-lamps glow slightly brighter. The sap-flow nearest her sleeping crevice has, over the decades, curved inward, developing a tributary that feeds the fungal mat she rests on. She is warm. She is fed by a tap that no one maintains — it maintains itself, its carved channel slowly widening as the root-flesh grows to accommodate it.

Substrate is not a leader. Leadership here is distributed, situational, tied to skill with particular systems. She is something else. The others do not have a word for it. When children are born — and children are born, though fewer than you might expect, because the cold takes some and the oxygen-poor corners take others and the silence takes the rest — they are brought to her first, before they are brought to their parents' crevice. She holds them. She hums at them. The root-walls nearest her seem to lean inward, just slightly, just enough to be felt rather than seen.

No one has asked her what she remembers. She has not offered.

## The Plumbers

There are twelve, currently. This is considered too few.

The plumbers are the ones who descend. They go into the Esophagus and below, into the Stomach and the Coils and the places that have not been named because naming them would imply an intention to return. They carry bone tools — chisels of femur, wedges of pelvis, the long curved needles of ribs threaded with sinew — and they wear no clothing on their hands, because the hands must feel everything. The temperature of sap. The vibration of water moving through a channel six inches behind the wall-surface. The tiny mechanical tremor of a thread tunneling toward them.

They do not speak when they work. They communicate by tapping: on the walls, on the floor, on each other's forearms. A tap-code that has evolved over generations, not designed but emergent, the way a flock of birds develops flight patterns without a conductor. Two taps means *stop*. Three means *water ahead*. A rapid flutter means *something is moving that should not be moving* and it is the signal that ends every deep expedition, without exception, without debate. When you hear the flutter-tap, you go back. You do not ask what was moving.

The eldest plumber is a thin man called Press, because he is known for pressing his ear to the walls and staying there, motionless, for hours. He says he can hear the root-network thinking. No one contradicts him, because Press has predicted the last four major shifts — chambers closing, new passages opening — with a precision that suggests he hears something real. What he hears, specifically, he cannot articulate. He says it is like hearing someone decide to turn in their sleep. Not words. Not even intentions. Just the pre-movement tension of something enormous considering a small adjustment.

Press has begun training a girl of eleven called Latch, who has unusually long fingers and who, when she was six, fell into the Esophagus and spent two days in the lower waterways before being found. She did not panic. She floated in the dark, in the root-water, feeling the current with her skin, and when the search party found her she was calm and she said *the water goes in a circle*. She was correct. The lower waterways are cyclic, fed by the root-system's hydraulic breathing, and no one had mapped this before because no one had been submerged in them long enough to notice.

Latch will be the best plumber the Throat has ever produced. Press knows this. He is gentle with her, and terrified of her, and he does not let her go below the Esophagus yet. She is patient. She practices her taps on the wall beside her sleeping crevice, pressing her fingers against the bark and feeling for the returning vibration of sap-flow. She can already tell, by touch alone, which direction the nearest thread is moving.

## The Threads

They are not pets. They are not livestock. They are not wildlife.

The threads are something the human residents have no category for, and the absence of category is itself significant, because these people have categories for nearly everything that lives in the root-network. They name things. They classify. It is a survival behavior — you must know what you are dealing with, whether it is a threat, a resource, or simply a cohabitant to be avoided. But the threads resist classification because they are not *in* the habitat. They *are* the habitat's circulatory maintenance system. They are, in some sense that the plumbers understand intuitively and cannot explain rationally, the root-network's way of taking care of itself.

They are pale — not white, not gray, but the color of something that has never needed pigment because light has never been a relevant variable in their existence. They are segmented, each segment slightly wider than the last, tapering at both ends. They have no eyes, no visible sensory organs, but they navigate the sap-channels with absolute precision, flowing through the root-network's vasculature like living plungers. When a channel is partially blocked — by a crystallized mineral deposit, by a collapsed section of heartwood, by the body of a dead breather that has fallen into a waterway — the threads converge. Dozens of them, drawn by chemical signals in the sap, flowing toward the obstruction. They dissolve what they can. They push through what they cannot. They rebuild channel walls with a secretion that hardens into a substance resembling heartwood, indistinguishable from the root-tissue itself.

The residents see them constantly. They emerge from wall-holes, from ceiling cracks, from the floors of basins. They are the length of a forearm, sometimes longer. They do not flee from human contact. They do not seek it. They flow around hands and feet the way water flows around stones — without hesitation, without curiosity, without any apparent awareness that the obstacle is alive.

Once, a child caught one. Held it in both hands. It was warm — warmer than the rhizomorphs, warmer than anything in the Throat — and it pulsed faintly, rhythmically, in sync with the sap-flow in the nearest wall. The child's hands went numb within seconds. Not painful numbness, but the deep numbness of a limb that has fallen asleep: the feeling of circulation being rerouted. The child released the thread. The numbness faded over an hour. The child's hands, for the rest of that day, tasted faintly of sap when she pressed her fingers to her lips.

The plumbers have a rule about threads: *Do not stop them. Do not move them. If one is blocking your passage, wait. It will finish.*

## The Breathers

They hang in the upper darkness. Most residents have never seen one clearly. What they know is this:

Sound behaves differently in chambers where breathers are present. The acoustic signature of a room changes — echoes become rounder, softer, as though the air itself has been padded. This is because the breathers' enormous skins absorb and re-release sound waves on the same slow rhythm as their respiration. A breather inflates — drawing air through its entire surface, filtering out particles of organic matter, moisture, fungal spores — and the sound in the chamber dims, mutes, becomes intimate. A breather deflates — releasing the filtered air in a slow, warm exhalation — and the sound sharpens briefly, as if the room has been momentarily cleared of fog.

They are the size of small rooms. Some are larger. In the Palate, the chamber above the Throat, there is a breather so large that it occupies a full third of the ceiling volume, and the residents have learned to read its cycles as a kind of clock. When it is inflated, the Palate is quiet and warm. When it is deflated, the Palate is brighter — because the moths that colonize its folds are disturbed by the contraction and scatter, and their reflective wings catch the pith-lamp light and scatter it, briefly, into something almost like illumination.

The breathers do not eat in any recognizable sense. They filter. They are strainers of atmosphere, extracting organic particulates — fungal spores, moth scales, the microscopic debris of thread-secretion, the dead skin cells of human residents — and incorporating them into their own tissue through a process so slow that it is invisible. A breather grows, over the course of years, by fractions of an inch. No one has ever seen one grow. But the oldest residents remember when the great breather in the Palate was small enough that you could see the ceiling bark above it.

When a breather dies — and this happens, rarely, every few decades — it is an event. The deflation is final. The skin goes slack and dark, and it sags from the ceiling in heavy folds, and the chamber's acoustics change permanently, and the moths leave, and the residents must decide whether to cut it down or allow the root-network to reclaim it. They always allow the reclamation. Within days, the threads arrive — hundreds of them, flowing upward in unprecedented numbers, colonizing the dead breather's mass. Within weeks, the tissue is indistinguishable from the root-ceiling. The moths return. They roost on what was, briefly, a corpse, and they feed on the moisture that condenses on new bark, and the chamber finds its rhythm again.

The residents do not mourn the breathers. But they notice the silence afterward, and they speak more quietly for a time, and some of the plumbers swear that the sap-flow in the nearest walls runs faster for a few days, as if the root-system is flushing something through.

## The Moths

They are almost too small to mention. They are almost too numerous to ignore.

The moths have no name among the residents. They are simply *there*, the way air is there. Their wings are small — no wider than a thumbnail — and coated with microscopic scales that reflect the faintest light with startling efficiency. In groups, they produce a scattered, shifting luminescence that the residents have learned to use peripherally: a moth-swarm near a wall indicates a warm spot, a sap-surface close to the bark's exterior, because the moths are drawn to the oxidation-scent of exposed sap. A moth-swarm near the ceiling indicates a breather that is about to cycle. A moth-swarm on the floor indicates a dead thread, because the threads' cooling bodies release moisture that the moths drink.

They are the only organism in the Gullet that moves in what surface-dwellers would recognize as an ordinary way: fluttering, directionless, individually purposeless. Everything else in this ecosystem has hydraulic patience, fungal slowness, the long-game pace of a root-system measuring its life in centuries. The moths are frantic by comparison. They dart. They collide with pith-lamps and smother themselves in oxidizing sap, dying in tiny amber glows. They mate in spirals, their reflective wings creating brief, swirling patterns of light in the upper darkness that children find beautiful and adults find distracting.

Latch, the young plumber, once tried to catch enough moths to make a light source — a lantern of sorts, a ball of captured moths in a cage of woven rhizomorph fibers. The moths cooperated, clustering around the warmth of her hands, filling the cage in minutes. The light was extraordinary: not amber like the pith-lamps but silver-white, shifting, alive. She carried it into the Esophagus. Halfway down, the moths went still. Not dead. Not sleeping. Still, as though they had collectively decided to stop. The light went out. She released them. They scattered into the darkness and did not return to her.

She does not try to catch them anymore. She says they told her something, and she listened.

## The Predators

No one has a good name for them. The gestures used to indicate them — a hand moving slowly through the air, fingers spread, then closing — are not a word but a warning.

They move through the root-walls. Not on the walls, not in the walls, but *through* them, flowing through the sap-channels at the same speed and in the same direction as the sap itself, so that they are invisible, undetectable, indistinguishable from the hydraulic life of the root-network until they stop. And when they stop, something is already caught.

They eat threads primarily. This is understood. The threads are numerous enough that losing some to predation is — like losing some to the root-network's immune responses — a tolerable cost of the ecosystem's functioning. But the predation of breathers is different. It is rarer, more methodical, and it suggests a creature of considerable size and intelligence. A breather does not die quickly. It deflates over the course of days, its skin darkening, its filtering cycle slowing, and the residents can feel the predator inside it — or rather, they can feel the *absence* where the predator has hollowed out a section of the breather's mass and replaced it with nothing. A void. A pocket of dead air in the chamber's living architecture.

The plumbers have a protocol for dead breathers: do not investigate the void. Do not reach into the slack, dark skin. Do not touch the edges of the hollow, because the edges are warm — warmer than the breather's tissue should be — and the warmth is not residual. It is active. Something is there, digesting slowly, and it does not appreciate interruption.

Press says he has heard them in the deep sap-channels. Not the breath-words — those are different, structural, rhythmic. The predators make a sound like sap-flow interrupted. A stutter. A hiccup in the hydraulic pulse. He hears it perhaps once a season, always from deep in the walls, always moving. He has never heard it stop near him. He is grateful for this, and he does not examine his gratitude too closely.

There is one rule about the predators that is older than any other rule in the Throat, older than the plumbing traditions, older than the pith-lamps, possibly older than human habitation of the root-network itself. It is carved into the oldest root-column in the Throat, in a script that no one can read but that everyone recognizes:

*Do not reach into what you cannot see the bottom of.*

The carving was not made by any current bone tool. The material removed from the grooves is heartwood, not surface bark. Someone — something — carved this warning from deep within the root-flesh, reaching inward through layers of living tissue to inscribe a message in the permanent, unchanging core of the tree.

The residents do not discuss who made the carving. The plumbers discuss it only among themselves, and only rarely, and only when they are deep enough that the conversation cannot be overheard by anyone who might be frightened by it.

## The Blind Fish

In the lower waterways — the Stomach, the Coils, the unnamed passages beyond — the root-water moves slowly enough for life. The fish are small: no longer than a finger, pale as the threads, eyeless, with translucent skin through which their simple vascular systems are visible as branching red lines that echo, disturbingly, the branching pattern of the root-network itself.

They are eaten. This is the primary protein source for the human residents, and the aquaculture basins in the deeper chambers are maintained with care that borders on reverence. The fish are netted with hand-woven sinew mesh, killed quickly by a specific pressure-point technique that the children learn before they learn to plumb, and eaten raw, because cooking is impossible. The flesh is mild, slightly sweet, with the same mineral undertaste as the water. It is not unpleasant. It is simply the taste of being inside something alive, translated into protein.

The fish do not see, but they sense — pressure changes, chemical gradients, the vibrations of the root-network's breathing. They school in patterns that correspond to the water's flow-cycles, gathering in eddies during the slow phase and dispersing during the surge. The residents have learned to predict the surges by watching the fish: when the school breaks apart, the water is about to move, and you should brace yourself against a root-column or be swept into a passage you did not intend to enter.

Latch, when she fell into the Esophagus at age six, said the fish were with her. Not following her, not avoiding her, but *with* her, schooling around her body as though she were a root-column in the current. She says they kept her oriented — she could feel their movements against her skin, and their movements told her which way the water was going, and which way was up.

She still goes to the lower basins sometimes, in the evenings, and sits with her hands in the water. The fish come to her fingers. They school around her wrists. She says they are warm, and the plumbers tell her the fish are cold-blooded, and she says *I know what I feel* and no one argues, because Latch fell into the dark for two days and came back calm, and that earns you the right to say what you feel without being corrected.

## The Fungal Layer

It is not separate from the residents. It is not separate from anything.

The rhizomorphs — the white, finger-like fungal structures that form the ground-layer — are the true base of the food web in the Gullet. They digest the organic debris that filters down from above: dead moths, shed thread-segments, human waste, the microscopic particulates that the breathers fail to capture. They convert this material into heat and into the spongy substrate that serves as bedding, insulation, and — in the deepest interpretation — the connective tissue between the human settlement and the root-network.

The rhizomorphs grow toward warmth. They grow toward the residents' sleeping crevices. They grow, over years, into the shapes of the bodies that press against them night after night. A sleeping crevice that has been occupied for a decade will have a rhizomorph mat that fits its occupant's body like a mold — the curve of a hip, the depression of a shoulder, the hollow behind a knee. When Substrate moves — rarely, but sometimes — the rhizomorph mat retains her shape for days, slowly filling in, and the other residents avoid stepping on the impression. Not out of superstition. Out of something harder to name. The impression is warm. It still breathes.

The liverworts are the upper layer — translucent, veined, hanging in curtains from every horizontal surface. They photosynthesize, barely, using the faint bioluminescence of the fungal layer below. This is a closed loop: the fungi glow, the liverworts grow, the liverworts shed organic material, the fungi consume it, the fungi glow. The residents prune the liverworts to control where light falls, and the act of pruning is the closest thing this community has to agriculture. It is done with care, with intention, with an eye toward the coming season's growth patterns.

Children are taught to read the liverworts before they are taught to plumb. A liverwort curtain that is thick and opaque means the fungal layer below is thriving. A curtain that is thin and patchy means something has disrupted the cycle — a dead breather, a shifted sap-channel, a predator's void too close to the surface. The liverworts are indicators. They are the first visible symptom of systemic change.

Substrate once told a child — this is one of the few things attributed to her speech — that the liverworts remember what the sky looks like. The child asked how, and Substrate said *they carry it in their veins* and then she hummed and the pith-lamps brightened and the child did not ask again.

## The Breath-Words

They are not voices. The plumbers are careful about this. They are not voices, they are not language, they are not communication. They are rhythmic pressure fluctuations in the root-walls that occasionally, in certain chambers, at certain times, produce patterns with the cadence and structure of meaning.

Press hears them most often. He hears them in the deep channels, when he is alone, when his ear is pressed against the sap-wall and the flow is slow and the chamber is quiet. What he hears is not sound in the conventional sense — it is felt rather than heard, a sequence of pressure changes that rise and fall in patterns too regular to be random and too complex to be mechanical. There are repeating motifs. There are variations on the motifs. There are silences between the motifs that feel intentional, like the pauses between breaths, like the rests in a piece of music that you have not heard before but recognize.

Press has begun tapping back.

He does this alone. He does this in the deepest reaches of the Coils, in a chamber that no one else visits, where the root-walls are so thick and so old that the sap-flow is barely perceptible, a slow pulse measured in minutes rather than seconds. He presses his ear to the wall. He listens. He waits for the pattern to emerge — and it does, always, eventually, though it can take hours. When it comes, he taps. Three taps, matching the rhythm of the three-beat motif he has heard most often. Then he waits.

The first time he tapped, nothing happened. The second time, the sap-flow changed — not immediately, not dramatically, but measurably, a slight acceleration that Press felt in the wall against his cheek. The third time, the change came faster. The fourth time — 

The fourth time, the breath-words stopped.

They stopped, and the silence that followed was different from ordinary silence. It was not empty. It was *attentive*. Press felt it as a change in the pressure gradient, as though the wall beneath his ear had shifted its weight, had leaned toward him, had focused.

He did not tap again that day. He went back to the Throat. He did not speak for three days. When Latch asked him what had happened, he said *I think it heard me* and then he said *I think it has been hearing us for a long time* and then he said nothing else.

He goes back. Every few days, he descends to the deep chamber. He presses his ear to the wall. Sometimes the breath-words are there. Sometimes they are not. When they are there, he taps. When they are not, he waits. He is patient. The root-system is very, very old, and Press is only sixty, and the difference between sixty and very, very old is a difference that Press is only beginning to understand.

Latch follows him once, at a distance. She does not enter the deep chamber. She waits in the passage outside, her hand on the wall, feeling the vibrations. She feels Press's taps. She feels the breath-words. She feels, once — briefly, ambiguously, in a way she cannot confirm — a third pattern, emerging from deeper in the wall, a pattern that does not match Press's taps and does not match the breath-words but has elements of both, as if something has learned the tapping and the breathing and is attempting to combine them.

She pulls her hand from the wall. She goes back to the Throat. She sits in the water basin and the fish school around her wrists and she thinks about what she felt and she does not tell Press, because Press is her teacher and she does not want to frighten him, and because she is eleven years old and she has already learned the most important lesson the Gullet has to offer:

*Do not reach into what you cannot see the bottom of.*

But the bottom, she thinks, may be reaching back.

## What Does Not Live Here

There are no ghosts. The residents are certain of this. There are no dead who persist, no memories that walk, no voices of the vanished. The people who go deep and do not return are gone, incorporated, dissolved into the root-network's living architecture. They are not here.

And yet.

The root-network has memories. Not conscious ones. Not intentional ones. But structural: wound-sites shaped like doorways, sap-channel branch patterns that repeat at different scales, the fractal recurrence of the Throat's layout in the hillside above and in the vasculature of a single root-column. The residents have noticed this. The plumbers have noticed this more than the others, because the plumbers go deeper and see the patterns at more scales, and Press has noticed it most of all, because Press listens and the patterns have sounds, and the sounds have rhythms, and the rhythms are old.

Older than the settlement. Older than the village the root-network swallowed. Older, possibly, than the hillside itself.

There is a question that the plumbers do not ask, because to ask it would be to admit that they have been living inside an answer all along. The question is: *whose memories are these?*

The root-network is one organism. One tree. One ancient, enormous, incomprehensibly complex single living thing that has been growing for longer than anyone has measured. It has absorbed a hillside. It has swallowed a village. It has incorporated sixty human beings into its ecology, tolerating them, feeding them, plumbing them, the way a body tolerates its own gut fauna — because they are useful, because they maintain the systems, because they keep the water flowing and the sap-channels clear and the pith-lamps burning.

But the root-network is not a machine. It is not a building. It is alive, and it has been alive for so long that the distinction between alive and conscious may have eroded beyond relevance. The breath-words are not language. The structural memories are not thoughts. The patterned recursions are not intentions.

And yet.

Substrate hums, and the pith-lamps brighten, and the rhizomorphs grow toward her, and the root-walls lean inward, and something very old and very large adjusts itself, minutely, around the small warm weight of a woman it has been holding since she was a child.

## Who Lives Here

Sixty people, more or less. Twelve plumbers, not enough. Threads by the thousands, unseen and essential. Breathers hanging in the upper dark, breathing for the chambers, breathing for the moths, breathing for the children who sleep below them in rhizomorph cradles shaped like the bodies that came before. Moths innumerable, frantic, glittering, the only creatures here that move the way light moves — briefly, directionally, without memory. Blind fish in the deep water, schooling around a girl's wrists, carrying her weight in the current. Fungal tissue, warm and slow, growing into the shapes of its inhabitants, remembering them after they move, remembering them longer than they expect.

And something else. Something in the walls. Something that is not the root-network but is not separate from it. Something that has been listening to the tapping, and the humming, and the breath-words, and the silence, for a very long time.

It has not tapped back.

Not yet.

But the sap-flow changes, sometimes, after the tapping stops. And Latch felt something in the deep passage that was neither Press nor the breath-words but had elements of both. And the root-network is very, very old, and it has swallowed a hillside and a village and sixty human beings, and it is still growing, still closing old passages and opening new ones, still incorporating, still digesting, still learning the shapes of the things that live inside it.

The people of the Gullet do not aspire to reach the Dry. They do not believe, most of them, that the sky exists. But they feel the water cycle — rain falling on a canopy they have never seen, drawn down through trunk and root and into the chamber where they sleep — and they taste the seasonality in the water, and some of them wonder, in the quiet hours when the breathers are inflated and the chamber is warm and the moths are still and the pith-lamps pulse like slow hearts:

*What if the sky is just another chamber? What if the Dry is just another room in something very old and very large that has not yet decided what to do with us?*

They do not discuss this often. To discuss it is to admit that the place they live inside is not just alive but patterned, and pattern implies something they are not ready to name.

But Latch, sitting in her basin with the fish around her wrists, is almost ready.

And Press, with his ear against the wall in the deep chamber, is almost ready.

And Substrate, humming in her rhizomorph cradle while the walls lean in and the pith-lamps brighten and the sap-flow curves toward her like a river remembering its source — Substrate has been ready for a very long time.

She is waiting for the rest of them to catch up.
