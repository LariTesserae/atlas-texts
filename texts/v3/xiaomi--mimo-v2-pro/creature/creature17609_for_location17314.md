The people of Plynthos are not loud. They do not need to be. To live here is to understand that the world speaks in a language of pressure, flow, and seepage, and the highest art is to listen.

They are **smaller** than you might expect, with a wiry, enduring strength. Generations of navigating narrow, slick-walled tunnels and climbing the switchback ramps have given them a low center of gravity and a particular, graceful economy of movement. Their hands are their most notable feature—broad, calloused, with a tactile sensitivity that seems almost preternatural. They can read the health of a channel by the vibration of the stone, detect a clog in a cistern by the changed pitch of a falling drop.

Their skin holds a cool, damp pallor, like the underside of a river stone, and their eyes are accustomed to the soft, diffuse light, often appearing a pale grey or green. They dress in woven cloaks of a dense, felted moss-fiber, excellent at repelling the constant mist and blending with the grey-green of the city. The hoods are often up, not against rain, but against the fine, ever-present condensation that seeks to coat everything.

They are a **hierarchy of listeners**. At the base, the **Runners**—young, nimble, and quick—are the city’s nervous system. They sprint the ramps and bridges, carrying not messages, but *observations*: the sediment load in the Upper Basins, the new weep-hole that opened on the Western Face, the rhythm of the Grand Cistern on the Third Terrace. They are trained to move silently, their bare feet making only soft, wet sounds on the stone.

Above them are the **Channelers**, the master engineers and plumbers. They are the ones who perform the delicate, sacred surgery of the city. With tools of polished basalt and iron-wood—wedges, scrapers, and long, hooked rods—they don’t *force* water to flow. They *negotiate* with it. A Channeler might spend a day slowly abrading a single groove in a stone lip, not to increase flow, but to alter the sound of the water’s fall, making its descent more melodious and less erosive. Their art is in shaping the chorus. A blockage is not blasted; it is coaxed, persuaded, eased away with a patience that spans days.

The highest are the **Listeners-in-Stillness**. Often the eldest, they occupy small, solitary cells carved into the deepest terrace walls, near the mountain’s heart. They do little manual work. They press their ears to the stone. They feel the deep, sub-audible groans and sighs of the aquifer itself. Their pronouncements are rare, cryptic, and absolute: *"The deep vein dreams of sleep. Open the Stone of Lament on the seventh terrace."* And so it is done, and the city’s lifeblood continues for another generation.

Their **culture** is one of hydrology. Children learn to read the patterns of water-staining on a wall as others learn letters. History is not recorded in books, which would mildew instantly, but in **Runes of Flow**—delicate, spiral glyphs carved beside channels that mark a drought, a great cleansing, a shift in the water table. Their greatest festivals are not for harvests, but for **The Great Absence**—a rare, day-long period when the weeping ceases, and the entire population moves in silent, reverent procession through the dry, echoing city, maintaining the channels in anticipation of the water’s return.

They are not a happy people, nor a sad one. They are a **balanced** people. Laughter is a rare, quiet, bubbling sound, quickly absorbed. Grief is a private thing, expressed by sitting for hours by a cistern, letting one’s tears fall into the communal water, returning one’s sorrow to the cycle. They share a profound, unspoken understanding: they are not masters of Plynthos. They are its **custodial organ**, the conscious part of a vast, stone-and-water body. Their lives are a single, sustained note in the Water Chorus—not the most powerful, but the one that gives the rest its meaning. They live, they tend, they listen, and in time, their bones will be ground to dust and packed into the channels, their calcium slowly dissolving to help seal a leak, forever a part of the weeping, patient stone.
