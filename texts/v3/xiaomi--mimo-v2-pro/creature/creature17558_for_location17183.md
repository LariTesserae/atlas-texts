# What Lives Here

Almost nothing, at first glance. And that is the first thing that must be said, because it is wrong.

---

The algae is alive and it knows its business. Not "knows" the way you mean it, but knows the way a stream knows to follow a gradient — with absolute certainty, with no possibility of doing otherwise. The bright green mats at the waterline are not a single thing but a community, a thin felted world of cyanobacteria and single-celled green algae pressed flat against the stone, each one oriented toward whichever of the three light shafts falls nearest. They have arranged themselves into a living cartography of illumination. Where the beams reach, the mats are thick and vivid, almost luridly green, slick with mucilage. Where the light fades — on the shadowed faces of blocks, on the underside of the ledge — the algae thins to a haze, a rumor, a faint brownish-green that you might mistake for staining. Between these extremes there is a gradient so precise it could be measured with instruments, but you can see it with your eyes: a map of light made by organisms that have nothing but light and mineral-laden water and the chemistry to turn one into the other.

They grow slowly. The water is warm but not tropical, mineral-rich but not extreme. The light comes for only part of the day. Still, they have been here long enough to build up in layers — the bottommost films are dead, calcified, incorporated into the very mineral crust that furs the walls above the waterline. The living algae grow on top of their own dead. They are building the room, in their small way. Adding to it. The tufa was laid by human hands, but the smooth pale frost on the upper walls is partly biological in origin — calcium carbonate precipitated out of the warm water, yes, but seeded and shaped by the metabolic activity of organisms that have been quietly, steadily, working the boundary between water and stone for years.

The root system dangling from the ceiling crack is alive too, though it shows nothing down here to prove it. Somewhere above this room, in the structure of whatever building or terrace or garden sits atop the reservoir, a plant has found a seam in the masonry and sent its roots downward through the damp stone. It drinks from the reservoir. It is not parasitic — it does not drain the chamber, which is fed from below and spills from the weir regardless. It takes only what condensation and capillary action bring it, water drawn upward through its pale threadlike roots by the pull of transpiration from leaves you cannot see. The roots are white, almost translucent, finer than sewing thread where they enter the water, and they sway in the current from the channel with a slow, meditative rhythm — back and forth, back and forth, never breaking, never tangling, flexible beyond what their delicacy would suggest. They have been here long enough to have colonized the crack fully. The roots branch as they descend, and some of the smallest tendrils have fixed themselves to the stone below the waterline, gripping the mortar joints with tiny holdfasts. Where they grip, the algae does not grow. The plant and the algae are in quiet competition for the same wet surfaces, and the plant holds its territory absolutely where its roots reach.

The small plants in the stone trays at the waterline are something else — placed, not volunteered. Someone set them here. They are low, leafy things, not quite herbs, not quite mosses, something that thrives at the margins of water: the kind of plant that grows on the edges of fountains in old cities, green and fleshy-leafed, rooting in shallow soil that stays perpetually damp. There are perhaps eight of them spread across six trays, though more trays sit empty. The plants are not all the same species. At least two varieties are present — one with rounded, almost succulent leaves pressed flat to the stone; another taller, with thin upright leaves that move in the faint air currents the warm water creates. A third, in the farthest tray, looks half-dead, its leaves yellowing and curled, struggling with something — too much mineral, perhaps, or too little light in that corner where the shafts don't reach well. The others seem content. They grow. Slowly, but they grow.

These plants are the remnants of an intention. Someone tended them. Someone descended the worn steps, checked the trays, maybe replaced soil, maybe brought new starts from wherever these species grow in the world above. The trays that sit empty still hold dark, damp earth — soil that was brought here, not native to the chamber, rich with the biology of the outside world. If you were to examine that soil closely, you would find it teeming: nematodes, tardigrades, rotifers, springtails — an entire microfauna imported with the dirt, surviving in the perpetual wetness, feeding on fungal hyphae and bacterial films that decompose the organic matter in the soil. The empty trays are not empty. They are merely unoccupied by anything visible. Beneath the surface, the soil is alive with a community of organisms so small they might as well be abstractions, each one performing its brief life in the dark wet earth, indifferent to the grandeur of the stone chamber around them.

And then there is the water itself.

The water that enters through the channel is not sterile. No water is. It comes from somewhere — a spring, a cistern, a larger aqueduct — and it carries with it its own population. Bacteria, predominantly. Not the dangerous kind. Not the kind that cause disease in whatever creatures built this place. These are thermophilic and mesophilic organisms adapted to warm, mineral-rich water: rod-shaped bacteria that oxidize iron, sulfur-oxidizing microbes that thrive in the alkaline conditions, filamentous bacteria that form delicate web-like structures on the underside of submerged stones where the current is slowest. You cannot see them. But if you were to take a sample of the water and place it under a microscope, you would find it populated — not thickly, not turbidly, but with a steady, quiet diversity of single-celled life going about the business of chemosynthesis and heterotrophy in water that is warm and rich with dissolved minerals.

These bacteria form biofilms on every submerged surface. The channel walls, the floor of the chamber, the lower courses of masonry block — all are coated in a thin, nearly invisible slime of bacterial life. The biofilm is structured, stratified: aerobic organisms on the surface, where the dissolved oxygen from the churning entry is greatest, and anaerobic organisms deeper in, in the still layers close to the stone, where they reduce sulfate or fix nitrogen or break down whatever tiny amount of organic matter drifts in from above. This biofilm is the foundation of the chamber's invisible ecology. Everything else that lives here — the algae, the microfauna in the soil, even the translucent shrimp-thing — depends on it, either directly or through the web of nutrient cycling it facilitates.

And yes. The shrimp-thing.

It was real. It was not a trick of the light, not a mote of sediment, not a fragment of root. It was a living organism, and it is still in the room, though you will likely not see it again without patience. It is an amphipod — small, nearly transparent, adapted to warm freshwater environments. It lives in the slow water near the walls, in the spaces between the submerged masonry blocks where the current eases and biofilm accumulates on the stone. It grazes. That is what it does: it moves along the submerged surfaces and scrapes biofilm and algae with small mouthparts, feeding on the microbial world that is itself feeding on the chemistry of the water. It is nearly invisible — its body is so translucent you can see its gut through its exoskeleton, a thin dark line of ingested biofilm running the length of its body. It is maybe eight millimeters long. It has been in this room for its entire life, or nearly so — it was likely born here, from an egg laid by an adult that was itself born here, in a lineage that has persisted in this chamber since the water first began to flow.

There are more than one. There are perhaps two dozen, though you will never count them, because they live in the crevices and only emerge into the open water to feed, and they freeze or dart away at the slightest disturbance — a shadow, a vibration, a change in the water's pressure. They are the only animal visible to the naked eye in this room, and they are so slight, so transparent, so reluctant to be seen that the room can reasonably be described as having "no animal life" and feel truthful. But they are here. They are always here. They have nowhere else to go, and the room provides everything they need.

There may be one more. In the deepest water, near the channel mouth, where the current is strongest and the churn is loudest, there is a slightly larger organism — also translucent, also nearly invisible, but with a different body shape, more elongated, with small appendages that beat in a rhythm distinct from the amphipods. It could be a freshwater copepod. It could be a larval form of something that completes its life cycle elsewhere, drawn into the chamber through the channel from the water source above. It lives in the turbulence itself, filtering particles from the rushing water, sustained by the constant flow that would exhaust or crush something larger. It is alone of its kind here, as far as you can tell. It has been alone for a long time, or it is one of a small number that cycle through the chamber as the water brings them in and carries them out through the weir. It does not seem to mind. It feeds. It persists.

---

Below all of this, below the threshold of sight even with instruments, the room is dense with life. Viruses — bacteriophages — infect and lyse the bacteria in the biofilm, releasing nutrients back into the water column in a constant low-level rain of cellular debris. Archaea in the deepest, most anaerobic layers of the biofilm perform methanogenesis, producing tiny bubbles of methane that cling to the underside of stones until they grow large enough to release and rise and dissolve or escape through the surface. Fungal hyphae thread through the soil in the planting trays and through the mortar joints of the lower masonry, decomposing organic matter and mineralizing nutrients. The entire chamber — stone, water, light, warmth, sound — is a habitat. Not a rich one. Not a diverse one by the standards of the world outside. But complete in its own way, and persistent, and in its quiet, microscopic, translucent fashion, *alive*.

The room was built to hold and move water. But water is never just water. It is a medium, a habitat, a world. And wherever there is warm water and light and stone, there will be life finding its way to the surfaces, gripping on, feeding, reproducing, dying, and feeding something else. This room has been doing its one job for a long time, and for almost as long, things have been living in it — silently, invisibly, in the margins and the crevices and the thin bright films on the stone, in a community so small and so quiet that a person can stand on the ledge and look out across the bright churning water and believe, sincerely, that nothing lives here at all.
