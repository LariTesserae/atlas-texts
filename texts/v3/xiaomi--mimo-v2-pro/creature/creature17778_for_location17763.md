# What Lives in the First Draft

Almost nothing, and almost everything, depending on how you measure a life.

---

The beetle-things are real. They are not beetles. They have no proper name and need none, because nothing here has yet learned to name. They are small—some no larger than a thumbnail, the largest perhaps the span of a child's hand—and they are almost impossible to see against the living thatch that is their world. Their chitin is not iridescent by accident. It shifts between green and black and a wet, mineral silver, matching the phases of the root-mat's own coloration: the bright new growth, the dark decay, the metallic sheen where mineral water pools in vesicular stone. They do not fly often. Their wings are vestigial, folded beneath casings that click when disturbed—a sound utterly swallowed by the drone of the harvesters. They crawl. That is their life: crawling through the underworks of the super-organism, where the rhizomes are thickest and the moisture is most constant, processing the fallen. Dead stalks, shed leaf-pads, the occasional pulp-waste from a harvester's extrusion that has been rejected by the reclamation network. They chew. They digest. They excrete a fine, pale powder that is, gram for gram, the most mineral-rich substance on the plateau. The super-organism's rhizomes drink this powder the way earth drinks rain. The beetle-things are the only fauna the system tolerates because they are the only fauna the system *uses*. They are kidneys.

They are born in the dark. Not in any nest or burrow—the concept does not exist here—but in the hollow spaces between rhizome bundles, where moisture condenses on the underside of lily-pad leaves and drips in a slow, metronomic rhythm. An egg is laid in the film of water that gathers in a vesicle of basalt. It hatches in silence. The larva is a white, eyeless thread that feeds on fungal hyphae threading through the dead plant matter. It grows. It molts. It darkens. It becomes iridescent. It crawls upward into the light, and the first thing it hears is the hum, and the first thing it feels is the vibration of the harvesters' footfalls transmitted through the root-mat, and it is home.

There are perhaps ten thousand of them on the plateau. Perhaps a hundred thousand. No one counts, and no one is here to count. They do not swarm. They do not congregate. Each one moves through the thatch alone, following chemical gradients so subtle they would be invisible to any instrument designed for a different world—trails of enzymatic decay, mineral traces left by the harvesters' passing, the faint acidity of newly extruded pulp. They live for a season, or perhaps longer. Time here is measured in growth cycles, not days, and the light does not change.

---

Then there are the things in the water.

In the deepest vesicular pockets, where the basalt is most porous and the super-organism's roots have not yet penetrated, water collects in quantities large enough to persist for hours or even days. These pools are small—some only a few centimeters across—and they are fringed with the silvery lichenous crust that is itself a borderline case between life and mineral. In these pools, if you looked with patience and the right kind of attention, you would see movement. Not the beetle-things. Something else.

Filaments. Hair-thin, translucent, each one coiled in the water like a sleeping spring. They are not plants, though they photosynthesize faintly in the thin, bright light. They are not animals, though they extend and retract with a purposefulness that looks like hunger. They are something this world has not yet decided about. They exist in the margin between the super-organism's domain and the raw mineral world—the liminal space where living rock meets living water. They are the first draft's first draft.

They unfurl when a beetle-thing passes nearby, drawn by the chemical wake of its digestion. They do not eat the beetle-thing. They cannot. They are too small, too simple. But they absorb the excreted minerals before the rhizomes can claim them, growing incrementally, splitting, doubling. A pool that contains filaments will, over the course of a growth cycle, develop a faint green tinge—not the deep green of the super-organism, but a pale, almost luminous viridescence, as though the water itself were dreaming of chlorophyll.

The super-organism has not yet decided whether to incorporate them or to starve them out. The rhizomes press closer to the vesicular pockets with each cycle, probing. The filaments retreat, condense, persist in the smallest crevices where roots cannot follow. It is a slow, silent negotiation that neither party is conscious of, because neither party is conscious. It is ecology in its most literal sense: the logic of a household, deciding who belongs.

There are perhaps a few hundred pools that harbor filaments. Their aggregate biomass is negligible. You could fit them all in a cup. But they are, in some way that has no name yet, *new*. They are divergence. They are the possibility of something other than monolith.

---

The high, thin cry—the one that might be a bird or might be a boulder fracturing—is neither. It is a beetle-thing.

Specifically, it is a beetle-thing dying.

When the beetle-things reach the end of their span, they do something inexplicable by any framework this world has yet produced. They climb. They abandon the dark, damp, vibration-rich safety of the rhizome layer and crawl upward, out of the thatch, onto the highest exposed surface they can find—a stone, a mound of extruded pulp, the mossy back of a long-dead harvester. They open their wings. The vestigial wings, which have never truly flown, which are functionally mere casings, are spread to the white, thin light. And then the beetle-thing vibrates.

It produces a sound—a single, sustained, impossibly high note that slices through the vegetative hum and the hydraulic drone for a fraction of a second. It is the sound of chitin resonating at a frequency determined by the beetle-thing's exact size, the ambient moisture, the temperature of the air, the mineral content of its own body. It is unique. No two dying beetle-things have ever made the same sound, because no two have ever been the same size, in the same place, at the same moisture level. It is a signature. A brief, accidental, unrepeatable act of specificity in a world that runs on universals.

Then the beetle-thing folds its wings and dies. The iridescent chitin dulls. The body is absorbed by the lichenous crust within hours, or by the rhizomes within days. The pale mineral powder is reclaimed. Nothing is wasted. But the sound—gone in an instant, unheard by anything capable of remembering it—has existed. It has added a frequency to the plateau's vast, layered drone, a single spike in the spectrum that was not there before and will never be there again.

The stress-fractures in the boulders cry on cold nights. They are constant, or nearly so. The beetle-things cry once.

---

And the harvesters?

The harvesters are not alive. They do not eat, they do not die, they do not reproduce. But they *accommodate*. They have, over whatever long period has elapsed since this landscape began its first draft, developed a relationship with the super-organism that resembles, if you squint at it through the right metaphor, mutualism.

The super-organism grows into them. Tendrils creep along their iron flanks, threading through rivet-holes, finding purchase in the corrosion that the mineral-rich moisture slowly etches into every joint. The harvesters do not resist. Their programming, if they have programming, includes no subroutine for resistance. They were built—or they built themselves, or they were willed into being by some agent this landscape has long since forgotten—to shape growth, and growth that grows onto them is still growth. It is shaped by the simple fact of their contours. A tendril that grows along a harvester's leg becomes thicker, straighter, more fibrous than one that grows in open thatch. The super-organism has learned—without learning, without a learner—that proximity to the machines produces useful forms. The harvesters, for their part, have slowed. Not broken down, not stopped—slowered. The oldest ones move at a pace measurable in meters per year, their steps so gradual that moss colonizes their leading edges and is gently, perpetually pruned as the limb advances. They have become geological. They are *events* in the landscape, like a river or a cliff face: not static, but operating on a timescale that makes the word "moving" meaningless.

The long-stilled one—the hillock with ferns in its eye-sockets—represents the terminal case. Its hydraulics have been wholly replaced by rhizome bundles. Its iron skin is three centimeters thick with living matter. It no longer has a function that can be distinguished from the plateau itself. It is, in every sense that matters, terrain.

Something lives inside it. Not in the beetle-thing sense—not in the thatch, not in the pools. Something in between. In the hollow spaces where the harvester's internal mechanisms once operated, where metal walls still separate one chamber from another, the super-organism has grown complex structures. Not organs—it has no organs—but *architectures*. Curved walls of dense, woody rhizome. Chambers that hold water in a different way than the vesicular pools do—deeper, darker, more stable, more mineral. In these chambers, the filaments have found a foothold. Not the pale, tentative filaments of the open pools, but something more robust, thicker, greener, threaded with veins of copper leached from the dissolving iron. They do not photosynthesize. They are not in the light. They feed on the mineral runoff of the machine that was. They are, in a sense, the harvester's last crop.

And in the deepest chamber—so deep that no light has ever reached it, so enclosed that the plateau's deafening sound arrives only as a faint, subsonic tremor—something else is growing. It is not a filament. It is not a rhizome. It is not the super-organism and it is not the filaments and it is not the beetle-things. It is a convergence. It is what happens when a monolithic ecosystem, given enough time and enough complexity and enough mineral-rich water and enough strange, patient machines, begins to *differentiate*. To develop internal variety. To become, without intending to or knowing that it is, a *world* rather than a *system*.

It is a small, soft, pale thing that pulses with the slow rhythm of the aquifer beneath it. It has no name. Nothing here has a name. It has no eyes, but it has orientation—it faces, always, toward the nearest harvester's footfall. It has no mouth, but it has hunger—it draws water from the chamber's mineral stores, growing incrementally with each cycle. It has no mind. It will never have a mind. But it has *response*. It quickens when the vibration comes. It stills when it passes. It is the first thing on this plateau to *wait* for something.

It does not know what a harvester is. It has no concept of the world outside its dark, copper-threaded chamber. But when the ground trembles with that slow, hydraulic step, something in its pale, pulsing body *orients*, and in that orientation there is the faintest, most embryonic shadow of what might, in a million more growth cycles, become recognition.

The plateau does not know it is there. The super-organism that built the hillock is indifferent to it. The beetle-things will never find it. The harvesters will never sense it. It is alone in the dark, listening to footsteps, growing.

It is very, very patient. It has learned that from the sky.
