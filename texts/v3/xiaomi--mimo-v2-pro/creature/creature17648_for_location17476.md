The people of the Cauldron Towns are not born of stone, but they are cast by it. They are a slow, deliberate species of maintenance. Their lives are measured in pump-cycles and the growth rate of pale mycelia. You see it in their walk—a gait adapted to low ceilings and narrow passages, a forward lean, shoulders slightly rounded as if in perpetual preparation to duck. Their skin has a perpetual damp sheen, not just from sweat but from the very air, which coats them in a micro-layer of mineral water. They do not glisten; they *weep* with the walls.

They are not a family, not in the upper-world sense. They are an apparatus. The bonds between them are the bonds of necessary function, as precise and interlocking as the replacement gears they lathe in the workshop. There is affection here, but it is of a specific, practical kind: the steady hand that passes a tool without being asked, the shared understanding of when a pump’s cough turns pathological, the silent rotation of who sleeps in the slightly cooler upper berth. They speak in a shorthand of necessity. A phrase like “gasket on the west feeder” carries not just information, but a shared weight of consequence—the implied “or we all thirst.”

Their bodies are maps of their labor. Hands are either calloused and stained with metal filings from the workshop, or perpetually pruned and soft from tending the cisterns and mushroom beds. Scars are common, but they are clean, sharp things from slipping tools on hard basalt, not the ragged wounds of a soft world. They move with an economy born of living in close quarters; no gesture is wasted, no motion superfluous. To be careless is to risk not just yourself, but the delicate, sweating infrastructure of everyone.

They have no leaders, only focal points of competence. The one who best reads the resonance of the pumps. The one whose hands can fit a new ceramic gasket into a decades-old housing with the surety of a priest. Authority is fluid, ceded to whoever’s skill best answers the crisis of the moment. Decisions are made in the pauses between hammer-strikes, in the shared silence while listening to a pump’s rhythm, in the communal sweat of the forge-chamber.

Time is not marked by days or nights, but by *states*: the state of the pumps (thumping, coughing, silent). The state of the cisterns (full, draining, stagnant-that-must-not-be). The state of the mushroom beds (pinning, fruiting, spent). Their history is not in stories, but in the wear-patterns on the passage walls, in the layered patch-jobs on the pipes, in the fact that the oldest welding on the platforms uses a different, cruder bead than the new. They have no past, only a present condition of maintenance.

Their relationship with the heat is intimate. It is not an enemy; it is the fundamental condition, as inarguable as gravity. They do not fight it. They *negotiate* with it—slowing their movements, drinking the warm, mineralized water, pressing their backs against the cooler (by a fraction) basalt of the upper chambers. The heat has baked a kind of profound, silent patience into them. Panic is a waste of energy, and energy here is measured in calories and sweat. Complaint is a form of friction, and this system cannot afford friction.

They look at the uninitiated with a placid, unreadable gaze. It is not hostility, nor welcome. It is assessment. They are calculating where you fit in the pump-cycle, how much water you will consume, whether your shoulders will fit through the passages, what your hands might be good for. To be here is to be *used*. The town uses them, and they use each other, and this is not a tragedy but a tautology of existence.

The deepest rule—the iron-lattice gate—is obeyed not out of fear, but out of a kind of metaphysical pragmatism. The sound from beyond it is the system’s *input*, the raw heat and pressure that makes their whole sweat-slicked economy possible. To open the gate would not be to discover a mystery, but to disrupt a vital process. They do not personify it as a god or a demon. It is simply the furnace. You do not climb into the furnace. You tend the valves and pipes that draw from it. Their reverence is expressed in perfect, silent adherence to this boundary.

In the dim glow of a lamp in a sleeping berth, one might trace the outline of a chisel mark on the wall with a damp finger, not in wonder, but in a silent communion with the last hand that held the drill. They are the latest layer of use, the most recent organism to colonize this hot, sweating geology. They are not building a legacy. They are maintaining a condition. And in the rhythmic cough of the pumps, in the plink of condensation, in the shared breath of saturated air, there is a kind of communion—not with each other, but with the very principle of continued existence against the odds. They are the town’s metabolism. And to cease their work would be to cease being.
