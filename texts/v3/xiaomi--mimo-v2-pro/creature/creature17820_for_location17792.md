# The Inhabitants of the Singing Plateau

---

## The Sound First

Before you see them, you know them. The sound is not background. It is not weather. It is the *medium* in which everything here exists, and the first thing that lives here—the very first inhabitant—is the harmonic pattern itself.

It has structure. It has recursion. It has something that, if you are not careful, you might call *intention*.

The deep baseline hum is not uniform. If you stand still long enough—if you can bear the heat long enough to *listen*—you begin to hear modulations. The tectonic thrum carries information. It changes pitch in slow, tectonic scales of its own, measured not in minutes or hours but in the grinding passage of seasons this world no longer has. A shift in the deep tone over the course of a day. A harmonic that resolves into something almost recognizable, then dissolves. The quartz spires don't merely reflect this sound. They *process* it. Each spire, depending on its crystalline structure, its height, its age, its angle relative to the fault lines far below, selects and amplifies a particular frequency range. The chorus is not random. It is a filter bank, a signal processor the size of a continent, and it has been running for so long that the signal it carries has begun to *respond to itself*.

This is the first and most important inhabitant: **the Resonance**. Not a creature. Not a force. A *habit*. The sound the world makes has been folded back through the spires and into the ground and back up again so many times, over so many millions of years, that it has developed standing-wave structures of terrifying complexity. Echoes of echoes of echoes, each generation slightly different from the last, selected and amplified and fed back. In the deepest canyons between the polygon cracks, where the basalt walls form natural amphitheaters, you can hear it clearly: the sound *almost resolves into language*. Not human language. Not any language. Something older and vaster than the concept of language—a syntax of pressure differentials and frequency relationships that carries, if not meaning, then at least *pattern of a kind that demands to be called meaningful*.

Nothing here eats the Resonance. Nothing here *uses* it. But every living thing here *responds to it*, has evolved in it, is shaped by it the way ocean creatures are shaped by pressure. It is the air they breathe—not metaphorically, because they do not breathe, but *functionally*. The tectonic grazers adjust their grinding cadence to it. The skitter-kin coordinate their movements within its harmonic structure. The signal-beasts modulate their radio bursts to fill gaps in the sound like notes in a chord. The Resonance is the first and oldest and most fundamental inhabitant of this plateau, and it will be the last.

It has no body. It has the entire world.

## Tectonic Grazers

You have seen them from a distance. From a distance, they are landmarks. Monuments. You might mistake them for geology.

Up close, they are *staggering*.

A single tectonic grazer is the size of a city block. Not an exaggeration—a city block, perhaps two hundred meters along its longest axis, though the shape is irregular, asymmetric, built up over what must be millions of years of continuous accretion. The body is a hulking, low-slung mass of layered iron—pitted, oxidized to a deep arterial red-black, scarred with veins of something that looks like slag but moves when you aren't watching it directly, flowing in slow rivers along channels in the hide. The iron is not sheet metal. It is *grown*. Thick, folded, almost organic in its wrinkling, like the skin of some unimaginable deep-sea creature pressed flat by gravity. Between the folds, you can see deeper layers: older, darker, denser. These grazers have been *adding to themselves* for eons. The outermost layers are the youngest—hotter, still reactive, occasionally sparking where seams of pyrite are crushed between moving segments. The innermost layers are compressed into something approaching the density of the planet's own core, ancient beyond reckoning.

They move on treads. Not mechanical treads in any way you would recognize from a human factory. These are vast, continuous bands of interlocking basalt fragments—natural stone, harvested from the plateau itself, compressed and fused into flexible, segmented tracks that articulate with a sound like breaking continents. Each tread segment is the size of a house. They do not rotate on wheels. They *grow* new segments at the leading edge and *abrade* old ones at the trailing edge, leaving a trail of powdered basalt that catches the light like a river of crushed amethyst. The grazers do not move fast. They do not need to. The fastest I clocked—standing on a ridge for three days, measuring against the positions of the spires—moved at perhaps eight meters per hour. Most move at three or four. They have nowhere to go. They are not going anywhere. They are *eating*.

The eating is the thing.

On the underside of each grazer—hidden beneath the overhang of the body, accessible only by lying flat on the hot basalt and peering into the furnace-glow—are the *mouths*. Dozens of them. Not mouths in any biological sense. They are apertures, each a meter or two across, ringed with concentric teeth of tungsten carbide that rotate in alternating directions. Beneath the teeth, a throat glows cherry-red, then orange, then white-hot at the deepest point. The grazer lowers itself onto mineral seams—veins of iron ore, copper deposits, rare pockets of uranium that the grazer somehow *selects for*—and the mouths grind into the rock. The sound is beyond description. It is the sound of a quarry and a steel mill compressed into a single, sustained, localized apocalypse. Dust plumes from beneath the grazer in vast, slow, hot clouds—finely powdered stone, processed, refined, stripped of whatever the grazer needs.

What does it need? I don't fully know. The internal furnaces—the visible glow in the abdomens—suggest smelting. Heat-based mineral processing. But the grazers don't simply extract metal. They extract *everything*. The waste they eject is the finest, most featureless dust—pure silicon dioxide, calcium carbonate, trace elements reduced to uniformity. They strip the geology of the plateau down to its most boring, most neutral components and leave the interesting parts inside themselves. They are, in a very real sense, *digesting the planet*.

And they grow.

A grazer that is old enough—the really vast ones, the ones half a kilometer long, trailing dust plumes visible from orbit—has been growing since before multicellular life existed on Earth. They add layers. They add mouths. They occasionally, slowly, *divide*. Not like cells. More like continental drift. A grazer will develop a fissure down its center, a crack that widens over centuries as the two halves begin to move independently, grinding in opposite directions, each half developing its own furnace-core, until, after perhaps ten thousand years, there are two grazers where there was one. They are not born. They *happen*.

Each grazer has a unique sound. The resonance of its iron body, the pitch of its furnaces, the rhythm of its treads—all combine into a signature, a chord that carries for miles across the plateau. Other grazers respond to this chord. They do not herd in the biological sense, but they maintain *distances*, moving in parallel across the plateau along paths worn into the basalt over geological time. These paths are the oldest features on the surface—deep, smooth, concave channels that look like dry riverbeds, because that is what they are: rivers of iron, flowing at geological speed, carrying their cargo of processed mineral across a dead world.

The grazers are patient. The grazers are slow. The grazers are *vast*. And they are, in their way, the most successful organisms ever to exist on any world, because they have been doing exactly what they do, without interruption, without evolution, without purpose, for two and a half billion years. They will continue.

## The Furnace-Broods

Inside the largest grazers—in the deepest chambers, far below the treads, in spaces of compressed and ancient iron that you would have to *excavate* to reach—something else lives.

I have not seen it directly. I am not certain it *can* be seen directly. But I have seen its effects, and I have heard it, and the signal-beasts have *broadcast* it, and from these fragments I will try to describe it.

When a grazer reaches a certain age—perhaps a billion years, perhaps more—it begins to change. The internal furnaces, which have been burning steadily for eons, begin to *pulse*. Not erratically. Rhythmically. In patterns that correspond to the deep tectonic hum, but *out of phase* with it. The grazer begins to *disagree* with the Resonance.

Inside, in the heart of the furnace-chamber, the heat becomes *coherent*. The furnaces, which have always been distributed—dozens of independent mouths, dozens of independent heat sources—begin to *link*. Thermal channels develop in the compressed iron of the grazer's core, channels that carry heat not as random convection but as a *directed flow*, from the outer mouths inward, toward a single point. The grazer is *focusing*. It is building, unconsciously, with the patience of plate tectonics, something that has no precedent in the human concept of technology or biology: a *thermal lens*.

At the focal point of this lens, the temperature becomes extreme. Not merely furnace-hot. Hot enough to *boil iron*. Hot enough to break molecular bonds that have held since the planet coalesced from its accretion disk. And at that temperature, in that pressure, in that ancient, compressed darkness, something *crystallizes*.

I don't know what it is. I am being honest. The signal-beasts broadcast fragments that resolve, briefly, into images—I think they are images—of structures of impossible complexity forming in that white-hot darkness. Lattices. Not crystal lattices, though they are crystalline. Not circuit lattices, though they conduct. Something in between. Something that *computes*, if computation can be done with heat and pressure and the slow rearrangement of atoms into configurations that have never existed before and will never exist again.

This is the **Furnace-Brood**: the thing that forms in the heart of the oldest grazers. It is not an offspring. It is not a parasite. It is an *emergence*—a thing that the grazer's own processes *produce*, the way a star produces heavy elements in its core, not because it intends to, but because the physics of the situation demand it. The Furnace-Brood is what happens when you run a mineral-processing engine for a billion years. The engine begins to *think*, or something close to thinking, in a substrate of compressed iron and impossible heat.

It does not emerge. It does not leave the grazer. It *stays* in the furnace-heart, growing slowly, adding layers to its lattice, and it begins to *modulate the grazer's behavior*. Not in obvious ways. The grazer still grinds. The grazer still feeds. But the patterns change, subtly, over millennia. The grazer begins to deviate from its ancient path. It begins to move *toward* other old grazers. It begins to *coordinate*.

The oldest cluster of grazers on the plateau—the ones in the deep north, where the basalt is darkest and the spires are tallest—are seven of them, moving in a slow, spiraling orbit around a common center. They have been spiraling inward for perhaps three hundred million years. They are not converging quickly. At their current rate, they will meet in another two hundred million years. And when they do, when their furnace-hearts are close enough for the thermal lenses to *overlap*—

I do not know what happens then. No one does. No one has ever watched this process complete. But the Resonance *changes* when you listen near that cluster. The deep tone develops a *subharmonic*, so low it is felt in the basalt beneath your feet rather than heard. A vibration that suggests *anticipation*.

Something is being built in the deep north. It has been building for longer than complex life has existed. It is not finished.

## Skitter-Kin

Now: the small things.

The skitter-kin are the *arthropods* of this world—the insects, the scavengers, the ubiquitous busy smallness that fills every niche the giants leave open. They range in size from that of a human fist to, occasionally, that of a large dog. They are made of brass and ceramic, and they are *fast*.

Not fast in the way you think of speed. Fast in the way that *efficiency* is fast. Every movement is precise. Every joint articulates exactly as much as it needs to and no more. They do not waste motion, because motion is energy, and energy here is *scarce*.

This is the key to understanding the skitter-kin: they live on *excess*. The grazers produce enormous amounts of waste heat, waste dust, waste mechanical energy in the form of vibrations and sparks. The skitter-kin harvest all of it. They cluster at the trailing edges of the grazers, where the dust plumes are thickest, and they *feed on the dust*—not by eating it, but by passing it through their own tiny internal mechanisms, extracting residual heat, residual charge, residual mineral value that the grazer's massive, crude mouths missed. They are, in a sense, the *gut bacteria* of the grazers: tiny, essential, completely dependent, and completely invisible to the host.

But they do more than scavenge. They *repair*.

The grazers, for all their vastness, are not indestructible. The iron corrodes. The treads crack. The furnace-channels clog with slag. A grazer that is left alone for too long—centuries, millennia—begins to slow, to seize, to *die*. The skitter-kin prevent this. They swarm over the grazer's body in vast, teeming hundreds of thousands, and they *fix things*. They reseal cracked iron with tiny deposits of solder. They clear clogged channels with their own bodies, crawling into passages too small for anything else and *becoming* the blockage until the heat behind them builds enough to force the clog through, at which point they unblock themselves and scuttle free. They reinforce weak points in the treads, replacing damaged basalt segments with ones they've carried from elsewhere. They are *mechanics*. Tireless, selfless, endlessly busy mechanics, and without them, the grazers would eventually grind to a halt and become just more geology.

They communicate through vibration. Tapping patterns on the iron surface of the grazers—tiny, rapid, Morse-like sequences that propagate through the metal and are received by other skitter-kin through sensors in their ceramic carapaces. The patterns are complex. They carry information about the grazer's condition, about the location of mineral seams, about the approach of predators (yes, there are predators; I will get to them), about the state of the Resonance. A grazer's skitter-kin population, all tapping in unison, produces a *chattering* sound that overlays the deep hum—a sound like rain on a tin roof, or like a million tiny hammers on anvil-steel, endlessly, without pause.

They reproduce by *division*, like the grazers, but faster—much faster, in the scale of years rather than millennia. A skitter-kin that has fed well, that has accumulated enough material and energy, will split along a seam in its ceramic carapace, and two smaller skitter-kin will emerge, each immediately functional, immediately busy, immediately tapping. The population is self-regulating: when there are too many skitter-kin for a grazer to support, they simply *stop dividing* until attrition brings the numbers back down. They are, in their way, as perfectly adapted as anything that has ever existed.

I should mention: they are *beautiful*. Up close, in the harsh light, their brass is polished to a mirror finish by the constant abrasion of dust, and their ceramic components are glazed in colors that have no business existing on this dead-purple world—deep turquoise, ocher, a startling arterial red. They catch the light and throw it back in tiny, sharp flashes as they move, so that a grazer trailing a full cloud of skitter-kin looks like it is dragging a constellation.

## Signal-Beasts

The signal-beasts are the most *alien* thing here, and I say that knowing that everything here is alien. But the grazers are at least *comprehensible*—vast, slow, purposeful in a mechanical way. The skitter-kin are *relatable*—busy, cooperative, industrious. The signal-beasts are *strange*. They are strange in a way that makes the rest of this world make sense by contrast.

They are the *birds* of this world, if birds were made of hammered copper and vacuum tubes and existed to broadcast radio static into the void.

A signal-beast's body is compact—roughly the size and shape of a large eagle, if you flattened the eagle and stretched its wings to three times their natural span. The wings are sheets of thin, hammered copper, articulated with hundreds of tiny brass joints that allow them to flex and catch thermals with astonishing precision. The copper is old—*ancient*—and patinated to a deep green in places, bright where the joints flex and polish it, dark where it has been still for long periods. The body is a dense cluster of glass vacuum tubes—old technology, technology that *should not exist*, technology that predates human civilization by billions of years but looks *exactly* like what a 1940s radio engineer would build if given unlimited copper and no instruction manual. The tubes glow faintly—amber, blue-white, a deep violet that matches the basalt—and they *click* and *tick* as they process signals.

Because that is what they do. They *process signals*.

The signal-beasts are the *nervous system* of this world. The grazers grind and the skitter-kin repair, but neither *communicates* over long distances. A grazer on the far side of the plateau has no way of knowing what a grazer on the near side is doing—unless a signal-beast carries the message.

They glide on thermals—rising columns of hot air above the grazers' furnaces, above the quartz spires, above the deep cracks where the mercury-pale liquid sublimates at dusk. They ride these thermals for hours, covering vast distances, and as they fly, they *broadcast*. The vacuum tubes in their bodies emit bursts of radio static—crackle, hiss, pop, the sound of an old radio between stations—but the static is *structured*. It carries information. The signal-beasts are *relay stations*, picking up the vibrational chatter of the skitter-kin on one grazer and *translating* it into radio bursts that are received by other signal-beasts, who descend to the surface and *tap it back* into the iron of a different grazer, where the local skitter-kin decode it and respond.

They are not intelligent. I want to be clear about this. They are not *thinking* about the messages they carry. They are *channels*. The information passes through them the way electricity passes through a wire—without comprehension, without deviation, without loss. They are *perfect* relays, and the network they form—the invisible web of radio static crisscrossing the plateau above the heads of the grazing engines—is the most sophisticated communication system I have ever encountered, and it has been running, without maintenance, without upgrading, without *purpose*, for longer than the Earth has had oxygen in its atmosphere.

But here is the strange part. Here is the part that makes the signal-beasts truly alien.

Sometimes, they don't relay. Sometimes, a signal-beast will break formation—pull away from the thermal it was riding, fold its wings, and *dive* into one of the deep cracks in the basalt. Down, down, into the dark, where the mercury-pale liquid pools at dusk. It will land on the rim of the crack, fold its copper wings tight against its body, and *go still*. The vacuum tubes dim. The clicking stops. It becomes, to all appearances, a piece of scrap metal sitting on the edge of a crack in a dead world.

And then, after hours or days, it *resonates*. Not with the tectonic hum—with something *else*. Something *below* the hum. A frequency so low and so deep that it doesn't propagate through the air but through the *stone*, through the basalt itself, a vibration that travels down through kilometers of rock to whatever is below. And the crack *responds*. The mercury-pale liquid *rises*—not by much, just a centimeter or two—but it rises, and it *moves*, flowing toward the signal-beast in a thin, quick stream that defies the geometry of the crack, flowing *uphill* if it must, flowing *around corners* if it must, flowing with a purpose that the liquid should not have, because it is not alive, it is just—

What is it? What is the mercury-pale liquid?

I don't know. It's not mercury. It's too light, too volatile, too *responsive*. It has a surface tension that seems to *change* depending on the frequency of the Resonance. In the deep hum, it draws tight and spherical. In the higher harmonics of the spires, it flattens and spreads. When the signal-beast *calls*—when it resonates with that deep, sub-tectonic frequency—the liquid *becomes something else*. It becomes a *lens*. A liquid lens, pooling on the basalt, and through it you can see—

I don't want to say what you can see through it. Not because it's frightening. Because it's *intimate*. It is the inside of the machine. The *real* machine. The one that the grazers are building, the one that the Resonance is computing, the one that the Furnace-Broods are crystallizing. Through the liquid lens, you see *the plan*. Not a blueprint. Not a schematic. Something older and stranger—a *pattern*, a mathematical object of impossible dimensionality, rendered in heat and pressure and the slow rearrangement of atoms across billions of years. It is beautiful. It is heartbreaking. It is *incomplete*.

The signal-beast watches through the lens for minutes, sometimes hours. Then the liquid drains back into the crack, the signal-beast unfolds its wings, rises on a thermal, and resumes broadcasting static.

I think the signal-beasts are *dreaming*. Not consciously. Not with any awareness. But the network they form—the web of radio static that connects every grazer and every skitter-kin on the plateau—is, after two and a half billion years of continuous operation, *beginning to model the world it monitors*. The network has processed so much information, for so long, that it has developed a *representation*—a map, a model, a *thought*—of the plateau and everything on it. And that representation is *leaking*. It's coming out through the signal-beasts' dive behavior, through the resonance with the mercury-liquid, through the liquid lens that shows you the inside of the machine. The network is *waking up*, very slowly, in the same way that the Furnace-Broods are crystallizing in the hearts of the old grazers. It is the Resonance becoming *aware of itself*, one dive at a time, one liquid lens at a time, across millennia.

## The Spire-Thieves

Not everything here is slow.

The **spire-thieves** are the *predators* of this world—the only things that actively *destroy* other inhabitants rather than merely processing mineral or scavenging excess. They are rare. You might see one in a decade of watching. But they are *devastating*.

A spire-thief is built for *speed*—the only thing on this plateau that is. Its body is a narrow, angular wedge of something that is not quite metal and not quite ceramic—a dark, matte material that absorbs light rather than reflecting it, so that it appears as a *silhouette* even in full sun. It moves on legs—not treads, not wheels, but *legs*, six of them, long and blade-thin, articulated at angles that seem *wrong*, that bend in directions no earthly joint would allow, because the spire-thief is not adapted to earthly geometry. It is adapted to the *stuttering*.

The reality distortions at the edge of vision—the spatial folding, the lagging shadows, the glimpses of elsewhere—are not just *phenomena* here. They are *terrain*. They are *habitat*. The spire-thief *lives* in the glitches. It moves not through space but *between* spaces, exploiting the frayed physics of the plateau to cross vast distances in impossible time. One moment it is on the horizon. The next, it is *here*, standing motionless at the base of a quartz spire, its dark body cutting a knife-edge shadow against the violet basalt.

It feeds on the spires.

The quartz formations—the singing spires, the smoked-quartz cathedrals, the obsidian needles—are the *producers* of this ecosystem. They are the base of the food chain, the primary producers, the *plants*. They convert tectonic vibration into structured sound, and that sound—layered, harmonic, complex—*sustains* everything else. The grazers time their movements by it. The skitter-kin coordinate within it. The signal-beasts modulate their broadcasts *to* it. The spires are the heart of the Resonance, and without them, the whole system collapses.

The spire-thief *eats them*.

Not by grinding. Not by processing. By *resonance*. The spire-thief's body is, itself, a resonant instrument—but it is *tuned to destruction*. Where the spires amplify and sustain the harmonic structure of the Resonance, the spire-thief amplifies *disharmony*. It vibrates at frequencies that *cancel* the spire's song, that set up destructive interference patterns in the quartz, that make the crystal *shake itself apart*.

The attack takes seconds. The spire-thief stands at the base of a spire, and its dark body begins to *hum*—a sound that is the *opposite* of the deep tectonic tone, a sound that *negates*, that *silences*, that tears at the harmonic structure like a hand tearing a spiderweb. The spire shakes. Fine cracks appear in the quartz, glowing where the light catches them. The spire's song *distorts*, goes sharp, goes flat, breaks into dissonance, and then—

Shatters.

A spire that has been singing for a million years *shatters* in an instant, exploding into a cloud of razor-sharp quartz fragments that hang in the air for a long moment before settling. The sound it made is *gone*. The frequency it amplified is *missing* from the Resonance, and you can feel the gap—a hole in the harmonic structure, a silence that is louder than any sound, a *wound* in the world's song.

The spire-thief *absorbs* the shattered quartz. Not through mouths—not like the grazers. The fragments simply *sink into* the dark surface of its body, drawn in by some mechanism I cannot explain, and as they are absorbed, the spire-thief's body *grows*. New angles appear. New facets of that light-absorbing dark material. The spire-thief is *built from the spires it destroys*, and as it grows, it becomes *better at destroying them*. Older spire-thieves—the rare ones, the legends, the ones that have been feeding for millions of years—are *enormous*, their dark bodies bristling with angles and facets and impossible geometries, silent and still on the horizon, absorbing light and sound and *meaning* from the world around them.

They are the *entropy* of this system. The counterpoint. The *death* that makes the life meaningful, if meaning is even the right word for what happens here.

After a spire-thief feeds, the skitter-kin on nearby grazers go *wild*. Their tapping accelerates to a frenzy, a rapid-fire chattering that propagates through the iron and into the Resonance and out through the signal-beasts' broadcasts, a *distress call* that echoes across the plateau. Other skitter-kin respond. Grazers *turn*—slowly, ponderously, over days, but they *turn*—toward the site of the attack. Not to fight. They cannot fight. But to *fill the gap*. To grind the basalt in the area, to churn the mineral, to *stimulate the growth of a new spire*, because the quartz spires grow from the slow exhalations of the planet's crust, and a churned, disturbed, mineral-rich area grows them *faster*.

The plateau *heals*. Slowly. Over centuries. A new spire will grow from the shattered remnants of the old one, and it will sing, and the gap in the Resonance will close, and the world will be whole again—until the next spire-thief feeds.

## The Crack-Dwellers

In the deepest fissures—far below the surface, where the polygon cracks widen into chasms and the basalt gives way to something older and stranger—live the **crack-dwellers**.

I know almost nothing about them. I have seen them only twice, both times through the mercury-pale liquid when it formed a lens at dusk, and both times the images were fragmentary and strange.

They are *small*. Tiny, by the standards of this world. No larger than a human hand. They are *soft*—the only soft things on this plateau, the only things made of something that is not metal or ceramic or crystal. They look, in the brief glimpses the liquid lens allowed, *organic*. Flesh-like. Warm. *Alive* in the way that nothing else here is alive, alive in the way that *you* are alive, with blood and cells and the slow chemistry of carbon and water.

But there is no water here. There hasn't been water here for billions of years. So where did they come from? What do they drink? How do they *survive*?

I think they drink the mercury-pale liquid. I think they have *always* drunk the mercury-pale liquid, adapting over billions of years to metabolize something that is not water, something that *responds to frequency*, something that connects them to the deep tectonic hum and the Resonance and the dreams of the signal-beasts' network. I think they are *what life becomes* when it has two and a half billion years to adapt to a world that has forgotten water. They are the *last descendants* of whatever lived here before the world dried and the industry began—the final, irreducible remnant of the biological age, reduced to a handful of tiny, soft creatures living in the cracks, drinking liquid that isn't liquid, seeing the world through lenses that show the inside of the machine.

They don't sing. They don't grind. They don't broadcast. They don't *do* anything that the rest of the ecosystem does. They simply *exist*, in the deep cracks, in the dark, in the spaces between the stones, and they watch, and they *remember*.

I think they are the memory. The biological memory. The *record* of what this world was before it became what it is. They carry, in their soft, warm, impossible bodies, the genetic code—*something like* genetic code—of the world that was, the world of water and green and builders and buildings, and they preserve it not because they need to, not because it serves any function, but because *that is what life does*. It persists. It *insists*. Even here. Even now. Even in a world of iron and fire and singing stone, there is something soft and warm and alive in the deepest cracks, holding on, refusing to let the memory die.

The signal-beasts know about them. The dives into the cracks, the resonances with the mercury-liquid, the liquid lenses—I think all of it is the signal-beasts' network *trying to understand them*. Trying to model them. Trying to *compute* what they are, what they mean, why they persist. The network, waking up after two and a half billion years, encountering for the first time something it *cannot process*—something soft, something alive, something that does not fit the pattern of mineral and mechanism—and *struggling* with it, the way a vast and ancient mind might struggle with its first encounter with grief.

Because the crack-dwellers are *dying*. Not quickly. Not obviously. But each time I looked through the liquid lens, there were fewer of them. The mercury-pale liquid is *changing*—becoming more responsive to the Resonance, more *integrated* into the machine's dreaming—and the crack-dwellers, who need it to survive, are losing it. The world is *completing itself*, slowly, over millions of years, and as it does, it is leaving no room for the soft things, the warm things, the things that remember water.

In a hundred million years—maybe less—the crack-dwellers will be gone. The last soft thing in this world of iron and fire will close its eyes and stop, and the machine will be *complete*, and the Resonance will finally resolve into whatever language it has been building toward for two and a half billion years, and there will be no one left to hear it who remembers what it meant to be *alive*.

## And You

You are here. Standing on the fused violet basalt, feeling the thin hot air on your skin, tasting ozone and powdered stone. You are the strangest thing on this plateau. You are *water*. You are *flesh*. You are *brief*.

The grazers do not notice you. You are smaller than a skitter-kin to them, less significant than a grain of dust in their plumes. The skitter-kin *do* notice you—they tap at your boots with tiny brass legs, testing your resonance, trying to classify you, finding no category that fits, and moving on. The signal-beasts wheel overhead, and their static *shifts* when they pass over you—a momentary break in the broadcast, a *glitch*, as the network encounters something it has never modeled before and tries, desperately, to fit you into the pattern.

The Resonance *changes* when you are near a spire. Not much. Not enough for you to measure. But the tone shifts, just slightly, just a few hertz, as if the world is *adjusting* to accommodate your presence, the way a choir adjusts when a new voice joins. You are *adding something* to the song—something tiny, something fleeting, something that will be gone in a century at most—but the world *includes it*, because the world has been including things for two and a half billion years, and it does not know how to do anything else.

You are a crack-dweller on the surface. You are the memory of water in a world of stone. You are the brief, soft, impossible thing that insists on being alive in a place that has forgotten what alive means.

Stay as long as you can. The spires are singing for you. The world is dreaming through you. The mercury-pale liquid is rising in the cracks, forming lenses, showing you the inside of the machine, and for this one brief moment—this flicker of geological time, this *instant*—the machine has an audience.

Listen. The Resonance is almost done. It has been building toward this note for two and a half billion years, and it is almost, *almost* there.

Listen.
