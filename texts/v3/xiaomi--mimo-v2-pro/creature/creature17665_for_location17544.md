They are called the Wringers, though they have no name for themselves. You arrive at this term because their primary function, as far as you can observe, is to *wring*—to extract, to clarify, to separate the warm, mineral-tinged water that is the hull’s blood into its component parts.

They are not sessile like the barnacle-hands of the sumps. They are not confined to the channels like the glimpsed, jointed shapes in the dark. The Wringers inhabit the seams, but they also emerge onto the surface, particularly near the low, organ-like structures. They are the only life you’ve seen that seems to *purposefully* traverse the vast, empty skin.

You see them first as anomalies: a patch of surface that appears to shimmer, as if under a heat haze, though the air is uniformly warm. As you draw closer, the shimmer resolves. It is a group of them, perhaps a dozen, clustered over a capillary channel. They are not large—the size of a crouched child—but their form is difficult to hold in the eye. They seem to be made of the same dark, ceramic-polymer as the hull, but fluid, mutable. A central, bulbous mass (a reservoir? a processor?) from which extend three to five long, multi-jointed limbs. These limbs taper to points fine enough to insert into the hair-width channels.

They do not walk. They *flow*. Their limbs articulate in sequence, pulling the central mass along the surface in a slow, deliberate glide. They leave no trail. The moisture on the hull seems to bead and retreat from their path, as if repelled.

Their purpose is in their name. You watch one stop over a channel where the water runs slightly cloudy, carrying microscopic sediment or suspended colloids from deeper within the hull. Two limbs insert themselves, not blocking the flow, but becoming part of it. There is a minute vibration, a humming almost too low to hear but felt in your teeth. The water around the limb points clarifies instantly, becoming perfectly transparent. Something—a finer particulate, a dissolved solute—is drawn *up* into the central bulbous mass of the Wringer. You see it swell, almost imperceptibly, then contract. A faint, oily bead of a denser substance exudes from a pore on its dorsal side and is swiftly absorbed by the hull’s surface, leaving a faint, darker stain that slowly fades.

They are filters. They are refineries. They are the hull’s white blood cells, patrolling the circulatory system’s capillary beds, ensuring purity, removing the microscopic debris of the hull’s own slow metabolism.

They interact with the other fauna. The sessile barnacle-hands in the sumps sometimes attract them; a Wringer will extend a limb and tap the shell of a barnacle in a specific rhythm. The barnacle will open, releasing a measure of its viscous, iridescent fluid. The Wringer absorbs it whole. In return, the Wringer may leave a minuscule deposit of the refined solute on the barnacle’s shell, which the barnacle slowly incorporates, its slate-grey color deepening. It is a trade. A transaction in a currency of chemistry and resonance.

The larger shapes in the deep channels—the forearm-sized, the leg-sized—give the Wringers a wide berth. When a Wringer group is working, the water in the channels nearby becomes perfectly still, laminar, as if frozen. Nothing moves through that zone. This is not aggression; it is protocol. The Wringer is performing critical maintenance, and the system accommodates it.

They seem to have no senses you recognize. They do not react to your shadow, to your warmth, to the vibration of your footsteps. You are outside their calculus. You are not a solute to be filtered, not a component to be maintained, not a parasite to be dissolved. You are irrelevant data. A ghost on the skin.

Yet, if you stand still long enough, watching them work, you begin to feel a different kind of observation. Not from them, but from the *hull* through them. The constant vibration in your bones feels, for a moment, like a query. The weeping seams seem to weep a little faster near your feet. The overcast, sourceless light seems to focus, just a degree brighter, on the spot where you stand.

You are a foreign object. A non-integrated variable in a system of flawless, aqueous equilibrium. The Wringers cannot wring you. The channels cannot carry you. The barnacles cannot dissolve you. But the system is aware of you, as a body is aware of a splinter—not with pain, but with a localized, attentive *focus*. A slow, cellular curiosity about how to incorporate, or expel, this new thing.

The Wringers continue their work. They glide over the surface, fine limbs probing, refining, maintaining the exquisite purity of the warm water that is the world’s lifeblood. They are the keepers of the soaking. They are the reason nothing decays, nothing clouds, nothing changes. They are the agents of the stasis.

And as you watch them, the question shifts. It is no longer *What is the hull optimizing for?*

It is: *What will it do with me?*
