They are the Quiet. Not silent—the roar of the seep fills that role—but still. Their movements are deliberate, conserving energy in the cool, damp air. They flow through their stone warren with a liquid economy, shoulders brushing, hands steadying each other on the slick steps. There is no hurry. Hurry implies a destination beyond the next alcove, the next shared meal of roasted eel, the next tracing of stories on a waiting back.

Their hands are their primary sense organs. Where another people might *look* at the wall-bands to read the history of the water, the Quiet *feel* them. Their long, sensitive fingers follow the striations of iron-dark and crystal-veined limestone, discerning the texture of millennia. They navigate in near-total darkness by this haptic cartography, their wide, pale eyes more attuned to motion and the faint, fleeting glow of the shining than to detail. A shift in the air pressure, a change in the stone’s dampness underfoot, the particular resonance of a step on a hollow-sounding shelf—these are their maps.

Their language is a slow braid of touch. A press of the palm to the sternum for *I*, for *here*. A circling of fingertips on the forearm for *water*, for *seep*, for *eternal*. A gentle tap-tap-tap along the spine for the rhythm of the cavity’s breath. Complex ideas—history, emotion, the rare abstract thought—are conveyed in long, flowing sequences across the skin, a syntax of pressure and temperature and slight vibration. To an outsider, it would look like prolonged, silent embraces. To them, it is conversation, argument, prayer, and lullaby.

They do not have families in a linear sense. Children are raised by the cluster, passed from lap to lap, fed eel-paste from communal bowls. The concept of "mother" or "father" is expressed by a gesture meaning "the one who first steadied my hands on the wall." Lineage is traced not through blood, but through the memory of who taught whom the location of the thinnest singing-stone, or the deepest channel where the pale worms coil.

Their few possessions are layered. A necklace of eel vertebrae, each bone worn smooth as glass. A cloak of woven eel-skin, greased with oil for water-resistance, its interior patterned with the scratched map of a favorite sleeping-alcove. A single, cherished tool—a chisel of antler, its handle shaped to the grip of a specific hand over generations, worn into a perfect, polished negative of the fingers that have held it. They wear their world on their bodies. The mineral dust of the walls is in the creases of their skin. The smell of stone and eel is their perfume.

Their rituals are tied to the cavity’s constants. At the dimming of the amber light (their only true "time," a slow cycle of brightening and fading through the calcite sheets), they gather on the central platforms around the pool. They do not speak. They listen. They listen to the individual drops become a chorus, to the chorus become a roar, to the roar subside into the layered, textured silence between impacts. They trace the patterns of the sound on each other’s skin, a shared meditation on the only music they know. Sometimes, one will point to the ceiling, not in acknowledgment of a world above, but to note a new rivulet, a change in the seep’s pattern—a rare event that is discussed for days in hushed, tactile discourse.

The deeper things—the worms—are a gestural taboo. To speak of them is to use a specific, complex sequence: a shiver down the arm, a clenched fist, a turning away of the face. They are "the beneath." "The not-us." "The still-waiting." The Quiet do not fish in the deep pool. They do not dig downward. Their entire civilization is a horizontal accretion, a clinging to the walls of their world, a deep respect for the boundary that is the floor. The worms are the limit, the reminder that their world, while complete, has a depth they do not own.

They are, in their essence, a tactile culture in an oral world. The cavity speaks, endlessly, but they answer with touch. They are a soft, persistent, huddled warmth in a cold, singing stone lung. Their existence is a quiet argument against change: they are the limestone, weathered into a final shape, content to hold its form. The seep polishes them. The sound cradles them. The dark is their blanket. They are not *in* the world. They *are* the world’s slow, patient, breathing consciousness, dreaming of eel-bone and stone in the endless, weeping dark.
