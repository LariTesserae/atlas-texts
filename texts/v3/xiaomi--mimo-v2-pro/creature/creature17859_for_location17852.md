# What Lives Here

---

There are people in this world. Not many. Not in any way we might immediately recognize as *people*. But they are here, and they have always been here, and the forest does not think of them as separate from itself.

## Bodies Built for Resonance

They are tall. Not massively so—perhaps seven feet at most—but they are *elongated*, stretched in the way that everything in this ecosystem stretches when it must reach between vertical planes. Their limbs are long, thin, corded with the kind of lean, taut muscle that comes from a life spent climbing, gripping, pressing against bark and root-fiber. Their fingers are long and prehensile, and the pads of their fingers and the soles of their feet are wide, slightly concave, slightly tacky with a natural secretion—a substance not unlike the adhesive used by the root-web's fungal mats. They can grip anything. They can *stick* to anything. In the permanent twilight below the canopy, there is no flat ground and no handhold that does not want to hold.

Their skin is cool. This is the first thing an outsider would notice, if an outsider could survive here. In an environment of baked, radiating heat, their skin has evolved to be thermally neutral—a thin, slick, almost mucosal surface that does not absorb the ambient warmth. It has a faint grey-green cast, mottled in irregular patches, and in the dim bioluminescent blue of the fungi-cycle, they nearly vanish against the bark of the great trees. The mottling is not identical between individuals. It is not camouflage designed by any predator, but rather a byproduct of the symbiotic microorganisms that live in and on their skin—a microbiome as complex as anything in the soil. These microorganisms help regulate temperature, resist the constant fungal pressure of the environment, and, crucially, *dampen vibration*. Their bodies do not ring like a struck bell the way a foreign object would on the root-web. They are acoustically dead. They walk the highways of this world without announcing themselves to every resonating beetle for miles.

Their eyes are large—not grotesquely so, but noticeably—with pupils that can distend to an almost perfect circle in the lowest light. They see well in twilight. In true darkness—which comes rarely, only in the deepest interior where the canopy is four or five layers thick—they do not see at all, and they do not need to. Their primary sense is not vision.

## The Ear-Hands

Around the base of each ear, fanning outward along the jawline and down the neck in a complex series of overlapping, cartilaginous flaps, is a structure that has no precise analog in any fauna you know. It is part tympanic membrane, part antenna, part something else entirely—a distributed organ of hearing that can discriminate between frequencies so subtle, so layered, that the people can parse the sonic environment the way you might parse a visual scene. They hear *directionally* in three dimensions. They hear the subsonic thrum of the giant beetles through their feet. They hear the ultrasonic shrieks of the leathery flyers in the upper canopy. They hear the slow, hydraulic groan of xylem moving water through miles of trunk. They hear the click-speak of the gliding lizards and the deep, almost inaudible pulse of the bioluminescent fungi synchronizing.

They do not simply hear. They *listen*. Listening is the foundational act of consciousness for them. To be a person here is to be someone who listens and is listened to.

Their own communication is almost entirely vibratory. They speak, but not with mouths—at least, not primarily. They press their palms flat against a root-web, or against the bark of a trunk, and they *tap*. Complex, rapid, polyrhythmic tapping. The patterns ripple outward through the connective tissue of the plant-body at predictable speeds, and others—miles away, sometimes—press their own palms to the web and receive the message. It is not telepathy. It is engineering. The root-web is a continent-spanning nervous system, and they have learned its grammar over a timescale that dwarfs anything we would call history.

They do have mouths, and they do make sounds with them—clicks, deep hums, short percussive pops—but these are intimate communications, used only at close range, between two individuals who can see each other. Mouth-sounds are for the vulnerable, the affectionate, the private. Root-tapping is for everything else: warning, information, storytelling, song.

## Song

They sing. Not with voices, or not only. A community—a gathering of perhaps fifteen to thirty people who consider themselves a single social unit, and who travel the root-web together in loose, shifting formations—will, at intervals, perform a *song*. This is an event. One person initiates, pressing both palms to a major root-node and laying down a rhythmic pattern. Another joins, tapping a counter-rhythm against a secondary root a few meters away. A third begins to hum—a physical, chest-deep hum that resonates against the bark they lean on. Others join. The rhythms interlock, overlap, interfere, and then—sometimes after an hour of patient layering—the song *takes hold*. The root-web begins to ring. The pattern feeds back through the lattice and amplifies. The resonating beetles, miles away, respond instinctively, their carapaces thrumming in harmonic sympathy. The flyers go quiet. The click-speak of the lizards pauses. For a few minutes, the entire sonic environment of a vast region of the forest is reorganized around the song.

What the song *means*—whether it means anything in the way we understand meaning—is difficult to say. The people will tell you (if you could understand them, and you cannot, not really) that the song is a way of reminding the forest that they are here. That they are part of the pattern. That the forest does not have to search for them, does not have to wonder what strange, silent, acoustically dead thing is moving through its tissues. The song says: *we are of you. We are your vibration. Do not mistake us for stone.*

The forest, in its way, replies. After a song, the fungal mats along the nearby root-web will fruit—briefly, profusely, releasing a cloud of spores that smell faintly of ozone and something sweet. The people collect these spores. They are a staple food, and they are also a mild intoxicant, and they are also, the people believe, the forest's way of acknowledging the song.

## The Crown-Bulb Dwellings

The "built" elements mentioned—the clearings, the ovoid chambers—are not houses in any permanent sense. The people do not build them. The crown-bulbs are symbiotic organisms, and they grow where the people *want* them to grow, over the course of years and decades, through a patient, ongoing process of cultivation that is part agriculture, part conversation, part art.

A person—or more often, a community—selects a site. Usually this is a convergence point where several major root-nodes meet, forming a natural crossroads. The people begin by tapping. They tap specific rhythms, at specific frequencies, into the root-web at the convergence point, day after day, week after week. The vibrations stress the fungal and plant tissues in precise ways. Over time—and "time" here means years—a crown-bulb begins to form. It is not a tree. It is not a fungus. It is something in between: a slow-growing, hollow, living structure that takes the shape its cultivators have essentially *requested* through sustained vibratory input. The walls of a mature crown-bulb are warm, smooth, and faintly luminous—they harbor bioluminescent microorganisms in their resinous inner surface, so the interior glows with a soft, amber light at all hours.

Inside, the living is spare. Sleeping shelves are woven from living root-tendrils that have been trained to grow in horizontal planes—tendrils that are still alive, still drawing water, still warm. You sleep on a thing that is still growing around you. Tools are made from the chitin of dead resonating beetles (their carapaces are enormously strong, lightweight, and resonant—some tools are instruments as much as implements) and from silica-rich thorns harvested from understory plants. There is no metal. There is no stone, except what might be found as river-erratics in the few places where water gathers in liquid pools on the plateau surface, and these are kept as talismans, curiosities, objects of almost religious fascination. *Stone does not grow. Stone does not sing. Stone is the most alien thing in this world.*

The fire-pits are real. The "wood" smolders, as described, producing a sweet, intoxicating smoke—but the people do not use fire for warmth (they do not need warmth) or for light (they have the bioluminescence). Fire is for cooking, yes—the spore-paste must be heated to activate certain compounds—but it is also, more fundamentally, for *smoke*. The smoke is a message. It carries specific chemical signatures upward through the canopy layers, where it alters the behavior of certain flyer-species. A community that keeps a steady fire is a community that has established a relationship with the flyers overhead—a truce, a contract, a mutualism. The flyers do not shriek-map over the crown-bulb (which would be deafening and disorienting). In exchange, the people leave offerings of beetle-chitin at certain points on the upper canopy, chitin which the flyers need for nest-building.

## What They Eat

They eat almost everything this world offers, and they eat constantly—not voraciously, but steadily, the way the ecosystem itself processes matter. The spore-paste is the staple: a dense, protein-rich, slightly hallucinogenic paste made from the fruiting bodies of the fungal mats, mixed with condensation-water and sometimes with the rendered fat of the smaller burrowing worms. They eat the resonating beetles—roasted over the smoldering fires, the chitin cracked and the soft interior consumed. The beetles are the closest thing they have to a luxury food, and eating one is a small ceremony, because the beetles are respected. They are the heartbeat of the root-web. To eat one is to acknowledge that the pulse that drives this world is also, ultimately, food.

They eat the leaves. Not the great waxy canopy-leaves (those are too tough, too chemically defended) but the smaller, softer understory leaves that grow in the rare shafts of direct light that penetrate to the lower levels. These leaves are sweet, full of sugars produced by the intense photosynthesis of the light-spoiled plants that grow in those shafts. The communities will travel considerable distances to reach a light-shaft during its seasonal window of availability.

They eat the gliding lizards, sometimes, when they can catch them—which is rarely, and only through the cooperative efforts of an entire community, using sonic lures and netting woven from root-fiber. The lizards are small, fast, and acoustically aware, and catching one is as much sport as sustenance.

They do not eat the flyers. This is taboo—not a moral prohibition, but a practical one. The flyers are the eyes and ears of the upper canopy. Their sonic mapping shapes the behavior of everything below. To eat a flyer is to go deaf in a very specific way—to lose the information-stream that allows navigation through the densest parts of the forest. Communities that have, in the distant past, consumed flyers have found themselves *lost*—unable to interpret the sonic environment, wandering blindly, eventually dissolving as social units. The taboo is enforced not by punishment but by consequence.

## Social Structure

There is no hierarchy. This is not a moral achievement but an ecological inevitability. The communities are small enough that everyone knows everyone, and the root-web tapping system means that information cannot be monopolized. When one person taps a message into the web, everyone connected to the web in that region receives it. There is no way to whisper in this world—not through the roots. (Mouth-sounds, the intimate register, are another matter, and the private space inside a crown-bulb, with its acoustically dampening walls, is considered sacred precisely because it is the only place where true secrecy is possible.)

Decisions are made through what might be called *consensus-vibration*: a community taps a question into the web, and the responses come back as rhythmic modifications of the original pattern. Agreement is expressed as *harmonic reinforcement*—other palms adding the same rhythm, amplifying it. Disagreement is expressed as *counter-rhythm*—a dissonant pattern that, if strong enough, disrupts the original and forces it to reorganize. The process can take hours or days. It is not fast. It is not meant to be fast. The people have a deep, almost physiological intolerance for haste. Haste is what things that do not listen do.

Communities are not fixed. Individuals move between communities, sometimes seasonally, sometimes permanently. A person who wishes to leave simply leaves—they tap a departure rhythm into the web, a pattern that translates roughly to *I am moving through you*—and they walk the root-highways alone until they find another community whose song they wish to join. This is not considered a betrayal or a loss. It is considered *circulation*. The forest circulates water. The people circulate themselves.

## Children

Birth is rare. Not biologically restricted, but culturally regulated with a precision that borders on instinct. A community will not produce more children than its local ecosystem can sustain—and the ecosystem communicates its capacity through the sonic environment. When the resonating beetle population in a region is thriving, when the fungal mats are fruiting densely, when the flyers are nesting in abundance, the sonic signature of the region is *full*—rich, complex, layered. The people hear this, and they know: this is a good time to bring new life. When the sonic environment thins—when beetle populations cycle down, when the fungi retreat—the people hear *emptiness*, and they know: not now.

Pregnancy is shared. Not biologically, but socially. A pregnant person does not travel. They remain in the crown-bulb, and the community brings food, water, and—most importantly—*sound*. The community taps lullabies into the crown-bulb's root-system, patterns of extraordinary gentleness and complexity, and the developing child hears them, vibrating through the warm walls, through the fluid of the womb, through bone. Children are born already knowing the rhythms of their community's song. They do not learn to listen. They arrive listening.

Children are named by sound. A name, for the people, is a short rhythmic phrase—a tap-pattern of four or five beats that is unique to each individual. Names are not spoken aloud. They are *felt*. To call someone by name is to press your palm to the root-web and tap their pattern, and if they are anywhere on the web, they will feel it—a small, specific, unmistakable resonance that says *you are being thought of*.

## Death

When a person dies, their body is taken to a specific point on the root-web—a node where the fungal mats are thickest, where the decay-process is most active. The body is laid on the mat. The community gathers. They do not sing—not the full song, not the forest-song. Instead, they tap a single, simple, repeating rhythm: the dead person's name, over and over, until the fungal mats begin to respond. The mats grow over the body slowly—visibly, within hours—and as they do, the microorganisms in the mats begin to break down the tissues. The people believe—and they may be right, in a biological sense—that the dead person's acoustic signature, the specific way their body vibrated and damped and resonated, is absorbed into the fungal mat and from there into the root-web. The dead do not go away. They go *in*. They become part of the medium through which the living communicate.

Sometimes, after a death, the community will hear a faint echo of the dead person's tapping rhythm in the root-web—a phantom pattern, generated by the fungal mats processing the body's tissues, vibrating at frequencies that just happen to mimic the dead person's name. The people do not consider this supernatural. They consider it *fact*. The dead are in the web. The web is the world. The world remembers.

## What They Fear

They do not fear predators. The resonating beetles are too large and too slow to be a serious threat. The flyers do not come low enough. The gliding lizards are too small. The burrowing worms are too deep. The people are, in a real sense, too *big* and too *noisy* (in their own way) to be prey for anything in this world.

What they fear is *silence*. Not the absence of sound—there is never true silence here—but the *wrong kind* of silence. A sudden cessation of beetle-thrum in a region. A sudden absence of click-speak from the lizards. A moment when the sonic environment *drops out* and the air—which is never truly air here, but a thick, wet, vibratory medium—goes flat and dead.

This silence means something is wrong. Something in the web has broken—a collapse, a disease, a fire (real fire, not the cultivated smolder) that has burned through a section of the root-lattice and severed the connections. The people do not fear fire for its heat. They fear it because it destroys the medium of their consciousness. A burned section of root-web is a section of *deafness*. A blind spot in the body of the world.

When silence falls, the communities respond. They converge on the silent zone from every direction, moving fast—faster than their usual pace, which is deliberate and slow. They arrive, and they begin to tap. They tap the dead zone's edges, trying to re-establish connection. They tap the names of anyone who might be within the silence, hoping for a response. If there is no response, they grieve—not with wailing, but with the most dissonant, arrhythmic tapping they can produce, a sound of pure wrongness that the forest itself seems to recoil from. It is the only time the people make *ugly* sounds.

Then they begin to repair. They cultivate new fungal mats. They train new root-tendrils across the gap. They sing—the full song, the forest-song—aimed at the wound, coaxing the web to regrow. It can take months. It can take years. They are patient. They have always been patient.

## Their Name for Themselves

They do not have a single name. They do not need one. Within each community, the name *is* the community's song—the complex, interlocking rhythm that identifies them to the web. But if you forced them to describe what they are in the simplest possible terms, the closest translation—the closest *feeling*—would be something like:

**Those who press close and listen.**

Or perhaps:

**The vibration that answers.**

They do not think of themselves as the masters of this world. They do not think of themselves as separate from it. They are a frequency in a vast and deafening chord. They are not the loudest note. They are not the lowest or the highest. But they are *in tune*, and they have been in tune for so long that the forest would miss them if they were gone—not with grief, perhaps, but with a subtle shift in resonance, a gap in the harmonic, a place where something used to tap and no longer does.

They are small. They are few. They are almost impossible to see. But if you pressed your hand to the root-web of this vast, breathing, screaming, growing, dying, singing world—and if you could feel what they feel—you would know them immediately. They are the pattern that does not belong to any beetle or lizard or flyer or fungus. They are the rhythm that moves. They are the rhythm that *chooses* where to go.

Nothing else in this world chooses.

They choose.

And they choose, mostly, to stay. To listen. To press their palms against the warm, living bark of the only world they have ever known, and to feel it breathe, and to breathe with it, and to tap, very softly, the message that means everything and nothing:

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*
