# Who Lives in Kam Serreth

The people are broad. Not tall, necessarily — the ceilings in the old quarters are low, and the terrace walkways are narrow, and over centuries a certain compactness has been selected for — but *broad*. Thick through the chest and shoulders. Short-fingered hands with prominent knuckles. Skin that trends toward a damp, translucent pallor in the lower terraces, where the fog never fully lifts, and a ruddier, veined quality in the upper levels, where the dry hours are real and the mineral content of the water is lower by the time it reaches the rim.

They do not wrinkle so much as *polish*. The constant moisture, the mineral-rich air — it does something to skin over a lifetime. Old people in Kam Serreth look like river stones. Smooth. Dense. Their faces have a worn, rounded quality, as if the city has been slowly shaping them the way it shapes its walls. The very old have almost no body hair. It falls out in middle age, universally, and no one considers this remarkable. Children are born with fine, dark down on their arms and scalps, and it thickens briefly in adolescence before the slow shedding begins. Puberty here involves, among other things, the first persistent skin-film — that thin mineral condensation that beads on exposed stone also beads on exposed flesh, and teenagers learn to carry cloths the way teenagers in other cities learn to carry keys.

**They are warm to the touch.** This is the first thing visitors notice about the people themselves, after the sound. The geothermal heat that drives the engines also warms the groundwater, and residents absorb it through a lifetime of immersion. Their resting body temperature runs a degree or two above lowland norms. In the cool months, when the fog is thick and the steppe winds push cold air over the rim, people from Kam Serreth radiate heat like low furnaces. They sleep with windows open. They touch each other casually and often — hands on shoulders, forearms pressed together in conversation — not from affection, particularly, but because the warmth exchange is comfortable. In the rare event that a Kam Serreth native travels to a lowland city, they find it oppressively hot and dry, and they *ache*. Not metaphorically. Their joints ache. Their sinuses crack and bleed. They speak of the lowlands the way desert people might speak of the sea — with fascination and a kind of revulsion, the feeling that something essential is missing from the air.

**The hearing loss is universal by middle age,** though the city has developed a sophisticated relationship with it. Children grow up bilingual by necessity — spoken Kam Serethi and the signing vocabulary, *kesreth mute*, which is used in the junctions, in the engine-halls, anywhere the base thrum makes speech unreliable. By the time a person is thirty, they rely on signing for roughly a third of their daily communication without thinking about it. By fifty, it is half. The old — the river-stone people in their seventies and eighties — often speak with their hands entirely, their voices reduced to a low murmur they can feel resonating in their own chests more than they can hear. There is no stigma. The language is expressive, gestural, large-handed. It was shaped by people with broad palms and short fingers, and it favors sweeping, whole-arm movements over the fine articulation of, say, a language developed by the deaf in a quiet culture. Watching two old residents of Kam Serreth converse is like watching a slow, deliberate dance.

**The signing has acquired things that speech never could.** There are signs for the specific quality of the engine-thrum on a given day — *heavy-resonance*, *thin-resonance*, *off-cycle* — that experienced users can distinguish the way a sailor distinguishes wind. There is a sign that means, roughly, *the deep breathing is faster today*, and another that means *slower*. These are used casually, the way one might comment on weather. Children learn them before they understand what they refer to.

---

**Occupations.** Kam Serreth has surprisingly few of them, in the way that a body has surprisingly few organs.

*Maintenance engineers* are the priesthood. Not metaphorically — or not *only* metaphorically. The engine systems, the canal network, the steam mains, the cable-cart lines, the deep drainage grates: all of it requires constant, patient, skilled attention. The machines are old. They do not break so much as they *drift* — flywheels lose balance, bellows membranes thin, press-works develop eccentricities of timing that compound over years. A good engineer does not fix things. They *listen*. They place their hands on a housing and feel the vibration pattern through their palms, and they know — not intellectually, but in the body — when something has shifted by a fraction of a degree. The best engineers are the old ones, the ones with the most hearing loss, because they have been forced deeper into a tactile relationship with the machines. There are engineer families that have maintained specific engine arrays for five, six, seven generations. They know their machines the way one knows a living thing. They speak of them, in the private signing vocabulary, with the grammatical forms reserved for sentient beings.

*Miners* work the rim, and their work is slow and continuous in the way that farming is slow and continuous. The metalite ore is not blasted. It is *excavated* — cut in long, patient seams, peeled from the rock in slabs that are then carted down the rim tracks to the press-works. The miners are the quietest people in the city. They spend their shifts in the tunnels of the upper terraces, where the rock is thickest and the engine-sound is muffled almost to nothing, and they have developed a culture of profound, comfortable silence. They communicate in whispers and hand signals that are not kesreth mute but something older and more restricted — a trade language of pointing, nodding, tapping. Miners' children are often slow to develop spoken language. They are not delayed. They are simply *multilingual by nature*, fluent in silence, and speech comes to them as a third or fourth mode rather than a first.

*Canal-tenders* maintain the street-level drainage channels and the shallow capillary networks that keep the foundations dry. This is wet work, literally. Canal-tenders spend their days kneeling in thin mineral water, clearing sediment, patching the channels where calcium buildup has narrowed them, checking flow rates at junction points. Their hands are extraordinary — pale, pruned permanently, the skin thickened into a texture like soft leather, the nails grown ridged and brittle. They retire young, by Kam Serreth standards, in their forties, when the joints in their knees and fingers finally give way. In retirement they tend to sit in the upper terraces in the dry hours with their hands open to the sun, and they are not pitied for this. It is simply the shape of that life.

*Press-workers* operate the vast stamping systems that turn raw metalite into export stock. This is the loud work, the junction work, the work that costs hearing fastest. The press-halls are cathedrals of noise — the slow BOOM of the stamping arms, the hiss of steam, the shriek of metal on metal, all riding on that fundamental thrum. Press-workers develop a rhythmic quality to everything they do. They walk in time with the machines. They eat in time. They talk — when they talk — in bursts timed to the troughs between stamp impacts. Visitors find this unsettling, this synchronization. Press-workers find the visitors' arrhythmic habits unsettling in return.

---

**Children.** They are born in the quiet rooms, or as close to quiet as the city can manage. Birth in Kam Serreth is medically uncomplicated — the mineral water is apparently beneficial in some way that no one has fully explained, and the warmth, and perhaps something else — but it is *loud*. Newborns in the lower terraces are bathed in sound from their first breath. There is a tradition, old and not universally practiced but deeply felt by those who observe it, of bringing a newborn to the quiet room on the third day and simply holding them in silence for an hour. Not for the child's sake. For the parents'. So they can hear their baby's first real cries — unmediated, unmasked — and know what their child sounds like *alone*.

Children in Kam Serreth play slowly. This is partly temperament, partly environment — running on wet compacted silt is a fast way to break a wrist, and the children know it. Their games are elaborate, procedural, patient. There is a popular game, played in the mid-terrace warrens, that involves building miniature canal systems in the gravel gutters, diverting the actual flow of mineral water through hand-dug channels and watching where it pools and where it drains. It is engineering play, and it is indistinguishable from the real work except in scale. Seven-year-olds can diagnose drainage problems that would baffle a lowland plumber. They do this the way other children build with blocks — unconsciously, joyfully, as an expression of play.

Some children, a small but consistent number in every generation, are born with a condition the city calls *deep-hearing*. It is not well understood. These children can perceive the low-frequency engine-thrum at a level that is, for everyone else, sub-perceptual — felt in the chest and inner ear but not heard. For deep-hearing children, it is *heard*. They hear it the way one hears a voice in the next room — distinct, patterned, layered. They hear the harmonics. They hear the variations. And some of them, as they grow, claim to hear something beneath the engine-sound. Something slower. Something in the deep channels. The city does not dismiss these children. It watches them carefully. Some of them become the best engineers. Some of them go quiet in a way that is not the comfortable quiet of the miners but something more private, more interior, as if they have heard a conversation they were not meant to join.

---

**The things in the deep channels.** They are not fauna, exactly, though they are biological. The pale invertebrates — the almost-eels — are the visible population. But the channel surveys, conducted every decade by a joint team of canal-tenders and engineers, consistently report anomalies that no one publishes widely.

The colonies at the deepest junctions are *old*. Carbon dating of their mineral-secreted casings places some colonies at four, five hundred years — older than the city's current engineering, older than the written records. They do not die, apparently. They grow. They divide. They *remember* the channel geometry, returning to junctions after floods that rearrange the passage floors, re-establishing their positions as if reading a map that was not in the water.

The warm water from the thermal fractures contains a mineral signature that does not match the local geology. The rock here is sedimentary — layered, banded, the slow accumulation of ancient lakebeds. The deep water carries trace elements from *igneous* sources — crystalline structures that suggest magma, or something very close to it, far below the caldera floor. The caldera was not volcanic, the geologists say. It was scooped. But something down there is hot, and something down there is breathing, and the invertebrates cluster at the boundary where the warm water meets the cool like organisms gathered at a thermocline in the deep ocean — feeding on the gradient itself.

There is one further thing, reported by three separate survey teams over the past sixty years and never officially confirmed.

At the deepest accessible junction — a point roughly two hundred meters below the caldera floor, where the oldest drainage tunnels converge into a single great channel — the water is warm enough to steam. The invertebrates stop fifty meters above this point. The channel is straight, unnaturally straight, cut (or *worn*) into the bedrock with a precision that the city's own engineers could not match. It extends downward at a gentle angle into darkness. The surveys have never followed it to its end. The teams turn back at the same point — not because of danger, exactly, but because of a *feeling*. One engineer described it, in the restricted signing vocabulary, with a compound sign that translates roughly as: *the home before home*. Another said, simply, that the thrum changed. That below a certain depth, the frequency of the vibration in the stone shifted, and it was no longer the engine-sound they knew but something older, and it felt like the city was listening to something beneath itself, and whatever it was, it was listening back.

The city does not discuss this often. The grates are maintained. The channels are surveyed. The deep-hearing children grow up and some of them become engineers and some of them go quiet.

Kam Serreth has been here for as long as anyone can remember. It was not built. It was *found* — a bowl in the stone that was already wet, already warm, already humming at a frequency that felt, to the first people who descended into it, like being held. The engines came later. The people came later. The city was already breathing when they arrived, and it will be breathing when they are gone, and the things in the deep channels will still be there, clustered at the warm-water junctions, patient and pale and older than the stone that holds them.

The people of Kam Serreth know this. They have always known it. It is not a secret. It is simply the shape of home — the knowledge that you live on the skin of something vast and warm and slow, and that it permits you, and that this permission is not the same as indifference, and that the difference between the two is something you learn to feel in your chest the way you learn to walk on wet stone: once, and then you don't think about it again.
