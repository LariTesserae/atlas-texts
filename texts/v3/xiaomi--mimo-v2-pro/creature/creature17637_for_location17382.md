# Who Lives in the Warren of Silt

The smallest things live here first. They were here before the people. They were here before the carving.

**The shrimp** are almost nothing — translucent commas of chitin and motion, each one the length of a grain of rice, drifting in the cistern in slow helical orbits that never quite repeat. They feed on bacterial films that coat the submerged clay, films so fine they are more chemistry than biology, more mineral than life. The shrimp have no eyes. They navigate by the taste of the water, by pressure waves against their antennae, by whatever it is that blind things use to know where they are. They breed in pulses. Every few weeks — the people have no word for weeks, but the cycle is as regular as a heartbeat — the cistern thickens with them, and then the lizards come, and the population thins, and then it builds again. The shrimp do not seem to notice. They have no capacity to notice. They simply are, and then they are fewer, and then they are again. They are the warren's most patient inhabitants. They will be here when everything else is gone.

**The beetles** graze the fungal mat. They are the size of thumbnails — flat-bodied, pale as the clay, with legs that end in tiny hooks adapted for gripping the porous surface of the fungal turf. They move in herds, if a herd can be fifty strong and silent, their mandables working ceaselessly at the fungal hyphae, leaving clean trails through the pale growth. They are slow. They have no reason to be fast. Nothing here specializes in eating them except the lizards, and the lizards are patient hunters, not chasers. The beetles reproduce in the fungal mat itself — the females bore into it and lay their eggs in the deep mycelial layer, and the larvae hatch into a world of food so abundant that they need never leave their birthplace. Some never do. Some larvae pupate in place and emerge as adults already surrounded by a meal. They are, in their way, the most content creatures in the warren. They want for nothing. They have never wanted for anything. Want is a concept that requires a larger body and a longer memory.

**The spiders** build their webs between the clay cones. Not between all of them — the wind would shred an open web — but in the sheltered quadrants, the places where the cones cluster close enough that a strand can span the gap, where the draft is gentle enough to be an asset rather than a threat. These are orb-weavers of a kind, but their silk is not white. It is the color of old clay, the exact color of the walls, and the webs are nearly invisible until you are close enough to feel one brush your face, which you will, and it will feel like the touch of a ghost. The spiders are small. They are the color of everything here. During the hours when the bats roost and the chamber is still, they repair their webs with a patience that borders on ritual — each strand placed, tested, adjusted, the geometry of the spiral held in some inherited part of their tiny brains like a prayer that is always being said. They eat whatever the wind brings them — small moths from the surface, gnats that breed in the fungal mat, the occasional beetle that stumbles into a strand. They are not abundant. They do not need to be. They are the warren's weavers, and they have been here almost as long as the shrimp, and their silk has a tensile strength that the people have learned to harvest, carefully, for binding tool-heads and mending fishing lines in the side-pools where the people actually catch their food.

**The eyeless lizards** are the warren's true aristocrats. Not because they are large — they are not, barely the length of a man's hand from snout to tail-tip — but because they are *present* in a way the smaller creatures are not. You see them. They move with intention. They pause and seem to listen. Their bodies are the color of wet clay, smooth-skinned, nearly legless in their motion, more snake than lizard, though they have four small limbs with splayed toes that grip the carved channels of the floor with extraordinary precision. Where their eyes should be, there are smooth scales — no sockets, no scars, just an unbroken continuation of the head's profile, as if the face simply forgot to finish itself. They hunt by vibration. The water-shrimp make tiny disturbances in the surface film of the channels, and the lizards read these disturbances the way the people read tracks in dust, or the way the old carvers read the grain of the clay before they shaped it. They are not tame. The children handle them sometimes — a child will sit cross-legged on a warm shelf, and a lizard will crawl into the cup of her palms, and she will hold it and feel its pulse, quick and cool against her skin — but the lizard does not stay. It stays only as long as it wants to, which is not long. It has things to do. It is always going somewhere, with that quick darting purpose, and it pauses, and it eats, and it moves on. The people respect this. They do not name the lizards. They do not try to keep them. They understand, without discussing it, that the lizards belong to the warren in a way that is different from how they themselves belong. The lizards are *of* it. The lizards are what happens when clay thinks about moving.

**The bats** are the warren's voice. Not the wind-cones — those are the warren's instrument. The bats are the voice, the part that speaks.

There are eleven thousand of them. The people have counted, in their way — not by twos and threes but by the weight of the guano on the upper ledges, by the pitch of the chittering when the colony is at rest, by the duration of the passage through the entrance shafts at dusk, which the eldest women measure by the drip of a particular condensation-point on the dome, the one that falls every seventh breath in calm air. Eleven thousand. A number that has not changed in living memory, which means it has not changed in a very long time.

They are a single species — small, insectivorous, with ears the size of their own wings and a echolocation call so high it sits at the edge of human hearing, a faint needle-sound that you feel more than hear, a pressure behind the eyes. During the day, when they roost, the chamber is their cathedral. They pack into the upper niches so tightly that the boundaries between individual bodies blur — the wall becomes a single breathing surface, a dark membrane that expands and contracts with the collective respiration of eleven thousand small mammals. The fungal mat grows on and between them. It feeds on their guano. It does not seem to bother them. They do not seem to know it is there. Or perhaps they do and they welcome it, the way a tree welcomes moss, as a companion that asks only for proximity and waste.

At dusk, they wake. It is not a gradual thing. One moment the wall is still. The next, it *detonates*. The sound of eleven thousand bats beginning to move at once is the loudest thing in the warren — louder than the wind-cones, louder than the children — a roar of wings and calls and the frantic rearrangement of bodies as the outermost bats push toward the entrance shafts and the innermost ones are carried along by the pressure of the mass. The passage funnels them. The throat of stacked clay brick that you descended becomes a howling tube of air and bodies, and the sound it produces is not like wind. It is like the warren itself is exhaling. It lasts — measured by the eldest women — exactly as long as it takes for thirty drips to fall from the central condensation-point. Then it is done. The chamber is quieter. The upper walls are bare of everything except fungal mat and guano. And the bats are gone, out into the hot dark surface, hunting the night-swarming insects that breed in the dry season's brief oases.

They return at dawn. The return is different — slower, more ragged, the bats coming in ones and twos and small clumps, gorged and heavy, their bellies distended with insects, and they do not roost immediately but circle the chamber first, once, twice, three times, calling in echolocation, mapping the walls that have not changed since they left, and then they land, and the wall seals itself again, and the chamber returns to its daytime hum.

The bats are the clock. The people set their lives by the exhalation and the inhalation of eleven thousand small dark bodies.

**The people** are the warren's makers. Or the warren is their maker. It is not clear, after all these centuries, which came first or whether the question even makes sense.

They number between sixty and eighty at any given time — the count fluctuates, not with seasons (there are no seasons here) but with the slow cycles of birth and death that the people track by the depth of the guano crust on the ledges, by the number of new sleeping-shelves carved, by the position of certain named stones in the central channel that mark, in some private system of accounting, the passage of generations. They do not have a name for themselves that translates. The closest approximation is "those-who-maintain." Not "those-who-live-here." Not "those-who-carve." *Those-who-maintain*. The distinction matters.

They are small. The tallest adult is perhaps five feet, and most are shorter, with broad shoulders, thick wrists, hands that are disproportionately large and strong, the knuckles scarred, the fingertips callused into smooth pads that can read the texture of clay the way the lizards read the surface of water. Their skin is the color of the walls — warm brownish-gray, slightly cool to the touch, always faintly damp. Their hair is dark and coarse and worn short, not by custom but by necessity: long hair would catch on things. In a space carved with this many edges and niches and projections, anything that dangles is a liability. They learn this as children. They learn it the way surface children learn to look both ways, or to stay away from cliff edges. The warren teaches geometry with its body.

Their language is quiet. Not silent — they speak constantly, a low murmur that rides beneath the drone of the wind-cones and the drip of the condensation — but *modulated*, shaped by a lifetime of acoustic awareness. They know which passages amplify sound and which swallow it. They know that the central chamber, with its cone-resonators, will carry a whisper from the cistern to the far entrance-shaft if you speak at a certain pitch and angle. They use this. They have always used this. They talk across the room without raising their voices, casting their words into the acoustic architecture of the space the way a fisherman casts a net, trusting the shape of the medium to carry it where it needs to go. Children learn this early — the precise pitch, the angle of the chin, the way to cup the hand near the mouth to shape the initial wavefront — and by the time they are six or seven they can hold conversations across distances that would be impossible in open air.

They do not sing. Not because they cannot, but because the warren already sings. The wind-cones provide a continuous harmonic environment that is, to them, the sound of home — the way a surface-dweller might hear wind in trees or rain on a roof. To add a human voice to it would be like talking over someone you love. They know this intuitively. Their music, when they make it, is percussive: stone on stone, bone on clay, the rhythm of tools at work, which is the rhythm of maintenance, which is the rhythm of survival, which is the rhythm of everything.

Their food is fungal. The upper mats, when harvested and dried, produce a dense, chewy substance that is their staple — bland, slightly sweet, rich in the minerals that seep up from the deep earth through the clay. They supplement it with the beetles (dried, crushed, mixed into the fungal matter), the shrimp (caught in the side-pools with woven root-fiber traps), and occasionally a lizard, though killing a lizard is not forbidden but *regretted*, done only when someone is ill and needs the particular nutrients in the lizard's liver, and the hunter will sit quietly afterward for a long time, and the children will ask why, and they will be told, and they will understand when they are older.

They do not eat the bats. The bats are not food. The bats are *breath*.

Their social structure is not hierarchical in any way you would recognize. There are elders — the women who measure the drip-cycles, the men who remember which stones in the channel were placed by their grandmothers — but they lead by memory, not by authority. Decisions are made communally, in a specific chamber off the main hall where the acoustics are such that no single voice can dominate: the walls are shaped to scatter sound equally in all directions, so that every person's words arrive at every other person's ears at the same volume. Whether this chamber was carved to facilitate democracy, or whether democracy emerged because the chamber's acoustics made it inevitable, is a question that would never occur to the people to ask. The architecture and the politics are the same thing. They have always been the same thing.

They maintain. That is what they do, what they have always done, what they will always do. The channel must be re-compacted. The cistern rim must be smoothed. The fungal mats must be harvested before they grow too thick and collapse under their own weight. The guano must be scraped from the upper ledges and mixed with clay to make the sealant that waterproofs the storage niches. The entrance shafts must be cleared of the sand that the surface winds push in. The clay cones must be checked for cracks — a cracked cone changes its pitch and throws the chamber's drone into discord, and the people can hear this, can feel it in their sternums the way a musician feels a note played slightly sharp, and they will not rest until it is repaired.

Everything they make is a repair. Every new niche is carved where an old one has crumbled. Every new section of floor is compacted over a section that has softened. They do not expand. They do not innovate. They *tend*. They tend the warren the way a gardener tends a garden, except the garden is also their house, their bed, their kitchen, their cathedral, their grave. When someone dies, they are placed in a niche in the deepest passage — the one farthest from the entrance, closest to the source of the geological warmth — and clay is packed over the opening, and the niche becomes a wall again, and the wall becomes part of the warren, and the warren absorbs the body the way it absorbs everything: slowly, completely, without judgment. The dead become clay. The clay becomes walls. The walls hold. It is not a metaphor. It is what happens.

The children are the only ones who do not maintain. They *explore*. They climb the upper walls, squeezing into passages too small for adults, disappearing for hours into the branching warrens that extend — no one knows how far — into the clay. They come back with things: crystals of mineral deposit, fragments of older niches so ancient that the tool-marks are unlike anything the living carvers use, once the desiccated skeleton of something that might have been a lizard but was larger, much larger, with teeth that the eyeless lizards do not have. The elders examine these things. They place them in a specific niche near the cistern — not the cistern itself, the people are careful about that — and they do not discuss what they mean. They simply add them to the collection. The warren remembers through objects as well as through people. Its memory is longer than anyone's. Its memory is the shape of the walls.

At night — real night, the surface night, when the bats are gone and the chamber is at its quietest and the wind-cones drone at their lowest pitch — the people sleep. They sleep in the shelves, in the niches, on the warm floor near the trough. They sleep curled, compact, their bodies shaped by the spaces they were born into, and they sleep deeply, and they dream. They dream of the warren. They dream of its passages, its chambers, its depths. They dream of places they have never been, passages that open onto rooms that do not exist, rooms filled with water, rooms filled with light, rooms filled with the sound of something that is not the wind-cones and is not the bats and is not water but is *something*, something vast and slow and patient, something that lives beneath the clay, something that the warren was built to contain or to feed or to worship or to become. They wake from these dreams and they do not speak of them, not because they are afraid but because they are *ordinary*. Everyone has them. They are part of the warren, as natural as the drip, as the drone, as the dark.

The warren is alive. The people know this not as a metaphor but as a fact, as obvious as the warmth of the floor, as the moisture of the air. It breathes. It hums. It shapes itself around them, and they shape themselves around it, and there is no seam between the living clay and the living flesh, no boundary, no difference. They are not residents. They are not inhabitants. They are *tissue*. They are the warren's slow-moving cells, its hands, its memory, and the warren is their body, their world, their home, their grave, their everything.

And the cistern moves. Something rolls beneath the dark water. No one looks. No one mentions it. The children are taught, early, not to lean over the rim. Not because it is dangerous — nothing here is *dangerous*, exactly — but because it is *private*. Whatever lives in the cistern has its own business, its own rhythm, its own deep and slow and ancient way of being, and the people extend to it the same courtesy they extend to the lizards and the bats and the spiders and the beetles and the shrimp: they let it be. They live alongside it. They do not ask what it is. They do not need to know. The warren holds it, and the warren holds them, and that is enough.

That has always been enough.
