# Who Lives Here

## The Inhabitants

They are fewer than anyone outside the plateau would believe. Estimates from outside—made by the rare cartographer or naturalist who ventures far enough to sight a settlement and then turns back—place the total population at perhaps a thousand, maybe less. The inhabitants themselves do not count. Not because they cannot, but because counting implies that the number might change, and the number does not change, not on any timescale that registers as meaningful. People are born. People die. The moss grows. The walls drift. The springs shift. The population of a settlement might fluctuate by three or four people across the span of a century.

They are human. There is no question of that, though visitors who have visited—a small enough number to be almost theoretical—sometimes struggle to articulate *how* they know this. The inhabitants are not deformed, not altered, not divergent in any anatomical way that a physician could name. They are simply *adjusted*. Their movements have a quality that outsiders describe, after fumbling for words, as *completed*. There is no wasted gesture. No fidgeting. No idle motion. When an inhabitant reaches for a tool, the hand goes directly to it, with the same unhurried precision as a root finding a water channel through rock. When they walk, they walk with the flat, economical stride of people who have internalized, in their muscles and tendons and the deep architecture of their bones, the exact texture of gravel underfoot, the precise angle of slope, the subtle resistance of wind at a given hour of a given season.

Their faces are weathered, but not in the way of farmers or sailors elsewhere. The weathering is even, as though the wind has had its way with every surface simultaneously rather than scouring from one direction. Their skin has the tonal quality of the local stone—dark, dense, matte. Their eyes are steady. They blink less often than people elsewhere, though whether this is adaptation or affectation is impossible to determine.

They age. They age slowly, and then all at once. A woman of seventy might look forty to an outsider's eye. Then one year, the accumulated weight of decades will settle into her frame in the space of a season, and she will become old with a sudden totality that suggests the aging was always there, held in reserve, waiting for permission. The old among them are very old. The oldest woman in the largest remaining settlement—though "largest" here means perhaps sixty people—is said to have been alive for more than a century, though the inhabitants do not measure years in the conventional sense. They measure in moss-growth. In wall-drift. In the number of times a particular spring has gone dry and returned.

She is the one who remembers the last nearing that passed directly through a building and did not leave.

## What They Do

They build. They tend. They watch.

The building is the most visible activity, the one most likely to be observed by a hypothetical outsider who somehow reached a settlement and was permitted to stay long enough to observe. A master builder—and each settlement has one, sometimes two—does not draw plans. The plans are *remembered*, carried in a tradition of oral recitation that is not quite chanting and not quite conversation, spoken aloud in the half-dark of a semi-subterranean chamber while the other builders listen with their eyes closed. The recitation describes angles, counter-leans, the expected drift-rate of a given type of stone in a given season. It includes corrections accumulated over generations: *the south wall of the granary in [name] drifted two finger-widths more than the counter-lean anticipated in the forty-third generation; increase the counter-lean for southern exposures by this amount.* The recitation can last hours. It is spoken once, and then it is done, and the builders go to work with the understanding that the recitation has given them everything they need, the way a single glance at a familiar room gives you everything you need to cross it in the dark.

The tending is slower.

Each settlement maintains its nearest moss patch the way other cultures maintain their fields or their flocks. But the analogy breaks down immediately. You do not plant moss. You do not water it in any conventional sense. You *attend* to it. A moss-tender walks the perimeter of their settlement's patch each day—a walk that might take hours, given that the patches sprawl for hectares—and they look. They look for changes in color that indicate a shift in subsurface water flow. They listen for the particular quality of drip that means a new springlet is forming at the patch's edge. They press their palms against the sphagnum and feel the temperature, the saturation, the slow pulse of hydraulic pressure that the plants transmit from the deep rock beneath. When they find a bare stone on the patch's margin, they might transfer a fragment of liverwort from the patch's interior, setting it down with the care of a jeweler, and then return to that spot once a week for the next three years to see if it has taken.

They do not hurry the moss. They cannot. But they make themselves available to it, positioning themselves as the means by which the patch can extend itself over distances it would otherwise take decades to cross. A moss-tender who has spent twenty years at their work can point to a lichen colony on a stone and say: *I placed the first fragment there when I was a child, with my mother's hand guiding mine.* The colony might be the size of a palm.

This is agriculture, after a fashion. But it is agriculture without harvest, without surplus, without the concept of yield. The moss is not consumed. It is *cohabited with*. The settlements nestle against their moss patches the way a hermit crab nestles into a shell—not for shelter, exactly, but for completion. The moss provides moisture regulation, wind buffering, the thin organic layer from which the few medicinal and structural plants are carefully, sparingly harvested. The settlement provides attention. The relationship is old enough and mutual enough that neither side, if asked, would describe it as a relationship at all.

## Language

They speak, but their language is not like other languages.

It is *sparse* in the way the plateau is sparse—few words, arranged with extreme precision, each one carrying an enormous amount of contextual weight that can only be understood by someone who has heard the word used in dozens of prior contexts across the span of a lifetime. A single word might mean "the sound of water moving through a spring that has recently changed its course" or "the quality of light on gravel when fog is about to form." These are not compound words or descriptive phrases compressed into a single utterance. They are *specific words*, each one an indivisible unit of meaning, developed over generations of precise observation.

There are, as noted, no words for animal. But the absence goes deeper than vocabulary. The language has no grammatical structures for predation, no verb forms for hunting, no metaphors drawn from chase or flight. The metaphors that *do* exist are almost entirely geological and hydrological. To say that someone is clever, you say they are *deep-permeable*. To say someone is stubborn, you say they are *basalt*. To describe grief, the word translates roughly as *a spring that has gone dry and may not return*—and the emotional weight of this word, to someone who has grown up knowing what it means to depend on a spring, is considerable.

The language has seventeen words for silence. Not as a poetic indulgence, but as a practical necessity. The silence of a settlement at night is different from the silence of the gravel plain at midday, which is different from the silence inside a nearing, which is different from the silence of a moss patch in deep winter when even the water has stopped dripping. These are *experienced* silences, each with a distinct phenomenological texture, and the inhabitants navigate them the way a mariner navigates currents—not by naming them, but by *being in them* and knowing, from long familiarity, what they mean.

## The Ones Who Walk

Not all inhabitants live in settlements.

There are those—few, always few—who walk. The settlements do not exile them. They do not leave in disgrace or in search of something. They leave because the walking *calls*, the way the softening calls, the way the nearings call. They are usually young, though not always. They are usually those who have spent too long listening to the wind and found that it was saying something they could not hear from inside stone walls.

They carry almost nothing. A waterskin, though water is everywhere if you know where to listen. A copper blade, soft and easily bent, for cutting moss when food runs low—though the inhabitants can go days without eating, their metabolisms slowed to match the plateau's pace. A set of bone tools for inscribing, should they feel the need to record what they have seen.

They walk between settlements, which means they walk for weeks across the gravel plain, alone, in silence, with nothing but the wind and the shifting polygons and the fog. They learn to read the nearings—entering them deliberately, walking through zones where distance becomes negotiable, where a day's travel might cover what should take ten days, or might cover nothing at all. Some of them come back changed. *Permeable*, in the language's own term—opened up, made receptive to something that living inside stone walls, however thick, does not fully allow.

What they have experienced inside the nearings, they do not describe with words. They describe it with *building*. A walker who returns to a settlement will sometimes take up a mason's tools and begin to build—not with the careful counter-leans of the tradition, but with a new angle, a new geometry, something they learned from the softening itself. These buildings are the ones that drift fastest, that lean the hardest, that seem almost *eager* to become something other than what they were built to be.

The other inhabitants watch these buildings with the same attentive calm they bring to everything. They do not judge. They adjust. If a building drifts too far, they abandon it. If it settles into a new configuration that is stable—or as stable as anything on this plateau ever is—they incorporate it into the settlement, and the oral recitation of the builders adjusts to account for the new data.

Some walkers do not come back. The settlements do not mourn them in the way other cultures mourn. There is a word for it—roughly *a spring that has gone deeper*—and the emotional register of the word is not loss but *depth*, a sense that someone has followed the water down to wherever it goes and has become part of the circulation. Whether this is a euphemism for death or an accurate description of something that actually happens, no one can say. The plateau does not give back what it takes.

## The Fungi

Beneath the moss, beneath the thin organic layer, in the dark and the damp and the cold, the fungi.

The inhabitants know about them. They do not speak of them often, but they know. The fungal hyphae are the connective tissue of every moss patch—the vast, invisible web that links every sphagnum mat, every liverwort sheet, every lichen-encrusted stone into a single, slow, unified organism that is not, strictly speaking, one organism or many but something the language does not have a word for because the concept, like the thing itself, resists boundary.

The hyphae extend down. Farther than the roots of any plant. Into the upper layers of the softened bedrock itself, threading through the micro-fractures and permeable zones where the stone has begun its long transition from solid to something else. The fungi are, as far as anyone can determine, the only living things that directly touch the softening. They are the interface between the biological and the geological, between the quick and the immeasurably slow.

The moss-tenders know this. They feel it when they press their palms to the sphagnum—that faint, subsonic thrum that is not the heartbeat of the plant but of the mycelial network beneath, transmitting something upward from the deep rock. What is being transmitted, no one can say. Nutrients, certainly. Water, certainly. But also something else—a *signal*, perhaps, or a *memory*, or simply the slow, cumulative pressure of a world that is in the process of becoming something it has never been before.

The mushrooms are small. Rare. They appear in the moss patches without warning—tiny, pale, almost translucent, growing in clusters of three or five and lasting only a day or two before dissolving back into the substrate with a softness that suggests they were never fully solid to begin with. The inhabitants do not eat them. They do not touch them. A moss-tender who finds a mushroom cluster will mark the spot with a small cairn of dark stone and visit it each day until the mushrooms are gone, and then visit it still, for weeks afterward, pressing their ear to the moss and listening.

What they hear, they do not say. But the word they use translates as: *the ground is speaking to itself*.

## Children

They are rare. They are precious in a way that has nothing to do with sentimentality and everything to do with mathematics: each child represents a measurable fraction of the settlement's total population, and the loss of even one—through accident, through illness, through the quiet, unexplained disappearances that sometimes happen near the edges of a nearing—alters the social geometry of the community for decades.

They are raised collectively, not by design but by necessity: in a settlement of forty people, there might be three or four children, and every adult has a role in their formation. They learn the oral recitations early—sitting in the semi-subterranean chambers with their eyes closed, absorbing the builders' knowledge the way the rock absorbs water, slowly and without apparent effort. They learn to walk the gravel in the flat, economical stride of their elders. They learn the seventeen silences. They learn the names of the mosses, the shapes of the polygons, the sounds a spring makes before it changes course.

And they play. In a place this quiet, this still, this devoid of the stimuli that drive children's play elsewhere, they play with an intensity that is startling. They run across the gravel, shouting—not the low, deliberate speech of adults, but genuine *shouting*, their voices thin and sharp against the wind, carrying across the settlement with a transgressive energy that the adults tolerate with visible patience and something that might be longing. They play in the nearings, daring each other to walk deeper, to see how far the geometry will bend. They chase each other through buildings that lean and shift, shrieking with delight when a doorway opens onto a courtyard that was not there yesterday.

They are the only source of genuine, unstructured noise on the plateau, and the plateau seems to *absorb* them—not harmfully, but gently, the way the gravel absorbs fog, the way the bedrock absorbs water. A child who grows up on the plateau becomes, over the years, quieter, slower, more *completed*. The transition happens so gradually that it is only visible in retrospect: a young woman of twenty who still shouts when she runs across the gravel, and then at twenty-five does not shout but still runs, and then at thirty does not run but still walks quickly, and then at forty walks with the unhurried, deliberate stride of the plateau itself, and when she sits in a room and is silent, she is *fully* silent, and the silence she inhabits has a name in the language, and the name means something like: *the quiet that exists inside the stone before the stone remembers it was once something else*.

## The Thing That Does Not Live Here But Is Not Absent

There is no god on the plateau. No animistic spirit. No personification of the land or the water or the wind. The inhabitants do not worship. They do not pray. They do not make offerings to the springs or the moss or the bedrock.

But they are *in relationship* with the plateau in a way that is so old, so total, so deeply embedded in their language and their architecture and their daily practice that the Western distinction between "living in a place" and "being part of a place" does not apply. They do not *believe* the plateau is alive. They do not *believe* it is conscious. These are categories that belong to other places, other ways of thinking. What they *know*—in the bodily, pre-linguistic, hyphae-threading-through-bedrock way that they know anything—is that the plateau is *doing something*. That the softening is not random. That the nearings are not accidents. That the drift is not malfunction.

The ground is remembering.

What it remembers—what it was before it was ground—no one knows. The oldest records do not reach back that far. The deepest bore-holes, driven with copper tools and infinite patience, reveal only more basalt, more granite, more of the same immeasurably ancient crystalline uniformity. There is no fossil layer. No transition. No moment of origin. The plateau simply *is*, and it has been *is* for so long that the question of what it was *before* becomes not a question of history but of ontology.

But the softening continues. The boundary between the surface and whatever lies beneath loosens, and the loosening accelerates, and the nearings grow more frequent, and the springs multiply, and the moss patches spread—not quickly, not dramatically, but with the slow, inexorable certainty of a tide.

And the people watch. They maintain. They build their walls at counter-leans and walk their gravel paths and tend their moss and listen to their springs and speak in their sparse, stone-dense language and raise their children in the wind and the fog and the quiet. They do not prepare for an ending. They do not stockpile or flee or build higher walls. They do what they have always done, which is to *be here*, fully and attentively, in a place that is in the process of becoming something that has never existed before.

And if you asked them—any of them, the oldest woman in the largest settlement, the youngest child still learning the names of silences, the walker returned from a nearing with a new geometry in their eyes—if you asked them what they think will happen when the thinning completes, when the softening reaches whatever it is reaching toward, when the ground finally remembers what it was before it was ground—

They would not answer.

They would lead you to the edge of the settlement, and they would point outward, across the gravel, toward the horizon where the polygons lose their names and the fog gathers and the wind makes a sound like a world exhaling for the last time, or the first time, or perhaps both at once.

And they would say: *yes. It does that.*
