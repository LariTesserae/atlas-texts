The people of Cairn-tha do not live upon the stone. They are its custodians, its nervous system, its brief, warm-blooded punctuation in a sentence written by geology. They are not numerous. Perhaps eighty souls in all, but to count them feels a violation, like counting the water beads on a slab of granite. You do not see them at first. You see their evidence.

The **Weaver** is a woman whose name is the same word for the tight, spiral pattern of lichen on a windward rock. Her fingers are stained with the dyes of bog myrtle and rust, her knuckles as rounded as the pebbles in the cistern. She does not look up as she works. Her vertical loom is strung with threads of goat hair and twisted moss-fiber, and the cloth she creates is not for decoration but for necessity—a dense, breathable fabric that holds the dampness at bay. Her rhythm is the *thump-hiss* of the beater, a sound as regular as drip-water.

On a sheltered, sun-facing ledge sits the **Knapper**, an old man with dust in the lines of his face. He sits cross-legged before a buckskin apron, surrounded by the sacred debris of his craft: flakes of flint the color of storm clouds, antler billets for pressure flaking, and the small, precious nodules of bog iron, dark and pitted. His movements are a study in conservation. He does not strike wastefully. Each blow is a question asked of the stone, each flake a considered answer. The *scrape-thud* heard earlier is his hammer-stone on a granite core, roughing out a new blade for a skinning knife. His tools are extensions of his will, and his will is to endure.

A **child**, no older than seven, moves with the silent sure-footedness of a goat. Their task is the cisterns. They carry a small, tightly-woven basket of dried moss and use it to skim the floating particles of grit and leaf from the still, black surfaces. They watch the water level with an elder’s gravity, their small face reflecting the profound importance of this duty. They are learning the first rule: *nothing is wasted, everything is accounted for*. Their play is mimicry of work—stacking pebbles into miniature cairns, tracing the patterns of iron stains with a fingertip.

There is an **Elder**, genderless and slow, who sits on the edge of the central hearth-court. They do not tend the fire—that is a shared, rotating duty. They simply *are*. Their hands, gnarled as the rowan roots, rest on a smooth, thigh-worn granite slab beside them. They are the memory. They remember the lean year when the cisterns ran low and the moss had to be wrung for dew. They remember the great storm that scoured a roof clean. They speak rarely, but when they do, their voice is the rasp of stone on stone, and the words are not advice, but facts of balance. "The wind took more this year," they might say, and the community understands: the hearth-fire will burn an hour less each evening.

A **goatherd**, a young person with eyes the grey of the mist, tends three nimble, grey-coated creatures. These goats are not livestock; they are partners in the enterprise. They browse the tough heather on the slopes the people cannot easily reach, they provide milk and hair for yarn, and their droppings are carefully gathered for the tiny, precious plots of soil tucked behind stone windbreaks where a few medicinal herbs and root vegetables struggle to grow. The goatherd speaks to them in a soft, clicking language, a dialogue of movement and gesture.

Their **Leader**, if such a title applies, is not a ruler but a **Stabilizer**. It is the person whose hands are most skilled at the art of balance—fitting a new stone into an ancient wall, adjusting the pitch of a schist roof-slab, knowing where to cut a new peat-root without destabilizing a slope. They are chosen not by vote or lineage, but by a quiet consensus of competence. They are the one who, when a fissure in the central hearth-slab was found, spent a week studying it before declaring it stable, a decision that allowed life to continue unchanged.

They are a single organism. The Weaver’s cloth warms the Knapper’s shoulders on a chill morning. The Knapper’s new flint knife is given to the Goatherd to mend a tether. The Goatherd’s goat finds a rare patch of fungus that the Elder identifies as a dye-source for the Weaver. The Child’s careful work ensures the water that boils the roots for the evening meal is clean. The Stabilizer’s quiet adjustments ensure the fire that cooks that meal burns safely.

Their stories are not of heroes or conquests, but of **endurance**. They tell of the Time of the Long Mist, when the sun was a forgotten rumor and the water-veins in the rock swelled. They tell of the Great Stillness, when not even a raven croaked for a fortnight. Their highest virtue is to make no mark, to take only what is given by the slow, cyclical generosity of the ridge, and to leave the stone as they found it—occupied, but not changed. They are the tenants of a cathedral built by ice and time, and their tenancy is one of profound, silent respect.
