# What Lives Here

**The Builders — Gone, but Not Absent**

No one alive remembers them. Nothing they left behind says *we were here* in any language that translates. But the hand-marks on the ceramic say *someone was here*, and that is enough. Whoever they were, they built with the patience of geology and the intimacy of a whisper. They shaped this place grain by grain, adze by adze, over generations that must have counted into the thousands. They understood something about permanence that we have lost—that if you build slowly enough, carefully enough, in a place where nothing will knock your work down, you can eventually stop building because you are finished. You have made the world.

They are gone now. They left no bones, no tools embedded in the walls, no inscriptions that could be mistaken for anything other than decoration. Whatever happened to them—whether they changed into something else, whether they simply stopped, whether they are still here in a form no longer recognizable—it is complete. The place is finished. It does not need them anymore.

But the work remains, and the work is a kind of voice.

**The Filter-Dwellers — the Living Film**

The oldest and most abundant residents are almost too simple to call creatures. They are the microbial mats and biofilms that coat every surface of the ceramic in a living skin, microns thick, in shades so faint they register more as texture than color—a slickness, a slightly different quality of sheen where the bioluminescence catches the wall and the light seems to sink into it rather than bounce back. These films are the foundation of everything. They metabolize minerals from the water, drawn from the slow seeps that weep through the mountain's basalt bones. They crack molecules no one has named. They are the patient, silent economy upon which every other life here depends.

Some patches are denser than others, and these denser patches have a faint, warm flavor—if you could taste the water here, which of course you cannot, because you do not exist in this place—that draws grazers to them in circuits that repeat over years, decades, whole lifetimes. The films know when they are being grazed. They thicken and thin in slow rhythms, not as a defense, but as a kind of conversation. The grazers keep them from overgrowing their own edges. The films keep the grazers fed. Neither party would recognize the word *cooperation*, but they are deep into it, immemorial partners in a contract neither can break.

**The Dart-Shoals — the Silver Coins**

These are the first things you would see, if you could see. Schools of small, eyeless organisms, each one no longer than a finger joint, with flattened, scaleless bodies that flash with reflected bioluminescence. They move in sudden, coordinated bursts—like a handful of coins thrown across a dark room, catching the light as they spin, then gone, then flashing again from a different angle. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see by except the cold glow of other creatures, and that is enough. They navigate by the faint electrical fields their neighbors produce, a constant, involuntary broadcast of position and intent. The school moves as a single thought, a distributed mind with no center, each individual responding not to a leader but to the collective pressure-wave of the whole.

They feed on the microbial films, darting in to scrape a mouthful from a pillar or a vault's lip, then wheeling away in a glittering arc. They are fast by the standards of this place, which means they can cross a chamber in what might be a minute or might be ten. Time does not move here the way it does elsewhere. The shoals are the closest thing this world has to urgency, and even they are patient, patient, patient.

They are preyed upon, occasionally, by things larger and slower. When a predator approaches, the shoal does not flee so much as *dissolve*—spreading apart, dimming their collective flash, becoming invisible scatter instead of visible pattern. The predator, navigating by electric field, suddenly finds itself surrounded by a thousand tiny signals instead of one coherent school, and the hunt falters, slows, and often ends. The silver coins regroup. They flash again. They continue.

There are perhaps dozens of distinct shoals in this chamber. They do not mix. Each shoal has a slightly different rhythm of flashing, a slightly different resting electrical signature, and they seem to recognize their own. When two shoals pass near each other, there is a brief, bright moment of interference—flashes stuttering, patterns overlapping—and then they separate again, each maintaining its own grammar of light.

**The Tendrils — the Caressing Lights**

Along the walls, the pillars, the edges of every niche and vault, there are things that glow. They are soft-bodied, sessile, rooted in the microbial film itself by some attachment that may be adhesive, may be structural, may be something else entirely. They extend tendrils—long, fine, translucent filaments that trail in the slow current like the hair of drowned women, like the fingers of something dreaming. Each tendril is tipped with a bioluminescent organ that pulses in a slow, irregular rhythm, blue-green to ghostly white, never quite the same interval twice. The effect is of a field of cold stars rooted to the walls, swaying gently, flickering.

They feed by absorption, drawing nutrients from the water through their filament walls. They are also, in their quiet way, predatory. A dart-shoal that passes too close, that brushes a tendril, may find itself briefly entangled—not grasped, exactly, but *adhered to*, the filament's surface releasing some mucus or enzyme that slows movement for just a moment, long enough for the tendril to absorb a few scales, a mouthful of flesh. The silver coin struggles free. It loses a little. It does not die, usually. The tendrils are not hunters; they are opportunists, and their diet is mostly the invisible soup of dissolved organics in the water. But they take what comes to them.

In some places, the tendrils have grown thick, their filaments intertwining into mats that drape entire pillars in living light. These clusters pulse in unison—not because they are coordinated by any nervous system, but because they share a chemical rhythm, a slow metabolic tide that passes from one filament to the next through the water itself. A pillar draped in tendrils looks, for all the world, like it is breathing.

**The Drifters — the Living Clouds**

These are the gentle presences, the large and slow things that move between the vaults like weather. They are gelatinous, translucent, ranging in size from a cupped hand to a spread blanket. They have no fixed shape; they billow and fold and slowly rotate, their bodies a constant, quiet negotiation with the currents. Some are nearly invisible, seen only by the way they dim the lights behind them as they pass—a shadow of glass. Others are densely threaded with bioluminescent filaments of their own, so that they glow from within like lanterns wrapped in silk, casting slow-moving halos across the carved walls as they drift.

They filter the water. They eat the dart-shoals, sometimes, swallowing a straggler in a slow, enveloping fold that takes an hour to complete. They eat each other, rarely, one drifter absorbing another in a merging so gradual it is difficult to say where one ends and the other begins. Mostly they eat the microbial films by settling onto a surface and simply *resting* there for days, absorbing what grows beneath them, then lifting away on some imperceptible change in buoyancy or intention, leaving a clean, faintly glowing patch of ceramic where the film is thinned but not gone, already regrowing.

They are the clouds of this world. They drift. They are so slow that a single drift across the chamber might take a season. They are so quiet that a dart-shoal can pass through one without either party seeming to notice—the silver coins glinting inside the drifter's translucent body like stars inside a fog, emerging unharmed on the other side, except for the one that isn't fast enough, the one that touches an inner filament and stops, and is slowly, slowly folded into the soft interior, its light going out.

The drifters are the largest things here. They are also the most passive, the most patient, the most like the architecture itself. A drifter that settles into a niche and stops moving can become, over years, indistinguishable from the carved forms around it—a new stalactite of living glass, its light fading to the faintest ember, its body slowly mineralizing as the ceramic's chemistry seeps into it and it becomes, finally, part of the wall. Whether this is death or completion, no one is here to say. The drifters do not seem to resist it. Some of them seek it out, drifting not toward food or current but toward a niche of exactly the right size, settling in with a finality that feels deliberate.

The oldest niches have shapes inside them that are neither ceramic nor creature but something in between. They glow faintly. They are warmer than the surrounding stone. They might be dead drifters. They might be something else—something that grew from the merger of architecture and organism, a third category of thing that this place invented on its own, without instruction.

**The Hum — the Sound That Listens**

There is a sound in the water, and it is not made by any of the creatures described above. It is a sub-vocal hum, below the threshold of hearing as most creatures understand it, felt more in the bones and the fluid of the body than in any ear. It rises from the ceramic itself—from the resonant quality of the material, from the way the warm currents from the planet's interior set the walls vibrating at a frequency so low it is almost a pressure wave. The hum is constant. It varies, but it never stops.

The creatures respond to it. The dart-shoals synchronize their flashes to it, unconsciously, their electrical pulses falling into step with the ceramic's vibration the way a heartbeat can synchronize with a drum. The tendrils' pulses are modulated by it, their glow brightening and dimming in rhythm. The drifters' buoyancy seems to change with it, rising and falling in slow cycles that match the hum's great, patient undulation.

It is possible—this is speculation, because there is no one here to confirm or deny it—that the hum is the place itself, still functioning. That the architecture was designed not just as shelter and substrate, but as an instrument, a vast ceramic resonator tuned to the planet's own frequency, humming the same note it has humd since the last builder set the last adze down and stepped back and let the water in. That the creatures did not find this place by accident but were drawn to it, or grew from it, or are a function of it—symbiotes not with each other but with the architecture itself, their bioluminescence a response to the hum, their lives a kind of singing along.

If you could slow down time enough, if you could sit in this chamber for centuries and watch with infinite patience, you might see it: the place is not merely inhabited. It is *alive*, in a way that includes the ceramic, the creatures, the water, the hum, the heat from the planet's core—all of it a single organism, slow beyond comprehension, dreaming in the dark.

**What Does Not Live Here**

Nothing here has teeth, exactly. Nothing here screams. Nothing here builds, or wars, or remembers the sun. There are no plants, no predators fast enough to be called hunters, no prey smart enough to be called afraid. There is no day, no season, no reason to hurry. The things that live here do not compete so much as *occupy*, each species filling a niche so narrow and so old that the niche and the creature are indistinguishable, two names for the same slow fact.

There are no parasites in the way that word is usually meant—nothing that takes without giving. But there are dependencies so deep they look like design: the dart-shoals need the films, the tendrils need the films and the current, the drifters need the niches and the hum, the hum needs the ceramic, the ceramic needs the heat, the heat needs the planet, and the planet needs nothing, having no outside, having no comparison. It is a closed loop, a circuit so complete it has no remainder. Every input is an output is an input. The place breathes. The place feeds. The place is.

It is not waiting for anything. Waiting implies expectation, expectation implies a future that differs from the present. Here, the present is the future is the past. The ceramic erodes at a rate that will take millions of years to matter. The creatures reproduce at a rate that exactly replaces what is lost. The hum does not change. The light does not brighten or dim over centuries; it maintains, as steady as a held breath that will never need to be released.

If you were here—if a being from the world above, from the sunlit, hurried, noisy world, were to find this place—you would feel the pressure first, the warm weight of the water and the silence. And then you would feel something else: the sensation that the place is *aware* of you, not with intelligence, not with intention, but with the total, undivided attention of a system that has been watching itself for so long that any change, any intruder, any new pattern in the electrical field or the drift of sediment, registers immediately, is absorbed into the hum, is incorporated.

You would not be harmed. You would not be welcomed. You would simply become part of the circuit, your warmth adding a degree to the water, your breath—if you breathed here—adding a molecule to the microbial films, your body brushing a tendril and feeding it a little, your presence sending the dart-shoals into a brief, glittering scatter before they regroup and flash and continue.

And in time—years, decades, if you stayed long enough—your bones would mineralize. Your flesh would feed the films. Your shape would be smoothed by the water and the microbial growth and the patient, relentless ceramic, and you would become a new feature in the wall, a niche, a vault, a place where a drifter might settle and dream itself into stone.

You would become part of what lives here.

You would become what lives here.
