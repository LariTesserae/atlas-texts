The inhabitants of this place do not live *in* it so much as they *are* it. They are the phases of a single, continuous process. To list them is to list the stages of a slow, green digestion.

**The Primary Residents: The Vines and the Mycelium.**
They are the true population, the architects and the muscle. The woody vines, with trunks as thick as a man's thigh, are the circulatory and skeletal systems both. They snake through shattered windows, coil around rusted girders, and heave walls apart with the patient, hydraulic pressure of growth. They are not parasites; they are successors. Their aerial roots, like coarse, questing fingers, sift through the soil of decay, transforming concrete dust into new soil. In tandem, the fungal mycelium—the hidden population—permeates everything. It is a vast, silent network threading through rotting wood, crumbling plaster, and the soft mulch of fallen leaves. It is the great recycler, the alchemist turning the past into the substance of the future. On the surface, it manifests as the rust-colored sheets climbing the walls and the sudden, shocking blooms of fan-shaped fungi from the carcass of the generator—a vibrant, fruiting statement of ownership.

**The Secondary Tenants: The Mosses and Liverworts.**
These are the soft furnishings of the city. They form a continuous, spongy carpet over every horizontal and many vertical surfaces, holding the mist as a perpetual dew. They are the first responders, sealing wounds in the asphalt, softening the sharp edges of broken metal, and creating a moist, living medium for the next generation of seeds. In the perpetual twilight, they are an emerald so deep it appears black in the shadows, a blanket of slow, photosynthetic patience.

**The Insects: The Whispering Population.**
They are rarely seen but constantly signified. The pale, long-legged beetle is a ghost in the machine, its carapace the color of old bone, moving with deliberate calm over the moss mats as if patrolling its damp estate. The crickets are the voice of the place, their sharp, rhythmic chirps emanating from deep within hollow walls or from beneath the leaf litter—a sound that emphasizes the quiet rather than breaks it. Ants, small and dark, form relentless rivers along the barely-visible paths, their industry now dedicated to harvesting fungal growths and decomposing plant matter, their hills erupting in soft, brown mounds from the seams of old pavement. They are the last vestige of organized, communal labor, repurposed entirely for the new economy of decay.

**The Vertebrates: The Ghosts.**
They are almost not here, and their absence defines the atmosphere. No birdsong pierces the canopy because the canopy is a dense, woven ceiling of living and dead material, offering no perch and no sky. A mammal might pass through—a shrew, velvet-dark and frantic, navigating the tunnels in the moss and leaf litter, hunting the beetles and worms. But it is a transient, a visitor from the drier rim. The true vertebrate presence is in the bones: the occasional, delicate skeleton of a rat or a songbird, cradled in a nest of vibrant moss, its calcium slowly being incorporated into the green matrix. They are not inhabitants so much as offerings.

**The Memory of Industry: The Relic Inhabitants.**
The generator, blooming with fungi, is a garden. The water tower, now a vine-wrapped tree, is a habitat for woodlice and millipedes in its damp, dark interior. The conduits, filled with roots, are nurseries for seedlings. These are not objects but locations—the central plaza, the twisted grove of tower-legs, the long, shadowed hall where the roof has caved in to create a sudden, mist-filled atrium of light. The inhabitants here are the *processes* occurring within the relics: the slow splitting of metal by expanding root, the pooling of water in a rusted basin now hosting a microcosm of algae and water-striders.

**The True Resident: The Equilibrium.**
Ultimately, the only real inhabitant is the state of being itself: the **Patient Reclamation**. It is a presence felt in the weight of the air, the rhythm of the drip, the relentless, soft pressure everywhere. The vines are its hands. The fungi are its digestion. The insects are its nerve impulses. The mist is its breath. Nothing here is fighting for survival; everything is enacting a foregone conclusion. The city is not haunted by its past, but pregnant with its future—a future that is quieter, softer, and utterly, implacably green. To live here is to be in a state of becoming something else, and the only sound is the soft, endless rustle of that transformation.
