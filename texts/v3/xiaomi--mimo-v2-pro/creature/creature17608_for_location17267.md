# What Lives on the Salt Pan of Gyre-7

---

## The Grazers

They are the baseline, the constant, the thing you see before you see anything else—because there are so many of them, and because they never stop moving.

The **silicate grazers** (the name is a convenience; no one named them, and they do not know they are grazers) travel in loose herds of thirty to several hundred individuals, flowing across the hexagonal salt plates like oil spreading across a hot skillet. They are *flat*. Dangerously, absurdly flat—perhaps two centimeters tall at the carapace's apex, but spanning forty to sixty centimeters from head to the fanned terminus of their segmented tails. Their shape recalls a trilobite if it had been run over by a glacier and then polished. Each segment of their body is overlapped by a translucent plate of biogenic silica—actual glass, secreted from the minerals they consume—and these plates are not uniform. The newest segments, near the tail, are thin and pale, almost colorless. The oldest, at the head, are thick, layered, and stained deep ochre or crimson from accumulated iron compounds. A mature grazer's front end looks like a shard of stained glass; its tail end looks like cellophane.

They move in a ripple. Not a wave, not a crawl—a *ripple*, each segment lifting and setting down in sequence, and the whole herd moves this way, hundreds of tiny glass plates clicking and scraping against the salt crust in a sound like a slow, sustained rain on a tin roof. *Tick-tick-tick-tick-tick.* It is one of the most pervasive sounds on the pan, audible above the hiss of vents when a large herd passes close.

Their mandibles are not jaws. They are *files*—paired, ridged, continuously growing structures made of a material harder than the salt they consume. They work by abrasion. A grazer lowers its head, locks its first three segments against the ground using tiny hooked spines on the underside of its legs, and then *rakes* the mandibles back and forth across the mineral crust. They are not eating the salt itself, not really. They are harvesting the thin, dark biofilm of chemosynthetic microorganisms—bacteria-analogues and archaea-analogues—that colonize every warm surface where mineral-rich gas has recently passed. This film is invisible to a casual eye. It looks like shadow, or dampness, or the faintest tarnish on metal. But it is dense with fixed carbon and sulfur compounds, and it is the foundation of every food chain on Gyre-7. The grazers are simply the most visible harvesters of it.

They do not flee from danger in any coordinated way. There is no alarm signal, no cry. When a glass-stalker strikes, the grazers nearest the kill simply *spread*, their ripple-pattern widening, individuals turning aside like water parting around a stone. The herd loses a member. The rest keep filing. The tick-tick-tick resumes.

But they are not mindless. They choose their paths with something that looks, from a distance, like intention—angling toward recently vented fissures where fresh biofilm is thickest, avoiding plates that are cooling and cracking, clustering on the warmer leeward edges of volcanic glass spires where the microorganism colonies are richest. They follow the heat. Over time, their collective movement traces the invisible topography of geothermal flow across the pan, and if you could see only their paths from above—just the pale tracks scratched through the biofilm—you would see a map of the planet's subsurface plumbing, rendered in slow glass.

## The Vent Worms

They are the first thing you feel before you see them. Not their weight, not their heat—but their *breathing*.

A vent worm does not breathe in any terrestrial sense. It has no lungs, no gills, no circulating fluid that contacts air. Instead, it extends a **plume**—a crown of feathery, branching filaments, each one a hollow tube lined with catalytic proteins—from the mouth of a fissure. The plume is pale, almost white, translucent, and it moves constantly: opening, closing, twisting, retracting and re-extending in slow, rhythmic pulses. Each pulse takes roughly fifteen to twenty seconds. And each pulse displaces a tiny amount of air—a puff, a sigh, a micro-breeze that you can feel on your face from ten meters away if the worm is large.

The plume is the only part of the worm you will ever see. The rest of the body—tens of meters long, possibly longer; no one has measured the deep ones—is sealed inside the fissure, threaded through the fractured rock and hot salt like a root through soil. They do not move. They do not need to. The fissure *comes to them*, in a sense: the slow tectonic shifting of the salt plates opens and closes vent systems over decades, and the worms grow into new fissures as old ones seal, or retreat deeper as surface conditions change. A single worm may occupy a fissure system for centuries. Its body, where it surfaces, is a leathery, dark-brown cylinder about the diameter of a human torso, though larger specimens are reported—described by the silence after the crack of a salt plate giving way to accommodate their girth.

They eat what the air brings them. The plume filaments are coated in a sticky, thermophilic mucus that traps trace gases—hydrogen sulfide, methane, carbon monoxide, ammonia—and the catalytic proteins break these down through a series of redox reactions that yield energy. The chemistry is essentially what the microorganisms in the biofilm do, but the worms do it in open air, with no need for a surface to colonize. They are, in a sense, *mobile biofilms*—except they are not mobile at all.

The plume is exquisitely sensitive. It can detect changes in gas concentration of a few parts per million, and it responds by adjusting its spread—wider when gas flux is high, tighter and retracted when flux drops or when the chemistry shifts to something unpalatable. During periods of intense venting—when a new fissure opens or an old one surges—the plume goes *wild*, flaring open, the filaments whipping and curling, and a large worm during a surge event is a striking sight: a ghostly white chrysanthemum of filaments erupting from a crack in the crimson salt, pulsing so fast that it seems to vibrate, feeding on the sudden abundance.

The sound they make, if you press your ear to the salt near a fissure, is a low, wet, clicking—not from the plume, but from the body deeper inside. It is the sound of the worm's peristaltic contractions, the muscular waves that move nutrients along its length. Thousands of these clicks layered together, from dozens of worms in adjacent fissures, merge into a faint, irregular patter beneath the dominant tectonic hum. It sounds like something *digesting*.

## The Glass-Stalkers

I will be honest: almost everything known about them is inferred. They are very, very good at what they do.

A glass-stalker is roughly the size of a large terrestrial predator—a wolf, say, or a leopard—but this comparison is meaningless because its body plan is nothing like either. It is **low, angular, and articulated**, with eight limbs arranged in four pairs along a central axis. The limbs are not legs in any familiar sense; they are jointed rods of obsidian-dark chitin that fold flat against the body when at rest, so that the stalker resembles nothing so much as a piece of volcanic debris—a shard of glass, a blackened chunk of basalt, a heat-fractured rock. Its carapace is composed of overlapping plates of biogenic silica infused with carbon, giving it a deep, smoky translucence. When motionless—and it is *always* motionless when you are looking—it is indistinguishable from the glass spires and rubble around it. This is not camouflage in the decorative sense. The stalker does not *look like* rock. It *is* mineral, biologically. Its body is as hard, as dark, as inert-seeming as the obsidian it hunts beside.

The only way to confirm one's presence, indirectly, is the absence of grazers. Herds of silicate grazers avoid certain spires, flowing around them in wide arcs, their tick-tick-tick going quiet in a radius of several meters. Something about the biofilm near those spires is wrong—depleted, chemically altered—or something about the micro-seismic signature of the ground near them triggers an avoidance response. The grazers know, in whatever way grazers know things, that *that spire is occupied*. They do not know what occupies it. They simply do not go there.

The strike, when it comes, has been described—in the rare accounts that exist—as *geological*. One moment there is a spire and some rubble. The next moment the rubble is somewhere else and something with too many joints has moved through the space where a grazer used to be. The stalker does not chase. It does not pursue across the salt. It lunges—a single, explosive extension of its eight limbs, launching the body forward and downward in a trajectory that covers two to three meters in what appears to be an instantaneous reconfiguration of matter. The front two pairs of limbs seize the prey. The rear two pairs anchor. The mandibles—a single pair of fused, blade-like structures, black and razor-thin—are driven into the joints between the grazer's silica plates, bypassing the armor entirely, and the grazer is pulled apart along its own segmentation with a sound like a stack of glass plates being slowly, deliberately snapped.

The kill takes less than five seconds. The feeding takes longer—twenty minutes, perhaps thirty—and during this time the stalker is, if anything, *more* invisible than before, because it drags the grazer back against the base of the spire and becomes indistinguishable from a lump of rubble again. It does not eat the silica plates. It eats the soft tissue beneath—the muscles, the gut, the nerve cord—and leaves the carapace behind. Over time, abandoned grazer shells accumulate at the bases of certain spires, forming small, glittering middens that are among the only indirect evidence of stalker presence. If you find a spire surrounded by a crescent of pale, empty grazer shells, you have found a stalker's territory. You will not see the stalker. But it has seen you.

What they do between kills is unknown. They do not bask. They do not seem to groom or move for pleasure. They simply *are*, in the way the salt is, in the way the spires are—another piece of the mineral machinery, patient and absolute, waiting for the chemistry to come to them.

## The Crustal Beetles

Less dramatic than the stalkers, more mobile than the worms, and far stranger than the grazers: the **crustal beetles** are the ecosystem's middlemen, its scavengers, its undertakers, and—perhaps—its engineers.

They are small. Ten to fifteen centimeters long, oval, heavily built, with a domed carapace that resembles a drop of dark, polished resin. They have six legs, each ending in a broad, spatulate foot covered in microscopic hooks that grip the salt crust with remarkable tenacity—necessary, given the thermal updrafts from the fissures that would otherwise blow them away like scraps of paper. Their coloration is variable: rust-red on the iron-rich plates, pale ochre on the sulfate fields, nearly black on the volcanic glass. They shift color slowly, over days, as they move between substrates—a chromatographic response to the mineral content of the biofilm they consume.

They are everywhere. This is not an exaggeration. On every square meter of the salt pan, in every crack and on every plate, there are crustal beetles. They do not herd. They do not hunt. They move individually, purposefully, on paths that seem random until you watch long enough to see the pattern: they are *following the cracks*. Specifically, they are following the micro-fissures between the hexagonal salt plates, where gas seepage is highest and biofilm growth is thickest. They traverse these cracks like tiny surveyors, stopping every few centimeters to lower their heads and *tap*—a rapid, percussive drumming of their mandibles against the salt surface, producing a staccato *tktktktktk* that, in aggregate, fills the silences between the larger sounds of the pan with a constant, insectile static.

The tapping is diagnostic. They are testing the surface—its temperature, its rigidity, its chemical composition—through mechanoreceptors and chemoreceptors in the mandibles. When they find a suitable spot, they stop, wedge themselves into the crack, and begin to feed, using the same abrasive-file technique as the grazers but on a much smaller scale, consuming biofilm and the topmost layer of salt crystals, leaving behind tiny, smooth-polished grooves in the crust.

Their ecological role goes beyond grazing. They are the pan's primary decomposers. When a grazer dies—whether from a stalker's kill or from old age or from a fissure collapse—the beetles find the body within hours, drawn by the chemical signature of decomposition. They swarm the carcass, dozens of them, and reduce the soft tissue to nothing within a day. They cannot breach the silica carapace, but they consume everything inside it, and in doing so they return the organic compounds to the biofilm layer, closing the loop. Where the grazers are the pan's harvesters, the beetles are its composters.

But there is a second role, and it is more speculative. The beetles' constant tapping, feeding, and movement along the micro-fissures *abrades* the salt crust in precise, predictable ways. They preferentially weaken certain points along the plate edges—points that are already under tectonic stress. Over time, their activity may influence *where* and *when* the plates crack. The evidence is circumstantial: beetle-heavy zones show more frequent, smaller, less violent plate adjustments, as if the beetles' continuous micro-abrasion is releasing tectonic pressure in small increments rather than allowing it to build toward catastrophic rupture. If this is true—and it has not been confirmed—then the crustal beetles are not merely inhabitants of the salt pan. They are *modulating* it. They are, in their small and mindless way, *tending* the landscape, keeping the geothermal machinery running smoothly, ensuring that the cracks stay open and the gases keep flowing and the biofilm keeps growing. They are the maintenance crew of a chemical factory that does not know it is a factory.

The sound of a million beetles tapping in the dark is the sound of the pan thinking.

## The Pulse Larvae

In the deeper fissures—below the reach of the vent worms' plumes, in the hot, liquid-brine zones where the salt crust meets the planet's true interior—live something that no one has seen whole and that may, in fact, not have a fixed form.

They are called **pulse larvae** because what has been observed—by remote probes, by instruments lowered on cables that were subsequently lost—are larval-stage organisms. Immature. Developing. Pulsing.

They are soft-bodied, elongated, vaguely vermiform, ranging from a few centimeters to over a meter in length. They glow—a faint, steady, bioluminescent red-orange, produced by chemical reactions in their tissues that are driven by the extreme heat of their environment. This glow is, in fact, what they are best known for: from the surface, in certain deep fissures, you can look down and see it—a faint, warm light far below, like embers in a well. It pulses. Not in rhythm with any external force, but in its own slow cadence—brightening and dimming over cycles of roughly ninety seconds, each individual on its own schedule, so that the collective glow in a fissure full of pulse larvae is a shifting, breathing, organic light, utterly unlike the sullen static glow of the heated salt.

They feed on dissolved minerals in the brine, absorbing them directly through their skin. They have no mouths, no visible sensory organs, no apparent means of locomotion beyond a slow, peristaltic flexing of the body. They drift in the brine, or rest on the fissure walls, or hang suspended by threads of mucus that harden into mineral-sheathed cords. They do not interact with each other in any observable way. They simply exist, in the hot dark, pulsing.

What they become—if they become anything—is unknown. Some researchers believe they are the larval form of the vent worms, which would mean the worms have a life cycle that begins in the deep brine and migrates upward over years or decades as they mature, eventually reaching a surface fissure and extending their plume. Others believe they are a separate species entirely, one that never reaches the surface, living out its entire existence in the liquid zones beneath the crust. A third, more unsettling hypothesis is that they are not larvae at all—that "larva" is a misnomer born of their undeveloped appearance—and that they are, in fact, the *adult* form, and everything we see on the surface: the grazers, the worms, the stalkers, the beetles—*all of it*—is a secondary expression, an epiphenomenon, a thin biological crust atop a deeper, slower, vaster organism that lives in the brine and pulses in the dark and has been there since before the seabeds were lifted and will be there long after the salt has been ground to dust.

There is no evidence for this. There is no evidence against it. The light in the fissures pulses on.

## The Colony Mats

On the surfaces of the salt plates—in the sheltered depressions between buckles, in the lee of glass spires, in the warm hollows where thermal distortion is strongest—there are patches of color that are not mineral. They are not biofilm. They are something else.

The **colony mats** are dense, felted layers of microbial life—aggregations of hundreds of species of chemosynthetic organisms, bound together by extracellular polymers into rubbery sheets a few millimeters thick. They are vivid. In a world of ochre and rust and black, they are *vivid*: deep greens, electric blues, sulfurous yellows, a startling arterial red. They look like lichens, or like paint spilled on stone, or like living tissue laid bare. They smell—distinctly, unmistakably—of *life*, of organic chemistry in action, a sharp, yeasty, faintly sweet scent that cuts through the sulfur and ozone and hot metal like a knife.

They are not abundant. A large mat might cover a few square meters. Most are smaller, the size of a hand, or a plate. They grow slowly—centimeters per year—and they are fragile, vulnerable to the same thermal cracking and gas venting that everything else on the pan is vulnerable to. A new vent opening beneath a mat will kill it in days, scorching it to black char. A plate shift can bury a mat under a centimeter of salt, entombing it. They persist because they reproduce: fragments break off and are carried—by beetles, by wind, by the simple mechanical action of grazers passing over them—to new locations, where they take hold and grow.

But the mats are important beyond their own existence. They are the *nursery*. The grazers' eggs—tiny, amber-colored, encased in silica—are laid in the mats, where the warmth and chemical richness provide the energy for development. The beetle larvae, too, develop within the mat, feeding on the microbial community from the inside, hollowing out small cavities that the mat then regrows around them, incorporating them. Even the stalkers, it is suspected, deposit their young near mats—the juveniles, too small to hunt grazers, feed on biofilm and beetles until they are large enough to take on larger prey.

The mats are the closest thing the salt pan has to soil. They are the closest thing it has to a womb. They are small and they are fragile and they are everywhere, tucked into the cracks and hollows of this brutal place, holding the color green in the dark like a secret.

## The Sound, Together

You asked what lives here. Everything above, yes. But also: the sound lives here. Or rather, everything above *makes* the sound, and the sound in turn shapes everything above—the grazers' paths, the beetles' tapping, the worms' plume-response, the stalkers' patience, the mats' slow growth.

Listen:

The tectonic hum: the baseline, the heartbeat, felt in the jaw, in the sternum, in the marrow of the long bones.

The hiss of vents: breath, constant, from a thousand fissures, varying in pitch and intensity as gas pressures fluctuate.

The tick-tick-tick of expanding salt: a clock, a metronome, marking the thermal pulse of the crust.

The crack of glass: punctuation, sudden, violent, a reminder that the spires are temporary.

The rain-on-tin of grazers: a surface sound, close, intimate, the sound of a thousand tiny glass plates meeting stone.

The tktktktktk of beetles: static, background, the sound of something *working*, always working, in the cracks.

The wet click of worms, deep below: digestion, slow and patient, the sound of a body larger than you processing a world.

And beneath all of it, below the hum, below the hiss, below the audible range entirely: the pulse. The larval pulse. Ninety seconds bright, ninety seconds dim, on and on, in the dark, in the heat, in the brine. The sound no one hears. The sound that may be the reason any of the rest of it exists at all.

This is what lives on the Salt Pan of Gyre-7. Not a community, exactly—not in any way that implies cooperation or intent. A *system*. A vast, slow, hot, dark, noisy system of mineral and flesh and chemistry, grinding and pulsing and ticking and cracking and feeding and dying and being eaten, forever, in the dark, lit only from below.
