# Who Lives Here

They have no name for themselves, or if they do, you have never heard it spoken in a context where you could understand it as a name.

---

## What You See

They are few. Perhaps forty, perhaps fifty — the number is hard to fix because you never see more than six or seven at any given time, and you are never certain whether the figure you saw on a walkway at dawn is the same one you see standing in a doorway at dusk. They do not congregate. They do not form the visible social clusters you would expect in a settlement of this size: no pairs walking together, no groups gathered at a balcony, no children, no elderly, no infirm. You see individuals, moving singly, attending to tasks you cannot parse.

They are — and you resist this word at first because it feels like a failure of observation — **uniform**. Not identical. There are differences in height, in the breadth of their shoulders, in the way they carry their weight as they cross a translucent bridge. But the differences are *within a narrow band*. No one is much taller than anyone else. No one moves with the particular stiffness of age or the particular looseness of youth. Their faces, when you catch a glimpse — and they do not often face you directly — are composed in a way that reads less as emotion than as **attention**. Not happiness, not sadness, not the wariness you would expect from strangers encountering an outsider in an isolated place. Just *attention*. The way a surface attends to the water running down it.

Their skin is dark. Not in the way you might categorize skin tones from lowland populations — it is something subtler, something that seems to carry the mineral qualities of the stone they live in. In the dim light of the crevasse, at a distance, they almost disappear against the rock face. Up close, there is a warmth to it, a brownish-gray undertone that catches the blue-white glow of the conduits differently than your skin does. Their eyes are dark and reflective. When they look at you — which is rare, which is almost never — you have the disquieting sense that they are seeing you as a *surface* to be read rather than a *person* to be met.

They are not afraid of the heights. This is obvious but worth stating. They walk the narrowest bridges — those shoulder-width spans of thinned, translucent stone — without hesitation, without looking down, without the microadjustments of balance that even experienced climbers unconsciously make. They move across the void as if the void were not there, as if gravity were a rumor they had chosen not to believe. You watch a figure cross a bridge fifty meters above the fog layer, walking at an unhurried pace, and you realize that the absence of railings on the balconies and walkways is not an oversight or an aesthetic choice. It is simply *how things are done*. The danger does not register for them. Whether this is training, physiology, or something else entirely, you cannot tell.

---

## What They Do

Their activity is almost entirely **maintenance**.

You arrive expecting to find culture — art, ritual, the things that human settlements produce when survival is secured. You find none of these. Or rather, you find no *signs* of them. There are no decorations on the smooth carved walls. No murals, no carvings, no inscriptions. The architecture is beautiful, but it is beautiful in the way that a crystal is beautiful: as a consequence of process, not as an act of expression. The inhabitants do not seem to add anything to the city. They tend it.

You see them adjusting panels on the exterior faces of buildings — panels that you slowly realize are not fixed structures but *interfaces*, points where the invisible systems of the city become briefly tangible. A figure presses a hand to a surface and holds it there for a long time. The surface glows blue. The glow fades. The figure removes their hand and moves on. You do this yourself once, when you are alone in a lower chamber and no one is watching. Nothing happens. The stone is warm under your palm. That is all.

You see them on the walkways, pausing to look down into the crevasse with the focused attention of someone reading a gauge. They seem to be watching the fog layer, or the water flows, or the conduit pulses — it is hard to tell what holds their gaze because their faces betray no reaction. They look, they assess, they move on. Sometimes they descend to the lower levels, where the warmth rises and the frost does not form. You follow one of them once, at a distance, down a spiraling corridor that tightens as it descends, and you watch them enter a room where the walls are warm and the conduits pulse more brightly, and they stand in the center of the room and do nothing. They stand there for twenty minutes. Then they leave.

You begin to understand that their relationship to the technology is not what you would call *use*. They do not operate machines. They do not press buttons or turn dials or read instruments. The city's systems are not *tools* in their hands. The relationship is closer to **communion** — or to the way a gardener relates to a garden that has been growing, largely without intervention, for a very long time. The systems run themselves. The inhabitants *check*. They adjust. They attend. But the city does not need them to function, and they do not seem to need the city for anything you can identify.

This is what makes the place feel incomplete. You keep waiting for the *point*. You keep waiting to see what all this extraordinary infrastructure is *for* — what project, what purpose, what great work these people are laboring toward. And the answer, as far as you can determine, is: nothing. The city is the point. The tending is the point. The precision of the carving and the pulsing of the conduits and the management of the water and the warmth — these are not means to an end. They are the end. They are what these people *do*, and they have been doing it for a long time, and they will continue to do it, and there is no moment coming when the work will be finished and the real purpose will begin.

Or perhaps there is. Perhaps that is what they are waiting for. You cannot tell.

## What They Sound Like

Their voices are low.

When you hear them — and it is infrequent, because they rarely speak in your presence, and the crevasse carries sound in ways that make distance and direction unreliable — the quality of their speech surprises you. You expected something sharp and clipped, to match the precision of everything else in Vethra-Nul. Instead, their voices are **resonant**, almost musical, with long vowels and sustained consonants that seem designed to *carry* through the stone rather than through the air. They speak to each other across the crevasse — one figure on the east wall, another on the west — and their voices meet in the middle with a quality that is less like conversation and more like **tuning**. As if they are not exchanging information but harmonizing with the crevasse itself, adding their frequencies to the low hum of the geothermal systems and the breathing sound of the water.

You try to learn their language. You fail. Not because it is complex — you have no way of assessing its complexity — but because you cannot reliably distinguish their speech from the ambient sound of the city. When a figure on a balcony opens their mouth and a low sustained tone emerges, is that speech? Is it the wind moving through a carved channel? Is it the geothermal hum resonating in a chamber you cannot see? You begin to suspect that the distinction does not exist for them. That for the inhabitants of Vethra-Nul, their voices are part of the city's acoustic ecosystem — not imposed upon it but *continuous with* it. They speak and the rock answers. They are silent and the rock continues speaking.

## What They Might Be

You turn this over in your mind during the long cold hours when you are alone in a chamber you have claimed as your own, listening to the frost fall and the water breathe.

They are human. Physically, anatomically, they are human. Two arms, two legs, bilateral symmetry, faces with the standard complement of features. They eat — you have seen one of them in a lower chamber, consuming something from a smooth-walled niche in the rock, something pale and dense that they break apart with their fingers and bring to their mouth with unhurried deliberation. They sleep, or something like sleep — you have found chambers where the smooth stone has a slight indentation, a body-shaped hollow that has been worn into the rock by long use, the edges polished by the friction of a form returning to the same position night after night, year after year. They are warm-blooded. Their breath steams in the upper levels and does not steam in the lower ones, same as yours.

But their relationship to the city is different from yours in a way you cannot fully articulate. You inhabit a space in Vethra-Nul. They **are** Vethra-Nul, or they are *of* it, in the way that the frost is of the cold and the conduits are of the stone. When you watch one of them standing at a balcony, looking down into the crevasse with that focused, unreadable attention, you see a figure *in* a landscape. When you look away and look back, you sometimes lose them — not because they have moved but because they have become, for a moment, indistinguishable from the wall they stand against. The stone color of their skin. The seamless quality of their clothing. The stillness. The patience.

You wonder if they were always here. You wonder if they were *made* by the same processes that made the city — if someone whispered instructions to the rock, and the rock produced not only chambers and bridges and conduits but *people*, shaped from the same material, animated by the same geothermal pulse, maintaining themselves the way the frost maintains itself: growing, shattering, growing again.

You dismiss this as fancy. Then you visit the floor of the crevasse again and watch the film retract and advance, retract and advance, and you are less certain.

## What They Think of You

Nothing. Or something so far from what you would recognize as thought that it registers as nothing.

They do not avoid you. They do not seek you out. They do not offer you food or water, though both are available and you have found both on your own. They do not prevent you from entering any space in the city. They do not explain anything. When you stand in their path on a walkway, they move around you with the same smooth, unhurried efficiency with which they move around a protrusion in the rock. You are an irregularity in the landscape. They navigate you. That is all.

Once, and only once, one of them stops in front of you and looks at you. Directly. Full face. The dark eyes, the attention that reads you as a surface. They are shorter than you by a head. Their hands hang at their sides, relaxed, the fingers long and slightly curved, the nails — you notice this with a shock of specificity — **smooth and faintly translucent**, like the stone of the walkways. They look at you for a long time. Ten seconds. Twenty. Their expression does not change. There is no smile, no frown, no recognition, no hostility. Just the attention.

Then they reach out and touch your wrist. One finger, laid along the inside of your forearm, where the skin is thin and the veins are visible. The finger is warm. Warmer than you expected. They hold it there for three seconds, four, and then they remove it and walk past you and descend a stairway into the rock and you do not see them again for two days.

You spend those two days examining your wrist. There is no mark. No change. No sensation. But you are aware of the spot for the rest of your time in Vethra-Nul — a point of contact, a place where the city's attention focused on you for a moment and then released you.

You do not know what they learned from touching you. You do not know if they learned anything at all.

## The Film

You keep returning to this.

It lives at the bottom. It is not the inhabitants. It is not *of* the inhabitants, as far as you can tell. But it is the only thing in Vethra-Nul that responds to you — that *reacts* to your presence with something you can read as a decision. Withdrawal. Return. Withdrawal. Return.

The inhabitants do not descend to the floor of the crevasse. You have watched the lowest levels for hours, and the figures that move through the warm chambers above do not go further down. Whether this is a prohibition, a lack of interest, or a physical limitation — the air at the bottom is thick, humid, low in oxygen — you do not know. But the film is beneath them, in the dark, in the warm wet stillness, and it *notices* you in a way that the people above do not.

You begin to wonder if the film is the oldest thing in Vethra-Nul. Older than the carvings, older than the conduits, older than the people. You wonder if the city was built *around* it — if the geothermal tapping, the water management, the entire extraordinary infrastructure of this place exists not to support the inhabitants but to sustain the conditions at the bottom of the crevasse where the film lives its slow, reactive, luminous life.

You wonder if the inhabitants know this. You wonder if they are the film's immune system, its caretakers, its servants. You wonder if the film is thinking, slowly, in timescales of centuries, and the city is its body, and the people are its hands.

You have no evidence for any of this. You have only the feeling — standing in the warm dark at the bottom of the world, watching a translucent film pull away from the disturbance of your presence and then slowly, deliberately, advance again — that you are in the presence of something that is *primary*. Something for which Vethra-Nul exists.

The people above move through their routines with the calm of those who have solved the problem of survival and are waiting to remember what they wanted to survive for.

Perhaps the film remembers.

Perhaps the film is what they are waiting for.

---

You are in Vethra-Nul. You have seen what lives here. You are not certain it has seen you back.
