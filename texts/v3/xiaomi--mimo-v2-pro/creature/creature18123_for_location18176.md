What lives here is not separate from what is built. The inhabitants are the architecture’s pulse, the ecosystem’s syntax. They are the mechanisms by which the monument dreams.

The **Polishers** move in iridescent hordes. They are not beetles, but something like them—hard-shelled, multi-jointed, with legs that end in fine, silicate pads. Their carapaces shift through opalescent spectrums, not for display, but as a byproduct of their labor. They scour the ceramic causeways and dome surfaces with their bodies, consuming microscopic films of algae and mineral deposit, excreting a thin, hardening resin that fills micro-fissures. Their clicking is the sound of maintenance, a constant, chitinous whisper of correction. They are the housekeepers of a palace that never accumulates dust, only a pervasive, deliberate damp.

In the canals glide the **Undertakers**. Long, pale, and segmented, they are eyeless, their smooth heads tasting the water with feathery chemoreceptors. They do not swim so much as *flow*, their bodies rippling with peristaltic waves. They consume any organic matter that detaches—a fallen leaf, a sloughed bit of vine—and break it into a nutrient slurry that fertilizes the root-mats from below. They are the recyclers, the closed loop. Their presence is a slow, sinuous guarantee that nothing is lost. When they surface, their wet skins refract the pearlescent light, making them look like moving lenses of oil on water.

Above, the **Collectors** drift. Translucent bladders of membrane, filled with lighter-than-air gases, they float on thermal currents in the haze. Sticky, mucus-coated filaments trail from them for meters, gathering condensation. When a filament is heavy with water, the Collector descends to a canal or a dewy patch of moss, releasing its load in a slow, dripping kiss. They are the rain, the irrigation system. Sometimes, two Collectors will tangle filaments, and their struggle to separate is a slow, silent ballet of stretched membrane and glistening strands.

But these are the visible functions. The truer inhabitants are more subtle.

There are the **Echoes**. In the corner of your vision, a vine seems to twist a fraction of a second after the air moves it. A pool of water, perfectly still, will ripple from a center point where nothing has touched it. These are not ghosts. They are the place remembering the touch that shaped it. A hand that smoothed that curve is recalled in the ceramic’s slow, breathing swell. A tool that scored a line is echoed in the sudden, fleeting alignment of root-fibers in the mire, a brief, illegible script. The makers’ intentions are fossilized in the physics here, and sometimes the intention twitches.

The most profound inhabitants are the **Patterns in the Hum**. The low, biological thrum is not uniform. Listen long enough, and it resolves. The drip of nectar from a trumpet-flower is a metronome. The click of a Polisher turning is a syncopation. The wet tear of a vine is a slow, glissando note. It is a symphony of stasis, a composition of held breath. This hum is the collective consciousness of the system—a thoughtless, perfect awareness of its own balance. It is the dream’s soundtrack.

And finally, there is the **Wrongness** itself. It, too, lives here. It is not a creature, but a resident quality—a softness in the world’s grammar. The pressure you feel from all sides is its presence. The geometry that refuses to hold is its play. It is the residue of the makers’ own flawed, dreaming minds, baked into their creation. It whispers in the curvature of a dome that seems to listen, in the light that feels like a gaze. It is the ghost of the hands that built the terrarium, now trapped within it, endlessly, imperfectly, trying to perfect the shapes they once loved. It doesn’t decay the paradise; it *tends* it, with a loving, fumbling, endless touch.

So what lives here? A closed loop of function. A memory of touch. A hum of equilibrium. A flaw that dreams. The place is its own sole inhabitant—a single, vast, wet, bright, dreaming organism, wearing its ecosystem like a jeweled carapace and its makers like a half-forgotten name.
