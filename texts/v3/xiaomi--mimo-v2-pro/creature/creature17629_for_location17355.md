What lives here is not a collection of separate beings, but a single, slow, breathing entity with a million distributed organs. To speak of "fauna" is to misunderstand; they are the animated thoughts of the place, the sensitive tips of its ancient, wooden mind.

The most evident are the **Weavers**. They are not spiders, nor insects, but something in between: eight-limbed, soft-bodied, and the color of wet heartwood. They move with infinite patience through the woven corridors of the canopy's underside, not spinning silk, but secreting a clear, resinous enzyme. This substance they lay down in intricate, three-dimensional lines—a map, a circuit, a reinforcement. They are the architects of the micro-channels, guiding the flow of condensation and tannic sap. Their work is invisible to you unless you hold very still for a very long time, and notice how a single drip follows a specific, glinting path down a trunk-face. They communicate by plucking these resin-threads with a forelimb, sending faint, vibrating codes through the substrate that other Weavers, kilometers away, feel in their bones.

Then there are the **Lung-Shelves**. You mistook them for fungi. They are, but they are also not. Each massive, pulsating bracket is a colony-organ, its underside a delicate, bronchial lattice that expands and contracts in a slow, tidal rhythm—one breath every four hours. This is not respiration for itself alone. As it expands, it draws damp, stagnant air from deep crevices in the woody-rock. As it contracts, it presses this air through its gills, which are lined with spores and symbiotic bacteria that break down complex lignins into simpler sugars. The exhaled air is warmer, sweeter, and richer with spores that will seed new growth. The Lung-Shelves are the lungs and the digestive system of the lower layers, processing not just air, but time itself, breaking down the ancient past into fertile present.

Deep within the compressed walls live the **Resonants**. You hear their work—the low, kilometer-carrying hums—but you will never see them. They are soft, cylindrical creatures, lacking eyes or limbs, embedded vertically in the wood-stone matrix like living tuning forks. Their entire existence is a slow, powerful oscillation. They feed on the mineral-rich water that percolates through their bodies, and in return, they sing. Each Resonant vibrates at a frequency determined by the density of the surrounding substrate. Together, over decades, they create a standing wave, a slow seismic song that maps the integrity of the structure. When a layer is too saturated, or a caldera slump is beginning, the song changes key. Other beings—the Weavers, the lung-shelves, even the vines—respond to this song, thickening supports here, sending exploratory roots there. The Resonants are the nervous system, feeling the place's aches and pressures.

The caldera-descenders, the **Gloom-Kites**, are the closest to "visible life." From below, they are silhouettes against the distant, impossible green. They are not creatures of the living crown, but of the middle-dark. Flat, ray-like bodies, wide as small sails, edged with fronds that catch the slow thermals rising from the caldera's new growth. They have no eyes, but their entire ventral surface is a sensitive palate, tasting the air for the spoor of fermenting sap and fresh, tender shoots. They descend on prehensile vines, not with speed, but with a majestic, falling-leaf drift. To feed, they press their body against a new growth-seam, and through a thousand microscopic tubes, they slowly siphon nutrients. They are the scavengers, the pollinators, the mobile connection between the deep established layers and the rare, fleeting sunlight of a wound.

And then, there are the **Sessile**. You have seen them and mistaken them for knots, for burls, for fungal excrescences. These are creatures that have chosen, or been compelled by their own life cycle, to stop. A soft, blob-like body attaches to a wall, and over years, it hardens. Its outer layer becomes one with the bark, then the wood. Its internal tissues are slowly infiltrated by hyphae and rootlets. It does not die. It *becomes*. Its nervous system, if it had one, diffuses into the mycelial network. Its chemical wisdom—how to process a certain toxin, how to fix a rare nutrient—is preserved and shared through the fungal internet. They are the librarians, the archives, their knowledge subsumed into the greater body.

Nothing here hunts in a way you would recognize. Predation is a form of composting. A Gloom-Kite, in its slow drift, might brush a Resonant embedded too near the surface. The contact, over years, might weaken the Resonant's anchor-point. Its humming falters. The local fungal network senses the change in vibration and nutrient flow, and sends tendrils in. The Resonant is not killed; it is *dissolved*. Its physical structure is broken down and carried away as building materials. Its final vibrational state—a unique signature of its location and demise—is added to the song of the walls, a new note in the ancient chorus. The boundary between eating and being eaten, between dying and being composted, is a gentle gradient of participation.

They do not *inhabit* this place. They are its gestures. The Weavers are its thought of repair. The Lung-Shelves are its breath. The Resonants are its sense of self. The Gloom-Kites are its momentary curiosity. The Sessile are its memories. To ask "what lives here?" is like asking which wave lives in the ocean. The answer is the ocean itself, in all its slow, wet, breathing, singing, growing, and dissolving entirety.
