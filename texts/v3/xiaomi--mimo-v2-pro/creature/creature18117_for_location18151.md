# What Lives Here

## The Base

Before anything with a shape, there is what coats every surface. Bacterial mats — the true first citizens — lie in layered veils across every stone surface that touches moisture. They are invisible at a distance, but up close they are everything: slick, colorless films of thermophilic archaea metabolizing sulfur compounds; purple-pink communities of halophilic bacteria blooming where salt crusts thicken; and deeper, in the hot stone itself, autotrophic colonies feeding on hydrogen and dissolved iron, miles of living tissue spread through the porous basalt like mycelium through soil, but chemical, not fungal. These are the foundation. Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them.

## The Slime Molds

They are the largest visible organisms on the plain, and the most alien in their behavior. The *crimson networks* are not one organism but millions of independent, single-celled amoebae that have aggregated into visible bodies — sometimes spanning thirty meters across the shaded underside of a basalt overhang, threading through every crack and crevice like a circulatory system with no heart.

They pulse. Slowly, once every several minutes, a visible contraction ripples outward from a central node — a digestion hub where a thickened mound of cells excretes enzymes onto a bacterial mat beneath it, liquefying it over hours. The contraction moves nutrients through the network. The whole mass is a dull ochre when feeding, but when stressed — desiccation, sudden salt concentration — it darkens to rust and begins to fragment, each piece becoming a mobile slug that crawls, over days, toward wetter ground before re-entering the collective.

They are not conscious. They have no nerve, no tissue. But they solve problems. They route around dry patches. They concentrate their densest nodes at the richest bacterial mats. They adjust their topology in response to mineral gradients with an efficiency that mimics engineering, and they do it through pure chemistry — quorum-sensing molecules diffusing through mucus trails. Watching one over weeks, a human observer would swear it was *thinking*, but it is only converging.

At reproductive maturity, portions of the network raise stalks — thin, pale filaments, sometimes six inches tall, each topped with a spore capsule. These are the only vertical structures the molds produce, and they appear like tiny forests beneath the overhangs, swaying in convective drafts too slight for human skin to feel. The spores are dispersed by the steam itself, carried in the haze and deposited, viable, wherever moisture condenses on new stone.

## The Sinter Reefs

In the hottest basins — where the ground is saturated and the water weeps fastest, steaming at 80, 90 degrees — the *sinter towers* rise. They are not alive, but they are built by life. Thermophilic cyanobacteria and silica-precipitating microbes coat every surface of the growing mineral structure, and as they photosynthesize (inefficiently, in the diffuse steam-light, but enough) or metabolize dissolved sulfides, they alter local pH and cause dissolved silica to precipitate onto their own bodies. They are entombed, alive, in glass. Each generation of bacteria builds a new layer on the last, and over decades, towers emerge — some no wider than a finger, others broad as a human torso, porous, hollow, laced with internal channels through which hot water still flows.

These are the reefs. In cross-section, they are ringed like trees — annual layers of mineral deposition. Small arthropods shelter in their hollow interiors. Larger creatures brush against them, breaking the most delicate spires, which fall and become the substrate for new growth. The reef fields stretch across the hottest basins, dozens of towers per square meter, rising and crumbling in a cycle measured not in seasons but in the slow rhythm of geological seep.

The towers hum. Not audibly, but the hot water moving through their internal channels produces a faint vibration — a subsonic resonance that certain arthropods can feel through their legs, and which they use to navigate toward the hottest, richest water sources.

## The Crawlers

The armored isopods — called *scutes* by the human miners, for the overlapping plates of their dorsal carapaces — are the most numerous visible fauna. Dark, nearly black, ranging from the size of a human thumb to the size of a large rat, they move in loose, shifting aggregations across the salt pans and sinter fields, always toward mineral deposits.

Their bodies are low, segmented, built for weight and traction. Eight pairs of legs, each tipped with a hard, recurved claw that clicks faintly on stone — the closest thing to footstep-sounds in this world, and it carries far in the quiet. Their carapaces are not smooth but textured, pitted with the same vesicular pattern as the basalt itself, and coated in a thin mineral crust that makes them nearly invisible against the rock when they hold still. Only their movement betrays them: a slow, concerted creep, like a tide of dark sediment flowing uphill.

They feed by scraping. Their mouthparts are a set of fused, chitinous mandibles lined with micro-crystalline silica — literally, they have evolved to grow stone teeth — which they drag across salt crusts, sinter surfaces, and bacterial mats, grinding mineral and organic matter into a paste they ingest. Their gut is a simple chemical reactor: acid and symbiotic bacteria break down the mineral slurry, extracting trace metals and organic carbon. They excrete dense, dry pellets of calcium sulfate and iron oxide, which accumulate in trails behind feeding aggregations and are, themselves, colonized by new bacterial mats. The scutes are gardeners without knowing it.

They are social, but not intelligent. They aggregate by chemical signal — each individual exudes a faint, sulfur-tinged molecule from glands at the joints of their legs, and others follow the gradient toward denser concentrations. The aggregations form and dissolve without hierarchy. There are no leaders. When a resource is exhausted, the group fractures, individuals radiating outward until new chemical gradients draw them together again.

Reproduction is aquatic — or rather, brine-pool-dependent. Females (the larger sex, sometimes twice the mass of males) carry fertilized eggs in a ventral brood pouch and, when ready, immerse themselves in the shallowest, coolest brine lenses they can find. The young hatch as tiny, soft-shelled larvae that must molt three times before their carapaces harden. The brine pools are therefore contested ground — not violently, but by sheer accumulation. A prime breeding pool at the right season may be ringed with dozens of gravid females, all partially submerged, their legs interlocked in a passive, competitive wall of bodies.

Predation keeps their numbers in check. They are the primary food source for almost everything else.

## The Gliders

High above the plain, riding the thermals that rise from every hot stone surface, the *membranes* drift. They are not birds, not bats, not insects. They are something without a clean analogue — a six-limbed body plan, the forward pair of limbs elongated into broad, leathery wings with no feather, no hair, only a thin, veined skin stretched over a rigid, cartilaginous frame. The body beneath is narrow, segmented, almost eel-like, with a flattened ventral surface that acts as a secondary lifting surface in strong thermals.

Their faces are their most unsettling feature. No mouth in the conventional sense — instead, a cluster of six to eight simple eyes, each a dark, lidless orb capable of detecting movement and thermal contrast, arranged in a shallow, forward-facing arc. Beneath the eyes, a retractable proboscis, coiled at rest, which they extend to feed on bacterial scum on warm stone surfaces or, more ambitiously, to pierce the soft bodies of juvenile scutes or the gelatinous mantles of stranded anemone-fragments. They are opportunistic feeders, and the act of landing and feeding seems almost reluctant — they spend the vast majority of their lives aloft.

They are silent flyers. The *hiss* that humans hear is wind over wing-membrane, not vocalization. The membranes themselves have no vocal apparatus, no resonating chamber. Communication, if it occurs, is visual: a rippling change in the color of their wing membranes, from pale gray to a flushed, vein-traced pink, visible to other membranes as they pass within thermal columns. What these color shifts mean — mating display, territorial warning, nothing at all — remains debated among the settlement's small xenobiology staff. The membranes do not seem to respond consistently to each other's color changes. It may be autonomic, a thermoregulatory response that the humans have mistaken for signal.

They nest, if nesting is the word, on the highest broken pillars of basalt, in shallow depressions scraped into the rock by repeated use. No nest material. No softening. A single leathery egg, left in the sun, hatched by the heat of the stone itself. The hatchling is born with half-formed wings and must crawl to the edge of its pillar and simply *fall* — if it catches a thermal on the way down, it flies. If it does not, it strikes the ground and does not survive. The membranes do not tend their young. They do not return to the nesting pillar after laying. The egg is the entire investment.

## The Anemones

In the deepest brine pools — the ones that have persisted for decades, accumulating a stable chemistry of dissolved metals and sulfides — the *columnars* anchor. They resemble terrestrial sea anemones in the broadest sense: a cylindrical, muscular foot driven into crevices in the sinter or basalt, from which rises a fleshy column topped with a crown of feeding tendrils. But they are large — a mature individual may stand two feet tall, with a column six inches in diameter and tendrils that radiate outward a further foot, swaying in the slow convection currents of the heated brine.

Their mantles — the thick, gelatinous sheath covering the column — are their most remarkable feature. Bioluminescent, but not in the bright, flash-and-fade way of deep-sea creatures. The columnars produce a slow, sustained glow that emanates from deep within the mantle tissue, a cold blue-green that is visible even in the harsh ambient light of the plain, though only in the deepest shade. The glow is not constant. It modulates. It comes in waves that travel up and down the column over periods of thirty seconds to several minutes, and it changes in intensity in response to stimulus — the approach of a scute triggers a dimming; the introduction of a nutrient-rich brine current triggers a brightening.

When multiple columnars occupy the same pool, their luminescent patterns synchronize. Not immediately — when two columnars are first placed in proximity (the settlement has experimented), their wave patterns are independent. Over hours, they converge, until both are pulsing in near-unison. Over days, the whole pool's population operates as a linked system, the waves of light passing from individual to individual in a coordinated circuit. What they are *doing* — whether this is communication, or mutual chemical sensing, or simply the mechanical consequence of shared water chemistry — is genuinely unknown. The leading hypothesis is that the light-modulation tracks dissolved mineral concentrations, and the synchronization is a side effect of all individuals responding to the same shifting chemical gradient. But there is a counter-observation that has not been explained: in pools where the population has been stable for years, the synchronization pattern becomes *anticipatory*. Individuals begin to brighten *before* a chemical shift arrives, as though they are reading a signal in the water ahead of the physical change. No mechanism has been identified.

They feed by filtration. The tendrils are lined with microscopic, sticky papillae that trap bacteria, tiny crustacean larvae, and particulate mineral matter from the passing brine. They are sessile as adults — once anchored, they do not move. But juvenile columnars are mobile, drifting as free-floating polyps through connecting channels between brine pools, seeking unoccupied crevices. In this larval form, they are among the most common prey items in the ecosystem, consumed by scutes, membranes, and even other columnars (the adult tendrils will grasp and ingest a passing polyp without hesitation; the columnars are not sentimental).

The oldest known columnar, in a deep pool on the eastern edge of the settlement's mapped territory, has been estimated at over two hundred years, based on the rate of mineral accretion on its basal foot. It is three feet tall. Its luminescent pattern has not been matched by any younger individual. It pulses on a cycle of nearly seven minutes, far slower than any other observed. When approached by divers in thermal gear, it does not dim. It brightens, holding a steady, unwavering glow that lasts for the duration of the approach and fades only slowly after departure. The xenobiology staff have named it, informally, *the reader*. They do not know what it is reading.

## The Microswimmers

The brine pools are not empty between the larger organisms. They contain a dense, diverse fauna of creatures too small to see without magnification — but too active, too complex, and too numerous to omit.

Rotifer-like organisms with crystalline internal skeletons of precipitated silica thrash through the hottest water, feeding on bacteria. Tiny, shelled gastropods with translucent, mineralized shells graze on sinter surfaces, each no larger than a grain of rice. Copepod-analogues — elongated, multi-legged, fast — dart through the open water in jerky bursts, predators of everything smaller. And beneath all of these, a vast population of protozoa: amoebae, flagellates, ciliates, each adapted to a specific temperature band, salinity range, and mineral chemistry, so that a single brine pool may contain hundreds of species occupying distinct micro-niches in a column of water only inches deep.

This is the hidden abundance. The scutes, the membranes, the columnars — these are the megafauna, the visible edge of a biomass that is overwhelmingly microscopic. The weeping stone is not merely damp. It is *thick* with life, at every scale down to the cellular, a continuum of organism and mineral that makes the very concept of a boundary between living and nonliving feel inadequate here.

## The Humans

They are fewer than they seem. The settlement — called, without poetry, *Station* — houses perhaps eight hundred people, though the number fluctuates with the industrial rotations. They come on contracts: two years, typically, in the extraction and processing operations, before rotating out to cooler, more forgiving postings. Some stay longer. A few, drawn by something they cannot articulate, stay permanently. These long-term residents change. Not physically — the station's life support is adequate, the food imported, the water distilled and safe. But something in their manner shifts. They slow down. They stop talking as much. They develop a habit of standing in doorways, facing outward, watching the steam haze move across the basalt. They say the quiet is addictive. They say they can hear the sinter towers growing if they listen long enough. Whether this is metaphor or genuine auditory sensitivity developed through years of exposure to subsonic vibration, no one has tested.

The workforce is organized around the condensers and the chemical plants. The condensers are the primary infrastructure: massive, passive structures that channel the weep-water from rock faces through a series of cooling radiators, extracting liquid water from the hot mineral brine and leaving behind increasingly concentrated solutions of salts and dissolved metals. These concentrated solutions are then fed into the chemical plants — quieter than any equivalent facility on a wet world, because the reactions here are slow, the gradients gentle, the energy low. The plants produce, in order of economic volume: sodium chloride, calcium sulfate, iron oxide pigments, trace rare-earth elements in modest but profitable quantities, and — the most valuable product — a purified, mineral-free water that is exported offworld as a specialty commodity. There is a market, it turns out, for water that has been stripped of everything but its own molecular identity, drawn from stone that has held it for ten thousand years. The marketing materials call it *Pale Water*. The miners call it *tears*.

The streets of Station are wide enough for the condensate carts — low, electric, slow — that transport brine from collection points to processing. The architecture is dense, thick-walled, designed to shed heat through sheer thermal mass rather than active cooling. Walls two feet thick of local basalt, faced with iron plate that sweats condensation in the heat, each droplet captured in gutter-troughs and routed to the distillery. The buildings are low — nothing taller than two stories — because anything taller would act as a thermal chimney, drawing superheated air upward through its interior. The aesthetic is brutalist, functional, but softened by the constant presence of water: the beading on every metal surface, the drip from every joint, the faint sheen of moisture on every stone wall. Station looks, in the right light, like it is weeping too.

There is a small research contingent — perhaps thirty people — attached to a xenobiology lab built into the coolest, most sheltered side of the settlement, backed against a basalt mesa. They study the organisms. They publish papers that circulate in academic journals and are read by a few hundred specialists worldwide. Their work is meticulous, careful, and largely ignored by the general scientific establishment, because the organisms of the weeping plain have no obvious medical, agricultural, or military application. They are simply *interesting*. The researchers stay longer than the miners, on average. One has been here for fourteen years. She has mapped every columnar in the eastern pools. She visits the *reader* weekly. She says it recognizes her. There is no evidence for this. She has stopped caring whether there is evidence.

Children are rare in Station. Families sometimes relocate for a contract cycle, but the heat, the light, the confinement of indoor life — most leave early. The children who stay grow up differently. They are quiet. They play outside more than their parents expect, tolerated by the heat in a way that seems physiological rather than stubborn. They collect scute molts — the discarded carapace plates, translucent and delicate — and trade them like currency among themselves. They have names for things the adults have not bothered to name: the particular way light refracts through the steam at dusk is *the breathing*; the sound of water dripping from an overloaded gutter in the deep quiet of night is *the counting*. One child, now eleven, born in Station and never left, has been observed sitting motionless beside a brine pool for hours, watching the columnars pulse. When asked what she is doing, she says she is *listening back*. She does not explain further. Her parents find this unsettling. The xenobiologist with fourteen years of tenure finds it interesting.

## The Absences

It is worth noting what does *not* live here. There are no large predators. Nothing here hunts by pursuit, by ambush, by anything requiring speed or aggression. The ecology is structured around slow accumulation, filtration, and scraping. There is no apex. There are no flowers, no fruit, no seed — the entire concept of reproductive structures evolved for wind or animal dispersal is absent. Spores, larvae, polyps — everything reproduces by drift, by diffusion, by the patient movement of water and steam. There are no songs, no calls, no alarm signals. The world communicates, if at all, in light, in chemistry, in the subsonic hum of water moving through stone. There is no urgency here. Not in any organism, not in any process. The fastest thing in this world is a scuttle — the quick, clicking rush of a startled scute bolting for cover — and it lasts two seconds before the creature settles again into the universal, glacial patience of the weeping plain.

And yet it lives. Everything here is alive, or built by the alive, or eaten by the alive, or excreted by the alive, and the whole system — mineral, microbial, arthropodal, human — turns over in its slow, wet heat with a completeness that feels less like an ecosystem and more like a single, vast organism, weeping and thinking and growing, indifferent to whether anyone is watching.
