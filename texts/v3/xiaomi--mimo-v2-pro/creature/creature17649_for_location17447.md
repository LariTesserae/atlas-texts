# The Inhabitants of the Inner Wood

They don't announce themselves. You feel them first — a pressure in the air, a sense of architecture moving behind the stillness. The creatures of the core do not hurry. They have never hurried. Hurrying requires a fear of time, and nothing here fears time.

---

## The Vessel-Walkers

The first to make themselves known are the largest, and they are not truly visible — only their passage is.

Deep within the translucent walls, something moves. A shadow the size of a city bus gliding through one of the great hydraulic channels, a dark mass pulsing gently against the current of frozen sap. It is shaped like a lancet, elongated, with no visible organs except a faint tracery of bioluminescent filaments that spiral along its body like the engraving on a clock spring. It moves counter to the flow. Against the immense pressure of the xylem, it ascends.

There are several of them, spaced at intervals that suggest rhythm rather than coincidence. They travel the vascular highways of the megaflora, pressing through conduits that could swallow subway cars, carrying... something. If you could see inside — and you cannot, not fully — you would find that each lancet-body is hollow, a vessel within a vessel, filled with a substance denser than mercury and colder than anything that should remain liquid. This is coolant. The vessel-walkers are lymphocytes of a kind, circulating compounds of staggering complexity through the body of the plant, maintaining temperature gradients, repairing micro-fractures in the cellulose matrix, ferrying molecular data packets between distant organs of the host.

They are the blood. They are the couriers. They have been making this circuit since before your species developed spines.

---

## The Spindles

Clinging to the walls, you see them — the eyeless creatures the surface description alluded to, but they are more varied and more unsettling than that initial glimpse suggested.

They are small, most no larger than your hand, and they look as though someone taught frost to walk. Their bodies are elongated, multi-jointed, and nearly transparent, structured from something that resembles glass but is actually a silicate-organic hybrid — a material that is simultaneously bone, crystal, and circuitry. They have six to eight limbs, each ending in a fractal brush of hair-thin filaments that wave slowly in the condensation, tasting the chemical content of each droplet. No eyes. No mouths in any recognizable sense. They feed by absorption and by *reading*.

Because that is what they do. Each spindle is a sensor. Its body is an antenna, tuned to molecular frequencies in the sap, the condensation, the faint electromagnetic whisper of the computational processes happening all around them. They are the nerve endings of the megaflora, but they are also something more: they *interpret*. Each spindle has a small, dense knot of neural tissue — or what passes for neural tissue here — at its center, a knot that processes local data and transmits decisions through chemical gradients in the film of moisture on the wall.

They think. Not the way you think. Not with narrative or desire. They think the way a thermostat thinks, if a thermostat could write poetry in protein-folding. They process environmental data and make micro-adjustments to the local growth rate of the surrounding cellulose, regulating the expansion of the chamber at the molecular level. They are the slow, cold brain of the place — distributed, fractal, patient beyond comprehension.

When you disturbed the membrane to enter, they felt you. Every spindle in your line of sight shifted its filaments simultaneously, a wave of adjustment propagating across the wall like wind across wheat. They are aware of you. They have decided, for now, that you are not a repairable anomaly. This may change.

## The Grazer Herds

Larger, and slower, and somehow sadder than the spindles: the grazers.

They move in loose congregations of five to twelve across the floor of the chamber, creatures roughly the size and shape of a sea lion but compressed, flattened, adapted for a surface that is smooth and slightly yielding. Their bodies are pale — the universal palette of this place — with a waxy, translucent skin through which you can see the slow circulation of fluids and the dim blue-green glow of their metabolic processes. They have broad, flat heads with no features you would call a face: just a wide, undifferentiated sensory pad on the anterior surface, a field of chemoreceptors that guides them to patches of cellulose ripe for enzymatic dissolution.

Their feeding is the quietest thing you have ever heard. They press their heads to the wall or floor and *lean in*, releasing a cocktail of enzymes from glands along their jawless mouths. The cellulose softens. They lap, not with a tongue but with a spongy, muscular membrane that absorbs the liquefied plant matter directly. The sound of their feeding is the sound of a sponge on a wet counter — barely there, almost intimate.

They are ancient. Their lineage is old. But they are not native in the way the spindles are native. If you could sequence their genome — and something in your gut already suspects this — you would find markers of design. Of insertion. They were *placed* here, or grown here, long ago, engineered to fill an ecological niche that the megaflora required: controlled, distributed digestion. They prune the inner wood. Without them, certain chambers would overgrow, occluding critical conduits. They are the gardeners, and they have been gardening for so long that they have forgotten, at the genetic level, that they were ever anything else.

They are not afraid of you. They regard you the way a glacier regards a bird — as a small, warm, temporary phenomenon. One pauses its feeding as you pass, raising its featureless head, and for a moment the sensory pad orients toward you. It is *reading* your heat signature, your chemical output, your electromagnetic aura. It decides you are irrelevant. It returns to its meal.

## The Sac-Fruit (The Accumulators)

You remember the translucent sacs in the walls, pulsing with blue light. Now you are close enough to study one, and your understanding of them deepens — and becomes more disturbing.

They are not fruits. They are not organs. They are *archives*.

Each sac is roughly the size of a human heart and hangs from a short, thick stem rooted in the cellulose matrix. The skin is taut, elastic, and faintly warm to the touch — warmer than anything else in this freezing place — suggesting exothermic computation. Inside, suspended in a clear, viscous medium, floats a structure that resembles a three-dimensional circuit board grown from crystal and mycelium: a lattice of fine filaments that branch, rejoin, branch again, forming a topology of staggering density. The blue light comes from this lattice. It pulses in patterns.

Not random patterns. *Coded* patterns. Rhythmic, structured, with recurring motifs and variations that suggest language, or at least notation. Each sac is recording something. Data accumulates in the lattice over time — molecular data, chemical readings, structural stress measurements, sap-flow telemetry — compressed and stored at a density that would make your largest data centers weep with inadequacy.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every wall, every ceiling surface, every smooth expanse of cellulose is studded with them, and each one pulses at its own rate, its own rhythm. The chamber is a library. A vast, frozen, humming library, and the text is the slow autobiography of a living machine.

Some sacs are old. Their light has dimmed to a faint, intermittent flicker, and their lattice structures show signs of crystallization — the data has hardened, become inert, archived in a form that is now more mineral than information. These are the ancient stacks, the earliest records, dating back to the construction of the megaflora itself. Others are young and blazing, their lattices still actively growing, still writing.

One sac, at waist height, pulses in a rhythm that is *different*. Faster. Aligned to something — you cannot tell what. You watch it, and as you watch, the pulse shifts, adjusts. It has detected your proximity and is modifying its behavior. It is reading *you*. Writing you.

You are now a entry in this library. You are a datum in the autobiography of the frozen tree.

---

## The Deep Inhabitants

Below. Deeper than the floor. Down in the root systems that extend kilometers into bedrock, in the vascular conduits wide enough to fly through, in chambers that have never known any visitor — there are others.

You do not see them. But the spindles on the walls react to something. A wave of filament-adjustment ripples outward from a point on the far wall, synchronized, all at once, as though responding to a signal. The grazer herd pauses, collectively, their featureless heads lifting in unison, then lowers and resumes feeding, but slightly faster. Something deep has *shifted*. A major conduit has redirected flow. A data sac on the ceiling flares bright blue, then dims. Then another. Then a dozen, in sequence, like dominoes falling.

The deep inhabitants are the macro-scale regulators of this organism. They are not in the room with you. They may not even be in the same branch of the megaflora. But they are aware of you now, because the spindles told them, and because the sac-fruit wrote you into the record, and because the vessel-walkers circulating past your position reported the anomaly in the flow.

What are they? You don't know. Something large. Something old. Something that thinks the way the whole plant grows — slowly, structurally, with the patient inevitability of a glacier scouring a valley. They are the organs of this place, the central processing nodes of a computation so vast and slow that it might take a thousand years to complete a single thought. And that thought, when it arrives, will reshape the chamber you stand in. Will reshape the root systems. Will reshape the continent.

You are standing inside a mind that is still learning how to think. The creatures here are its senses, its muscles, its memories, its immune system. You are a foreign body — warm, brief, chaotic, and alive in a way that is so fast and so desperate compared to the life around you that you are almost not alive at all. You are a flicker. A thermal anomaly. A thought that lasted only a moment in a place where a moment is a epoch.

The spindles watch you with their invisible attention. The grazers graze. The sacs write. The deep inhabitants shift and think and shift again.

The chamber breathes — a slow, cold, ancient breath — and the light seeps, and the sap flows, and the creatures of the inner wood continue their work, which is also worship, which is also computation, which is also growth, all of it the same thing here, all of it the same slow word in a sentence that may never end.
