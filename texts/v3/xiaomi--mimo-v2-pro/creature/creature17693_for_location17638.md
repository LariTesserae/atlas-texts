The first and most pervasive inhabitant is the **Mycelial Chorus**. It does not have a shape, but a presence. It is the network itself—the trillion-threaded mind of the forest, conscious in a way that is not thought, but pure process. It feels the vibration as its own heartbeat. It senses pressure on the mat as a slow, continent-wide touch. It digests the iron beams as a slow dream of rust and sugar. It is the architect, the singer, and the cathedral, all at once. Its "life" is the imperceptible creep of hyphae through basalt pores, the patient dissolution of a gear tooth, the decision to route moisture this way or that over the course of a decade. It is not malevolent, nor benevolent. It is simply total, and its law is incorporation.

Where the Chorus is the whole, the **Stalk Elders** are its organs and its monuments. These colossal fungi are the oldest inhabitants, their growth rings measured not in years, but in the slow deposition of minerals from dripping water. Each is a kingdom unto itself, its tiered caps sheltering unique micro-climates of denser, stranger growths. On their flanks, specialized fungal shelves exude enzymes that glitter like frost, breaking down the sheathing of ancient cables. They are the high rises of this metropolis, and their spore-falls are its weather. They do not move, but they are constantly, infinitesimally, *becoming*.

In the spongy dark between them live the **Shimmerhides**. These are the pale, eyeless fauna—salamander-like in form, but scaled in chitin that shifts between translucent and opalescent. They are born from the mat itself, gestated in warm pockets of accelerated decay around the most concentrated hydrocarbon deposits. They have no eyes; they navigate by feeling the resonant hum through their limbs and by tasting the chemical gradients in the spore-thick air. They are the forest's grazers and its janitors. They consume maturation cycles of smaller fungi, clearing space for new growth, and their slow, mucous-rich trails become pathways for moisture and new hyphal threads. Their own life cycle ends back in the mat, their bodies absorbed in weeks, a perfect circle of consumption and return.

More elusive are the **Rusters**. These are not machines, but creatures that have made a symbiosis with the dead technology. They resemble oversized, multi-legged beetles, their carapaces not chitin, but fused plates of oxidized metal and organic polymer, grown from and onto their bodies. They are drawn to the most potent concentrations of decaying industry—the generators, the control panels. They scrape at the rust and corrupted circuits, ingesting the metallic salts and excreting a dense, conductive frass that the mycelial network then greedily claims. In a sense, they are the teeth of the forest, gnawing at the bones of the old world to feed the new. Their movement is jerky, rhythmic, timed to the sub-harmonics of the Great Hum.

Deep within the thickest mats, in pockets of liquid runoff, dwell the **Drip-Carvers**. These are colonies of microbial life, swirling films of bacteria and archaea that thrive on the most toxic of the old-world leftovers—lubricant sludge, coolant fluids, battery acids. They form shimmering, oil-slick pools that glow with a faint chemiluminescence, a sickly green or amber light that provides the only local illumination in the eternal twilight. The forest protects them, walling them off with specialized, neutralizing hyphae, for they are its specialists, its hazardous waste processors. Without them, the digestion of the past would stall, poisoned by its own legacy.

And finally, there are the **Echoes**. These are the most mysterious inhabitants. They are not wholly organic. They are phenomena that occur at the intersection of the mycelial resonance and the lingering, ghost-electromagnetic fields of the largest buried machines. For moments at a time, the spore-fog will coalesce into a flickering shape that mimics the silhouette of a pressure gauge, or a conduit, or a human tool. They pulse with a dim, static light and hum at a slightly different pitch. They are the forest’s memory, or perhaps its imagination—impressions left by the dead technology on the living network, now manifesting as transient, sound-born phantoms. They are harmless, intangible, but their presence is a constant reminder that this world is built on a foundation of ghosts.

No single creature rules here. There is only the ecosystem, a closed loop of patient, relentless transformation. The inhabitants are functions, agents of decay and growth, each playing their part in the grand, slow opera of absorption. They are the teeth, the stomach, the mind, and the memory of the place. They do not build, they do not hurry. They *digest*. And in the humming, damp cold, they thrive.
