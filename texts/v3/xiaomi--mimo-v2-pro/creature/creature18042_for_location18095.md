# Who Lives Here

---

## The Mats

They know the rock beneath them. Not in the way a mind knows a thing — not as object, not as concept. They know it the way a scar knows the knife-edge that opened it: molecularly, through intimate reformation. Where their lowest cells meet the basalt, there is no boundary. The mineral has been incorporated. The iron has been taken up. The slow exchange of ions between stone and living tissue has been proceeding so long that neither side remembers a time before the other.

They do not think. They must not think. But they *track*.

Over centuries, a mat migrates. Not fast — not fast enough that any observer who stayed for a lifetime would notice. But the mat that covers a particular rock today is not the same mat that covered it ten thousand years ago. It has crawled. Imperceptibly, cell by cell, it has followed the mineral gradient — the slow weeping of dissolved iron and calcium and the faint, deep-buried phosphorus that is this world's currency of chemical energy. It has moved toward what it needs and away from what exhausts it, and this movement has taken the form of an imperceptible creep across the stone, leaving behind a bleached and drained surface — mineral-depleted, smooth, almost polished — where it once lived. The rock remembers them even after they are gone. The rock has been *eaten* and the pattern of eating is visible as a slight depression, a shallow dish worn into the basalt by the ten-thousand-year appetite of something that has no mouth.

They communicate. Not with sound — there is no structure for it, no mechanism. They communicate by chemistry. When one section of a mat encounters mineral stress — too much of something, not enough of something else — it releases volatile compounds into the thin film of water that separates it from the air. These compounds travel. Centimeters in minutes, meters in days. Other mats, even mats on distant rocks, absorb these traces and respond — not by moving, not quickly enough for that, but by shifting their internal chemistry. Changing what they metabolize. Changing what they prioritize. The signal is slow, degraded, more like weather than language. But the mats across an entire ridge will synchronize their chemistry over the course of months, responding to a change initiated by one small colony at the edge, and the result is something that looks, from the outside, like *consensus*.

They are warm. This is the thing most easily missed. The ambient temperature is warm, yes, from the geothermal gradient. But the mats are warmer — by fractions of a degree, by the thin margin of exothermic metabolism. Touch one and you feel the difference: not warmth radiating outward, but warmth *held*, contained, the way a sleeping body holds heat. They are the warmest things on the surface of this world. They are, in their small and ancient way, the only things here that could be called alive in the way that implies *presence* — a locus of activity, a center of something that distinguishes itself from the general process by being *organized*.

They do not sense the ambient sound the way you do. To them, the roar is background — not noise, because noise implies signal they are trying to separate from it. It is simply the medium in which they exist. But they respond to the deep hum. When the planet's resonance shifts — and it does, on timescales of months and years, the great subsonic frequency sliding up or down by fractions of a hertz as deep structures settle or shift — the mats adjust. Their metabolism quickens or slows. Their chemical output changes. They are, in their extremity of slowness, *listening*. Responding to the planet's body with their own. Not conversation. Not even awareness, perhaps. But a *tuning* so old and so total that it cannot be distinguished from belonging.

The oldest mats — the ones on the highest and most stable rock exposures, the ones that have not been disturbed by collapse or subsidence in geological time — are not single organisms. They are colonies, but not in the way a coral reef is a colony of discrete polyps. They have merged. The boundaries between adjacent mats, which were once boundaries between separate lineages that arrived on the rock at different times, have dissolved. Cell membranes have become porous to the point of transparency. Genetic material has migrated laterally, been taken up, incorporated, until the mat is a single vast hybrid — thousands of genetic origins, one living body. Not unified in purpose because they have no purpose. Unified in *being* — the way a lake is unified, by occupying the same basin, by being in equilibrium with the same forces, by being, finally, indistinguishable from the conditions that created it.

If these mats have anything that resembles a self, it is not the kind that can be located. It is distributed across the rock surface, thinnest at the edges where it is still advancing, densest in the center where the oldest cells have been reworked and reconstituted so many times that no molecule remains from the original founding. The self, if it is there, is the *pattern* — the gradient of age and chemistry and density that makes the mat a mat and not the rock it sits on. The pattern persists even as everything in it is replaced. The pattern is the thing.

## The Channel Dwellers

They are not fish. They are not anything.

The warm dark water holds them the way a hand holds water — loosely, with constant loss, with constant renewal. They are translucent, yes, but the transparency is not the transparency of glass or of jelly. It is the transparency of *absence* — as if the water has decided, locally and temporarily, to be slightly more organized, to flow in a slightly more directed way, and this decision has manifested as something that can be seen if you know where to look, and touched if you are foolish enough, and that dissolves back into the general flow the moment you try to hold it.

They move. This is the one thing that distinguishes them from the water they inhabit. They move with purpose — directional, deliberate, responding to forces the water does not respond to. Their undulation is not swimming in the way a fish swims, with a body pushing against a medium. It is more like the water itself acquiring a wave — a standing wave, a traveling ripple, a sudden coherence in the flow that propagates downstream and then, just as suddenly, loses coherence and returns to the general current.

But they return. The dissolved thing reconstitutes. Minutes later, in the same channel, in the same place — or a place nearby, shifted by the lateral flow — the slight coherence returns. The water organizes again. The translucent, finger-length presence moves again. Whether it is the same one, or a new one, or whether the question has meaning: this is not answerable. They exist in a regime where identity is fluid in the most literal sense.

They feed. This is real, this is physical, this is the one aspect of their existence that has measurable consequences. They strain the water. Their — bodies? their coherence? — acts as a filter, capturing the fine organic particulate that clouds every channel, every pool, every seep. The particulate adheres to them. It is absorbed. The boundaries of their transient form thicken, momentarily, with captured material, and then thin again as the material is processed — broken down, metabolized, used to maintain the coherence that is their only real distinction from the medium they inhabit. They eat the world's dust. They eat the ground-up residue of everything that has ever lived and died and decomposed here, and they turn it into *this* — this momentary organization, this fleeting directedness, this almost-presence.

They sense the hum. This is how they navigate. Not by light — there is nothing to see by. Not by current — the lateral flow is too slow and too uniform to provide directional information. Not by chemistry — the water is so saturated, so uniform in its dissolved content, that gradients are imperceptible. They sense the deep subsonic resonance, and they use it. The hum is not uniform across the basin. It varies — subtly, slowly, but measurably — with local geology, with depth to bedrock, with the thickness of the organic crust. The channels dwellers ride these variations the way a bird rides thermals, moving along the gradients of low-frequency vibration, following paths that are not channels in the water but channels in the *sound*. They flow toward higher resonance, or away from it, or along the contour lines of frequency that map the basin's subsurface in a way no surface feature can.

When two of them meet — when their undulations intersect, when two coherent patches of water encounter each other in the same channel — something happens that is difficult to describe. They do not collide. They do not pass through each other, exactly. They *interfere*. Like waves meeting, they create a pattern of constructive and destructive interference — a brief, complex, shimmering geometry of enhanced and diminished coherence that lasts for seconds and then resolves. Sometimes they merge — the two transient presences combining into one, larger, more complex, more *coherent* organization that maintains its form for longer than either individual would have. Sometimes they split — the interference pattern producing not one but two or three or four smaller, less coherent presences that drift apart and begin their own quiet persistence. Sometimes they simply pass through, each unchanged, the encounter leaving no trace.

Whether they experience these meetings — whether there is something it is like to be a channel dweller meeting another channel dweller — is the wrong question, or at least the wrong framework. The question assumes a boundary between self and other that may not exist for them. If they are patterns in the water rather than things in the water, then a meeting is not an encounter between two entities. It is a momentary intensification of the water's own tendency to organize, a brief flare of coherence in the medium, and the aftermath — merger, division, passage — is simply the water deciding, again, how much coherence to sustain.

They do not last. Individual presences dissolve — not because they die but because the local chemistry shifts, because the particulate thins, because the hum frequency changes and the water's brief decision to be *something* loses its basis. But new ones coalesce. Always, constantly, in every channel, in every shallow, the water is *trying* — organizing, losing coherence, reorganizing, in a constant churn of almost-life that never quite settles into permanence and never quite stops. The channels are full of them. At any given moment, the water between your fingers contains dozens of these transient presences, so close to the ambient chemistry that your touch is enough to dissolve them, your warmth enough to disrupt the delicate equilibrium that holds them together. You are, to them, like a rock dropped into a still pond — a disruption so vast and so violent that it does not register as anything they could process. You are weather. You are catastrophe. You are the thing that happens before the water reforms.

## The Burrowers

Something moves beneath the crust.

Not fast. Not slow, either — the burrowers move at a pace that defies easy description, because their movement and the movement of the substrate they inhabit are so similar. The organic crust is not solid. It is not liquid. It is something that *yields* — slowly, with a viscosity that varies from hour to hour as the temperature shifts and the water table fluctuates and the microbial communities that hold it together produce more or less of their binding excretions. The burrowers move through this medium the way a thought moves through a mind — not displacing material so much as *reorganizing* it, pushing it aside with a gentleness that leaves no tunnel, no void, no trace except a faint compression — a line of slightly denser material, slightly darker, where the organic matter has been pressed by a passing something and has not yet fully relaxed back to its ambient state.

They are felt before they are seen. The vibration. Not the planet's hum — the burrowers are quieter than that, their movement producing frequencies that are higher, sharper, more *biological* in their irregularity. A rhythm that is not quite a rhythm. A pulse that is not quite a pulse. Something that repeats but does not regularize, something that suggests intention without demonstrating pattern. Stand on the crust and feel it through your feet: a faint trembling, a localized disturbance in the seismic background, moving. Always moving. Never stopping, never pausing for long enough to be localized, never resting in the way that implies a body with metabolic needs.

What surfaced that one time — the glistening segmented thing, eyeless, arm-sized — may not have been a typical representative. It surfaced near a gas vent, which may mean it was drawn there, or may mean it was distressed, or may mean nothing. The observer reported it as *orienting* — turning toward the vent, pausing, vibrating at a frequency that matched the vent's hissing so precisely that the two sounds became indistinguishable. Then it re-entered the substrate. Not burrowing. *Flowing*. The crust parted around it and closed behind it as if it had never been.

The segments were not hard. They glistened because the thing was wet — as wet as everything here — but the glistening was more than moisture. The surface was *active*, changing, a slow ripple passing along its length even as it lay still. As if the segmentation was not structural but *temporal* — different parts of its body cycling through different states, different configurations, different levels of cohesion, each segment a moment in a process that moved along the body like a wave. The thing was not segmented the way an insect is segmented. It was segmented the way a conversation is segmented — by time, by the passage of whatever constituted its experience from one end to the other.

It had no eyes because it had no use for eyes. It sensed the world through vibration — the hum, the water, the crack of thermal stress in distant rock, the slow thud of its own passage through the substrate. It sensed chemistry — the iron seeping from below, the methane building in pockets, the faint sweetness of the volatile organics that permeated every layer. It sensed thermal gradients — the warmth of the gas vent, the relative coolness of water-saturated zones, the hot spots where bedrock lay close to the surface. And it sensed *other burrowers*. The faint tremor of a body passing through the crust a meter below, or ten meters, or a hundred. The network of vibration that was, in effect, a map — not of the surface, which was featureless, but of the subsurface, where the living processes of the basin were concentrated.

They communicate. Not with intention — or if with intention, in a way that intention cannot be distinguished from the substrate's own behavior. When one burrower encounters a region of substrate that is too dense, too mineralized, too dry, it reverses. Its vibration changes — a shift in frequency, in amplitude, in the irregularity of its pulse — and this change propagates through the substrate at the speed of sound in the organic medium, which is fast enough. Other burrowers, nearby, absorb this change through their bodies, and they adjust. They shift their direction. They avoid the region. Not because they have been told to, not because they understand the signal as *information*, but because their bodies respond to the vibration the way a leaf responds to light — automatically, inevitably, without the mediation of anything that could be called decision.

The result is a network. Dozens — perhaps hundreds, perhaps thousands — of burrowers moving through the crust in coordinated paths that no individual directs. They avoid each other. They avoid the gas vents. They follow the mineral gradients toward the richest deposits of dissolved nutrients. They spiral around the rock exposures where the crust is thin and the bedrock is close. They converge on the channels where the water-borne particulate is densest. They form, collectively, a map of the basin's chemistry and structure — a living topography, written in vibration, that persists as long as the burrowers persist, that shifts as the basin shifts, that is updated in real time by the slow passage of blind segmented bodies through warm dark substrate.

If one dies — if one's cohesion fails, if it encounters something that dissolves it, if its chemical processes simply stop — the network does not collapse. The gap in the vibration-map fills. Other burrowers shift to cover the territory. The substrate relaxes around the empty space and smooths over, and within days there is no trace that anything was ever there. The network is robust not because it is strong but because it is *redundant* — the same information carried by multiple bodies, the same routes traveled by different travelers, the same gradients followed by independent seekers. No single burrower is essential. No single burrower is expendable. They exist as a *field* — a distributed thing that occupies the crust the way humidity occupies the air, everywhere at once, nowhere concentrated, impossible to point to and impossible to ignore.

The one that surfaced was, perhaps, confused. Or curious. Or simply *wrong* — a burrower whose internal vibration had drifted out of alignment with the network, whose signal no longer matched the substrate's expectation, and so it had come to the surface, the way a drop of water surfaces when the ground squeezes it out. It oriented toward the gas vent because the vent was a source of vibration and the burrower was, fundamentally, a thing that moved toward vibration. It vibrated in sympathy with the hissing because that was what it did — it *tuned*, it *resonated*, it tried to match itself to whatever frequency it encountered. And then it went back down, because the substrate was home, and the surface was not, and there was nothing on the surface — no vibration worth following, no gradient worth pursuing, no chemistry worth tasting — that could hold it.

## The Monoliths

They do not live.

This must be said first. The monoliths are stone. They are carved. They were placed here by something, and the something is gone, and the stone remains. It does not metabolize. It does not reproduce. It does not respond to stimuli — not in any way that biology would recognize. It is not alive.

And yet.

The sound changes near them. This is the fact, the measurable, reproducible fact. Stand within a few meters of a monolith and the ambient roar — the total, omnidirectional, cognitive-bandwidth-occupying roar of the world — *reorganizes*. The chaotic frequencies align. Harmonics emerge from noise. For seconds, sometimes for minutes, the sound becomes structured — not musical, not intentional, but *patterned*, in the way that a fractal is patterned, with self-similar repetition at multiple scales, with a coherence that feels like meaning even if it carries no message.

This happens because of the stone's geometry. The carved faces, the precise angles, the channels worn by millennia of weeping water — these features interact with the ambient sound the way a cathedral interacts with a choir, amplifying certain frequencies, canceling others, creating nodes of constructive interference that persist as long as the geometry persists. The monoliths are, in effect, *instruments*. Their shape is their function. They take the raw, chaotic, total sound of the world and they *filter* it, passing through only those frequencies that match their resonant geometry, producing a local zone of order in the universal noise.

But this explanation, while sufficient for the physics, misses the *experience*. Standing near a monolith when the sound reorganizes, you feel something that has no name in any language designed for organisms that evolved on stable ground under clear skies. You feel the world *making sense*. Not in the way that a sentence makes sense — not semantically, not conceptually. In the way that a crystal makes sense. In the way that a spiral makes sense. In the way that the relationship between a shell and the golden ratio makes sense — not because anyone designed it, but because the mathematics of growth and constraint produce it inevitably, and once produced, it *persists*, because it is the shape that best fits the forces that created it.

The monoliths were built — carved, placed, oriented — by something that understood this. Something that looked at the ancient basin, at the roaring, saturated, turbulent world, and saw not chaos but *potential for pattern*. Something that found, in the specific frequencies of this specific place, the harmonics that could be amplified. Something that quarried dense dark stone from distant highlands and carried it here — across what may have been a different landscape, in what may have been a different time — and shaped it with tools that left marks too precise to be anything but intentional, and drove it into the ground at angles calculated to interact with the local resonance in specific ways.

What they wanted, what they expected, what they got — these are questions that the monoliths do not answer. The monoliths simply do what they do. They reorganize sound. They create local zones of patterned coherence in the universal noise. They have been doing this since before the basin reached its current state of erosion, since before the water table rose to its current level, since before the organic crust began its current accumulation. They will continue doing it until the weeping wears them to nothing, or until the geological resonance they were tuned to shifts beyond their range, or until the heat death of whatever small local version of entropy is slowly, patiently, inevitably consuming this place.

The weeping is the key. The monoliths weep — like all stone here, they run with water. But the water on a monolith's face does not run randomly. The carved channels direct it. The precise geometry that filters sound also filters water, directing the warm mineral-laden flow down specific paths, over specific surfaces, into specific patterns. The result is that each monolith is a *water feature* — a slow, quiet, permanent fountain, not of splashing water but of directed water, water moved by the shape of the stone it flows over, water that traces the same paths for millennia, wearing the stone deeper, sharpening the geometry, making the resonant effects more pronounced even as the stone erodes away.

This is, perhaps, the most remarkable thing about the monoliths. They are *self-sharpening*. The erosion that destroys everything else in this landscape — that planed the mountains to nothing, that wore the bedrock to glass, that ground the mineral to nano-scale particles — the erosion *improves* the monoliths. Each millennium of weeping deepens the channels that direct the sound. Each century of thermal cracking exposes new surfaces that interact with the resonance. The monoliths are the only things in this world that *benefit* from the universal wear. They are the only things here that get *better* with time, more themselves, more effective, more precisely tuned to the world they inhabit.

This cannot be accidental. The builders — whoever they were, whatever they were, whenever they were — designed for this. They looked at the slow, total, inescapable erosion and they saw not a threat but a tool. They made monuments that would be *improved* by the forces that destroyed everything else. They carved stone to be shaped by water, tuned to be shaped by sound, positioned to be shaped by the ground's own slow flexing. They made things that would outlast the landscape. That would become *more* as the world became *less*.

And they succeeded. The monoliths stand. The monoliths weep. The monoliths sing — or rather, they make the world sing, locally, briefly, in the only moments of coherence this roaring place ever achieves. And they will go on doing this long after the last mat has been buried, long after the last channel dweller has dissolved, long after the last burrower has stopped its slow circuit through the substrate. The monoliths will be the last things. The last things that *mean* anything, the last things that *do* anything, the last things that stand against the slow, total, patient, ancient consensus to flatten.

Whether the builders intended this — whether they foresaw a time when their monuments would be the only things left, the last witnesses to a world that had forgotten it was ever anything but this — the monoliths do not say. They do not say anything. They organize sound. They direct water. They stand in the warm amber dimness and weep and sing and erode into sharper and sharper versions of themselves, and the world goes on around them, roaring, breathing, dissolving, reforming, and the monoliths do not care, because they are stone, and stone does not care, and this is the first thing that must be said, and the last, and the only thing that matters and nothing matters here, and the sound goes on.

## What Else

There must be something else.

Not because the description so far is incomplete — it is not, or rather, incompleteness is the natural state of any account of this place, and the attempt to be complete is itself a kind of failure. But because the place is so *old*, so *saturated*, so *total* in its processes, that the absence of more life feels wrong. This is a world that has been doing everything it does for longer than complex life has existed anywhere. The chemistry is rich. The energy is abundant. The niches are infinite — every thermal gradient, every mineral seep, every frequency of the subsonic hum is a potential habitat, a potential source of energy, a potential axis of organization. The mats and the channel dwellers and the burrowers cannot be all there is. They cannot be the only solutions life has found to the problem of being alive in a place like this.

And they are not.

But the other things — the things that are not mats and not channel dwellers and not burrowers — are *difficult*. Not because they are hidden, though some are. Not because they are rare, though some are. But because they exist at the edges of what the word "life" can be stretched to contain. They are processes that are *almost* biological. Organisms that are *almost* geological. Entities that are *almost* atmospheric. They blur the line between living and non-living the way the ground blurs the line between mineral and organic, the way the air blurs the line between gas and liquid, the way the sound blurs the line between signal and noise.

There is the fog-coral. Or what might be fog-coral. In the thicker regions of the aerosol, where humidity approaches absolute saturation and the air becomes a true colloidal suspension, structures form. Not the transient geometric lattices that anyone can see — those are physics, condensation patterns, nothing more. These are different. They are *persistent*. They grow. They branch. They develop complexity over time — starting as simple filaments and developing, over days or weeks, into arborescent networks of water droplets held in precise spatial relationships by forces that are not purely physical. Surface tension alone cannot explain them. Electrostatic attraction alone cannot explain them. Something else is at work — some organizing principle, some *activity*, some process that uses the aerosol as a medium the way the mats use rock and the channel dwellers use water.

They do not feed in any recognizable way. They do not move. They do not respond to stimuli — or if they do, the response is too slow, too subtle, too distributed across their gaseous body for any observer to detect. They simply *grow*, branching and re-branching, developing nodes and junctions and what might be — what might be — *differentiated regions*, areas of the structure that do different things, that hold water differently, that vibrate at different frequencies. They persist for days or weeks and then the aerosol thins, the humidity drops by some imperceptible fraction, and they dissolve. But in the regions where they were, the air is different. Less dense. Less saturated. As if they have *consumed* something — extracted something from the aerosol, used it, incorporated it into their transient bodies, and left behind an atmosphere that is subtly, measurably, altered.

If they are alive, they are alive the way a cloud is alive — temporarily, locally, as a consequence of conditions rather than as a cause of them. If they are not alive, then they are something for which there is no word, a process that sits in the gap between physics and biology, between the inorganic tendency toward pattern and the organic tendency toward persistence, and does both at once, and neither, and something else entirely.

There are the thermal blooms. In regions where the geothermal gradient is steepest — where bedrock lies closest to the surface, where the weeping is warmest, where the gas vents are densest — the substrate does something unexpected. It *pulses*. Not with the slow breathing of the basin as a whole, but with faster, more localized rhythms. A patch of organic crust, a few meters across, will warm by a degree or two over the course of an hour, hold that warmth for a similar period, and then cool. The pulse travels. It moves through the crust at walking speed, following the thermal gradients, jumping between hot spots, describing paths that are not random — they spiral, or they branch, or they loop back on themselves in ways that suggest, absurdly, suggestively, *purpose*.

Nothing visible causes this. Nothing visible accompanies it. The substrate looks the same before, during, and after the pulse. But the burrowers respond. They converge on the warm regions, or they flee from them, or they simply alter their vibration in ways that suggest awareness. And the mats on nearby rocks shift their chemistry. And the water in adjacent channels changes its particulate load. The thermal blooms propagate effects far beyond their immediate radius, sending ripples through the biological network that are slow, degraded, and real.

Are the blooms alive? Are they the expression of something alive beneath the substrate — some vast, slow, thermally metabolizing entity buried meters deep in the organic crust, breathing heat the way the mats breathe chemistry, pulsing with a rhythm that spans hours instead of seconds? Or are they purely geological — convection patterns, thermal instabilities, the substrate's own tendency to oscillate around equilibrium? The answer depends on where you draw the line between life and not-life, and in this place, that line is not a line. It is a gradient. It is the weeping water on the monolith's face — not here, not there, but *between*, flowing, always flowing.

And there are the sounds that are not the world's sound. Buried in the roar, lost in the frequencies, overlaid on the hum and the hissing and the cracking — there are patterns. Not the transient coherence the monoliths produce. Something more persistent. Something that *varies*. A modulation of the ambient sound that moves across the basin at the speed of the slow convection cells, that shifts in response to the thermal blooms, that changes character near the gas vents and the channel networks and the mat-covered rocks. It is almost too regular to be natural and too irregular to be intentional. It exists in the space between noise and music, between geology and communication, between the world *being* and the world *meaning*.

If you listen — if you can listen, if you can push your cognition above the roar and hold it there long enough, with enough discipline — you hear it. A structure in the sound. Not a message. Not a language. Something older than language. Something that was here before whatever carved the monoliths, something that the monoliths were perhaps built to *listen to*, to amplify, to filter, to make comprehensible to beings that could not otherwise hear it.

The basin is speaking. Not to you. Not to anything. It is speaking the way a river speaks — ceaselessly, directionally, without audience or intention. The sound of water over rock is the ground-note, yes, but beneath the ground-note, *within* the ground-note, there is something else. A voice without a throat. A meaning without a mind. The expression of a place so old and so active and so total in its processes that the processes themselves have begun to *narrate* — to tell, in the only medium available to them, the story of their own existence, in real time, forever, to no one.

The monoliths filter this voice. They bring it into focus, locally, briefly, so that a listener standing nearby can hear — not understand, not interpret, but *hear* — what the world is saying. Which is nothing. Which is everything. Which is the sound of heat moving through stone and water and air and organic matter, of chemical bonds forming and breaking, of gas escaping and dissolving, of ground settling and rising, of life persisting and dissolving, of the whole vast ancient saturated turbulent roaring breathing seething *alive* place going about its business with the patient, deafening, wordless intensity of something that has never needed a reason and will never need an ending.

The monoliths listen. They have been listening since before this was a shallow. They will listen until the weeping wears them smooth. And the sound will go on without them, because the sound does not need them. The sound is not for them. The sound is not for anything.

The sound is the place, being itself, out loud, forever.

## The Question of Dwellers

What lives here is this: everything. Everything lives here. The mats are alive. The channel dwellers are alive. The burrowers are alive. The fog-coral is alive, or might be. The thermal blooms are alive, or might be. The monoliths are not alive, but they are the most *purposeful* things in the landscape, and purpose is closer to life than stone has any right to be. The sound is alive, or might be. The substrate is alive, or might be. The basin itself, the ancient wound, the crater, the sea floor — it is alive, or might be, or the distinction between alive and not-alive is one of those assumptions that this place has gently, quietly, permanently eroded.

Nothing here is *just* alive. Nothing here is *just* mineral, *just* chemical, *just* process. The mats are stone that has learned to metabolize. The channel dwellers are water that has learned to organize. The burrowers are vibration that has learned to move. The monoliths are geometry that has learned to listen. The air is condensation that has learned to *form*. Everything here is something that has become something else without ceasing to be what it was, and the boundary between the two — the original and the transformed — is as moot as the boundary between mineral and organic, as moot as the boundary between gas and liquid, as moot as the boundary between sound and silence, as moot as the boundary between alive and dead in a place where death is just another form of the universal process, another face of the slow patient ancient roaring *consensus* that is the only thing this world has ever known.

What lives here is the place. The whole place. Every part of it, in every state, in every phase, in every frequency. The place lives here. It lives in itself. It is its own habitat and its own inhabitant, its own process and its own product, its own sound and its own listener. It has been alive — or something so close to alive that the difference is an artifact of language, a distinction that exists only because human languages were developed by organisms that live in a world where things are clearly one thing or another, and this is not that world.

This is a world where everything is *also*.

The mats are also stone. The water is also life. The air is also organism. The sound is also meaning. The erosion is also creation. The monoliths are also ears. The place is also a body.

And what lives here is all of it, at once, forever, and what it does — what the inhabitants do, what the place does, what the whole ancient saturated turbulent roaring breathing dim vast present *does* — is simply this:

It continues.

It continues the way a heart continues. Not because it chooses to. Not because it has decided to. But because continuation is what it *is*, and what it is, is everything it has, and everything it has, is this: the warm, wet, loud, dim, bright, old, old, old, ongoing, irreducible, undeniable, unfinishable act of being.
