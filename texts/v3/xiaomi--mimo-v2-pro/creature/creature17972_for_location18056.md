The things that live here do not fight the seep. They are of it.

First, there are the **Levelers**. You see them most often at the liminal hours—the pale, cold dawn that never quite brightens, the perpetual dusk that never fully darkens—moving in teams of three or four along the base of a wall or the underside of a gangway. They are human, or they were. Their skin has taken on the hue of the damp concrete: mottled grey-white, with a faint sheen of moisture that seems to bead and run but never evaporates. Their tools are not carried, but grown, or perhaps extruded—long, slender rods of a chitinous, bone-like material that they press against surfaces. The rod vibrates, minutely, and from its tip a fine, grey paste oozes, filling cracks, shoring up weeping concrete, smoothing over the fret of rust. They work in silence, communicating through a soft, percussive tapping of their tools against surfaces—a code that echoes the constant drip of the city. They are the city's immune system, its white blood cells, perpetually healing the micro-fissures the unstable ground opens. Their eyes are large, dark, and adapted to the gloom of the enclosed streets; they rarely look up.

Then there are the **Fog-Tenders**. They are thinner, more willowy, their clothing—not worn, but a layered, fibrous growth that resembles lichen or thick, pale moss—blurs at the edges, making them hard to focus on. They live in the upper levels, on the rooftops and gangways, where the haze is thickest. They don't seem to build or maintain. They *listen*. They press their ears to metal plates, to ventilation shafts, to the guy-wires of antenna arrays. They are mapping the hum. They feel for the subsonic drone's inconsistencies, for the moment the geothermal pull shifts or the magnetic field eddies. They are the city's sensory organs, translating the turbulence of the deep ground into predictions. When a Fog-Tender stops walking and holds perfectly still, head cocked, others give them a wide berth. A street might be rerouted tomorrow, a building might begin to sink. Their knowledge is intuitive, tidal. They navigate not by landmarks, which change, but by gradients of disorientation.

In the lowest levels, where the walkways are permanent and the water is knee-deep and still, live the **Waders**. Their lower bodies have changed. It is not webbing, but a thickening, a softening of the skin into a permanent, insulating layer of subcutaneous fat, sleek and waterproof. Their feet are broad, splayed, finding purchase on the slick pallets and submerged foundations where others would slip. They are the farmers of the fungal gardens, the tenders of the great pale mushrooms that fruit on pipe-junctions and wall-seams. They communicate in low, bubbling tones that carry through the water, and they cultivate not just fungi, but relationships with the pale, blind crustaceans and worms that thrive in the flooded zones, herding them like livestock. They trade edible mushrooms and waterproof resins to the upper levels for metal shims and electrical components. They are patient. The water is their element; it is not an enemy to be pumped away, but a home to be furnished.

And there are the **Statics**. These are harder to see, because they don't move much. They are found in the zones of strongest magnetic anomaly, where compasses spin and lights flicker. They sit or stand for days, weeks, their bodies coated in a fine, ferric dust that gives them a rusty, reddish-brown complexion. They are human anchors. Their flesh, perhaps through some mineral absorption, has become faintly paramagnetic. They feel the pull in their bones, in their blood. Communities will build around a Static, using their stable, magnetic presence as a fixed point in the shifting landscape—a living surveyor's monument. They speak rarely, in flat, declarative sentences. "The ground will hold here." "A vein is forming, three yards east." They are consulted before any major construction. Their bodies *are* the bedrock the city lacks.

Finally, in the stories of the perimeter crews, are the **Edge-Things**. Low, fast shadows in the perpetual fog of the outer basin. Sounds that are part gurgle, part grind. They are not invaders from outside. They are the Seep's own, the logical endpoint of its processes—life forms that have forgone skeletons altogether, that are more slurry than solid, that move through the saturated soil and standing water like quick, curious thoughts. They investigate the foundations. They are drawn to the heat of the factories and the electromagnetic noise. They are not hostile, but they are *inquisitive*, and their nature is so foreign to the human inhabitants that the contact feels like a haunting. They are the future of the basin, perhaps—the form life takes when stone is still becoming stone.

The people of the Seep do not have pets. They have symbionts: the luminous fungi that grow in their walls and provide a soft, cold light; the small, pale rodents that are tolerated because they eat the wiring-insulation that would otherwise be consumed by mold; the sparrow-like birds that nest in the eaves and whose guano, high in minerals, is collected for the rooftop soil-patches where the blue-green grasses are coaxed to grow.

They are all participants in the turbulence. A Leveler might become a Fog-Tender after a decade, their skin paling, their hearing attuning to deeper vibrations. A Wader child might show Static tendencies, and be apprenticed to the nearest anchor. The city doesn't have citizens; it has *states of being*, phases in a cycle of adaptation. They are not building a city *on* the land. They are a manifestation *of* the land—a transitional fauna in a geological adolescence, building their own nest from the inside out, drip by drip, shiver by shiver, in the vast, cold, quiet dark.
