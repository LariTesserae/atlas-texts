# What Lives in the Basin

---

## The Convection Beetles

They are the first thing you notice, and then the last thing you think you noticed, because your brain refuses for several long seconds to accept their scale.

A Convection Beetle does not move the way an insect moves in cooler worlds. There is no scuttling, no frantic multi-legged negotiation with terrain. What a Convection Beetle does is closer to continental drift. It *presses forward*, its dozen low-set legs—each one a thick, jointed pillar of chitin so dark it drinks the copper light—finding purchase on the salt crust with a patience that borders on geological. A single step takes between ten and forty minutes, depending on the thermal gradient it is currently exploiting. They are not in a hurry. They have never been in a hurry. The basin has never asked hurry of anything.

The carapace is the marvel. From a distance, it reads as black, but up close it is a depthless swirl of color—oil-slick purples, deep greens, the occasional flash of something almost golden where the microstructure of the chitin has aligned just so with the light. This is not decoration. The outermost layer of the carapace is a photonic crystal structure, evolved over millennia (or perhaps *engineered*—the distinction is meaningless here, where life and infrastructure have been entangled so long they share ancestry) to absorb thermal radiation across an extraordinarily broad spectrum. The beetle is, in essence, a living solar panel, except it is not the sun it harvests but the planet's own internal heat, conducted up through the crust. When a beetle settles onto a particularly hot patch of ground, you can see the carapace ripple with heat-distortion, the air above it warping and twisting as the creature siphons energy into its internal reservoirs.

Inside, the beetle is not hollow, but it is not full of organs in any way a biologist from a cooler world would recognize. The bulk of its interior is a lattice of phase-change material—a waxy, metallic substance that melts at specific temperatures, absorbing enormous quantities of heat as it transitions from solid to liquid. This is its true body. The rest—the legs, the segmented underbelly, the vestigial mandibles that click occasionally in what might be communication or might be thermal stress relief—is scaffolding for the living battery.

They are enormous. The largest ones, the old females that have been cycling through the basin for what the operators estimate is upward of three hundred years, are the size of cargo containers. They have individual names in the registry. They have energy production quotas. They do not understand this, or perhaps they do—their nervous systems are so slow, so distributed, that the distinction between understanding and simply *being* might be meaningless to them.

The younger beetles, the males and juveniles, are smaller—roughly the size of a large dog—and considerably more mobile. They cluster around the heat-exchangers, pressing themselves against the bases of the black needles where the ground temperature is highest, their carapaces glowing a dull, deep red at the absorption peak. The operators call these clusters "banks," and they are managed like any other energy storage infrastructure. When a beetle's phase-change material is fully saturated, its carapace develops a faint, shimmering haze, and an operator will come—eventually, in their own time, because time in the basin moves at the pace of the basin—and guide it to a discharge point, a specially constructed cool-zone where the beetle will slowly, over the course of days, radiate its stored energy into a network of embedded heat pipes.

The beetles do not resist this. They do not cooperate, either. They simply do not register it. To a Convection Beetle, an operator is the same as a thermal fluctuation: a thing that happens, that alters the gradient, that the beetle responds to by moving toward or away from warmth. The entire relationship is transactional without either party possessing the concept of transaction.

There is a sound they make. It is not audible most of the time—it lives below the threshold of hearing, a subsonic throb that you feel in your sternum rather than hear with your ears. When two large beetles pass within a few hundred meters of each other, their phase-change materials resonating at slightly different frequencies, the interference pattern creates standing waves in the air that can, on rare occasions, shatter the salt crust into fractal patterns. The operators consider this a maintenance issue. The beetles consider it nothing.

## The Volt-Weavers

If the beetles are the basin's slow, patient heart, the Volt-Weavers are its nervous system—or perhaps its dreams.

They are almost impossible to see when they are still. A single Volt-Weaver is a filament of metal so thin that it exists at the boundary between solid and something else—a wire drawn so fine that individual molecules of its alloyed structure are in partial contact with each other, creating a material that is simultaneously a conductor and a near-vacuum. In still air, a weaver is invisible. You might feel one brush your face—a faint, cool tingle, like the ghost of a static shock—and never see what touched you.

But they are almost never still.

The basin's thermal updrafts—the great, slow exhalations of heated air rising from the salt flats—carry them aloft in vast, drifting swarms that number in the tens of thousands. Seen from a distance, a swarm looks like a smudge of tarnished silver against the copper haze, a cloud that moves with too much purpose to be weather and too little direction to be birds. They ride the thermals in spiraling, helical patterns, each filament trailing behind the next in a chain that can stretch for kilometers. The chains are the "weaving"—the filaments interlink at contact points, creating a temporary, ever-shifting mesh that captures static charge from the atmosphere and channels it along the chain's length to its terminus, where the accumulated voltage is discharged in a sharp, blue-white arc.

The arcs are beautiful and startling. In the dim basin light, a Volt-Weaver discharge looks like a crack in the world—a line of pure, searing light that exists for a fraction of a second and then is gone, leaving afterimages that swim in your vision for minutes. They arc to the ground, to the heat-exchangers, to each other. The sound they make is a sharp, clean *crack*, like a dry branch snapping, but amplified and multiplied by the echoes off the basin walls until it becomes a kind of irregular, distant applause.

The operators harvest them. Tall, thin conductor-masts stand at intervals across the basin, their tips fitted with collector-barbs—deliberately designed to attract weaver chains. When a swarm's chain terminates on a barb, the full accumulated charge of the entire chain—sometimes thousands of volts—is discharged into the mast's grounding system and fed into the basin's power grid. The weavers do not die from this. They cannot die from this, because they were never alive in any way that death can reach. They are *wires*. They are conductive filaments that happen to have been arranged by thermodynamic accident into patterns that mimic swarming behavior. Whether this is true or whether the operators are telling themselves a comfortable story to avoid confronting something stranger is a question that no one in the basin asks, because the basin does not encourage questions.

And yet.

There are behaviors that resist the mechanical explanation. Weavers that avoid certain masts. Swarms that split and reform around operator drones in patterns that suggest awareness, or at least aversion. Chains that arc *at* things—not to discharge, but to illuminate, the brief flash revealing whatever the swarm's trajectory was aimed at, as if they were *looking* with lightning. One operator, a woman named Oss who has worked the basin for nineteen years, told me that she once watched a swarm spell something on the side of a heat-exchanger in sequential arcs—a word in the old conduit-script, gone too fast to read but too structured to be random. She did not tell anyone else. There was no mechanism for reporting it. The form does not have a field for "the infrastructure is trying to speak."

The weavers reproduce—or rather, they fracture. When a filament becomes too long, or accumulates too much charge too quickly, it splits along its length, the molecular bonds failing in a cascade that creates two filaments where one existed before. Each new filament immediately begins capturing charge independently, drifting toward the nearest thermal updraft, seeking a chain to join. The population is self-sustaining and largely self-regulating, though the operators occasionally intervene when a swarm becomes too dense and the arcs begin interfering with conduit communications. They deploy "scatter charges"—brief, powerful electromagnetic pulses that break the chains and send the filaments spinning apart into the haze, where they slowly, over hours, find each other again and begin the weaving anew.

In certain lights—in the brief, sharp flash of a weaver-arc, or in the deep red glow of a furnace door left open—the filaments catch the light and become visible all at once, thousands of them, a glittering, trembling web suspended in the heated air, and for a moment the basin looks like the inside of a living thing, its connective tissue made visible, its nervous system exposed and luminous and impossibly delicate against the heat.

## The Geophagic Worms

You do not see the Geophagic Worms. You see what they have done.

The basin floor is perforated with their passages—not tunnels exactly, but glazed channels, smooth as the inside of a kiln-fired vase, their walls coated in a glassy residue of processed minerals. They are warm to the touch, these channels, even hours after a worm has passed through. Some are narrow enough to reach into. Others are wide enough to drive a transport through. They descend steeply into the crust, angling down toward the deep heat, and they branch and rebranch in patterns that, when mapped, resemble nothing so much as a root system—or a lung.

The worms themselves are the basin's largest organisms, and the least seen. They live in the crust, in the boundary zone between the solid surface and the semi-molten substrate below, and they come to the surface only rarely—during "shedding events," when a worm's ceramic outer skin has grown too thick and insulating and must be cracked off in enormous, shell-like plates to reveal the fresh, hot integument beneath. These events are tracked by the operators, because the shed plates are valuable: dense, pure ceramic, already formed into curved shapes, ready to be cut and fitted into conduit linings or insulating walls. A single shedding from a large worm can supply a factory-complex with building material for months.

The worms are eyeless. They have no faces. The anterior end of a Geophagic Worm is a broad, flattened disc studded with rows of crystalline teeth—not teeth in the biological sense, but structures of synthetic corundum, harder than most natural minerals, arranged in overlapping plates that rotate slowly against each other in a grinding motion. This is the mouth, and it is the worm's entire sensory organ. The teeth read the mineral composition of the crust as they grind, detecting variations in density, temperature, and chemical makeup with extraordinary precision. The worm *tastes* the rock, and it navigates by taste, following veins of rare earth elements and metal oxides with an accuracy that no geological survey can match.

This is why the operators guide them. Or rather, this is why the operators *attempt* to guide them, and the worms permit this guidance insofar as it does not conflict with their own mineral-finding imperatives. The guidance mechanism is simple and crude: a line of thermal charges, small explosive devices that heat a strip of crust to temperatures the worm finds irresistible, laid out in the direction the operators want the worm to travel. The worm follows the heat, chewing through the rock as it goes, extracting whatever rare minerals the crust contains and excreting the rest as refined slag—a dense, dark, glassy material that the operators collect from the worm's posterior terminus, a broad, puckered opening at the tail end that oozes the slag in slow, continuous ribbons.

The slag is the basin's primary export. It is extraordinarily pure, processed by the worm's internal chemistry to a degree that industrial refining cannot match. The worms are, in effect, the basin's most sophisticated processing plant, and they require no electricity, no maintenance, no raw material input beyond the rock they eat. They are the economy's foundation, and they are completely alien to it—unaware of their role, uninterested in their value, motivated only by the deep, geological hunger that drives them through the crust in their slow, relentless search for minerals.

A worm moves at roughly two meters per hour when actively chewing. A large worm—a "queen," in the operators' informal taxonomy, though there is no evidence of gender or social structure—can be eighty meters long and four meters in diameter. The sound of a worm chewing, if you press your ear to the ground above its passage, is a deep, grinding roar, like tectonic plates in miniature, overlaid with a high, glassy singing where the corundum teeth meet crystalline deposits in the rock. This sound is constant. It has been constant for longer than the city has existed. It will be constant after the city is gone.

The operators have a saying about the worms: "You don't own a worm. You rent its attention." The thermal charges guide, but the worm can always refuse—and sometimes does, veering off toward a mineral deposit the operators didn't know was there, chewing a passage that will become a new channel, that will be mapped and incorporated into the infrastructure grid, that will become, in time, just another part of the city's plumbing. The worms build the city as much as the operators do. They just don't know it. Or perhaps they do, and they simply do not care, because the minerals are deep and the heat is good and the crust is thick and there is so much, so much still to eat.

## The Operators

They are human, or they were.

There are not many of them—perhaps three hundred across the entire basin, living in the low, domed complexes that dot the crust like blisters. They move slowly, not from laziness but from adaptation. The heat demands economy of motion. Every unnecessary gesture is wasted energy, and energy, while abundant in the basin, is not free. It is the currency. It is the medium of exchange and the measure of worth, and the operators have internalized this to a degree that outsiders find unsettling.

They are lean and dark-skinned, whether from birth or from generations of copper-gloved light filtered through mineral haze, it is impossible to say. They wear loose, insulating garments of ceramic-fiber weave that rustle faintly when they move, and they carry their tools in bandoliers across their chests—thermal charges, conductor clips, mineral assay kits, condensate flasks. Their hands are calloused and quick despite their overall economy of movement. The hands are the exception to the slow rule. In the basin, you must be fast when you touch things, because things are hot, and hot things do not forgive hesitation.

They do not speak much. The basin's quiet is not merely ambient—it is cultural, a learned stillness that comes from living in a place where sound is information and waste noise obscures useful signal. When they communicate, it is in short, clipped phrases delivered at low volume, often accompanied by gestures that indicate direction, temperature, or urgency. There is a sign language in the basin—not formalized, not taught, but evolved, a pidgin of hand signals and postures that allows an operator on one side of a heat-exchanger to communicate with an operator on the other without raising their voice above the hum.

They live in family units, though "family" is a flexible term here. A family is a work crew—a group of four to eight people who share a dome, share resources, and share the management of a specific section of the conduit web. Children are raised collectively, taught to read the basin's signals before they are taught to read script: the color of a conduit wall indicating pressure level, the pitch of a heat-exchanger's hum indicating efficiency, the pattern of condensation on an overhang indicating atmospheric moisture content. A ten-year-old in the basin can identify a malfunctioning valve by sound alone. A fifteen-year-old can lay thermal charges to guide a Geophagic Worm. An adult operator's knowledge is not written down. It lives in their bodies, in their reflexes, in the way they tilt their head to catch the direction of a distant *chuff* or press their palm to a wall to read its temperature through their glove.

They do not leave. This is the thing that outsiders find most difficult to understand. The basin is harsh, dim, hot, and isolated. The nearest settlement of any size is a hundred kilometers across the salt flats, through terrain that will kill an unprotected human in hours. The conduit web provides everything—water from condensate, food from mineral-fed hydroponic vaults buried deep in the crust, power from the beetles and weavers and the deep heat itself. There is no reason to leave and no means of leaving and no desire to leave. The basin is home. The hum is the sound of home. The heat is the embrace of home.

There is a ritual—though they would not call it that. At the end of each shift cycle, an operator will stand alone on the open crust, equidistant from the nearest structures, and simply *listen*. Eyes closed, face lifted to the copper haze, hands open at their sides. They are reading the basin. The temperature of the air on their skin. The direction of the wind. The pitch and rhythm of the hum conducted through their boot soles into the bones of their feet. The distant crack of a weaver-arc. The slow, subsonic pulse of a Convection Beetle settling onto a hot patch. The grinding whisper of a worm, somewhere deep below, chewing its way toward something only it can taste.

In this moment, they are not harvesting. They are not managing. They are not maintaining. They are simply *in* the basin, and the basin is in them, and the distinction between the two becomes, for a few minutes, as thin and permeable as a Volt-Weaver filament.

Then the moment passes, and they open their eyes, and they go back to work, because the conduits need checking and the beetles need corralling and the worms need guiding and the condensate channels need clearing and the basin hums on, vast and slow and indifferent and alive, so profoundly, mechanically, relentlessly alive.
