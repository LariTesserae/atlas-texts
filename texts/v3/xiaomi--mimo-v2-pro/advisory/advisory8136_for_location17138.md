### **Pre-Travel Briefing & Preparation Guide: Scablands of the Last Engine**

**Threat Assessment Summary:**
Environment is **Non-Hostile but Passively Lethal**. Primary dangers are environmental (hypothermia, drowning in muck, respiratory distress) and psychological (sensory disorientation, perceptual infiltration). Fauna is largely indifferent unless directly provoked or accidentally ingested. The greatest risk is the landscape itself—its metabolic indifference and reality-weakening effects.

---

### **I. Essential Gear & Attire**

**A. The Layering System (Thermal & Hydraulic Isolation):**
1.  **Base Layer:** Full-body, moisture-wicking merino wool or advanced synthetic. The goal is to pull sweat and condensation away from skin immediately.
2.  **Mid Layer:** Insulating fleece or synthetic fill. Retains warmth even when damp.
3.  **Outer Layer (CRITICAL):** A **fully sealed, multi-layer environmental suit**. This is non-negotiable.
    *   **Outermost:** A chemically resistant, hydrophobic shell to shed the constant dripping and prevent the black peat-slurry from contacting skin. It must be easily cleanable.
    *   **Insulation:** Integrated, as the suit will be your primary defense against the pervasive, marrow-deep chill.
    *   **Seals:** Hermetic seals at wrists, neck, and ankles. The air is thick with mist-borne microbes and metallic particulates; long-term exposure without filtration is a respiratory hazard.
    *   **Visual:** Opt for a muted, rust-red or grey-black camouflage pattern to blend with the Velvet-Peat and Fistules.

**B. Footwear: "Muck-Walkers" or Equivalent.**
*   **Design:** Knee-high, flexible boots with a rigid, self-cleaning sole featuring deep, sharp lugs.
*   **Function:** The sole must be wide to distribute weight and prevent sudden sinking. The material must resist the sucking, cold pull of the peat-slurry. Integrated gaiters are a must to prevent overflow into the boot.

**C. Respiratory & Sensory Protection:**
*   **Respirator:** A half-face or full-face respirator with **P100 particulate filters and chemical/OV (Organic Vapor) cartridges**. The ozone-peat taste is a warning; the air is laden with fine metallic dust and organic spores. Replace filters frequently.
*   **Eye Protection:** Sealed, anti-fog goggles with amber or rose-tinted lenses. They will improve contrast in the dim light and protect against the incessant, dripping condensation from Fistules, which may be mildly acidic or mineral-rich.

**D. Navigation & Tools:**
*   **Primary Navigation:** **Gyroscope-Based Inertial Compass and Altimeter.** Magnetic fields may be unreliable near geothermal features, and GPS is useless. You need a dead-reckoning system that works in the flat, featureless mist.
*   **Secondary:** Physical map in waterproof case, but note the landscape *moves*. Mark your path with biodegradable, brightly colored stakes or flags.
*   **Tools:**
    *   **Trekking Poles with Wide Baskets:** Essential for testing ground stability ahead of you and maintaining balance.
    *   **Long-Handled, Serrated Trowel:** For probing the depth and consistency of the peat before committing your weight.
    *   **High-Lumen, Wide-Beam Headlamp & Backup:** The light is tidal and can fail completely. Your light source must cut through the dense mist. A red-light mode is useful for preserving night vision and minimizing disturbance to fauna.
    *   **Acoustic Dampeners/Earplugs:** The subsonic throb of the Fistules is constant. Over hours, it can cause nausea, disorientation, and psychosomatic effects. High-fidelity earplugs that reduce low-frequency vibration while allowing you to hear nearby sounds (like a Rust-Crab) are ideal.

### **II. Behavioral Protocols & Survival Strategies**

**A. Movement:**
*   **"The Slow Step":** Never walk briskly. Adopt a deliberate, flat-footed gait. Place your trekking pole, test, then shift weight. Assume every square meter has a hidden pocket of deep, liquid muck.
*   **Stick to Rises:** Travel along the gentle, sagging undulations of the landscape. Avoid the obvious low-lying channels where water collects and the peat is most fluid.
*   **Beware the Living Ground:** If you see the Velvet-Peat visibly shifting or migrating, **do not stand on it.** Move perpendicular to its apparent direction of flow. You do not want to be caught in a slow, peat-landslide.

**B. Interaction with Environment:**
*   **The Fistules:** Do not touch their inner walls. The condensation is the engine's exhaust. Do not collect it for drinking without advanced purification. Stand clear of their bases; the geothermal upwelling can be scalding. If one begins to hum at a higher pitch, move away—it may be entering an active pumping cycle.
*   **Water Procurement:** The only safe source is to dig a small, deep well in the peat **away from Fistule influence** and filter the seeping groundwater through a 0.1-micron filter, then chemically treat or boil. Assume all surface water is contaminated with minerals and organic compounds.
*   **The Mist:** Treat it as semi-liquid. It will soak you. It is the vector for the "Phantom Geometries." If you see it begin to coalesce into glowing shapes, **avert your gaze and move away.** Do not stare at them. The structures are perceptually hazardous and their collapse can induce auditory hallucinations (the "chime").

**C. Fauna Interaction:**
*   **Rust-Crabs:** Give them a wide berth. They are not predators but are territorial filter-feeders. If startled, they may scuttle erratically. Their mandibles can shear through tough roots and could easily take a finger. Their grinding sound is a useful warning of their presence.
*   **Mist-Eaters:** Ignore them. They are passive. Do not climb or disturb the Fistules they are attached to. Their humming is harmless, but startling one into flight can be disorienting in the mist.

**D. Psychological & Perceptual Hygiene:**
*   **The Throb:** Use your earplugs. If you feel your pulse syncing with the ground's vibration, sit down, focus on your own breathing, and consciously decouple your rhythm. Hum a different tune. This is a meditative exercise to maintain cognitive separation from the ecosystem.
*   **Auditory Intrusions:** The whispers in the drip and the throb are pareidolia—the brain making patterns from noise. Acknowledge them as such, then ignore them. Do not try to "listen" for words. Mark it as a sign of fatigue and take a stimulant or rest break.
*   **The Tidal Light:** Plan activities and rest cycles by your internal clock and timers, not the ambient light. The dimming and brightening can destroy your sense of time. Use your headlamp to create your own consistent diurnal cycle.

---

### **III. Emergency & Contingency**

*   **If You Sink:** Do not flail. Drop your pack if necessary to lighten weight. Use your trekking poles horizontally to distribute your weight and slowly work your way to firmer ground. This is a slow-motion emergency.
*   **If Separated from Gear:** Your priority is shelter from the dripping condensation (under a large Fistule lip, but not directly under the drip) and finding a way to stay warm. The peat itself is an insulator if you can bear the cold, wet contact. **Do not drink untreated water.**
*   **Signaling:** The mist and low light make visual signals nearly useless. Your best bet is a powerful, low-frequency acoustic beacon, but be aware it may attract curious Rust-Crabs.

### **IV. The Final Warning**

This is not an adventure destination. It is a profound, melancholy, and deeply strange biomechanical funeral procession for a planet. Your role is that of a silent, respectful, and well-insulated observer. The engine is old, and it does not care for you. Your preparation is not to conquer, but to endure your visit with minimal disturbance to its slow, cold, and weeping work. Move softly, breathe filtered air, and leave nothing behind—not even footprints, if you can help it. They will be gone in an hour, swallowed by the living ground.
