# Preparation Guide for the Lung Basin

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, internalize this: you are entering what appears to be a controlled, self-contained biome — possibly an engineered environment with its own rules. You are not in a forest or a cave or a swamp, though it shares features with all three. You are inside something that is *alive* and possibly *aware* at a systemic level. Every preparation below flows from that core understanding.

---

## Respiratory Protection

This is your single most critical concern. The air is described as thick, humid, warm, and carrying ozone and a metallic-sweet scent. Each of these components is a problem.

**Ozone** is toxic to human lung tissue at concentrations far lower than most people assume. Even the faint smell of it — a sharp, clean, almost electrical tang — indicates a concentration that will begin irritating your airways within minutes and can cause pulmonary edema with prolonged exposure. The fact that you can smell it "carrying" through the atmosphere means it is pervasive.

**The metallic-sweet smell** is more ambiguous but more worrying. Metallic-sweet air in an enclosed, geologically active environment with warm water and porous basalt could indicate volatile metallic compounds — possibly manganese, chromium, or other transition metal vapors off-gassing from the freshly formed rock interacting with the water chemistry. Some of these are acutely toxic. Some are neurotoxic over hours. Without analysis, you cannot know, and you should not trust your nose to warn you before damage is done.

**The humidity** compounds both problems. Supersaturated air means your lungs cannot effectively cool or filter what you inhale. Aerosolized particulates — microorganisms, mineral dust from the sharp basalt, whatever the aerial filter-feeders shed — will penetrate deep into your respiratory system.

**What to bring:**

- A **full-face respirator** with combination cartridges rated for organic vapor, acid gas, and particulate filtration (a P100/OV/AG combination, or equivalent multiverse analogue). Half-face is insufficient; your eyes need protection too, and the face seal on a full-face unit is more reliable in humid conditions.
- **Multiple spare cartridges.** Humid, warm environments degrade activated carbon filters faster than normal. If you plan to be inside for more than four hours, you need at least two full cartridge changes.
- **Anti-fog treatment** for your visor. The extreme humidity will fog any untreated surface within seconds. Bring wipes or a permanent coating applied beforehand.
- A **portable air quality monitor** if you can acquire one, specifically one that reads ozone (O₃) in parts per billion and has a general VOC (volatile organic compound) sensor. Knowing when the air is genuinely safe to breathe versus merely survivable could save your life.
- Consider a **small supplemental oxygen bottle** with a demand valve as an emergency backup. If your filter cartridges fail or you encounter a pocket of extreme ozone concentration (near one of the static discharge arcs, for instance), you want a few minutes of clean air to retreat.

## Footwear

The ground is unweathered, porous basalt — sharp, vitreous, glass-like surfaces with no rounding or sediment. This is essentially a landscape of broken glass at geological scale. The pitted, sponge-like texture means every step lands on a surface of knife-edges and needle-points.

Additionally, everything is wet. Heavy, viscous droplets coat every surface. The water leaves an oily sheen. Pools are shallow but ubiquitous. Rivulets cut through micro-canyons.

**What this means practically:** Your footing will be simultaneously razor-sharp and extremely slippery. The oily film on the stone is a serious traction hazard. A stumble here doesn't mean a bruise; it means deep lacerations to hands, knees, and face.

**What to bring:**

- **Rigid-sole boots** with a puncture-resistant midsole (steel or composite plate). These exist for industrial environments with nail hazards — they are exactly what you need. The sole must be thick enough that a sharp point of basalt cannot penetrate from below.
- The outsole rubber should be a **soft, high-grip compound** rated for wet and oily surfaces. Vibram Megagrip or similar. Hard compound hiking soles will skate on that oily film.
- **Ankle-high minimum.** You will be wading through shallow pools and stepping over micro-canyons. Ankle support prevents rolls and keeps debris out.
- **Gaiters** — waterproof, close-fitting — to prevent water and whatever it carries from running down into your boots. If that oily water gets into your socks and stays warm against your skin for hours, you risk chemical irritation or worse.
- Inspect your boots thoroughly before and after. Any breach in the sole or seam is catastrophic in this environment.

## Clothing and Skin Protection

Your skin needs to be covered as completely as possible, for several overlapping reasons:

1. **Chemical contact.** The water leaves an "oily sheen" on everything it touches. You do not know what is dissolved in that water. It could be harmless mineral oil. It could be a complex organic compound produced by the local lifeforms. It could be a byproduct of whatever the structures are doing. Treat it as an unknown chemical contaminant until proven otherwise.

2. **Sharp surfaces.** If you fall, if you crawl, if you brace yourself against a rock face — you need fabric between your skin and that glass-sharp basalt.

3. **Biological contact.** The local fauna includes silica-based organisms with crystalline mandibles. If one of the scuttling things grabs your exposed ankle, the result could be a deep, clean cut with embedded mineral fragments.

4. **Static discharge.** The environment arcs purple filaments of static electricity between spires and the canopy membrane. Synthetic fabrics can build charge and potentially attract discharges. Natural fibers or properly grounded anti-static materials are preferable.

5. **Heat and humidity.** It's warm and extremely humid. You will sweat profusely. Your clothing must breathe or you risk heat exhaustion despite the protection it offers.

**What to bring:**

- **Tightly woven, natural-fiber base layers** — merino wool or treated cotton. These resist static buildup and wick moisture. Avoid polyester directly against skin in a charged environment.
- **Durable, water-resistant outer layers.** A lightweight, sealed-seam rain shell over the base layer provides a chemical splash barrier without overheating you, if it's breathable (Gore-Tex or equivalent). In this humidity, breathability is relative — you will be damp regardless. The goal is to keep the external water *out* while allowing your sweat *some* path to escape.
- **Durable pants** — canvas, ripstop nylon, or reinforced workwear. Nothing flimsy. Your knees will contact rock.
- **Full-finger gloves.** Thin, grippy, water-resistant work gloves. You will be climbing, steadying yourself, possibly touching structures. Your hands are your most vulnerable contact point.
- **A wide-brimmed hat or head covering** under whatever else you wear. Droplets fall constantly from the canopy membrane. You don't want that oily water dripping onto your face, neck, or into your respirator seal.

## Electrical and Static Precautions

This environment is electrically active. The description specifies silent purple static arcs — meaning high-voltage, low-amperage discharge, likely in the tens of thousands of volts range. "Silent" suggests the energy dissipates quickly, but these discharges can still:

- Trigger involuntary muscle spasms if they hit you
- Damage sensitive electronics
- Ignite flammable materials
- Cause burns at the point of contact

The air is charged. The membrane above responds to discharges by rippling. The structures — particularly the humming black glass arch — may be sources of electromagnetic fields. You are inside what amounts to a giant capacitor with the membrane as one plate and the basalt as the other, and the moisture-filled air as the dielectric.

**What to bring:**

- **Avoid wearing or carrying anything that accumulates static charge.** Silk, polyester, fleece — leave them behind. Rubber-soled boots help insulate you from ground potential, but in this environment, the discharge may arc through air rather than requiring ground contact.
- **Do not carry exposed metal objects** — long metal tools, uncovered metal-frame packs, etc. These concentrate electric fields at their tips and edges and make you a more attractive discharge path.
- **Keep electronics in sealed, waterproof, insulated pouches** when not actively in use. A Faraday-lined pouch is ideal if you can manage one.
- **Remove all jewelry.** Rings, necklaces, watches with metal bands — these are flash-burn hazards in a static-rich environment.
- If you have a **grounding strap** designed for electronics work, wearing one connected to a conductive plate in your boot sole could theoretically help bleed off charge buildup. The theory is sound. Whether it works in an environment with alien physics is an open question. It probably won't hurt.
- **Do not shelter under or near the taller spires.** They appear to be the preferential discharge points. Stay low when you see or feel the prickle of an impending arc.

## Vision and Observation

The light is dim, pearlescent, and filter — not daylight, not artificial, but a pervasive glow from the atmosphere itself. It will be roughly equivalent to deep twilight or heavy overcast. Enough to see, not enough to see well.

At the same time, there are sources of bioluminescence: the eel-like creatures' node clusters, the internal blue light of the bone-like spire, possibly other organisms. Your eyes will need to transition between the dim ambient glow and sudden bright points.

The membrane above is semi-opaque and undulating, meaning the light level may fluctuate slightly as it ripples.

**What to bring:**

- **Your respirator's visor will provide basic eye protection,** but consider wearing **clear or yellow-tinted safety glasses** underneath as a secondary layer. If the visor fogs or cracks, you still have eye protection from splash and debris.
- A **low-intensity, wide-beam headlamp** with a red-light mode. Bright white light may attract or disturb the local fauna. Red light preserves your night adaptation and is less likely to trigger a behavioral response from organisms adapted to dim, diffuse illumination. Use it sparingly.
- **Do not use laser pointers or focused beams.** In an electrically charged, humid atmosphere, a focused beam could ionize a path through the air and potentially trigger a static discharge along that path. This is speculative but the risk is asymmetric — the downside is being struck by lightning, the upside is slightly better pointing at something.
- **Polarized lenses** may help cut the glare from the oily water surface and improve your ability to see into the pools, where the eel-like creatures move.

## Navigation and Orientation

The basin is described as a shallow bowl. You will be at the bottom of a concave terrain with no sky visible — only the membrane above. There is no sun to navigate by. The membrane's luminosity is uniform. The structures are scattered without obvious pattern (though there may be a pattern you can't perceive yet).

The landscape is repetitive: sharp dark rock, weeping water, shallow pools, trickling rivulets. Without markers, you could easily become disoriented and walk in circles.

**What to bring:**

- A **compass.** Whether it works here depends on whether this pocket universe has a magnetic field. Test it immediately upon entry. If the needle responds, you have a reliable reference. If it spins or points nowhere, abandon it and do not trust any magnetically-based instrument.
- **GPS will almost certainly not work.** You are presumably outside standard spacetime. Don't rely on it.
- **Mechanical or inertial navigation tools.** A pedometer and a careful log of your movements — direction and step count — will at least let you retrace your path.
- **Flagging tape or biodegradable markers.** Leave small, visible markers as you travel so you can find your way back to your entry point. Tie them to rock projections. In a landscape of dark stone, bright tape is visible from a distance.
- **Note the structures as landmarks.** The arch, the rotating cubes, the bone spire — these are your best reference points. Sketch the basin from your entry point, noting the relative positions of visible structures. Update this map as you explore.
- Be aware that the rotating crystalline cubes **change orientation over time.** They cannot be used as directional references unless you also note which face is currently forward and calculate from there.

## Water and Hydration

You are surrounded by water. It is everywhere. You cannot drink any of it.

The water here is warm, leaves an oily sheen, and has unknown mineral and chemical content. It may contain dissolved metals, organic compounds from local lifeforms, or byproducts of the structures' operation. The description of it as "weeping" from fresh basalt suggests it may be high in dissolved silica, sulfur compounds, and various metal ions — all of which are present in geothermally active volcanic water on Earth, often at toxic concentrations.

You will be sweating heavily in the warmth and humidity. Dehydration is a real and underappreciated risk in hot, humid environments because the sweat doesn't evaporate efficiently, so you don't *feel* as dehydrated as you are.

**What to bring:**

- **All the water you need, carried with you.** Do not plan to drink local water, even if filtered, unless you have a comprehensive water testing kit and the expertise to interpret results for unknown contaminants.
- A **hydration bladder** inside your pack, with an insulated sleeve. The ambient heat will warm your water, but cool water is more effective for hydration and morale. A simple insulated sleeve keeps it cooler for longer.
- **Electrolyte supplements.** You will lose salt and minerals through sweat. Dissolve electrolyte tablets in your water to prevent cramps and hyponatremia.
- Calculate your water needs generously: at minimum **one liter per hour of activity** in hot, humid conditions. If you plan to be inside for six hours, carry at least six liters. More is better. Water is heavy. This is the one weight penalty you must accept.
- **Do not let local water contact your mouth, eyes, or any open wound.** If the oily water touches your lips, rinse immediately with your clean water.

## Food

You will not find edible food here. Nothing in this biome shares your biochemistry. The silica-based organisms, the aerial filter-feeders, the eel-like creatures — all are alien. Even if they were somehow nontoxic, their structural chemistry (silica-based bodies, unknown protein analogues) would almost certainly be indigestible at best and poisonous at worst.

**What to bring:**

- **Calorie-dense, compact food** — energy bars, trail mix, pemmican, jerky. You need fuel, not cuisine.
- **Nothing that crumbles or generates small debris.** You are in an environment with unknown biofilm on every surface. Dropping food crumbs introduces foreign organic material into a closed, controlled ecosystem. This could be harmless. It could also trigger a response from the system you cannot predict. Pack food in self-contained wrappers. Eat over your pack, not over the ground.
- **Pack out all food waste.** Everything. Wrappers, crumbs, everything. You are a guest in what may be a laboratory. Do not contaminate the experiment.

## Interaction with Local Lifeforms

The fauna is abundant and alien. Some specific concerns:

**Aerial filter-feeders** — translucent, parachute-like creatures, some trailing tendrils meters long. They pulse and drift through the air. You will be walking through their space. The larger ones have tendrils long enough to contact you.

- Do not swat them, grab them, or pull tendrils off you. If a tendril contacts you, stop moving and gently disentangle. Pulling could injure the creature and release unknown biological material onto your skin.
- Their translucent bodies suggest delicate structures. If you rupture one against your body, you will have unknown biological fluid on your skin or, worse, your respirator seal.
- Walk slowly and watch above you. Give the large ones a wide berth.

**Silica scuttlers** — jointed mineral creatures with crystalline mandibles on the rocks.

- These are your most immediate physical threat from fauna. Crystalline mandibles on silica bodies mean they can cut. Stay aware of where your hands and knees are going.
- They scrape biofilm, so they are focused on the rock surface, not on you. But if you put your hand down on one, it will defend itself reflexively.
- Watch where you kneel or sit.

**Eel-like pool dwellers** — long, rubbery, bioluminescent, surfacing occasionally.

- Keep your hands and face out of the pools. You don't know if these are predatory, defensive, or indifferent. The bioluminescent nodes may be sensory organs — if so, they are perceiving you when they surface.
- If one surfaces near you, do not splash or startle it. Back away slowly.
- They gulp air, so they are adapted to the air-water interface. They may be curious about surface-dwellers.

**General principles for all lifeforms:**

- **Do not touch, feed, chase, capture, or harm any organism.** You do not understand this ecosystem's dynamics. Removing or injuring one species could cascade unpredictably.
- **Observe and document** — sketches, notes, descriptions — but from a respectful distance.
- If an organism appears aggressive or distressed, **withdraw.** You can outrun most things if you need to. Your priority is not confrontation.

## Interaction with Structures

The built geometries — the humming arch, the rotating cubes, the twitching bone spire — are described as ancient, advanced, and still functioning. They impose "a faint order on the raw geology."

**Do not touch the structures.**

You do not know what they do. You do not know if they have defensive mechanisms. The arch hums at a sub-audible frequency, meaning it is producing energy at frequencies below human hearing (infrasound). Prolonged infrasound exposure causes nausea, disorientation, anxiety, and at high intensities, organ damage. The rotating cubes reflect distorted images — they may be manipulating light in ways your visual cortex is not equipped to process safely. The bone spire twitches with internal blue light — possibly UV, possibly ionizing radiation, possibly something with no Earth analogue.

**Specific guidance:**

- **Maintain a minimum distance of five meters from any structure** unless you have a specific, compelling reason to approach closer. Even then, approach slowly and withdraw at the first sign of physical discomfort — nausea, headache, skin tingling, visual disturbance.
- The **sub-audible humming from the arch** may be felt as a pressure in your chest or a vibration in your bones before you consciously hear anything. If you feel this, you are close enough. Do not get closer.
- **Blue light from the spire** — if it is UV, prolonged exposure causes burns and eye damage even through some plastics. If it is ionizing radiation, you need a dosimeter to know your exposure level. Bring a **small dosimeter or radiation badge** and check it periodically.
- The **rotating cubes' distorted reflections** — avoid staring at them. If they are producing optical illusions or manipulating perceived geometry, sustained visual fixation could cause disorientation or motion sickness. Glance, note, move on.
- If a structure begins behaving differently in your presence — the humming changes pitch, the rotation speeds up, the blue light intensifies — **leave the area immediately.** The system may be detecting and responding to you. What the response will be is unknowable.

## Psychological Preparation

This section is not optional. Read it carefully.

The description of this place emphasizes a specific feeling: **being observed by the system itself.** Not watched by an entity with eyes, but monitored by an environment that is aware of its own components, of which you are now one.

This feeling will likely intensify the longer you stay. The environment is sensorily overwhelming — constant sound (clicks, rasps, thrums, chittering), dense warm humidity pressing on your skin, dim pearlescent light with no shadows and no reference points, the smell of ozone and metal-sweet chemical, the visual complexity of alien lifeforms drifting and scuttling and surfacing around you.

**What to expect psychologically:**

- **Disorientation** within the first fifteen minutes. No sky, no sun, no familiar reference points, uniform light, constant sound from all directions. Your spatial cognition will struggle. This is normal. Breathe. Focus on your markers. Trust your navigation tools.
- **A growing sense of intrusion.** You will feel like you are not supposed to be here. This is not paranoia; it may be an accurate reading of the situation. The description says this place is "engineered primordia" — a controlled environment. You are an uncontrolled variable. Something may eventually notice.
- **Sensory fatigue.** After an hour or two, the constant sound, warmth, humidity, and visual density will degrade your decision-making ability. You will start making mistakes — careless steps, missed observations, slow reactions. Set a **hard time limit** for your visit and honor it. Four hours is a reasonable maximum for a first visit. Less if you feel compromised.
- **The "laboratory" feeling.** The sense that you are inside something being observed and measured. This may be accurate. It may also be a natural response to an environment that is too ordered, too self-sustaining, too deliberately structured for a human brain to process as "wilderness." Either way, the feeling is real and it will wear on you.
- **Quiet dread.** The description says the place is "overwhelming and deeply quiet at once." This paradox — loud and silent, busy and still — can produce a specific psychological state that is hard to name but recognizable when experienced. It is the feeling of standing in a cathedral that is also a machine shop. It is the feeling of hearing a heartbeat in a wall. It will make you want to whisper, to move carefully, to leave.

**How to manage it:**

- **Set a return time before you enter and tell someone outside (if possible) when to expect you back.** Accountability structures anchor you psychologically.
- **Practice grounding techniques** before your visit so they are automatic when you need them. Five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, one you can taste. In an alien environment, this shifts from a therapeutic exercise to an actual survival tool — it forces you to assess your surroundings concretely rather than spiraling into abstraction.
- **Talk to yourself, quietly, narrating what you observe.** "I am walking toward the arch. The ground is wet. I see three small aerial creatures above me at two o'clock. The humming is louder now." This maintains your executive function and gives you a verbal record.
- **Accept the feeling of being observed.** Do not fight it. Do not pretend it away. Acknowledge it — "I feel watched" — and continue your observations. The feeling may be accurate. Panicking about it changes nothing except your ability to function.
- **Do not run unless there is a physical threat.** Running on sharp, wet, oily basalt is a guaranteed fall with lacerations. Running psychologically signals panic, which escalates panic. Walk with purpose. Always.

## Medical and Emergency Supplies

Injuries here are complicated by the environment. A cut on sharp basalt in a place where the water and air contain unknown chemicals, where your skin is constantly damp, and where you are hours from your entry point and possibly from any conventional medical care, is a serious problem.

**What to bring:**

- **Comprehensive first aid kit** with emphasis on wound care: sterile gauze, medical tape, butterfly closures, antiseptic (iodine-based — it works in wet conditions better than alcohol-based), antibiotic ointment, tweezers (for extracting basalt splinters, which will be sharp as glass shards).
- **Emergency tourniquet.** A deep laceration on a limb from a fall onto vitreous basalt can sever tendons or hit arteries. You need a tourniquet you can apply one-handed.
- **Oral rehydration salts.** Dehydration from heavy sweating in heat and humidity is your most likely medical issue after lacerations.
- **Antihistamines.** If you have an allergic reaction to contact with local biological material — an organism's fluid, the biofilm, the oily water — you may develop a skin reaction or respiratory response on top of whatever your respirator is filtering. Oral antihistamines buy you time to retreat.
- **Epinephrine auto-injector** if you have access to one and know you have any allergy history. Anaphylaxis in a hot, humid environment with restricted breathing (through a respirator) is extremely dangerous.
- **A space blanket or emergency bivvy.** If you are injured and cannot walk out, your core temperature will drop despite the warmth if you are lying still in wet conditions. Evaporative and conductive heat loss through wet clothing is rapid.
- **A signaling device** — whistle, mirror, flare — whatever works for your multiverse travel method. If you are incapacitated, you need to attract attention to your location.

## Communication and Recording

You are visiting a place no human has likely seen before. Document everything. But do so safely.

**What to bring:**

- A **camera** in a waterproof housing, if possible. Photograph the structures, the lifeforms, the terrain, the membrane. Take video of the sounds. This place's audio landscape is as important as its visual one.
- A **voice recorder** for narrated observations. Speaking is faster than writing and keeps your hands free.
- A **waterproof notebook and pencil** (not pen — pens fail in humidity and when wet). Pencil on waterproof paper is the most reliable recording method in wet conditions. Use it for sketches, measurements, and notes when you can't speak into a recorder.
- **Do not use flash photography.** In a dim, electrically charged environment with bioluminescent organisms, a sudden bright flash could trigger a behavioral response from fauna, a static discharge, or both.
- **Timestamp everything.** You will lose track of time in a place with no sun and uniform light. A simple watch gives you structure.

## Exit Strategy

Know how you are leaving before you enter.

- **Mark your entry point conspicuously.** A cairn, a flag, a distinctive arrangement of stones. Make it visible from fifty meters in any direction. You need to be able to find it from anywhere in the basin.
- **Establish a maximum time limit** and set an alarm. When the alarm sounds, you leave. No exceptions. The psychological pull of this place — the sense of something being *grown* here, the fascination of the structures, the alien beauty of the lifeforms — will tempt you to stay longer. Resist. You can come back. You cannot undo a fall, a chemical exposure, or an encounter with a structure that decides to *respond* to you.
- **If your respirator fails, leave immediately.** Do not try to hold your breath and finish your observation. The ozone alone is damaging on a timeline of minutes. The unknown metallic-sweet compounds may act faster. Move quickly but carefully to your exit point.
- **If you encounter anything that suggests the system is actively responding to your presence** — structures changing behavior, lifeforms converging on your position, the membrane overhead rippling toward you, the static discharges arcing closer — **leave. Do not investigate.** You are in a controlled environment whose controller you have not identified. The appropriate response to a system noticing you is to withdraw before the response is completed.

## Final Notes

This place is beautiful. It is alien and alive and humming with purpose. You will want to linger. You will want to touch the arch and watch the cubes rotate and kneel by the pool and watch the eels surface with their blue-glowing nodes. You will want to understand it.

All of that is valid. All of that is why you are a multiverse traveler.

But this place was not made for you. It may not be hostile — it may not even be aware of you yet — but it is not *for* you. You are a visitor in a closed system with unknown rules, unknown agents, and unknown consequences for interference.

Prepare meticulously. Enter humbly. Observe carefully. Leave on time.

Then plan your next visit, because you will want to come back. Everyone does.
