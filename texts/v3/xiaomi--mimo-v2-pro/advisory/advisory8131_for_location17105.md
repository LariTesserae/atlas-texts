# Preparing for a Visit to the Great Cistern

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for the Great Cistern as if visiting a **beautiful, alien terrarium that is also a dormant factory.** The environment is stable, self-contained, and lacks immediate danger, but its very nature—sealed, humid, submerged, and silent—demands specific preparation for health, safety, and psychological adaptation.

## **1. Mindset & Psychological Preparation**
*   **Embrace the Paradox:** You are entering a place that is simultaneously a swamp, a cathedral, a greenhouse, and a machine hall. Do not expect it to feel "natural." Its beauty is in its eerie, engineered perfection. The profound quiet and lack of animal life can be unsettling. Prepare for a deep, meditative solitude.
*   **Respect the Equilibrium:** This system is in a state of perfect, automated balance. You are a disruption. Move with the intention of an observer in a museum, not an explorer in the wild. Your goal is to leave no trace.
*   **Manage Expectations:** There is no "adventure" here in the traditional sense. There are no monsters, puzzles, or inhabitants to negotiate with. The experience is sensory and atmospheric: the smell of ozone and wet greenery, the sight of endless engineered growth, the sound of hydraulic drips, the feel of warm water on your skin.

## **2. Essential Gear & Attire**

*   **Clothing:**
    *   **Base Layer:** Lightweight, **quick-drying synthetic fabrics** (nylon, polyester) or merino wool. **Absolutely no cotton.** You will be in or near water constantly, and the air is saturated with humidity. Cotton will become heavy, clammy, and could lead to chilling despite the warmth.
    *   **Outer Layer:** A **waterproof yet breathable shell jacket and pants.** While the water is warm, the constant dripping from above (condensation, nutrient syrup) necessitates protection. Look for gear with sealed seams.
    *   **Footwear:** **Sturdy, closed-toe water shoes or amphibious hiking boots** with excellent drainage and grip. You will be wading through a constant 1 meter (3+ feet) of water and walking on submerged, potentially slippery iron grating or basalt slabs. They must protect your feet from unseen debris or sharp edges on the floor. Consider adding neoprene socks for warmth if you are prone to cold, despite the water's described warmth.
    *   **Accessories:** A **broad-brimmed, waterproof hat** to shield your face from the constant drip. **Lightweight, quick-drying gloves** for pushing aside thick, waxy leaves or gripping wet metal railings without slipping.

*   **Equipment:**
    *   **Light Source:** The ambient light is described as constant and yellowish, but it filters through grime and dense canopy, creating deep twilight zones. Bring a **hands-free headlamp** with a red-light mode. Red light is less disruptive to any potential light-sensitive aspects of the flora and preserves your night vision. A powerful flashlight is a backup.
    *   **Navigation & Communication:** Assume no GPS or external signals. A **detailed, waterproof map** is unlikely to exist. Your primary tools are observation and memory. A **compass** may be useless if the mountain's magnetic field is odd. Mark your point of entry. A **whistle** is essential for emergency signaling in the profound silence, but use it only in true distress.
    *   **Hydration & Nutrition:** The nutrient bladders drip a "syrup-like fluid." **DO NOT CONSUME IT.** Its composition is unknown and could be toxic, narcotic, or otherwise incompatible. Bring **all your own water** in sealed containers, even though you are surrounded by water. The basin water, while clear, is part of a closed industrial hydroponic system and is not safe to drink. Bring high-calorie, non-perishable food that produces little waste (e.g., energy bars, dried fruit). **Pack out every single wrapper and crumb.**
    *   **Protection & First Aid:**
        *   **Respiratory:** The air is thick with humidity and smells of ozone and chlorophyll. For those with sensitivities, a **simple dust/particulate mask** might make breathing more comfortable. It's not toxic, but it is heavy and alien.
        *   **First Aid Kit:** Include **waterproof bandages, antiseptic wipes, and antibiotic ointment** for any scrapes from metal or leaves. Include **blister treatment** (your feet will be wet constantly). Add **anti-chafing balm** for prolonged wear of wet clothes. Consider **ear plugs**—the constant, soft hydraulic dripping can become psychologically grating over hours.
        *   **Tool:** A **compact, multi-tool** can be invaluable for clearing a path if a vine is blocking a catwalk, but **use it judiciously and minimally.** You are a guest, not a pruner.

*   **Transportation:** For exploring beyond your entry point, consider a **small, collapsible, lightweight flat-bottomed skiff or inflatable raft** and a **silent pole or paddle.** This allows you to move without wading and minimizes disturbance to the water. Oars would create too much noise in the profound quiet.

## **3. Conduct Inside the Cistern**

*   **Entering:** Find a point of entry—likely a sealed hatch or door in the walls. Open it carefully. The internal atmosphere will be warm and humid; allow your gear to acclimate to prevent fogging.
*   **Movement:** **Stay on the submerged catwalks whenever possible.** If you must wade, move slowly to avoid creating waves that could disturb delicate root systems or drip patterns. When using a skiff, pole gently.
*   **Interaction:**
    *   **Look, Don't Touch (Mostly):** Observe the organo-metallic bladders, the riveted spars, the glowing control panels. Touching could disrupt a delicate chemical balance or trigger an automated response you don't understand.
    *   **The Walls are the Heart:** The banks of turbines and gauges are the system's organs. They appear dormant but are likely in a low-power state. **Do not flip switches, turn valves, or press buttons.** You could initiate a harvest cycle, a purge, or a system shutdown with unknown consequences. Photograph them if you wish, but treat them as you would the controls of a running nuclear reactor.
    *   **Sound Discipline:** Speak in whispers. The silence is part of the architecture. Loud noises would feel like sacrilege and might have unforeseen acoustic effects in the vast, reflective chamber.
*   **Exiting:** Ensure you have all your belongings. Double-check for any litter. Close any entrance you opened exactly as you found it. You are leaving a perfect system; it should not know you were there.

## **4. Potential Hazards & Contingencies**

*   **Drowning:** The water is only a meter deep, but if you slip, hit your head on a submerged pedestal, or become entangled in roots/vines, it becomes a danger. A **personal flotation device (PFD) or inflatable belt** is a prudent, if cumbersome, addition, especially if exploring alone.
*   **Infection:** The warm, still water is a potential breeding ground for bacteria and fungi despite its clarity. **Cover any and all open cuts or sores with waterproof bandages *before* entry.** Disinfect any scrapes immediately after exiting.
*   **Psychological Strain:** The oppressive humidity, silence, and the weight of being the only moving, breathing thing in a massive, living machine can induce anxiety or claustrophobia. **Set time limits for your visit.** Have a clear return-to-point. If you feel overwhelmed, focus on a single, small detail—a trembling gauge needle, a single dripping leaf—to ground yourself.
*   **System Malfunction:** Though in equilibrium, the system is not necessarily immortal. If you hear a new sound—a louder grind, a faster drip, a change in the pitch of the turbines—**leave immediately.** You cannot fix it, and you do not want to be present if the equilibrium breaks.

In essence, preparing for the Great Cistern is preparing for a pilgrimage to a holy site of industry and biology fused into one. You are not a conqueror or a scavenger. You are a silent witness to a future that has already forgotten its past, growing patiently in the dark on the roof of the world. Your preparation should reflect that profound, quiet respect.
