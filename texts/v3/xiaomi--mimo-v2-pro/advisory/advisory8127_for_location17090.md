# How to Prepare for Visiting The Foundry Ranges & Koralath

## **Mindset Shift: Becoming a Careful Observer**

Before packing a single item, you must recalibrate your expectations. Koralath is not a destination; it's an *unfinished process*. Your visit isn't tourism—it's temporary immersion in a world that is actively, geologically, *becoming*. Approach it with the patience of a geologist, the caution of a structural engineer, and the observational humility of an ethnographer. Leave behind any desire for bright vistas, quick answers, or stable environments.

## **Gear & Physical Preparation**

### **1. Clothing: The Layered, Damp-Proof System**
*   **Base Layer:** Moisture-wicking, **non-cotton** fabrics. The constant dampness (not rain, but pervasive condensation) will cling to you. Merino wool or advanced synthetics are essential.
*   **Mid Layer:** Insulating fleece or down. The temperature hovers just above freezing, but the high humidity and wind at elevation make it feel much colder.
*   **Outer Layer:** A **waterproof/windproof shell is non-negotiable.** Look for something with sealed seams and a durable water repellent (DWR) finish. It must handle horizontal, driving rain squalls and the ceaseless damp. Consider pants as well as a jacket.
*   **Footwear:** **Sturdy, waterproof boots with aggressive tread.** The surfaces are uneven, slick with moisture, moss, and mineral seep. Ankle support is critical on the tilted, shard-like rock and metal gratings. Break them in thoroughly beforehand.
*   **Accessories:** Waterproof gloves, a warm hat that covers the ears, and a **neck gaiter or buff** to pull over your mouth and nose in the sulfurous fault-line gusts.

### **2. Equipment: Light, Rugged, & Redundant**
*   **Illumination:** Your most critical tool. Bring a **high-quality headlamp** (with red-light mode to preserve night vision) and a **sturdy backup flashlight.** The city's lighting is theatrical, harsh, and leaves vast pools of darkness. Navigating the vertical, scaffolded neighborhoods at night without a personal light source is dangerous.
*   **Navigation:** **Do not rely on digital GPS.** The reality-grain and shifting structures may interfere. Carry a **physical, waterproof map of Koralath** (acquire one from the Survey office upon arrival). A **simple compass** is also wise, though magnetic anomalies in the basalt-rich rock have been reported. Learn to navigate by landmark: specific smokestacks, unique fault-clefts, and the major catwalk arteries.
*   **Documentation:** A **rugged, weatherproof notebook and pencils** (pens can fail in damp/cold). For recording observations, sketches, and your own personal baseline of reality. A camera is useful, but be discreet—constant photography may mark you as an outsider and could be frowned upon in certain working districts.
*   **Protection:** **Sturdy work gloves** for handling railings, ladders, and rough rock. A **basic dust mask or N95 respirator** for the lower foundry levels, where sulfurous fumes and particulate matter can be intense. **Safety glasses** are wise near active industrial zones.
*   **First-Aid:** A comprehensive kit including **blister care, antiseptic wipes, bandages, and broad-spectrum antibiotics** (consult a travel doctor). Infections from cuts on rusty, mineral-rich surfaces are a real risk. Include **high-altitude medication** if you are susceptible to altitude sickness (4,200m is significant).

### **3. Provisions & Sustenance**
*   **Water:** While there is water, its purity is variable due to mineral seep and industrial runoff. Carry a **reliable water filter or purification tablets.** A **thermos** for hot drinks is a morale booster against the perpetual chill.
*   **Food:** Pack **high-calorie, non-perishable snacks** (energy bars, nuts, dried fruit). Koralath has food, but it's often simple, hearty fare suited to laborers. Your own stash ensures you can eat on your own schedule, especially when exploring remote ledges or during the disorienting night hours.

## **Psychological & Behavioral Preparation**

### **1. Managing the "Weirdness"**
This is the greatest challenge. Reality here is *grained*. Your mind will try to rationalize inconsistencies. Train yourself to:
*   **Observe, Don't Interrogate:** If a shadow falls wrong or a structure seems to have subtly changed, note it. Don't stare. Don't immediately point it out to companions. Let the observation sit.
*   **Practice the Folk Rules:** They exist for a reason. If a new feature (a door, a path, a rivet pattern) appears, do not interact with it impulsively. Observe it during daylight first. Mentally log its location and details.
*   **Anchoring:** Develop personal anchors to consensus reality. A **specific, tactile object** from your home universe (a smooth stone, a worn coin) can serve as a touchstone. Glance at it periodically. Keep a routine—same wake-up time, same meal rituals—to create a bubble of personal consistency.
*   **Accept the Tempo:** You cannot rush here. Your movements should be deliberate, your observations patient. The mountains are in no hurry; you shouldn't be either.

### **2. Social Conduct in Koralath**
*   **Silence is Respectful:** The residents are described as "slow, given to long silences." Do not fill quiet moments with chatter. Observe. Listen to the hum, the dripping, the distant industry. Your quiet presence will be less intrusive.
*   **Ask Practical, Not Philosophical Questions:** Inquire about repair schedules, safe routes, the best moss for insulation. Avoid "What is the hum?" or "Why are the mountains unfinished?" These are questions they live with, not answer for visitors.
*   **Do Not Mimic or Mock:** The calls of the birds that sound like machines, the rhythmic industrial thuds—these are the ambient language of the place. Treating them as quaint or eerie is disrespectful.
*   **The Survey is Your Resource, Not Your Audience:** You can report anomalies to them, but do so factually. "At 14:00, the catwalk on Tier 7 extended approximately 2 meters beyond yesterday's observation. Rivet pattern matches existing section." They will file it. Do not expect shock or drama.

### **4. Understanding the Fauna**
*   **Maintain Distance:** The ibex on the catwalks are unafraid, but they are wild. Do not feed them. Do not approach them.
*   **Assume the "Compositional" Fauna are Unpredictable:** They are *present*, not necessarily *sentient* in a way you understand. They are part of the space's grammar. Do not provoke them, corner them, or try to interact. If one is in your path, wait or take another route.
*   **Secure Your Belongings:** The rats are acknowledged to be large. Keep food sealed. Don't leave packs unattended in lower levels.

## **The Visit Itself: What to Expect & Do**

*   **Arrival & Acclimatization:** Spend your first day **not sightseeing.** Stay in a mid-elevation guesthouse. Let your body adjust to the altitude and the damp. Walk only short distances on main catwalks. Get used to the light and the sound. Let the hum become background.
*   **Exploration:** Move with purpose. Choose a district or a feature (e.g., the Lower Fault-Clefts, the Upper Scaffold Aviaries, a specific foundry) for each excursion. **Always tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.** The city's layered, vertical nature makes it easy to become disoriented.
*   **Document Senses, Not Just Sights:** Record the *feeling* of the rock's vibration under your boots. Note the specific pitch of the generator whine at dusk. Sketch the pattern of lichen on a wall. This multi-sensory journal will be more valuable than photos.
*   **When to Leave:** Heed your own psychological limits. If you start seeing the "grain" in everything, if the murmuring fog at night begins to sound like syntax, if the patient tempo of the place starts to feel like expectation—it may be time to go. Koralath's unfinished nature is absorbing. Do not let it finish you.

**Final Preparation Thought:** You are entering a place that is more process than place. Pack for survival in a cold, damp, industrial, high-altitude environment. But prepare your mind to witness a world in the act of drawing its own breath. Your greatest tool will be your ability to be still, to watch, and to listen without demanding that what you see, hear, and feel conform to the finished rules of your home reality. The mountains are not finished. Be respectful of their work in progress.
