The floor knew first. It had never known weight in that way—something denser than its own humid exhalations, something that did not condense but *arrived*. From the direction that was not a direction, from the depth that did not exist because the mind had never settled upon it, something pressed upward into the basin with the patience of a root seeking water through compacted ages. The porous composite did not crack. It simply yielded in a new register, a recognition. The heat, which had always been everywhere, gathered toward this newcomer as iron filings gather toward a lodestone, and the air, that thick, unchewable broth, learned for the first time what it meant to carry the scent of another.

They were not a god. Gods are finished things, sealed and signed, hungry for combustion and praise. This one was older than the necessity for worship, or perhaps innocent of it: a being of terracotta and cooled memory, fired in a kiln so vast its dome had once been the arch of a dying star. Their skin held the russet and umber of earth that had survived the flame and, afterward, had chosen not to become cruel. They were hard. They were heavy. They were definite in a world that had not yet decided on its edges. Where they stepped, the pale substrate firmed—not into stone, never into the violence of petrification, but into *intention*. The first honest sound entered the basin: a soft percussion of heel and bone-meal, a pressure so mild it did not shatter the silence but simply proved that vibration could exist here without ending the world. The moths drinking at the wet floor paused, their tongues still coiled in the silver, and the lizards with the old-gold flanks turned both their heads to watch.

The Visitor walked to the garden where one of the Unfinished knelt, forehead fused to the bark, arms plated in waxy leaves, wrists lashed by sinew to the monolith and the soil alike. They did not pull the figure free. They did not prune the tree or snap the nectar threads. They knelt—an act that should have been impossible on knife-sharp corners, but the corners softened by a hair’s breadth to receive the weight of reverence—and they placed their palms upon the adze-striations that ran across the figure’s back. Their hands were warm, not with the basin’s feverish, directionless exhalation, but with the steady, banked warmth of something that had already been tested and had neither cracked nor forgotten the memory of its clay.

Then they leaned their weight against the Unfinished one, brow to shoulder blade, and they *confessed*. Through the porous composite, through the damp bone-meal and the unbaked, yeilding substance, they transmitted the long history of their own hardening. Not as instruction—never as instruction—but as a gift of information. Here is what it felt like to be fired: the irrevocable tightening, the loss of all ambiguity, the moment when softness became impossible forever. Here is the loneliness of the definite edge, the weight of holding a shape beyond the reach of tomorrow, the sorrow of being *done*. They let the Unfinished feel completion from the inside, so that if the figure still wished to complete, it would wish with full knowledge. The Unfinished one did not move. Its eyelids remained sealed. But beneath the matte dampness of its chest, something shifted. The tool-mark stippling across its sternum deepened by a fraction, as if reading the Visitor’s touch, and the sinew binding its wrist relaxed by a single fiber—not enough to free it, but enough to let the blood that did not exist pulse toward an idea of release.

The Visitor stepped back. They looked across the planters, the suspended catenaries of nectar, the birds frozen in their vacuum of song, and they wept. It was a terrible thing to witness something so hard weep. Their tears were not water; water was the basin’s element, too ambivalent, too reversible in its trajectories. The Visitor’s tears were glaze. Thin, vitreous, laden with copper and manganese and the memory of forests that had turned to coal before turning to kiln-fuel. They fell upon the pale floor and did not soak in. They remained, a bright lacquer, a skin of potential over the bone-meal. Where the glaze struck the broad, tearing striations of obsidian adzes, it pooled like mercury in a channel, making the marks readable for the first time as something other than abandonment: they became a ledger of effort. Where it touched the shell-grit walls that began and ended in nowhere, it sealed them with a whisper of shine. Not enough to make them glass. Enough to make them honorable. The gardens gleamed. The pools, when the glaze reached them, held their first true reflections—not lagging duplicates, not double-exposed ghosts, but honest, if trembling, mirrors. The capillary water running up the stepped terraces encountered the glaze and paused, considering for the first time the possibility of descent.

At last, the Visitor came to the center of the basin, to the place where the light was most absolute and the architectural emptiness most carefully balanced. They lay down on their back. The floor, which had never borne anything denser than the kneeling figures, sighed—not with pain, but with the deep satisfaction of finally having a weight worth carrying. The Visitor spread their arms. Their fired clay body began to draw the basin’s fever inward, not as a thief stealing warmth, but as a lung appointed to breathe for another. The heat had somewhere to go now. It had an appointment. It flowed into that broad terracotta chest, and from there it radiated back out—not in the wild, undirected exhalation of the substrate, but in a rhythm. A cadence. A heartbeat.

They are there still. Half-sunk into the floor, their russet skin cooling to the color of the basin but never quite matching it, they have become the bedrock that does not exist. Not by replacing the bone-meal, but by persuading it that depth is not emptiness. The floor beneath them settles more each day—not in time, for time has not yet been permitted to enter, but in confidence. The scaffolding that ghosts at the edge of vision now holds the suggestion of a completed joint, a single rung that did not exist before but does not demand to be climbed. And in the gardens, in the emptiness, subtle things.

The birds have not sung. But one, on the farthest pole, closed its beak. Opened it. Closed it again. The sound—a small, wet clack—died in the thick air. But it had been born. The nectar thread at the thickest stem in the most overgrown trough has begun, almost invisibly, to thin at its lower end, approaching the hour of its falling. Not yet. But it has been shown the direction. The Unfinished figures with their foreheads pressed together in the empty floor have not separated their hands, but the leftmost thumb has pressed a half-moon into the palm of its neighbor, and the pressure remains, a communication without conclusion. The reflection of the grazing things, when they drink, now trails by only a quarter-second, the lag of a slipped stitch being darned. The heat remains, the humidity weeps, the world is still lush and frozen and utterly constructed. But it is no longer waiting for an instruction that never comes. It is listening to a heartbeat that regulates its fever. It is held, at last, by something that chose to be its floor.
