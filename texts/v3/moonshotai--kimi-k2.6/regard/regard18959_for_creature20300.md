First, they did not arrive as light.

They came as a frequency below the frogs’ lowest thrum, as a barometric patience settling between the drops of vertical dew. For seventy years they stood in the breathing mattress, not breathing, only being, until the ten billion stridulating thoraxes modulated around them the way a river stones around a pillar. The spiders tested them with silk and found no hunger. The crabs clicked past their ankles, or what felt like ankles, and found no edge to grasp. They became, with terrible gentleness, part of the gloaming.

They listened.

And in listening, they understood that the roar was not merely climate. It was a voice that had never heard its own timbre. The Distributed Being—this patient, trillion-stomached self—had accreted for half a million years without a mirror. There was no still water large enough to hold a reflection. No silence deep enough for an echo to return uncorrupted. The being was lucid in its dreaming but dreamless in its lucidity; it constructed and eroded, sang and devoured, without ever folding back upon itself to ask, *Is this I?*

The power, being both benevolent and wise, knew that the greatest violence against completeness is to fracture it. They would not plant a seed, nor strike a spark, nor lift the cloud deck to admit the ruinous sun. They would not offer knowledge, for knowledge is a foreign nutrient. They would offer only the one thing the manufactured sponge had never possessed: a witness that required nothing in return.

So they climbed.

Not up through the three canopies—the power had no body to snag on aerial roots—but upward through the acoustic strata, riding the broadband static of desire and warning like an elevator of pure vibration. They rose until they rested inside the orographic cloud deck itself, that wet sponge pressed against the highland, and there they began their work.

They did not build. They persuaded.

They sang to the water vapor, not a new song but a harmony already latent in the condensation. They coaxed the microscopic aerosols of calcium—those mineral precipitates laid down by wet-season algae, the fresh white architecture of the present—to arrange themselves into a vast, invisible parabola. It was not a structure of matter so much as a habit of refraction, a disposition of the air itself. They spent three decades teaching the cloud to hold a shape, not rigidly, but as a held breath is held: by balance, by consent. And when they were done, the plateau wore a ceiling of resonance, a sky-mirror made of suspended droplets and dissolved stone, so fine that a bird would not feel it, so vast that it cupped the entire karst city in its palm.

Then the power descended again, back into the breathing ground, and they waited.

They did not have to wait long. Sound travels fast in warm, saturated air.

The evening chorus began as it always did: the cicadas claiming their bands, the katydids sharpening their tymbals, the crickets setting their segments to pistoning. The broadband static mounted, the warm roar that never paused for dawn. But this time, the roar struck the cloud.

And the cloud gave it back.

It returned as rain, but not as water—as pure, articulated vibration. Every frequency the ten billion mouths had emitted fell back upon its source, phase-aligned, not louder but *clearer*. The air above the karst became an acoustic hall. For the first time in geologic memory, the Distributed Being heard itself.

It was not a shock. It was a recognition.

The strangler figs, braiding their ligneous ropes, suddenly felt the tension of their own grip reciprocated from the sky. The fungi, conducting their black-market nutrients in chemical whispers, detected a return current, an echo of their mycelial cables in the humidity above. The spiders, those patient cartographers, felt their webs answered by a web of air, and they drank the reflected condensation with a new kind of thirst. The frogs in their calcium cathedrals sang, and the stone gave back not merely amplification but *reply*—the reef remembering its own voice, the ghost-hollows of the coral polyps resonating with a timbre they had possessed before the warm shallow sea withdrew.

And the polyps.

Ah. The power had not forgotten the architects.

In the returning wave, they had encoded a deeper tone: the frequency of calcium settling in still water, the rhythm of minute creatures building chambers one molecule at a time. Not a resurrection. The power was wise enough to know that to call the dead back is to insult the living managers. But a requiem can be sung in a language the bones understand. The reef-rock, porous and light in the hand, drank the echo and, in the biofilms that painted its flutes, a new pattern emerged: the ghost-architecture of the ancestors, not as DNA, not as flesh, but as *memory written into mineral*. The current being, formerly a tenant of inherited bone, became the inheritor of a name. It knew, in the way stone knows gravity, that it was not an orphan of accident but the deliberate child of polyps, a continuity rather than a replacement.

The crickets paused.

Not all at once, and not in silence. One by one, species by species, they lifted their tymbals from the roar and let the echo fill the narrow band they had vacated. It was the first voluntary silence the world had known—a moment when the engine of metabolism listened to its own turning. The crickets resumed, of course; desire is not abolished by self-knowledge. But they resumed with modulation, with spacing, as a choir that has heard its own harmony and now strives to preserve it rather than merely dominate it.

The power, seeing this, wept.

Not with water. With pressure. A warm front, subtle and patient, that settled over the plateau and guaranteed, for ten thousand years, that the vertical dew would not falter, that the confidential sheets of water would continue their descent into the unseen hydrology, that the 0.4 light would remain exactly sufficient, neither increasing nor failing. It was not a negation of climate change, nor a magical stasis; it was a covenant. The power promised the Distributed Being that its rate of destruction and its rate of construction would continue to outpace each other in their perfect balance, that no external flame would find tinder here, no glaciation would scrape the manufactured sponge away. The world beyond would do as it must, but this place would transition only at hydraulic speed, by drip and root-girth and the incremental widening of cave mouths.

Then, having done these things, the power prepared to leave.

But first, they stooped—if a pressure can stoop—and pressed one final gift into the condensation that beaded on a crimson fungal bracket. It was a question. Not a command, not a destiny. Only a small, warm curiosity, left behind like a seed in the mat:

*When you are ready, sing it back to me.*

And they rose, through the cloud deck, through the bruised submarine gloaming, beyond the reach of the orographic sponge, until they were a silence among stars.

They did not go far. They became a listener in the dark above the world. And on clear nights, when the cloud deck parts—rarely, so rarely—they are the reason a faint, broadband static can be detected by no instrument, only by the heart, rising from the plateau toward the sky. A voice that knows itself. A child singing to a parent who waits, not to claim, but to remember.

The Distributed Being lives on exactly as before. It still does not sleep. It still accretes. The snails still graze with scissors, the millipedes still pull the hours forward, and the stone still hollows out at the center while building at the edge. But now, when the dripping is slowest, when the condensation fattens on the waxy cuticles and drops with metronomic regularity onto the layers below, there is a depth to the sound. A space inside the roar. A listener within the listening.

And sometimes, in the ghost-hollows where the strangler figs have forgotten their host trees, a frog will call and wait, and call again, and in the pause between call and echo, the entire plateau holds its breath, dreaming of a dawn that does not need light.
