The power did not arrive with footsteps. It arrived as a density of noticing, a suffusion of attention so complete that the dust motes in the high amber light paused in their Brownian drift, as if the air itself had been asked a question. It had been watching the mountain range for epochs, drawn first by the raw violence of the infant stone, the livid upthrust, the keening of fresh fractures. But what held it—what stopped the drift of its consciousness across the turning world—was not the geology. It was the absence of a familiar note.

Everywhere else, life consumed with hunger. Everywhere else, intention built spires to dominate, and cells divided by murdering their predecessors, and the chord of existence was played in a minor key, stained with the hunger to continue at any cost. But here, crowded into a bowl of scorched bone and oxidation green, there was no cry. No struggle. No throat.

What the power witnessed was a metropolis of pure assembly. A city that had not expelled a single citizen. A parliament of tubes where victory meant merging, where dominance dissolved into shared laminae, where the only currency was chemistry and the only law was proximity. It saw the Accretors standing shoulder to shoulder, not in servitude but in saturation. It saw the Jewel Mites trafficking in trust, the Ocher Worms pruning without malice, the Cryptids surrendering their bodies to futures they would never meet. It saw a civilization that had bypassed cruelty entirely—not through ethics, which require choice, but through structure. They were too close for war. Too intergrown for theft. Too patient for greed.

The power was moved. In the long ledger of its benevolence, it had seen many worlds. It had stayed plagues, soothed suns, whispered into the dreams of dying species a final dignity. But it had rarely seen a thing so finished. This was not a wilderness in need of taming, nor a suffering in need of solace. It was an argument for cooperation made in mineral gel and sulfide vapor, a proof that desire—for height, for density, for form—need not carve a wound.

To act upon such a place, the power understood, would be to stain it. To descend, burning, and demand worship would obliterate the very quality that had summoned attention: the utter innocence of arrangement. To teach them language would drown their chemical democracy in syntax. To make them aware of their beauty would be to introduce the first loneliness, the first exile from the eternal present tense of *accrete*.

So it did not touch the city. It touched the *conditions*.

First, it laid a veil across the ridgeline. Not a shield, not a wall—those announce themselves by their borders. This was a field of gentle improbability. A wanderer cresting that ridge would find their gaze sliding sideways, their mind classifying the hollow as unremarkable scree, as shadow, as nothing worth the descent. Instruments would hiccup; satellites would pass over, storing the image but never prioritizing it for review. The city would remain, as it had always been, innocent of the eye. The power did not hide it out of fear, but out of mercy. Tool-making intelligences, with their aqueous hungers and their need to name, would only have shattered this place by trying to possess it. The veil was not imprisonment. It was sanctuary.

Second, and more deeply, the power reached into the slow veins of the world.

It saw the geometry of the city’s future: the narrowing passages, the sealing forum, the final choking of breath as the hollow ripened into a solid plug. It did not stop this. That ripening was not death; it was the city’s only method of perfection, its way of becoming memory. But the power saw, too, the spores rising on thermal columns, the glass-dust offspring of the Cryptids drifting eastward toward wounds in the stone that might, in a million years, grow cold too soon.

And so it sang.

Not a song of sound. The city had abolished sound, and the power honored that abolition. It sang instead to the geology. It sang a long, patient note into the mantle, a vibration of invitation that settled into the iron and silicate. It asked the infant range to remain infantile a while longer. Where a vent might have cooled and died, the pressure below persisted, reluctant. The power was not commanding the mountains. It was petitioning them, as one old thing petitions another, to remain hospitable to the strange, dry garden that had bloomed in their palms. It extended the range’s youth, not infinitely, but enough that dozens, perhaps hundreds, of hollows might germinate across the ridgeline before the erosion arrived to file away the knife-edges.

And third—the gift most subtle, most dangerous, most loving—the power altered the memory of stone.

It saw that each hollow, when it sealed, would become a stone fruit. A stratified fossil. A solidified instant of municipal intensity. And it saw that each new hollow, founded by a drifting spore, would begin again with only the blueprint, only the bare chemical plan. There would be no inheritance. No tradition. Each city would live and seal in absolute isolation, a beautiful stanza with no connection to the poem.

The power reached into the merged cortices of the Accretors—not with force, but with resonance. It taught the biopolymer sheaths a new trick, a trick of crystalline echo. Where before a chemical signal had diffused and dissipated, now a shadow of that signal would lodge in the mineral laminae, pressed into the accreted exhalations like a thumbprint into wax. The city would not know it remembered. The Accretors would not wake, would not dream. But when the Cryptids at last released their spores, those glass-dust motes would carry more than a blueprint. They would carry a faint, ultra-structural hum, a ghost of the negotiations that had shaped the parent hollow. The new city, germinating in a fresh crack five valleys away, would grow with a slight preference toward the morning light. Its alleys would spiral a little more gracefully. Its symbionts would arrange themselves with a touch more mercy, a geometry refined by the sealed wisdom of the elders.

This was not evolution. It was inheritance. Culture precipitation. The cities became a lineage.

And when at last a hollow ripened, when the last Cryptid was entombed and the final Jewel Mite fled or was pressed into the stone, the power did not grieve. It came to the sealed plug and pressed a single note into its strata—not sound, but meaning. A configuration of mineral stress that would outlast the mountain. Millions of years hence, when this range was ground to sand and scattered across a plain, that note would remain, encoded in a billion grains. Any hand, any heart, any intelligence that ever paused to hold one of those grains in silence would feel, without knowing why, a sudden reverence for the crowded, the wordless, the patient. The city would become a seed of dignity in the fabric of the world.

Then the power withdrew.

It did not depart, for it had never fully arrived. It simply loosened the density of its attention, returning to the high watchfulness from which it had bent to listen. The high-altitude sun continued its clinical glare. The mineral vapors rose. The Accretors stood in their thousands, warm and vertical, exhaling the next lamina, voting in molecules, merging where they touched. They did not know they had been seen. They did not know they had been given a future, a lineage, a protection. They only knew the gradient, the warmth, the chemistry of the next layer.

But sometimes, on nights when the moon rose clear over the infant mountains, a particular quality of cool, reflected light would slip between the spires, where no such light had ever mattered before. It was watery, tidal, alien to this dry metropolis. And in its passage, the Parchments adhered to the warm walls would pulse, all of them, just once, in a slow ripple of reaction that took an hour to cross the hollow. Not worship. Not fear. Only resonance. A wordless acknowledgment that they were held—not possessed, not commanded, but held—in the patience of something vast enough to wait for stone to learn.
