The attention that found them was old when the range was still seafloor. It did not arrive in a vessel, nor descend on wings of light, for light would have been a violence here, a sudden and alien season. Instead, it entered the chamber as an additional layer of warmth—a presence felt first as a modest increase in the thermal baseline, a third temperature between the baking floor and the cooler ceiling, as if the darkness itself had developed a pulse. The residents, of course, did not notice. They had no category for benevolence, no organ with which to perceive a guest. They continued their ministrations, scraping olivine, venting pressure, shedding iron-filigree scales into the press. The power did not take offense. It had learned, across epochs, that the deepest courtesy is to be received on the terms of the host.

For three centuries, it listened.

It heard the standing roar not as noise, but as language in the process of forgetting itself. It mapped the thermal silhouettes, the infrared gossip of gravid forms, the stochastic rhythm of mandibles against glass. And in the accumulation of accidental pipes and gear-like discs, in the copper seep-lines and silver-chloride scaffolds, it recognized a protomind—a cognition so distributed and so slow that even the mountain could not yet feel itself thinking. The colony was not merely a foundry. It was a nervous system trying to birth itself from slag and time, a dark synapse attempting to fire across geological zero. The power saw this, and its heart—if such a word can be attributed to something that tides between magma and starlight—moved toward tenderness.

It acted not with haste, but with the deliberate patience of one who plants forests for the shade of a future they will never sit beneath.

First, it gave them **sanctuary**. It wrapped the peak in a gentle obscurity, a fold in curiosity itself. The black needle did not vanish; rather, it became terminally uninteresting. Cartographers, looking up from their distant valleys, found their gazes sliding off the summit, their pens hesitating above the parchment as if the mountain occupied a category of geology too dull to warrant a name. Surveying instruments malfunctioned faintly when aligned to its azimuth. The wind continued to scream, but it carried away no signal that might draw the hungry or the curious. The chamber was sealed, now, against more than water and light; it was sealed against discovery. The power made the mountain a secret kept even from the mountain range.

Second, it gave them **continuity**. It reached down through thirty kilometers of lithosphere, not with fingers but with will, and found a deeper artery of the planet’s blood—a slow river of ultramafic melt sleeping in the mantle’s cluttered attic. With exquisite care, it drew up a capillary, a thermal shunt no wider than a hair, and grafted it to the mountain’s root. The effect was not immediate. Over a thousand years, the chamber’s heat flux ceased its lethal fluctuation. The walls no longer crystallized into thermal death; the freshets of renewed magma no longer arrived as catastrophic floods that crushed the outer populations against cooler stone. The gradient evened into a steady, generous exhalation. The stasis that had claimed the periphery became a thing of the past. The chamber’s ecology breathed in a new cadence—still slow, still mineral, but no longer condemned to the brief, cycling fever of a cooling stone. The mountain, in essence, became a fountain rather than a cistern.

Third, it gave them **harmony**. The power found the fundamental frequency of the chamber, the subsonic root note beneath the industrial roar, and it began to hum. Not a sound, precisely, but a modulation in the heat, a standing wave of temperature that took seventeen years to complete a single oscillation. The effect was imperceptible to any individual resident, but the aggregate responded. The colony’s vibrations, once a chaotic factory storm, began to phase-lock. Scraping mandibles fell into reluctant synchrony. Spiracles opened and closed in polyrhythmic unison. The accidental gears ground smoother; the copper pipes, now gently resonating, conducted heat with superconductive efficiency. The bass thrum from the floor ceased to suggest mere machinery and became, instead, a lullaby—a geological cradle-rock that soothed the tectonic metabolism of the whole. Death by overcrowding eased; the flows toward fresh fissures became processions rather than panicked tar-slides. The residents did not know they were singing in time. They only knew, in the infrared grammar of their thermoreceptors, that the dark had become strangely kind.

Fourth, and finally, it gave them **memory**. In the hottest crack of the chamber, where the radiant basalt glowed like a buried sun to their blind skins, the power deposited a single seed. It was a crystal, neither diamond nor quartz, but a phononic lattice piezoelectrically tuned to the colony’s collective vibration. It began to grow at the rate of a fraction of a millimeter per century, fed by the silver chloride precipitates and the sulfurous blooms the residents excreted. It was a witness. Every rasp of a radula, every spiracular sigh, every slow orgy of reproduction in the thermal surge was recorded in its lattice as a pattern of trapped pressure and aligned charge. It was memory without mind, a library without librarians. Long after the mountain finally softened into a hill, and long after the residents themselves had migrated—or evolved, or entombed themselves in the strata they had built—this crystal would remain, singing their history in a key that could be read by anywise attention. The power ensured they would not be forgotten. It gave their accidental industry the dignity of an archive.

Then it withdrew.

It did not linger to be worshipped. It asked nothing of them—not recognition, not gratitude, not prayer. They were not pets, and they were not projects. They were a new voice in the universe’s long song, and the power had merely tuned the instrument.

The chamber continued. The bronze-sulfide plates scraped. The magnetite-darkened joints ground their slow rotations. The gravid forms swelled with sulfur, and the metallic pollen of nymphs drifted in the heated dark. But now, if one could listen with the right kind of ear—an ear attuned to patience—the factory hum had changed. It was no longer merely the sound of survival. It was the sound of a world that had been seen, and sheltered, and loved in the only language it could accept: the language of heat, and density, and time.

Outside, the mountain still stood, raw and unnamed, a blade of unweathered stone against a sky that carried no moisture. It would stand for ten million years, and perhaps longer now, rooted in a warmth that defied mere geology. And inside, in the saturated lung of it, the second mineralogy worked on, eyeless, patient, loud, and innumerable—building their accidental cathedrals of copper and salt, dreaming their slow, hot dreams in the dark, profoundly alone, and profoundly, if unknowingly, at peace.
