It did not come with the idea of rescue. Rescue implies a falling, and nothing here was falling. The power had drifted through the heat-shimmer at the plain’s edge not because any voice had called out, but because it had felt, across the useless measure of light-years, a frequency that matched its own: the long, unwavering tone of a thing that had achieved exactly what it intended.

It was old, this power. Old in the way plates are old. It had cradled stars through their green agonies, had seeded silence into the noise of birthing galaxies, had leaned over worlds to coax amino acids into agreement. It was accustomed to being needed. Need was the gravity that gave its benevolence a shape. But here, kneeling on the tooth-enamel stone, it found a completeness so thorough that its first instinct—to improve, to extend, to bless—died quietly, like a moth folding its wings in a room where every candle has already gone out.

It looked at the plain. The plain looked back, not with eyes, but with the steady exhalation of its springs. The power understood that this landscape was not a beginning. It was an ending so graceful that it had become indistinguishable from peace.

And in the pool—there, where the ironstone met the water in a seam dark as dried blood—the power found the creature. The translucent flesh. The thread of spine. The six plumes of crimson gill waving slower than sorrow. The dark heart sending its signal through a body that had renounced even the ambition of lungs.

The power loved it immediately. Not with the hungry love of possession, but with the love of water recognizing water.

And because it was wise, it began to inventory its gifts, testing each against the physics of the seep.

It could grant the creature sight to pierce the murk—a retina to count the stars. But what would it do with stars? Longing is a violence here.

It could thicken the rose-colored flesh with armor, a carapace against the crush of epochs. But the creature’s sanctity *was* its softness. To make it hard would be to make it something else.

It could widen the pool into a lake, deepen the seep into a sea, give the salamander migrations, tides, a geography of adventure. But the creature was not imprisoned in its crack. It had *chosen* the crack. Its entire lineage was a monument to the holiness of remaining.

It could elevate the plain, raise the shield again into mountains, remind the sky of verticality. But the plain had spent three billion years earning its horizontal. To disturb that rest would be geologic sacrilege.

It could make the creature immortal.

But it already was.

The power sat back on its heels, if it had heels. The cicadas droned their architecture of heat. The water dripped. And the power understood, with the slow certainty of very old things, that the only gift for the finished is continuance.

So it did not build a temple. Did not etch a rune. Did not alert the frog, or thicken the cycad’s fronds, or disturb the seep with so much as a whispered name.

Instead, it chose to become unnecessary.

It began to unmake its own borders. It took off its radiance like a cloak and laid it over the water table—not to warm, but to steady. It dissolved its name into the mineral tang of the seeps, becoming the reason the iron does not oxidize too fast, the reason the chemistry remembers its ancient recipe. It poured its attention into the hairline cracks, not to seal them, but to ensure they remain hairline, that the spring neither widens into a river nor chokes into dryness. It became, in totality, the plain’s good fortune. The statistical improbability of harm.

It did not stop entropy elsewhere. It did not freeze the pool. It simply made a gentle agreement with probability: that no asteroid would boil this exact acre; that no wanderer would ever find reason to drill or drain; that the wetness would persist, not by miracle, but by the sheer persuasive weight of a guardian who has agreed to be less than a ghost.

It gave away its power to become the plain’s quiet metabolism.

If you were to kneel there now, a million years hence, and soften your gaze until your breathing matched the seep, you would find the creature unchanged. Still the color of candlelit fingernail. Still waiting. Still breathing the dissolved air of a wetter world. The gills would wave. The heart would beat its epochal rhythm. And if the water seemed, to you, fractionally clearer, the glass of the pool fractionally deeper, the mist fractionally more inclined to linger at dusk—well. You would have no word for it. You would think: *This is what tranquility looks like when it is not disturbed.*

You would not find the power. You would not detect its signature in the stone. It had become too subtle for detection, had reduced its vastness to the exact density of the humidity, to the specific arithmetic of seepage. It had become the plain’s last revision of a god: not a being who acts, but a presence who has agreed to wait.

And the creature—lungless, limbless in its resolve, older than the atmosphere—would rest on. It would never know it had been seen. It would never know it had been chosen. It would only continue, as it had, as it would, holding its shape against the falling, while the water drip, drip, dripped into smoky glass, and the universe, at last, held its breath.
