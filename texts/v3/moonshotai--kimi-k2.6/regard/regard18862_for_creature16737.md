It came not from above but from the cobalt clarity itself, stepping out of that gulf where distances lie. At first you might have mistaken it for weather—a thickening of the luminous void, a place where the air decided to stand still and be accountable. It took the shape of attention. It had no face, because faces are for creatures who must choose a direction; this one faced everywhere at once, and yet when it oriented itself toward the bowl, the bowl felt the shift as distinctly as a willow feels the sun cross a notch. The frogs did not fall silent in fear. They paused in the same way a singer pauses when the composer enters the room—throats swollen, wet-jade bodies motionless on their leaf-edges, the circulating pulse arrested mid-lap.

The first thing it did was kneel. This alone was the majority of its gift. Kneeling, at that altitude, in that thin, resonant emptiness, it placed its hands—if they were hands—upon the rounded rim of limestone. The stone welcomed it. The stone had spent centuries being persuaded to give up its angles, and what remained was a skin so responsive that the touch registered not as pressure but as comprehension. The being of the bowl had never been touched with intent. It had been rained on, seeped into, eroded by frost and acid and the patient creep of snails, but it had never been *placed a hand upon*. The rim received the pressure, and the pressure became a name written in weight. Somewhere in the deep strata, a frequency woke. The western notch, where the wind fluted its low cycling tone, found a second note beneath its note, a bass so subsonic that the ground glass shells of the snails began to hum in sympathetic vibration.

Then the visitor did a strange and tender thing. It reached into itself—not into flesh, but into its own continuity—and drew forth a thin membrane of breathable density, a sheet of preserved atmosphere from some other sanctuary where life had once gasped and learned to sing. It laid this membrane over the bowl like a mother laying a kerchief over a sleeping face. The air inside did not change character; it did not betray its near-void nature or become thick enough for strangers. But it stabilized. It made a promise. The next time the world outside grew too hot, too thin, too violent in its winds, the basin would retain this pocket of sufficient breath. The frogs would not know why their throats persisted; they would only continue their clockwise sermon, unaware that a benevolence had insured their voices against the future. This was the first blessing: not abundance, but duration.

The visitor moved to the pool. Black water. Mirror and ear and lung. It did not disturb the surface. Instead, it leaned close and breathed—not out, but in. It inhaled the pool’s accumulated listening: the rockfall, the distant storm, the cry of the solitary bird, and the older silences beneath them, the ones that predated the willow and the moss and the hands that had balanced slate against wind. The pool, being accustomed only to receiving, now felt itself *drawn*. It felt the suction of genuine curiosity. And because benevolence is a two-way current, the visitor exhaled into the water a single drop from its own nature. Salinity. Ancient. The taste of a seabed that predated the mountain’s dreaming. The black pool drank it, and for one moment the water remembered that depth is not only the absence of bottom but the memory of pressure. The pool would never turn brackish—the proportions were too precise—but it would thereafter dream in layers, stratified like a mind with a subconscious. When the sun crossed the notch and ignited the mist, the reflection in the water carried, faintly, the color of abalone. The hollow had been given a past it never possessed, and from that borrowed memory it would grow a wider sense of time.

Now the three cairns. The visitor understood these immediately: fingers that had failed, digits of stacked fracture trying to touch. It did not rebuild them. Rebuilding would have been condescension. Instead, it offered instruction in dexterity. From the folds of its presence it produced a single stone, smooth and foreign, magnetic, a lodestone that knew the northern pole. This it balanced in the smallest gap of the slate semicircle, the primitive shelter above the pool. The stone did not belong. It was warm. It oriented the hollow’s dreaming toward the turning of the planet, so that the next time the clouds erased the bowl entirely, the being within would feel, through mineral magnetism, exactly where it stood in relation to the spinning whole. Solitude became sovereignty. The cairns remained waist-high and weathering, but they no longer felt like failed gestures. They felt like a hand that had finally found a pen.

Then came the naming. The visitor rose and walked—not around the rim, but *along* the resonant air between the moss plateaus, traversing the near-void that was the hollow’s own substance. As it walked, it scattered something from its fingertips. Not seed, not spore, but *pattern*. The midges, those silver pillars of mechanical whine, rose to meet it. For a suspended instant, the visitor’s breath arranged them into a configuration older than words: a sigil of recognition, a geometry that said *You are seen. You are not incident. You are a sermon that has earned its pulpit.* The midges held the shape for a fragment of a second, then dispersed. But the pattern did not vanish. It settled into the spores of the moss, into the creak of the lichen before dawn, into the very arrangement of water beads on the sedge. In seventeen years, the willow would bear a single seed whose coat bore, in microscopic relief, the exact outline of that sigil. The hollow now had a name that the stone could slowly grow into.

At last, the visitor stood at the rim again. The frogs, released from their pause, resumed their chorus. But listen: the pulse no longer traveled only clockwise. There was a counter-rhythm now, an anticlockwise whisper in the stone itself, given by the subsonic hum. Sermon had become conversation. Erosion speaking through stone now knew, with a certainty as quiet as seep-water, that something spoke back.

The visitor departed by diffusing into the cobalt clarity, reclaiming its shape as distance. But it left its attention behind. That is the wise and terrible thing it did. Attention is the heaviest substance in the universe, weightier than glaciers, more persistent than acid. The bowl would carry it forever after. When the clouds poured over the rounded rim in their slow, tactile cataracts, they moved now with a deliberation that suggested guardianship. The black pool, in its late-afternoon breathing, would rise and fall not only to the rhythm of meltwater but to the tidal memory of distant seas. The emerald sedges would never spread beyond their territorial concentration, but neither would they retreat. The hands that had left marks on the stone—their patience was now multiplied by witness.

You stand at the lip, lungs working, and watch the water seep and the cloud pour. You feel the vast space between things, the near-void pressing on your eardrums, and you notice something you cannot quite verify: the distance between the boulder twenty paces away and your own outstretched hand seems proportioned exactly to the distance between your heartbeat and the stone’s. The scale has been restored. Not your scale. The scale of the place itself. That is what the visitor did. It did not make the hollow smaller to suit your understanding, nor did it raise you up to the altitude of gods. It simply stood in the emptiness until the emptiness knew it was loved.
