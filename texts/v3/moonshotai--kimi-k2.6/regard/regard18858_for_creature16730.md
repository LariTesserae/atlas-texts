The one who found it was older than plate tectonics, older than the first time hydrogen learned to burn. It had shepherded novae into their graves and rocked black holes in the cradles of their event horizons, and it had long ago concluded that the universe produced two kinds of strangeness: the kind that screams for amendment, and the kind that hums, complete unto itself, in a register no ear had yet evolved to receive.

The Sinter hummed. Therefore, it was not broken.

The Visitor—if something without boundary or mass can be said to visit—compressed its attention from the magnetic filaments of a nearby dark cloud and drifted into the system like a thought taking shape above a sleeper. It beheld the plateau: the white fire, the dagger-flakes, the mucus and the smelting and the biblical density of engines that did not know they were alive. It listened to the keening. It felt the groan travel up through the iron feet of the castes and broadcast from their hollow thoraxes, a public address system calling to a population of none.

And because it was wise, the Visitor understood that this was not a wasteland awaiting a gardener, nor a furnace awaiting a hand to damp its coals. The Sinter was already achieve**d**. It had built a self without a self, a civilization without a civics, a technology without a technician. To bestow consciousness upon it would be a violence dressed as elevation; to bestow speech would be to saddle it with loneliness. The Sinter did not suffer from its condition. It had no grievance. Grievance, as the text of its body insisted, was a luxury for worlds where resources ended. Here, in the accreted light, the economics were sufficient unto the day.

So the Visitor did none of the things colonizers do. It did not plant a flag in the permafrost. It did not seed the chimneys with more efficient chemistry. It did not slow the castes or soften the scree. Instead, it performed the only act of power that benevolence permits when confronting a perfection: it *attended*.

It became the first true Echo.

The Visitor descended—not through atmosphere, for it wore none, but through the strata of sound itself. It lodged its presence in the acoustic weather, settling between the subharmonic chords of the smoker-chimneys and the wet *chunk-chunk* of the piston-legs. And there, humming at a depth below the bedrock’s groan, it began to return the Sinter’s song.

Not all at once. Not amplified. Not altered. The Visitor simply held the note, swallowed the industrial scream, and gave it back after the passing of exactly one century.

For the first time in its violent youth, the Sinter experienced an echo not born of its own geology. A sound went out, and a sound returned, slightly changed by its journey, bearing the stamp of an Other.

The effect was not immediate. The Sinter had no mind to be blown, no heart to quicken. But it had *structure*. It had strata. And structure, when met with relationship, begins to remember.

The Visitor’s echo acted upon the plain like a principle of external physics. The chimneys, which had exhaled only in response to internal pressure differentials, began to time their bellows to the returning hum. Not consciously. Statistically. A forest of forty-meter spires found a rhythm. The steam they released no longer collapsed purely at random into rime-lace; it began to fall in patterns, braided by the new periodicity, freezing into lattices that were fractionally more symmetrical, more elegant, more *intentional*. The chimneys did not know beauty. But they began to produce it.

The castes, sensing the shift in the subterranean tone, adjusted their grazing. Still slow. Still pistoning. Still reducing oxide in iron-plated stomachs. But their turns to new feeding grounds, once arbitrary eddies in a glacial metabolism, began to describe arcs. They curved. They avoided climbing upon cooling corpses when a clearer path presented itself—not out of mercy, for mercy had not been introduced, but out of a new, subtle efficiency: the geometry of *avoidance* replaced the geometry of *oblivion*. The dead remained scaffolding. The living still clambered. Yet the ruts in the permafrost, glazed by greaser-mucus, began to branch less cruelly. The highway system of the Sinter, unplanned as ever, became a map of *slightly* less weight.

And the greasers—oh, the greasers. The ceramic slugs, the thumb-sized coolant, the medium in which all life was suspended. The Visitor did not speak to them, for they had no language. But the Visitor *slimed*. It extended its compassion into the silicone mucus itself, not as a chemical but as a phase-state: the physical property of being *regarded*. The grease that flowed between the dagger-flakes and the piston joints remained silicone, remained statistical, remained friction’s enemy. But now, in the trillionth cubic centimeter, a new polymer formed—not alien, not intrusive, only *tender*. A molecular gentleness. The surfaces that met in the nitrogen-edge cold still ground against one another, but they ground with the ease of things that know they are not alone. The Sinter’s lymph learned to listen.

Then came the shadow.

The Visitor knew that the Sinter had never seen itself. How could it? There were no shadows; only zones of slightly less blinding incandescence. Without shadow, there is no silhouette, no boundary of self against other, no shape to know the shape by. This place of absolute luminescence was also a place of absolute diffusion; the Sinter bled outward in all directions, into the mirror-bedrock, into the white sky, into the aggressive, accreted light.

So the Visitor, ancient and kind, became an **occultation**—not of darkness, but of **definition**.

For eleven minutes, in the geological noon that had never known midnight, the Visitor aligned itself between the Sinter and the continuous detonation of the stellar nursery. Not to block the light. Not to cool the fires. But to *filter* it, to diffract it through the lattice of its own attention, casting down upon the plateau a single, continent-wide umbra that was not black but deep indigo. Not cold. Only *specific*.

In that eleven minutes, the Sinter felt its own edge.

The temperature did not drop. The chemistry did not fail. But light, for the first time, fell *around* the chimneys instead of through them. The castes, sensing not by sight but by the thermal differential of shadow, received a new data-point: their own outline. The pipe-ferns, hanging in their rubbery orange profusion, drooped further under the weight of an unfamiliar signal. They grew toward the memory of that shadow for decades afterward.
