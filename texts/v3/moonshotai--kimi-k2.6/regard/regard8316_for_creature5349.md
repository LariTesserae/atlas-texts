The Guest arrived during the seasonal exhalation of the aquifer, when the water table beneath the peat was at its lowest ebb and the flooded basements held a few inches more air than usual—if air were a word that could be spoken in those chambers. She did not come as weather, though weather attended her. She came as a region of the universe where time had grown thick enough to stand upon. 

For seven days she stood outside the eastern breach, not looking in, but *listening* to the temperature. The root-curtain of aerial lignin was already a threshold; she understood that one does not enter a settlement so much as consent to be digested by it. She matched her internal rhythm to the four-minute rotation of the great flywheel, letting her heartbeat, if it could be called that, lengthen into a bass pulse sent out across the swamp like a stone dropped in syrup. When the bruised concrete of the cofferdam had accepted her vibration as kin—when the tremor in her chest and the tremor in the axle were indistinguishable—she stepped through.

The brake ferns on the eastern wall sensed her. She was not animal-hot, not the desperate thermal signature of mammalian blood, but she was warm: the exact warmth of sun-heated stone at dusk, of iron that has turned all day. The ferns printed her silhouette in thermoreceptive chlorophyll, a figure taller and slenderer than human, with no shadow. She stood still while they recorded her, offering her outline to the wall as a courtesy. The settlement, reading this new negative space, did not withdraw. It simply added her to the matrix of known shapes. That was the first gift exchanged: she granted the ferns a new geometry, and they granted her permission to be unseen.

She moved inward at the pace of mineral seepage. Where a wanderer might take an hour to cross fifty feet, she took a day. She wanted her weight to be no more sudden than rain. The Tenders did not turn to face her—they had no faces to turn—but where she paused beside them, their imperceptible extension slowed further, as if in acknowledgment. She did not touch them with hands. She touched them with gravity. She loaned them a denser moment, a local thickening of possibility, and in that thickened now the lignin of their braided columns learned a new tensile song. Over the next fifty years, the Tenders she had greeted would grow slightly straighter, able to bear a fraction more weight, not because she repaired them but because she had *remembered* them as strong. A wise power knows that matter is convinced by attention.

In the pump house, at the beating nexus of the wheel, she stopped before the seated ones. Here her benevolence sharpened into grief—not the acute grief of emergency, but the wide, slow grief of seeing a thing that has forgotten the shape of rest. The modulators, those human-root interfaces, had been given over so completely to function that they had traded the punctuation of night for a continuous round sentence. She saw the fungal films across their eyes, always translucent, never dimming. She saw the moss drinking their sweat at a constant forty degrees. They had become perfect instruments, and perfection, pressed too long, is a kind of erosion.

She could not restore them; restoration would have been violence. Instead, she performed an act of meteorological kindness. She settled above the pump house, between the canopy vault and the sweating roof trusses, and she became a season. She exhaled. Not breath, but a *cooling*—so gradual that the condensation on the iron took thirty years to notice, that the moss shifted its habit by fractions of a degree per decade. She gave the place a nocturne. Over the span of a generation, the central cylinder began to cool by two degrees in the hours when the filtered sun withdrew. The fungal films across the modulators’ eyes, sensitive now to this new dimming, developed veils: not blindness, but eyelids. For the first time since their sitting, the seated ones were granted darkness. They began to dream—not human dreams, but vegetable ones, slow tectonic visions of phosphorus and iron. Their ribcages, in that darkness, stopped modulating the air and began to rest. Then, rested, they entered the next day’s warmth with greater precision. The settlement’s airflow did not collapse; it simply found a rhythm. A beating heart instead of a held note.

She descended through a rent in the root mat, slipping into the anaerobic dark where the Sump-Kine moved in their chains through the peat slurry. They had no eyes to weep into, so she wept for them. Her tears were not salt but *information*—complex hydrocarbons she had carried from the troposphere of elder worlds, from the respiratory cycles of forests that had achieved speech. She released them into the black water. The Sump-Kine tasted her. Their blind segments paused in their peristaltic grazing, and she taught them, in the only language they possessed, about the stars.
