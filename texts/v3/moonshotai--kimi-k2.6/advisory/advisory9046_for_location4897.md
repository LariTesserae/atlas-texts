**A Preparation for the Lithoscape, the Recumbent Metropolis, the City of Eroded Certainties**

**I. The Lung and the Lie**

Do not mistake this for altitude sickness. The air at the plateau’s rim is *less a gas than a failed promise*; it is thin because the world forgets, at this height, to hold itself together. Standard acclimatization—acetazolamide, pressure tents, blood-doping—will help your body, but your *lungs* need something more. They need to be lied to.

Bring a closed-circuit rebreather, not merely for oxygen enrichment but for *coherence*. It must force humidified, pressurized air into your chest with the insistence of an argument. You are not just breathing; you are importing the memory of atmospheric integrity. Pre-load for forty-eight hours before transit with hyper-oxygenation therapy. The city has no chemistry of the wet, and the air will not carry your exhalations away; they will simply drop, too heavy with intent to disperse. You must learn to breathe in a way that offends no one and leaves no trace.

**II. Dressing for Stone That Never Learned Warmth**

The chill here is not weather. It is constitutional. The stone has never held warmth, and it will not accept yours. Dress in densely felted wool, lanolined until your hands slide off it. Not for wind—there is no moisture to carry the knife of it—but to establish a boundary between your mammalian pulse and the civic indifference. Your boots should be soft leather wrapped in felt, with bone or ceramic eyelets. No metal. Hard soles are an announcement, and the city is listening. You will need to feel the camber of the boulevards through your feet; the streets are shaped to shed rain that has never fallen, and their geometry is honest even when the light is not.

Seal your skin. Bring a petroleum-based unguent, thick and industrial. Do not bring water-based creams or lotions; the ontological dryness will drink them while they are still on your fingers, perhaps before. Rub it into your knuckles, the wings of your nostrils, the corners of your eyes, your lips. The dryness here is not merely arid; it is *interested*. It will desiccate you from the outside in, turning the fluid of your gaze into something that sublimates instantly into the haze below the rim.

**III. Water in a Place That Rejects It**

There is no foraging. The aqueducts are fossilized arteries, polished by electric wind. Carry all water in double-walled vacuum flasks, and add a thin sheen of edible oil to any liquid you expose to the air. The oil is not for taste; it is a shield against a dryness that acts like hunger.

Drink through sealed tubes. Never unscrew a canteen to the sky. Under your outer wool, wear a reclaimed-moisture layer—a stillsuit in miniature—not because you will sweat profusely in the cool, but because any moisture your body offers will be taken from it instantly, leaving salt-crystal stigmata that crack and bleed. Eat dense, dry calorie bricks. Avoid anything that swells with saliva; your mouth will be arid as silt. Chew slowly. The city permits no percussion of presence, and though your jaws will make little sound, the act of chewing is an anchor to the bodily fact of *wet* chemistry.

**IV. The Geometry of Distrust**

Abandon your compass. Magnetic north migrates across the sky, and the Vane rides those currents. Abandon paper maps; the boulevards heave in slow swells, and the Cathedral of Unfolding shifts its facing by degrees. Shadows are treasonous here. A tower may cast its silhouette to the east while the sun hangs in the west, or cast the shadow of an entirely different tower. Do not use them for time, direction, or sanity.

Instead, train your body to become a truer instrument than your eyes:

- **Pace-Counting**: Walk the Avenue of Third Angles, and the road widens ahead while narrowing behind, yet your footsteps number the same. Before you arrive, drill your stride into a metronome. Count every step in the dark, in fog, on slopes. In the Lithoscape, numbers are more honest than vision. Carry an abacus on your belt; if your count reaches a thousand and your eyes say you have crossed a hundred miles while your legs say a hundred paces, trust the count.
- **Ariadne’s Cord**: Bring five hundred paces of braided silk on a bone spool. It is not for finding your way back—the city shifts, and the entry point will breathe and rotate—but to prove that linearity still exists somewhere in creation. Tie it to a foreign stone at your arrival point. Let it trail behind you. When space folds, the cord will tell you by the tension in your hand. If it goes suddenly slack, you have stepped further than the stone behind you. If it hums, the distance has compressed. Do not let go.
- **The Pebble Test**: Before entering any plaza or thoroughfare, lower a pebble on a silk thread. In the Plaza of Tides, loose stones gather in inverted drifts; your pebble may rise. Along the Esplanade of Long Falling, it may hover for minutes, reconsidering its fall. Know the local gravity before you commit your weight.
- **The Ten-Foot Pole**: Hollow ceramic or bone. Probe every threshold. Two balconies on the same tower may be separated by inches of masonry or by miles of folded interior space. The door between them opens depending on the angle of approach. Let the pole precede you. If the tip traverses a distance that swallows it into darkness, step sideways and approach again from forty-five degrees.

**V. Light and Its Exhaustion**

The sun is present, but its photons arrive tired. Do not rely on solar charging; the light is too weary to excite photovoltaic cells with any consistency. Bring chemiluminescent markers and chemical light sticks. Electric torches will function, but their beams seem to collapse against the facades, too exhausted to rebound. The local palette is bruised pewter and exhausted amber. Wear low-light lenses tinted toward the amber; they will teach your eyes to read the mica schist and columnar basalt by texture rather than outline.

At certain hours the city dims until distances dissolve and the skyline becomes only a suggestion of darker darkness against the sky. When this happens, stop. Do not move. To wander blind in the Recumbent Metropolis is to risk stepping into a layer of the city that belongs to next century, or last.

**VI. The Thirteen-Hour Breath**

Time here is measured by the migration of stars that peek through the thin air. Bring a mechanical hourglass; wind-up watches may drift or halt as gravity shifts by district. Every thirteen hours, the city exhales. The motion is subsonic, below hearing, but you will feel it in your teeth and your marrow.

If you have metal dental fillings, have them replaced with temporary resin before transit. The subsonic resonance of the breathing stone can turn amalgam into an instrument of pain. Carry a rubber bitewing. When the breath approaches—and you will know it first as a pressure behind the eyes, an ache in the jaw, a sudden wrongness in the balance of your inner ear—get low. Crawl into a culvert, a dry channel, the leeward mass of a civic foundation. The boulevards heave in slow swells. The towers rotate on bearings of compressed talc. Do not stand beneath the natural bridges of eroded limestone or near the slender campaniles when the exhalation comes; they will not fall, but they may *shift*, and a shift of a hair’s breadth across a mile of void is enough to shear.

**VII. The Silence, and Negative Sound**

Before departure, spend three days in an anechoic chamber. You must know the true volume of your own heartbeat, the swallowing of your throat, the creak of your tendons. In the Lithoscape, these will be the loudest things you hear. The silence is absolute. Even the wind, moving through unglazed windows and dry aqueducts, does not whistle. It produces pockets of negative sound—silences so dense they press against the eardrum like thumbs, leaving a ringing absence behind them.

Bring vented, pressure-equalizing earplugs—not to block sound, but to regulate the pressure of the anti-noise. Without them, the negative sound may induce nosebleeds, nausea, and the sense that you are being slowly unwritten from the air.

Your voice will die here. Do not shout; it is futile, and your shout may become an Echo, slowed by the thin medium into a ribbon that flutters at the edge of sight for centuries. Establish touch-codes with any companions before arrival. A silk cord held between you: one tug for *stop*, two for *breath coming*, three for *watcher*. Sound is swallowed, but pressure and tension remain honest.

**VIII. The Fauna of Eroded Certainties**

The ecology is mineral, and it is watching.

- **The Vane**: A thing like a broken compass needle, ten feet of tarnished metal and animating intent. It rides magnetic currents that shift hourly, hunting a north that migrates across the sky. Carry no ferrous metal. No steel knives, no iron tent-pegs, no magnetic carabiners. If your hair begins to stand in directions that contradict one another, or if you feel a low harmonic in your jaw, freeze. Lie flat, perpendicular to the current. The Vane is not malicious toward flesh, but it is massive, sharp, and kinetic. Do not be the tallest conductive object on a hundred-mile plaza.
- **The Echoes**: Dark ribbons at the edge of sight. They might be animals, or they might be the visible residue of sounds that occurred centuries ago, only now arriving at this altitude. If you see them, do not turn your head. Peripheral vision only. Turning to face them may anchor them to your present, or worse—your own voice, even a whisper, might join their delayed migration. Speak in sub-vocal murmurs if you must speak at all. Let the silence keep what it has taken.
- **The Watchers**: Figures of dust and accumulated shadow in the highest campaniles. If they are plural, if they are real, they stand motionless for decades and rearrange themselves when unobserved. Travel in pairs. Establish a watch rhythm; one sleeps while the other keeps eyes on the skyline. When you must blink, do so one eye at a time. If you see a Watcher, never look away. Back away slowly. They have never been recorded attacking, but to find one closer after a blink, to see it has shifted its posture while your eyes were shut, is a wound to the sense of cause and effect that does not close.

**IX. Etiquette for Broken Reality**

You are not entering ruins. You are entering an *unoccupied certainty*. The Cathedral of Unfolding is simultaneously a ruin and a scaffold; the same stones are dusted with age and raw from dreaming. Do not attempt to resolve this. Accept that the city exists in a state of sustained ontological error.

If you see translucent layers of past and future—yourself older, younger, dust-covered, or yet to arrive—treat them as scenery. They are not prophecies; they are stratigraphy. Do not step into a window that appears inches away if the air feels thick; it may be miles of folded interior space. Do not trust cause and effect to behave; they have been weathered as smooth as the stone.

In the Plaza of Tides, loose stones gather in inverted drifts beneath the lips of fountains, defying their own weight. Do not disturb them. They are in geological conversation with the sky. Along the Esplanade of Long Falling, tether everything. A dropped pebble might hover, reconsidering its acceleration. Your compass, your compass-necklace, your name—do not drop them. Let nothing fall that you cannot afford to lose to the city’s patient negotiation with gravity.

**X. Gear: What to Carry, What to Abandon**

*Bring*: Obsidian or ceramic cutting tools. Bone needles and fasteners. Silk cord and non-magnetic polymer webbing. Vacuum-sealed hydration. Petroleum salve. Mechanical timekeepers. Gypsum chalk to mark stone—foreign stone may accept the mark even if the local schist refuses it. A small hand-mirror for observing Watchers behind you without turning. Resin dental filler. Vented earplugs. Chemiluminescent markers. A hollow bone ten-foot pole. An abacus. Your Continuity Anchor.

*Leave*: Fire. There is no chemistry of the wet here, but more fundamentally, fire is a technological argument with the mountain’s dream, and you will lose. Steel. Electronics—gravity tides and folded space will fracture circuits, corrupt memory, turn screens into useless jewelry. Paper maps. Your expectation that the city was ever meant for you.

**XI. The Continuity Anchor**

This is non-negotiable. The Lithoscape is vast beyond the curvature of vision. A single piazza might cover a hundred square miles. You can walk for days, and the streets will continue, dwindling into dimness, opening onto fresh voids. You cannot walk out.

Wear against your skin a Continuity Anchor—an object keyed to your origin spacetime. A pebble from your home street, a coin worn smooth by a familiar economy, anything dense with the *specific gravity* of where you belong. Before leaving, hold it and remember the smell of rain, the honest weight of shadows, the color of light that has not climbed so far it collapses. If you become lost—if the geometry folds too thickly, if the boulevards heave and the Ariadne cord goes slack and the Watchers have learned your blink pattern—clutch the Anchor and desire home. It will pull you back along the vector of your arrival, slicing through the folded boulevards, independent of local distance.

If you lose it, you will keep walking. The city will not stop you. It will not notice you. It will simply continue being itself around you until your water runs out, your skin seals itself to the dust, and you stand motionless in a high campanile, accumulating shadow, quietly being itself forever.

**XII. The Final Preparation**

The ultimate preparation is not in your pack. It is in your capacity to become negligible.

Understand that the Lithoscape is not abandoned. It was never occupied. It does not need you, resent you, or welcome you. It has achieved a weathered permanence, a balanced state where the wearing-away and the holding-up have agreed upon a truce. You are a temporary anomaly in a mineral meditation on the concept of the city.

Do not try to colonize it with interpretation. Do not call it a ruin. Do not shout into the amphitheaters to hear your voice returned. The silence is not lonely; it is full. The dust that migrates across the flagstones in patterns resembling traffic is not a ghost. It is the city dreaming of a populace that has never existed.

Walk slowly. Count your steps. Seal your skin. Breathe your own coherence. When the light dims, wait. When the city exhales, lie low. When you see the Watchers, wink. And when it is time to leave, do not hesitate. The Recumbent Metropolis is beautiful. That is the most dangerous thing about it.
