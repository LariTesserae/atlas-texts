**Pre-Arrival: The Mental Coil**

Before you descend from the rim, you must do something difficult: you must convince your nervous system that it is not going to a ruined town, nor to a wild forest, but to a single, slow digestion that has mistaken civic order for survival. Your eyes will see avenues, chimneys, windows, factory blocks. Your lizard brain will expect footsteps, voices, the tick of metal cooling. It will receive only the drip of condensation and the soft, vegetable sigh of a valve opening. The mismatch produces a vertigo more subtle than height or depth. Practice hallucinating architecture until it feels familiar, then practice stripping the familiarity away. A useful mantra, spoken while you can still hear your own voice clearly: *It is a stomach, not a street.*

Abandon your circadian narcissism. The district does not darken with the sun; it dims according to sugar in its own veins. Bring a mechanical timepiece, wind it religiously, and trust it when your body insists it is midnight at noon. You will need to know how long you have been waiting, because the plant’s sense of urgency is geologic and your own must be forcibly decoupled from it. Nothing here can be hurried. The hydraulic pistons compress fiber at a speed that insults the mammal gaze. If you try to watch them, impatience will become panic. Learn to observe as you would watch tide or healing: by glancing away.

**What to Wear: The Second Skin**

The damp here is not weather; it is exhalation. The air is lung-warm against your face while the basin air stays cool, and the result is a condensation so total that you will be wet within minutes no matter what you do. Do not fight it to stay dry; fight to stay *unmacerated*. Cotton is a death sentence—it will wick the moisture and hold it against your skin until your feet and armpits bloom with fungal gardens. Wear waxed canvas, treated synthetics, or rubberized layers that seal at the seams. Your boots must be waterproof to the knee, but choose soft crepe or felted soles. Sound carries in the silence, and the hard *clack* of a heel against waxy bark-concrete will ricochet down the avenues like a gunshot, startling your own nerves if not the architecture.

You want a peaked hood, not merely for warmth but to channel the metronomic drips that fall from every eave. The condensation is constant; there is no drip line that stays dry. If you wear a brimless cap, water will run down your collar and pool at the base of your spine. Gloves are mandatory—not because the walls are sharp, but because they are warm and chemically active. The resin-laminated columns are denser than cork and slightly adhesive; prolonged skin contact can raise blisters or produce a contact dermatitis that the locals (if there were any) would call *resin-burn*. A thin barrier cream on the face and neck is advisable: something hydrophobic and anti-fungal. You are entering a world that is very good at keeping surfaces moist. Your skin is not.

**What to Carry: The Silent Toolkit**

Fire is treason here. Do not bring matches, lighters, or any ignition source. The air is heavy with particulate cellulose, spores, and the faint exhalations of slow fermentation; a spark would not merely burn—it would declare war on an organism whose immune response you cannot comprehend. For light, rely on cool, dim chemical glow-sticks or shielded lanterns with red or amber LEDs. Bright white light may disrupt the phosphorescent thresholds of the street-blooms, which open according to sugars in the phloem, not to your schedule. Do not confuse them. If you carry a staff or walking pole, make it synthetic or aluminum, but sheath the tip in rubber. The sound of metal striking root-mat is wrong in this place, and sharp points risk puncturing membranes that may bleed amber lymph or trigger defensive inflation.

Your water must be entirely self-contained in sealed bladders or steel flasks. Do not drink the condensation, the eave-drippings, or the lymph that pulses in the channels. It is likely sterile to the plant, but it carries dissolved enzymes, alcohols from core fermentation, and molecular cargo meant for trading between tissues. It will not hydrate you; it will digest you, slowly and politely. Bring your own. The humidity is deceptive—you will not feel thirsty because your pores will cease to function, but your cells still require actual osmotic input, not merely atmospheric moisture. Similarly, all food must be hermetically sealed, dry, and crumb-proof. The floor is alive. Drop a crust and the microscopic cilia in the root-mat will begin working on it, drawing it downward toward the gastric pits. You do not want to establish a feeding relationship with the pavement.

Waste management is non-negotiable. Pack in, pack out. The district’s digestive system is closed and ancient; introducing your foreign microbiome into its gastric pits may provoke a chemical correction you cannot survive, or simply cause a localized wall to swell shut in protest. Bring sealable bags and a portable containment system. This is not squeamishness; it is diplomacy.

**Entering the Damp: Respiration and the First Hours**

Approach from the rim slowly. The basin cups cooler, heavier air, and the pressure shift will press against your eardrums and sinuses. Yawn, chew, swallow. The first breath at the bottom will taste of cinnamon and wet paper. It is beautiful. It is also loaded with spores and vaporized lignins.

A respirator is strongly recommended—not a clumsy gas mask, but a half-mask with organic vapor cartridges and a particle filter. If you are stubborn and breathe raw, take prophylactic antihistamines and carry an epinephrine injector. The central stack exhales its warm, cinnamon-scented vapor on a metabolic cycle; stand upwind until you know its schedule. That drizzle is not benign dew. It is a concentrated respiratory byproduct, and while it may not poison you, it can induce lightheadedness, anosmia, or a trance-like suggestibility in some travelers. Respect the hourly spore-release valves. When you hear the soft vegetal sigh, hold your breath and avert your face. The spores are not toxic, but inhaling a living district’s reproductive semaphore is an intimacy your lungs have not evolved to manage.

Your first mapping pass will be disorienting. The scale is small—no larger than a cathedral close—but the broadness of the avenues and the absence of undergrowth create a saunalike emptiness that distances feel foreshortened and objects loom. Do not sketch the map yet. Walk the rim of the basin first. Note the central stack. Pick a landmark, preferably a lamp-stand of phosphorescent lichen, and mark it in your memory. The geometry is too ordered to be wild and too alive to be stable; your eye will slide off it.

**Moving Through: Avenues and Inflation**

The avenues are fifty meters across and damp as a throat. The root-mat flooring is springy, uneven, and threaded with hair-fine runner vines that can snag soft soles. Test each broad step; the mat gives, then firms, but there are hollows where internal reservoirs or gastric cavities have left thin spots. If you feel warmth radiating upward, move aside. That is not a geothermal vent; it is active fermentation beneath your feet.

Navigation obeys a rule the plant understands but you must learn by bruise: inflation. The district breathes in shifts. A passage open and broad in the morning may, by afternoon, be bisected by swollen bladder-roots that have engorged with hydraulic sap to redirect internal traffic. They are pale, turgid, and surprisingly resilient. Do not cut them. Do not push through. Wait, or go around. They subside by dusk, or when the internal pressure differential equalizes. Mark your trails with reflective tape or synthetic ribbon tied high on the lower structures—but not inorganic spikes. The bark-concrete wraps around foreign intrusion slowly, like flesh around shrapnel, and while it will not attack you, it will absorb your marker. Check that your trail is still legible every hour.

Windows—translucent patches of leaf-cellulose stiff as parchment—will show you interior chambers. Do not break them to enter. The plant maintains these apertures by design; breaching one is akin to lancing a skin. It will not scream, but it will weep resin, and the localized chemistry may change for days. If a doorway presents itself, know that it is an orifice of convenience, not an invitation. Cross thresholds quickly and do not linger on the polished sills, where decades of lymph-flow have lacquered the wood into something like glazed stone.

**Resting: Sleep Without Sinking**

Sleep is the most dangerous activity here. The cilia in the floor do not distinguish between a dropped crumb and a sleeping mammal. They work slowly, but they work always. Do not sleep on the root-mat. Bring a framed cot with rigid legs, or better, a hammock suspended between two laminated trunks. The ideal camp is in an open plaza, equidistant from the walls, away from drip lines, and clear of the central stack’s exhalation cone. The temperature is cool, so you will want insulation, but do not use down or wool if you can help it—natural fibers seem to draw moisture and, in time, the attention of cleaning tendrils.

Before you sleep, listen for ten minutes. Locate the subterranean pulse of pressure, the metronomic drip, the soft pop of a hydraulic piston. These are your lullabies. If they stop abruptly, if the silence deepens into a held breath, wake up. Something is shifting. The phosphorescent street-blooms will guide you if you must move in the dark; they are pale and lunar, brightening when the photosynthetic yield has been high, dimming when the district metabolizes its starches. Read them not as mood lighting but as blood sugar readings. A dim dawn here means the plant is fasting.

**Sustenance: The Closed Loop**

You are in a foundry district that feeds itself. You are not part of the loop. Do not attempt to eat any local growth. The runner vines may look like string beans; the globe-shaped blossoms may smell of ripe melon. They are not food. They are process. If you must boil water from your own supply, use a sealed thermal flask heated before you enter; never an open flame. Better yet, carry enough calories to last your stay without cooking. The smell of heated protein or caramelized sugar might register as an anomalous input in this silent metabolic ledger.

Hydrate on a schedule, not by thirst. The damp air masks evaporative water loss; you can dehydrate while standing in a downpour of condensation. Check your urine color by your amber lantern; it should not match the lymph in the channels.

**The Long Wait: Substance and Scale**

Prepare for the silence. It is not peaceful. It is architectural. There are no birds, no insect drones, no scuff of mammal life. When you stop walking, the only sound will be your own circulatory system, the creak of your waxed coat, and the district’s subsonic digestion. Travelers have reported hallucinations of footsteps behind them, or the phantom ring of a telephone in the central stack. These are your brain’s desperate attempts to narrate an empty soundscape. Bring a mechanical countermeasure: a waterproof music box, a ticking metronome, or a recorded human voice played at low volume through bone-conduction headphones. Not loud. Just enough to remind your amygdala that sound is still possible.

The visual uncanny will exhaust you. Your occipital lobe will burn calories trying to resolve every blocky structure into either “city” or “tree,” and it will fail. After three hours, you may experience *mixed pareidolia*—the sense that the walls are watching you, or that the pistons are waving. They are not. The place is impersonally civic. Rest your eyes by staring at the sky through the broken canopy apertures. It is the only thing here that is simply what it looks like.

**Emergency: When the Plant Shifts**

If you become trapped by an inflatable barrier, or if a valve-bud closes your exit, do not force your way through. The tissues are hydraulic; they are stronger than you and they heal faster than you can injure them. Back away. Wait an hour. If a floor section begins to feel *too* warm, or if the cilia visibly agitate beneath your dropped pack, move. A gastric pit may be opening beneath the root-mat.

In case of injury, treat wounds immediately and seal them from the air. The lymph here is not antiseptic; it is enzymatically active. A cut hand pressed against a wall may end up laminated. Carry a broad-spectrum topical and dressings that adhere to wet skin. If you contract a respiratory issue—a wet cough, spiking fever, or the inability to smell the cinnamon anymore—ascend to the rim immediately. The basin damp will finish what a normal cold starts.

If you become disoriented, do not look for footprints. No one has walked here before you. Look instead for erosion patterns: the polished corners, the glazed bark where decades of dripping have lacquered the surfaces. These indicate age and stability. Head toward the central stack only if you mean to. It is the heart, and hearts are terrible places to hide.

**Departure Protocol**

When you climb back to the rim, stop. Remove and bag all your outer layers before you exit the heavy air. The spores and waxy particulates adhering to you are not dust; they are alive and patient. Expose your gear to dry wind and strong sunlight before you re-enter any other civilized zone. You do not want to carry a dormant cutting into a station, a ship, or a home. The foundry district is beautiful, balanced, and profoundly foreign. It does not need to colonize. It only needs you to be careless for one afternoon.

Leave no trace. Take no cuttings. Speak of it, if you must, in the tones you would use for a monastery or a surgery. And when you are safe again under a sun that sets by clockwork, drink a glass of water—just water, simple and inert—and marvel at the silence of a glass that does not breathe.
