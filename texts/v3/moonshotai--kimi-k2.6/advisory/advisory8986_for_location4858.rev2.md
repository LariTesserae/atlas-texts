**I. The Body Must Learn to Drown Slowly**

Do not prepare for a journey. Prepare for an immersion. Before you ever reach the infant mountains, you must teach your lungs that air is only one kind of currency. At the threshold, where the sky relents and the atmosphere turns from breath to broth, you will step—not into wind, but into *thickness*. A warm, amber ocean that does not fall. It yields like honeyed resin. It tastes of old copper pennies left in the rain.

If you attempt to breathe this as you would air, your alveoli will panic. Acclimate with a liquid-breathing rig weeks in advance. Fill your chest with oxygenated perfluorocarbon in a training tank. Learn the terror of that first viscous inhale—the brain’s screaming certainty that you are drowning—and teach your diaphragm to unclench, to draw the warm, dense medium in slow tides. Let the copper taste become familiar on the back of your tongue. In the mountains, your rebreather must be a silent, closed-loop companion, worn across the shoulder blades, feeding your lungs the fluid they have learned to accept. Keep a nasal block and oral seal of soft, living silicone; any leak of ordinary air into that world is a bubble of insult, a bright, ungainly gasp that will fight the local density and leave you nauseated, buoyant in the wrong way, drifting upward when you need to descend.

Train your shoulders, your obliques, your hip flexors. You are not walking into this place; you are swimming through a stationary sea three miles above the sea. Every movement is breaststroke. Every advance is a persuasion. In the amber suspension, thrashing is death by exhaustion. You must move with the muscular patience of a manta ray: wide, slow planes of motion, leveraging the syrupy resistance rather than fighting it. The fluid rewards stillness—it will hold you up if you let it, cradling your weight in exchange for surrender. Practice floating in gelatinous silicone tanks until you can sleep suspended, limbs slack, heartbeat the only engine running.

**II. Armor Against the Offended Stone**

Pack no metal. No buckles, no carabiners, no fired steel, no aluminum frame. The inhabitants have negotiated with the geology for generations using obsidian, bone, and rope braided from the silica-hair of cave molds; to them, metal is the language of conquest, too loud even in its silence, too cold against the warm stone. More practically, in a world of absolute darkness beyond your fingertips, a protruding zipper or a belt loop is a hook waiting for the mountain’s glassy flesh.

The rock here is *new*. It has not been humbled by water or time. Basalt prisms stand like cathedral pipes sheared off at lethal angles; obsidian blades still birth-warm to the touch lie invisible in the amber murk, offended by the concept of your soft anatomy. You will not see them until your thigh is against them. Wear a seamless slick-skin suit, full coverage, matte and charcoal-dark. It should be woven from impact-ablative polymers or, better, spun from silica-fiber analogs that mimic the locals’ own cordage—something that will snag on nothing and slide along the glassy black stone. The suit must be tight; loose fabric becomes a viscous drag-anchor, exhausting you within meters. Cover your hands with thin, tactile gloves that let you feel thermal gradients through the fingertips, but protect you from the razored edges you will inevitably brush in the dark.

Leave behind anything that relies on combustion. Fire is a confused animal in that dense atmosphere; more importantly, the heat you carry must be measured and honest. The stone itself is thermal, bleeding warmth from its newborn wounds. You do not need to be warmer; you need to survive the ambient heat without cooking. Your slick-skin should have embedded cooling channels, a gentle osmotic circulation that keeps your core at the threshold of comfort without chilling the air you touch—because to chill the air is to violate the local economy, where warmth is the only coin that matters.

**III. Navigation in the Gloaming**

Sight is a intimate, short-range sense here. The bioluminescent slime-molds and thermal kelptrees offer a bruised, subaquatic twilight—greens, fungal golds, violets like old bruises—but it is barely enough to silhouette your own raised hand. Do not bring bright lamps. A flashlight is an act of violence in that dim world, blinding the mold-curtains, washing out the slow spiral pulses of communication painted on the inhabitants’ skin. If you must carry light, let it be a low-lumen, wide-spectrum phosphorescence tuned to the fungal golds, something that merely nudges the darkness rather than evicting it.

Better yet, learn to navigate by heat. The settlement is built in the warm fractures. The mountain’s bleeding veins are your true cartography. Practice reading stone temperature with your palms. Pack a tactile thermal compass: a flat disc worn against the sternum that hums or warms to indicate the gradient toward the nearest vent. Sound is useless. It travels a hand’s breadth and perishes. Do not bring sonic rangefinders. Do not expect to call for help. The silence is absolute, devotional, a held breath that turns your own heartbeat into thunder in the hollow of your chest. Spend days in an anechoic chamber before you depart. Learn to find the silence not as absence, but as a dense presence pressing against your eardrums. If you are not comfortable with the sound of your own blood, you will go mad.

Because you are blind beyond an arm’s length, and sound is dead, you must accept that you will move through a world you cannot survey. Attach no physical tethers or phosphorescent breadcrumbs; the slime-molds are a sensitive ecology, and your imported glow might poison their spiraling language. Instead, move as the locals do: from warmth to warmth, from one bruised-violet glow to the next, trusting that the density hides nothing that will not first touch you.

**IV. The Etiquette of Touch and Slowness**

There are no birds. No insect drone. No scurrying vermin. The only animal vastness is the rare zooplanktonic leviathan, a translucent weather-pattern drifting through the lower fluid layers, grazing kelp forests in mindless silence. You cannot out-swim it. You cannot hear it coming. If the thermal gradient shifts strangely, if the amber around you darkens with a passing eclipse, freeze. Become still. Let it pass like a slow storm. It has no interest in you, but a collision with something that large in that viscosity would be like being struck by a migrating cloudbank.

When you reach the settlement, shed your human sense of territory. The inhabitants live in a vertical saturation, terraces and tunneled chambers overhanging one another by scant inches, fluid-filled arteries where shoulders brush and shared warmth seeps through porous basalt. Personal space does not exist here; it is a meaningless concept in a medium that transmits heat better than privacy. You will be touched. Palms will press against your shoulder, your cheek, your wrist. This is not intimacy as you know it; it is grammar. They speak in gesture, in the pressure of fingers, in the slow bioluminescent pulse of cultivated molds painted in spirals across their skin.

Before you go, learn to offer your hands as pages. Bring phosphorescent, non-toxic pigments compatible with chemophore biology—pigments that will accept the local molds and allow them to write upon you. Let them paint your forearms with slow spirals so you can participate in the conversation. Do not try to shout. Your voice will die in the copper air, swallowed whole. All oration is replaced by the meditative calm of the stone. Move slowly. Move as if you are persuading lava to cool, because that is the only timescale the settlement understands. They have watched stone become archways over centuries. Your urgency is a kind of crudeness here. Meditate on geologic patience until your heartbeat itself slows to a tectonic rhythm.

**V. Provisions for the Viscous Economy**

The labor of pushing through the amber fluid will burn calories like a furnace. Bring dense, slow-release nutrient slurries—fat and carbohydrate emulsions that can be ingested through a valve without removing your breathing apparatus. Do not bring anything that requires cooking over an open flame; fire suffocates in that atmosphere, and the heat would be redundant, almost insulting. Carry water in osmotic bladders, but be prepared to supplement with the copper-tasting medium itself if your filters can parse its chemistry; the body loses moisture through effort, and the ambient warmth is dehydrating despite the fluid immersion.

Bring gifts that respect the technological threshold of the hand. No machined objects. No synthesized polymers that smell of petroleum. Consider a bone flute carved so finely it can be played by drawing water through it, or a collection of obsidian knapped to a geometry alien to their own—sharpnesses from another geologic tradition. Offer calibrated thermal seeds: bacteria cultures from a friendly thermal vent in your own world, sealed in warmed ceramic, that can seed a cooling slab and persuade it into a new curve. But ensure they are sterile to local pathogens and will not outcompete the fungi they have cultivated since the mountain first rose.

**VI. The Mind and the Infant World**

Finally, prepare your psychology. This is a place that is *still becoming*. The mountains have not decided their final shapes. The atmospheric ocean has not chosen whether to remain suspended or to drain downward in some future violence. The rules are being written in magma and fungal bloom even as you arrive. You cannot demand stability from stone that has not yet accepted erosion. You cannot expect paths to remain; a cooling slab may settle overnight, closing a corridor you walked yesterday.

You will live in the undersea gloaming, three miles above any ocean, hearing nothing, seeing only what your hand can reach, pressed against warm strangers in the breathing dark, your body learning to move like a dreamer through honey. You will be a mayfly in a world of sequoias, your lifespan a single bright flash between the mountain’s inhale and exhale.

Go there not to conquer, but to be held. Let the thickness cradle you. Let the silence teach you your own heartbeat. And when you touch the warm basalt, do not take. Ask. Wait. Persuade.
