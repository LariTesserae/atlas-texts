To enter this place is not to travel but to be **absorbed**. The city does not greet you; it simply makes room for your water and your warmth, then forgets them. An ordinary human must prepare to become temporarily mineral, temporarily aqueous, and permanently humble. Below is what your body, your kit, and your mind will need to endure the lung of wet stone.

---

### 1. The Fissure and the First Breath
The entry is a shoulder-width crack. Leave behind anything that cannot be compressed, rolled, or strapped flush to your spine. Your pack must be narrow enough to slide through living rock, yet contain everything required for an environment where **walking is obsolete** and **terrestrial rules have been repealed**. Before the fissure, stop and don your respirator. Not a filter for toxins—there are none—but a compact, balanced rebreather or at minimum a sealed half-mask with dehumidifying cartridges. The chamber breathes water; the air is not air but vapor at the carrying capacity of stone. Untrained lungs will aspirate mist as if drowning in slow motion. You must acclimate your alveoli to breaths that yield less oxygen than they feel, or you will hyperventilate, panic, and freeze against the first corbel.

Go in stripped of bulk, but layered in hydrophobic skin. A full drysuit of neoprene-backed synthetic, with a thermal base layer beneath. The temperature is not hostile, but it is **conductive**; the wet stone and the saturated vapor will leach heat from your core without ever offering the mercy of shivering. You will not feel cold until you are hypothermic. Seal every cuff. The water does not fall here—it slides, threads, and pools in the crevices of your clothing with the patience of geology.

---

### 2. Feet, Hands, and the Art of Friction
The floor is a staggered, corbelled slope of river stone mortared in pale clay. You do not walk. You **climb upward through the room**, using terrace lips as ladder rungs. Your boots must be soft-soled with tacky silicone or felt impregnated with carbide grit, narrow enough to toe into irregular gaps but gentle enough not to crush the ancient clay facades. Hard mountaineering boots will skitter on the silica-lacquered surfaces; bare feet will macerate and tear. Choose the grip of a cave fish, the pressure of a mason.

Your hands must be bare or sheathed in thin neoprene gloves printed with ridges of latex. You will need to feel the flint chocks, the antler pegs, the mineralized sinew ropes. The flowstone pillars that replace timber are slick agate and calcite. They hold load by friction alone. Do not trust them with dynamic, bouncing rope forces. If you must anchor, bring wide, low-tension webbing slings and static (low-stretch) cord. Distribute your weight across multiple pillars like a spider. The architecture is genius, but it was built for the loads of a lighter, wetter, more patient people.

---

### 3. Hearing Protection: The Armor of Silence
This is the most critical preparation. The sound is not auditory; it is **physical**. A basso profundo organ-note that fills the sternum, liquefies thought, and vibrates the molars in their sockets. Ordinary earplugs are insultingly inadequate. You need a dual-layered skull sanctuary: custom-molded silicone in-ear plugs with deep insertion, over sealed by waterproof active-noise ear muffs tuned to cancel low-frequency phase. If your technology permits, wear a harmonic dampener—a collar or hood that generates a counter-resonance against the occipital bone. Without this, the sound will scramble your inner monologue. You will be unable to count your own heartbeats. You will forget your name. 

Practice thinking without language before you arrive. Learn to count by pressing fingertips against your thigh in patterns. Learn to recite your mission by tracing letters on your palm. The roar does not deafen; it **unminds**.

---

### 4. Light and the Tyranny of Phosphorescence
No fire. The atmosphere is a wet lung; a torch would gutter and drown before it sparked. The chamber provides its own illumination: a sourceless, grey-green mineral phosphorescence that reveals edges without offering comfort. Do not bring bright lights. A harsh beam will bleach your rod cells and destroy the delicate twilight your eyes will, after twenty minutes, learn to read. If you must carry artificial light, strap a dim, diffuse panel to your chest or waist—never your head. Water slides down from the inverted dome in glassy sheets; a headlamp invites the water directly into your eyes and down your collar. Use a phosphor-matched gel over your lens (blue-green, 480–500 nm) to supplement without competing.

Bring low-light optics if you have them, but expect fog. The air itself beads on glass. Better to train your vision in darkrooms lit only by glow-worm terrariums until the corbelled terraces resolve as ghost architecture in half-light.

---

### 5. Navigation in the Honeycomb
There are no streets. Only vertical shafts, crawl-tunnels, and the outer faces of contiguous cells packed like mineral honeycomb. You will enter a warren where every surface is slick, every shaft is irregular, and every lintel is green slate or bone-white limestone slicked with silica. 

**Do not rely on trail markers.** Chalk dissolves. Tape accretes with flowstone and becomes a geological feature. Spray paint would be the vandalism of a lunatic in a cathedral of equilibrium. Instead, use a **tether protocol**: a spool of monofilament line or, better, a braided spectra cord in a high-visibility unnatural color (magenta, orange) paid out behind you in the shafts. The city will not digest it; it is inert. Alternatively, if your multiverse kit allows, an inertial compass or quantum-position bead that records your path independent of landmarks.

Map the drainage. The water is the only honest architect left. It moves in scored channels, runnels, and floor-grooves. Learn its gravity. If you are lost, follow the water downward; it knows the margins where the chamber exhales into deeper sinks. Memorize the acoustic signature of your entry fissure—it will be the one place where the tectonic chord develops a slight edge, a break in resonance.

---

### 6. Sleep, Waste, and the Humiliation of Biology
There is no flat ground. Sleep in a climbing harness, reclined against a corbel, or in a hammock strung between two flowstone posts using friction hitches that grip without sawing. Sleep will be shallow. The sound massages your brain stem; the wetness never permits a dry cheek against a sleeve. Bring a bite guard. The drone will resonate in your molars; without protection, you will grind your teeth to powder in a single night.

**You are the only living contamination.** The ecology is chemical. There is no mold to eat your rations, no bacteria to decompose your waste—but that means your presence is an aberrant intrusion. Pack out everything. Vacuum-sealed food in non-reactive pouches. A compact still or osmotic filter for water: the ambient water is pure in a geological sense, but heavy with dissolved silica and calcium. Drinking it raw is like drinking liquid porcelain; it will bind your gut. Defecate into sealed, inert containers. The drainage is perfect, but it is not yours. You do not want to introduce phosphates into a system that has not known them for ten thousand years.

Apply antifungal ointment to every skin fold before entry and every six hours thereafter. The wetness is absolute; trench foot and skin maceration are certainties, not risks.

---

### 7. Psychological Conditioning: Becoming Impermanent
More than physical, the city demands **ontological preparation**. This is a world in frozen equilibrium. It does not change. The flowstone accretes by a molecule and flakes by a molecule in perfect stasis. When you touch the wall, the print of your presence will be reclaimed by the drip-grooves. The roar will swallow your echo. 

You must make peace with **impermanence enforced**. For humans, architecture is a dialogue: we touch, we alter, we leave scuff marks as proof of being. Here, there is no dialogue. You will shout and the city will not answer. You will weep and the moisture will not distinguish your salt from the aquifer’s. Train for this by spending days in sensory isolation—float tanks, anechoic chambers—while practicing the discipline of *non-legacy*. Journal extensively before you enter, because the city will offer no memory of you to carry back.

Bring a timekeeper. A mechanical watch in a waterproof case, heavy enough to feel on your wrist, or a haptic metronome that taps against your pulse. Without it, the timelessness will dissolve your circadian rhythm. There is no dawn, no hunger cycle, no cooling of air to mark evening. You must carry time like a torch.

---

### 8. Fauna and the Etiquette of Stillness
If you see the color of wet limestone move in a crevice—an eyeless articulation of mineral paste or a solitary crustacean—**do not pursue it**. It is not biological life as you understand it. It is the slow expression of the rock’s chemistry, and your interference may collapse the niche that holds it. Be a specter. Move slowly. The city’s balance is held by friction; your haste could dislodge a flint chock that has held since before your species learned to knap stone.

---

### 9. The Exit Protocol
When you leave, you will climb back down the corbelled slope toward the fissure, or perhaps you will find a deeper sink if the aquifer calls you. Do not look back for a final panoramic view. The grey-green light does not favor grandeur; it favors density. You will emerge narrower than you entered—stripped of ego, thoroughly wet, and vibrating in your bones with a chord that will take days to fade. 

Dry your kit in sunlight for a week; the mineral skins that lacquer your boots may outlast the boots themselves. Burn your journal if you must, or seal it. But know that the only proof you were there is the memory you carried out, and even that will feel, after a while, like something you dreamed inside the mineral sleep of the world’s wet lung.
