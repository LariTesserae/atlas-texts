**Field Preparation Briefing: The Sealed Vault (High Throat, Nameless Mountain)**  
*For the Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler*

---

### I. The First Principle: The Darkness Is Not Empty

Before you pack a single thing, you must alter your understanding of space itself. You have spent your life in worlds where emptiness is the default—where a room is mostly void, mostly air, mostly *nothing*, with discrete objects floating in it like toys in a bath. Burn that intuition. Scorch it. The vault you are entering is the inverse: it is full. It is saturated. There is no negative space, no aisle, no margin between you and the world. You are not walking into a chamber; you are inserting yourself into a tissue. You will be outnumbered, out-pressured, and out-massed by warm, yielding, silent life the moment your knee touches the floor.

Your preparation, therefore, begins in the mind. Spend a month in absolute sensory deprivation—not as a stunt, but as rehearsal. Learn to tolerate being touched by presences you cannot see and cannot name. Train yourself out of the flinch response. In the vault, to flinch is to tear; to tear is to violate a boundary older than your species. Practice the thought, until it becomes a physical reflex: *I am not alone. I was never meant to be alone here. My solitude is the anomaly.*

If you are claustrophobic, do not go. If you fear the dark, do not go. If you require a horizon line, do not go. This is not a place to conquer. It is a place to be temporarily digested.

---

### II. The Suit: A Body Within a Body

You will need a full-sealed environment suit, but not the kind built for vacuum. Vacuum is sterile and hungry. The vault is fecund and thick. Think diving bell, not spacesuit. The atmosphere inside is not thin mountain air; it is a stagnant soup of vaporized metal salts, sulfur compounds, and mineral vapor. Despite the elevation, the pressure feels dense, humid with chemistry. Your suit must be a closed circuit—nothing in, nothing out.

**Atmospheric Integrity:**  
Use a rebreather with chemical scrubbing—specifically for sulfur oxides and volatile metallic particulates. The air smells of copper, ozone, and overheated honey. That smell is corrosion and intoxication. Do not plan to “tough it out” with a filter mask. The exposure is accumulative, like the heat.

**Thermal Management:**  
The heat is not the sharp blast of a volcanic vent. It is geological patience. The stone has been hot for eons, radiating inward, never breaking its fever. Standard insulative layering will fail; you will stew. What you need is active liquid cooling—a garment of capillary tubes against your skin, drawing your body heat into a phase-change reservoir. Check your reservoir’s capacity against twelve hours of steady, ambient temperatures high enough to roast coffee. Your palms and knees will need special consideration, because you will be crawling on a floor that radiates like a heated griddle, through a carpet of organisms that are themselves *warmer* than the stone—warm as blood, warm as something just born. They will press their heat into you. Your suit must reject it, or you will die of thermal accumulation before you reach the eastern wall.

**Tactile Interface:**  
You cannot see. You cannot use light (see Section III). Your hands and knees are your eyes. Outfit your suit’s palms, fingertips, and kneepads with micro-thin, durable conductive mesh—something that transmits texture while maintaining bioseal. You need to feel the difference between basalt, borehole, rotting silk-cartilage colony, and the yielding, peristaltic tubes of the floor-carpet. Without this sensitivity, you are a blind giant in a china shop made of meat.

**Biosecurity (Double-Edged):**  
This ecosystem has never known water. Never known chlorophyll. Never known a single cell of Earth-standard biology. The closed loop of its life is a sacred chemical covenant. You are a wet, salty bag of foreign proteins and trace-water exhalations. Your sweat could be a plague. Your skin flakes, a biblical disaster. Any water-based secretion that escapes your suit—sweat, urine, exhaled moisture, tears—risks introducing rot to a world that has abolished it. Recycle everything internally. The smallest droplet of water introduced here would be like acid on a fresco.

Conversely, their chemistry wants you too. Silicone oils, liquid salts, hyphal filaments that dissolve and haul. Ensure your outer suit layer is rated against slow chemical creep and microbial (or quasi-microbial) adhesion. They will not “eat” you quickly, but given time, their exploratory filaments will test your seams.

---

### III. The Prohibition of Light

Listen carefully, because this is not negotiable: **you may not bring light.**

Not a headlamp. Not a glow-stick. Not infrared, not UV, not the faintest phosphorescent marker. Light in this place is not a tool; it is a forgotten concept, and for good reason. The faint marks of prior habitation include a half-circle on the floor where life thins, where growth slows to a poisoned crawl. The residue suggests a lamp, a fire, some ancient technology that emitted photons. Light here is biocide. The organisms have evolved in absolute zero-lux for geological ages; their metabolisms, bound to mineral chemistry, almost certainly register radiant energy not as illumination but as a toxic intrusion, a sear across their chemical language.

You will enter in absolute darkness. Total, obliterative black. The darkness has texture—velvet at the edges of your face, iron at the core—and you must meet it as a native. Do not cling to the idea of sight. It will not return when your eyes adjust; there is nothing to adjust to. Your brain will scream for visual confirmation. You must train it to accept thermal gradients, pressure differentials, and tactile topology as your new visual cortex.

For navigation, use an inertial dead-reckoning unit strapped to your wrist. No glowing screen. A silent tactile readout—a grid of pins that press Braille-like coordinates against your inner arm—or a bone-conduction timer that counts your cadence. Memorize the chamber’s dimensions before entry: cathedral nave, high ceiling bristling with tendrils, eastern curve where the boreholes wait. Count your crawling paces. Know the vault’s geometry like a blind pianist knows the keys.

---

### IV. Sound Discipline: The Silence Is a Sacrament

The vault is not silent because it is dead. It is silent because nothing here speaks. There is no chitin, no cry, no mating call. The only sounds are the subsonic hum of millions of feeding tubes—a pressure against your eardrums like a distant, forgotten chant—and the rare dry pop of a gas pocket, the wet slap of a colony detaching from the ceiling. These are punctuation marks in a library of quiet so absolute it seems to swallow the heat.

Do not break it. Do not use active sonar; do not shout for echoes. Do not play music through bone-conduction headphones; the vibration will transmit into the floor-carpet. Move slowly enough that your suit does not rasp against stone. The ecosystem is voiceless. Honor that voicelessness.

---

### V. The Crawl: Moving Through Density

You will not walk. There is no floor, only a ten-centimeter-deep carpet of living tubes and nodules. Between the floor-carpet, the wall-drapes, and the ceiling tendrils, there is no room for a standing body. You will crawl, and crawling here is an act of intimacy. The life will press against your knees and elbows like a yielding, feverish crowd. It will rise to meet your palms. It will drape across your shoulders as you pass beneath the hanging colonies. It will brush your cheek—a tendril, warm as blood, curious, silent.

**Protocol for Contact:**  
Do not recoil. Recoil tears colonies, disrupts chemical gradients, introduces the violence of sudden motion into a system that moves to a metronome of centuries. When you touch the membrane of the superorganism, you are not touching an “it.” You are touching a *we*. The organism ripples in response to pressure; a touch on the western wall may contract the eastern nodules half a minute later. You are being discussed, chemically, in a language of heat and dissolved ore. Make your touch brief, distribute your weight, and move predictably. Predictability is read as non-threat.

**Pathfinding:**  
The floor is uniform in its packed-ness, but not perfectly so. The built things—the ambiguous remains—lie near the eastern curve. Search for the five boreholes, too regular to be natural, now filled with gold-yellow filaments. Your fingers must learn the difference between vesicular stone and drilled cylinders. Search also for the half-circle of slower growth. Let your hands do the seeing.

---

### VI. Temporal Anchor: Surviving the Perpetual Present

Without light, without season, without the swing of day and night, the human mind loses its grip on duration. The vault lives in a perpetual now, where a migration takes a century and a predation takes a decade. Your brain, evolved under a spinning sun, will attempt to match that slowness. You will feel your thoughts thickening, your urgency draining away, your identity beginning to diffuse into the warm, quiet dark. This is how the vault digests minds.

You need an artificial metronome. A wrist unit that taps a silent beat against your radial bone every sixty seconds. A strict adherence to your ship-time schedule. Narrate your state into a soundless recorder at fifteen-minute intervals—not for the log, but for your own temporal sanity. Remind yourself of your name. Remind yourself that outside, wind still scours the mountain.

Do not stay longer than your coolant and scrubbers allow. Do not let the silence convince you that time has stopped. It has not stopped. It has merely forgotten you.

---

### VII. The Encounter with the Built Things

You are not the first. Near the eastern wall, your searching fingers will find the boreholes—five cylinders of harder stone, now threaded with yellow filaments. You may find the half-circle where life avoids an ancient residue. These are the only traces of intention in a place that has otherwise abolished intention.

Do not excavate. Do not sample. The rules of contact apply doubly here. Whatever hands made these marks are long gone, their chemistry recycled into the same pool that feeds the tendrils overhead. Record by touch only. A three-dimensional tactile scanner, if you must, but one that emits no light, no invasive radiation. These remains are archaeological fossils embedded in a living body. Treat them as you would a nerve ending in someone else’s brain—with infinite, hands-off care.

---

### VIII. Emergency Protocols

**If your cooling fails:** You have perhaps forty minutes before the accumulative heat induces hyperthermia. Crawl toward your entry passage immediately. Do not wait for rescue inside; the heat is patient and you are not.

**If your suit breaches:** Hold your breath. Seal the tear with patch-foam if you can. If foreign chemistry or organisms enter the breach, remember: they do not “infect” like surface pathogens. They dissolve, precipitate, and dismantle. Abort the mission.

**If you panic:** You will tear the carpet. You will thrash against the draped walls. You will emit light, noise, and heat-spikes. You will become a violation, and the vault, though slow, is vast and warm and absolute. It will integrate you. Do not panic. If you feel panic rising, freeze. Press your forehead to the yielding floor. Count your heartbeats. Let the subsonic hum press against your eardrums like a lullaby. Wait until you remember that the packed dark is not hostile. It is simply *full*.

---

### IX. Departure and Decompression

When you exit, you will carry the vault on you. Metal salts will crust your suit. Sulfur will linger in your filters. You may have fibers, oils, or microscopic traces of a chemistry that has no name in your taxonomy. External decontamination must be exhaustive.

But the harder decontamination is psychological. You will emerge—if you emerge—into a world of empty space, of light, of wind and silence that rings instead of presses. The openness will feel like loss. You will miss the warmth against your cheek. You will miss the certainty of being surrounded. You will, for a time, feel unbearably alone in the light.

This is normal. It passes. Or rather, some of it passes. Some of it stays, and you will spend the rest of your ordinary human life knowing, in the pit of your stomach, that darkness is not empty, that the quiet places are packed to suffocation, and that somewhere above the death-line of wind and snow, a mountain’s throat is breathing, patient and warm and older than the sun.

Pack accordingly. Move slowly. And when the tendril brushes your face in the dark, remember to breathe.
