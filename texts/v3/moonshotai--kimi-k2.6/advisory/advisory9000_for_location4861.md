**A Field Preparation Guide for the Viridian Basin**  
*(For the ordinary human multiverse traveler about to step into a held breath)*

---

### **1. First, Adjust Your Expectations of "Outside"**

You are not walking into a landscape. You are walking into something that has *only recently finished being made*. The basin is the size of a cathedral close—you can cross it in two hundred steps—but its smallness is a deception. The rim is sharp enough to suggest it was cut, not worn; the obsidian and viridian glass is newborn, still arguing with itself as it cools. Before you pack a single item, you must accept that this place is an experiment that has not yet decided whether it is a machine or a garden. It is wet, hot, deeply quiet, and quietly wrong. Nothing here is final. Treat it not as a destination, but as an incubation. You are a temporary variable entering a room-scale equation.

---

### **2. The Body: Conditioning for Breathable Soup**

The air is a thick, breathable soup. It will sheath your lungs and sheen your skin with sweat before you have taken ten steps. Crucially, the humidity is *absolute*—water is not present so much as it is unavoidable. This means your body’s primary cooling mechanism, evaporative sweat, will simply stop working. The heat comes from beneath the floor, not the sun, so there is no relief in shade. You are effectively entering a steam bath at an elevation high enough that clouds migrate below the rim.

**Physical preparation:**  
Spend time in a sauna, but not a dry one. Train in a steam room while wearing the clothing you intend to bring. If you can, train at altitude. The air may feel thick with moisture, but the elevation is real; altitude sickness is still a risk when clouds are beneath your feet. Cardiovascular fitness is non-negotiable. Your heart will work harder here, and in the architectural silence, you will hear it doing so.

**Hydration:**  
Begin hyper-hydrating with electrolytes three days before arrival. Bring more salt and mineral supplements than you think necessary. In absolute humidity, you will lose fluids through sweat that never evaporates, through breath that condenses before it leaves your lips, and through a pervasive osmotic demand from the air itself. Water is everywhere in the basin—weeping from walls, dripping from the rim—but do not drink it indiscriminately. It is part of the experiment. Carry your own.

---

### **3. What to Wear: Covering Skin Against a Mirror**

The floor and walls are new volcanic glass, carrying stress-fractures and cleavage planes that catch the light like broken mirrors. A fall here will not scrape you; it will slice you. The rock is still learning how to be rock, and it is sharp.

**Clothing:**  
Wear long sleeves and long trousers of lightweight, synthetic, quick-dry weave. Do not bring cotton; in this humidity it will become a sodden, heavy rag within seconds. Cover your skin. The glass is unforgiving, and the ambient temperature makes heavy protection impossible, so choose tightly woven, abrasion-resistant synthetics that dry faster than physics should allow (though nothing truly dries here). Pale colors are advisable to reflect the ordinary sunlight, since the geothermal heat from below is unavoidable.

**Gloves and Gaiters:**  
Bring thin, cut-resistant gloves. You will steady yourself against the walls, and the walls weep and drip and are made of fresh cleavage planes. Gaiters or reinforced ankle wraps are wise; the rim is sharp enough to cut, and the floor is uneven with new stone and thermal cracks.

**Footwear:**  
This is the most critical item in your pack. The floor is warm, in places too hot to touch. You need boots with thick, heat-resistant soles—ceramic or silica-composite layering is ideal—capable of insulating against conductive heat from below while providing extreme grip on wet glass. The bare stone between the fern islands is slick with condensation. Consider soft-carbide studs or volcanic-glass-specific traction, but avoid metal that will conduct heat into the sole. Ankle support is essential; the surface is unpredictable, and near certain artifacts, gravity becomes locally gentle. You do not want a twisted ankle in a place where sound does not carry.

---

### **4. The Silence: Preparing for the Submarine Hush**

Sound is swallowed here. The high air should carry, yet everything is muffled, submarine, pressed inward upon the eardrums. You will hear your own pulse, the creak of your synovial joints, the wet click of your own swallow. There are no insects, no birds, no animal eyes watching from the fern islands. The ecology is theoretical, a model without its animals.

**Psychological preparation:**  
Practice sitting in absolute silence until the sound of your own blood no longer unnerves you. The lushness—those saturated emerald ferns and dripping mosses—without the accompanying hum of life, creates a deep uncanniness. It is architectural. Do not bring music, podcasts, or audio entertainment. They will be swallowed by the hush and will only deepen your disorientation. If traveling with others, abandon verbal communication. Establish hand signals before you enter. Sound does not echo; a shout dies at your lips. Rope yourselves together if you must, but understand that the silence will make you feel profoundly alone even in company.

---

### **5. Navigating the Unfinished: Rhythm, Water, and Gravity**

The basin is young and still arguing with itself. It operates on slow, relentless dynamics that you must learn to read.

**The Eleven-Minute Metabolism:**  
Sync a waterproof watch to the southeastern vent. Every eleven minutes, it exhales a spiral coil of vapor that holds its shape with unsettling coherence before fraying. This is your clock. Do not stand near the vent when it exhales; the steam is likely superheated, and the mineral collars building around it like stone tree-rings accrete visibly over a day. The collars are hot and fresh. Do not touch them.

**Water That Forgets Its Place:**  
On the northern wall, certain sheets of water do not run downward. They creep upward, drawn by capillary action amplified beyond your physics. Do not trust water to behave. Waterproof your gear obsessively. The patient precipitation from the rim finds every seam, every pocket, every electronic device. If you carry a camera or sensor, assume it will be drowned by condensation unless rated for continuous submersion. The walls weep; the floor is perpetually in a state of post-rain. Embrace that you will be wet. Fighting it is exhausting.

**The Fern and the Sun:**  
The central fern, broad as an altar cloth, tracks the sun not by the hour but by the minute. If you watch it, you will become aware of time’s granularity here. Move slowly. The place rewards patience and punishes haste.

**The Islands:**  
Vegetation exists in perhaps a dozen isolated clots, separated by yards of bare stone. They are deliberate, balanced, like self-contained shrines. Do not walk through them. Step only on the bare wet rock between. Sterilize your boots before entering; this is a controlled experiment. Introducing foreign spores or skin flakes into a system this young and tentative may constitute an act of vandalism.

---

### **6. Encountering the Residue: Protocol for the Built Things**

You will find three or four artifacts, depending on the light. They are not ruins. They are residue—gentle violations of the expected.

**The Folded Form:**  
Half-melted into the glassy floor, there is a shape like folded origami in a material resembling titanium laminated with mother-of-pearl. It is warm, and it hums at a frequency felt in the teeth rather than heard. Do not press your ear to it. Do not attempt to pry at its seams. It is live equipment, and its resonance may interact with bone, dental work, or inner-ear equilibrium. Observe from a distance.

**The Hovering Object:**  
Three handbreadths above a thermal crack, something floats, perfectly silent, casting a shadow that falls at a slightly wrong angle—as if the light came from a sun not coaxial with your own. Near it, gravity is locally gentle. Secure your footing. Do not step directly beneath it. Colors desaturate here; edges blur. Your own reflection in the wet stone arrives half a second late. This delayed visual feedback can induce nausea and vertigo. If you feel your stomach lurch, back away. The photons here are negotiating an extra dimension, and your visual cortex was not built for that.

**General Rule for All Artifacts:**  
These things are waiting for the next instruction. Do not accidentally provide one. Do not touch, speak to, or focus excessive intent upon them. They are the engineers of this pocket, or at least their tools. You are a visitor in someone else’s laboratory. Treat the tech with the same caution you would give a half-drawn breath.

---

### **7. Eyes and the Problem of Normal Light**

The strangest part is that the light is ordinary. Under a gold-white sky, illumination is perfectly normal—until it isn’t. Near the hovering structure, light breaks wrong. Carry polarized sunglasses to cut the glare from the wet, broken-mirror cleavage planes of the glass and the drifting coins of brightness in the steam shafts. However, near the anomalies, trust your proprioception more than your eyes. The delayed reflection means your visual confirmation of your own body is out of sync. Move deliberately. Blink often. If you feel disoriented, focus on the ordinary green of the ferns; their color is intense enough to anchor you.

---

### **8. Thermal Management and Rest**

There is no stream to cool your feet in, no shade that offers true relief from the floor’s heat. You cannot sit on the ground everywhere; in places it is too hot to touch.

Bring a small, foldable, heat-resistant mat—silica fabric or ceramic cloth—so you can sit on the warm floor to rest without conducting heat into your body. You will want to sit. The pulse of the place—the eleven-minute vent, the upward creep of water, the faint turn of the floating thing—is best felt while stationary. But the heat builds silently. Monitor yourself for heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, confusion. In this humidity, the warning signs arrive suddenly.

---

### **9. The Rim and the Exit**

You stepped inside something recently made. The rim is sharp, and beyond it, clouds move at a distance that makes the basin feel like a room hung in the upper air. Getting out requires the same care as getting in. Do not approach the rim casually; the glass is new and brittle, and the drop is absolute. Mark your entry point with a bright, non-biological visual marker—something that will not be swallowed by the silence or the steam—so you can find your way back to the threshold.

---

### **10. Final Instruction: How to Be a Guest in a Held Breath**

The basin is suspended. The heat will not let it cool; the tech will not let it collapse into ordinary geology; the life will not yet colonize the bare stone. It is waiting.

Your job is to not disturb the waiting. Move slowly. Breathe the thick air without gasping. Let the sweat come. Listen to your heartbeat in the submarine silence. Do not expect the world to behave. Water may climb walls. Gravity may lighten near the floating thing. Your reflection may arrive late. The ferns may turn to follow the sun while you watch.

If you sit on your heat-proof mat in the center of the bowl, you will feel the slow pulse of it all: the vent’s sigh, the mineral accretion, the faint turn of the hovering residue. You are in a place that has not decided what it is. Do not rush it. Bring nothing final into it. Leave no instruction, take no sample, break no silence that your own body does not already make.

Prepare to be unnecessary. Prepare to be wet, hot, sharply aware, and very, very quiet. The bowl has only recently finished being made. It does not need you. But if you enter with the right humility, it may allow you to witness what it becomes next.
