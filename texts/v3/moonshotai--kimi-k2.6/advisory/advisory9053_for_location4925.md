**Traveler’s Brief: Preparing for the Saturated Works**

The Saturated Works is not a ruin you explore. It is a settlement you enter, one whose citizens are lignin and iron, whose metabolism is swamp water and slow torque, and whose patience is measured in decades. An ordinary human can survive a visit—can even dwell there for a time—but only if they surrender the impulses of speed, dryness, and dominion. Do not pack for a wilderness. Pack to become a temporary, tolerated anomaly in a municipal ecology that has already divvied up every cubic inch of space.

---

### **Rehearse the Proper Pace**

Before you cross the cofferdam, you must accept that *fifty feet may consume an hour*. This is not hyperbole; it is topography. The interior is strangled with a three-dimensional lattice of root, beam, and leaf. You will climb as often as you walk, squeezing between aerial roots that have become doorways and traversing catwalks where your weight is anticipated by bark rather than planks. 

Train for this by spending days in a crowded greenhouse or banyan grove, moving without breaking a twig. Practice stepping onto a surface that gives, that shifts, that requires you to redistribute your weight like a cat. If you cannot spend six hours in a sauna in absolute stillness, meditating on the drip of condensation, turn back. Impatience here is not merely rude; it is dangerous. Thrashing against the weave will dump you through a rotted root mat into a basement chamber of black, anaerobic water.

---

### **Dress to Be Occupied, Not to Conquer**

Everything you wear will become wet within minutes of breaching the boundary, and it will remain wet until you leave. Waterproofing is a fiction. Aim instead for *immersion compatibility*.

*   **Abandon boots.** A leather or synthetic boot fills with iron-brown water and becomes a fermenting anchor. The root mat expects a bare or near-bare tread; it has grown to accommodate footfalls. Wear close-fitting neoprene socks or thin-soled tabi booties that drain instantly and allow your toes to grip the braided roots and iron grating. The iron-flavored peat will squelch between your toes regardless; embrace it. Any covering on your feet is solely to prevent lacerations from rusted flanges, and it must be snug enough not to snag on strangler figs.
*   **Choose a single layer of close-fitting, hydrophobic fabric.** Long sleeves and long legs are necessary to protect against abrasion from lianas and the grit of rust, but the material must be slick and fast-draining. Silk-weight synthetics work; cotton and wool will absorb water, rot, and weigh you down like a sodden shroud. Avoid pockets, belts, buckles, and dangling straps. The Works will grab them. Use a seamless or flat-seam base layer, and accept that you will wear it until exit.
*   **Cover your head closely.** A wide-brimmed hat is a liability here; it catches condensation and drips a steady stream down your collar, and aerial roots will hook the brim. Wear a close-fitting cap or bandana solely to keep spores from matting in your hair. Your hair itself will be soaked; tie it back or shave it.

---

### **Carry Little, Seal What You Must**

The density of the interior makes a traditional backpack an exercise in frustration. Anything protruding from your back will catch on root curtains and I-beam lianas. Carry instead a small, smooth, cylindrical drybag slung at your hip or chest, where you can see and protect it.

*   **Double-seal everything.** The air itself is hydrophilic; paper becomes pulp in an hour, electronics corrode in days. If you carry maps, seal them in welded plastic pouches. Better yet, abandon paper. Your map is your memory. If you must record, carry a wax tablet and bone stylus—humidity will not destroy wax.
*   **Bring a probe.** A lightweight, collapsible carbon-fiber pole is your most vital tool. The root mat over the peat is treacherous; it looks solid until it is not. Probe every step. Test every catwalk for tremor before committing your full weight.
*   **Rope, not for climbing.** You are surrounded by living architecture; roots suffice for handholds. Bring fifteen meters of slick, non-absorbent spectra line solely to retrieve gear that tumbles through a rotted gap into a basement pool of black water. Do not enter those pools.
*   **No machete. No saw.** To cut a liana is to vandalize a load-bearing column. To sever an aerial root is to damage a thoroughfare. The Works permits visitors who pass through its existing channels; it does not forgive those who rearrange the street plan.

---

### **Calibrate Your Body**

This is an industrial greenhouse tuned to the temperature of blood and compost. Your physiology must be prepared as meticulously as your gear.

*   **Heat and humidity acclimatization:** Spend two weeks in a sauna or steam room, performing light calisthenics. You will not notice how much you sweat because the air is already saturated; dehydration creeps invisibly. Pre-load electrolytes and continue them religiously inside.
*   **Immunization and prophylaxis:** Tetanus is non-negotiable. The rust is not decorative; it is active. Begin a course of systemic antifungal prophylaxis three days prior. The spore load in the air is botanical, constant, and aggressive to human mucosa.
*   **Skin barrier:** Before entering, coat every fold of your body—between toes, groin, underarms, behind knees—in a heavy layer of zinc oxide or petroleum jelly. It will not keep you dry; nothing will. But it will buy hours against maceration and trench foot, buying time before your skin begins to slough.
*   **Hearing:** The sound is pervasive and low. The flywheel’s groan is felt in the sternum, and earplugs cannot stop bone conduction. However, wear semi-permeable musicians’ plugs tuned to attenuate the worst of the low-frequency pressure. The constant humid roar will otherwise induce a psychological fatigue—*green narcosis*—that slows reaction time to a dangerous degree.
*   **Lungs:** If you have any reactive airway disease, do not enter. The air is too saturated for sustained gasping. You are breathing warm vapor that has already passed through a thousand leaves. A damp bandana over the mouth will not filter spores, but it reduces the thermal shock to your throat.

---

### **Navigate the Lignin City**

Your compass is useless. The cast-iron lattice of pipes, flues, and machinery creates magnetic chaos. GPS signals, if you have them, are irrelevant; the canopy is a vault of chlorophyll and rust. Navigation is by memory, sound, and light.

*   **Learn the sonic landmarks.** The central flywheel groans once every four minutes; it is the heartbeat of the place. A standpipe water-hammers every ninety seconds—*thud*—a cardinal beacon. Condensation ticks on old fireboxes provide a granular texture. Learn these sounds as you would learn the bells of a foreign town.
*   **Read the light.** Unfiltered sun shafts appear pale and wet where the roof has fallen, backlighting root curtains. Where the canopy is intact, the light is emerald and slow-moving. Do not wear sunglasses; your eyes must adapt to the green tint, which alters depth perception. A bright, narrow-beam waterproof torch is useful for reading the textures of walls at close range.
*   **Use the street plan.** The Works is *municipal* in its organization. Root highways exist because weight has trained them. Follow the routes of bark and beam that link the eastern breach toward the center. Assign names to nodes: *The Fern-Held Wall. The Lily Piston. The Hot Pipe Cross.* If you cannot hold twenty such names in mind, do not venture deep.

---

### **Share Space with the Citizens**

The machines are still running. They do not hurry. They breathe.

*   **The Flywheel:** It turns at a geological pace, but it turns with unstoppable mass. Time your passage. Enter after the rim passes, move with its rotation, never against it. Do not touch the algae-fur on its axle; that living velvet is the bearing surface, and your skin oils are an insult to its economy.
*   **The Pistons:** A piston the width of a tree trunk rising through a cylinder of water lilies can still crush. It rises and falls in a cycle so slow that you may be tempted to rest in its shadow. Do not. Its patience outlasts yours.
*   **Hot pipes:** Mosses grow only on surfaces above forty degrees Celsius. If you see a pipe jacket naked of moss, bracketed by ferns, it is hot. Do not touch.
*   **Basements:** Where the root mat has rotted through, black water waits. It is the temperature of deep compost and the chemistry of an intestine. Do not wade it. Do not fall in it.

---

### **Eat, Drink, and Eliminate**

The ecology is nearly pure botanical; there are no insects to swarm your crumbs, but there is also no fast animal life to carry away your waste.

*   **Water:** Bring all of it. The brown water that weeps from the peat is iron-flavored and alive with anaerobic chemistry. Condensation on machinery is distilled, but collecting it is slow and requires sterile catchment. Plan for a gallon a day; the humid heat will demand it, even when you feel you are not sweating.
*   **Food:** Vacuum-sealed, high-density calorie bricks. Nothing loose, nothing crumbly. A single crumb dropped into a root crevice will bloom with mold in hours, becoming a wet green spot that marks you as a polluter. No chewing gum.
*   **Waste:** Remember the rule of the Works: *nothing is removed; everything is added.* Your urine introduces nitrogen. Your feces introduce bacteria foreign to this closed equilibrium. The citizens—roots, algae, slow engines—have reached a pact. Do not disrupt it. Seal all human waste in airtight bags and carry it out with you. You are permitted to visit because you are transient; do not make yourself a permanent amendment to the soil.

---

### **Sleeping in the Saturated Dark**

You cannot sleep on the ground; the ground is a sponge, and the black basements yawn beneath it. Bring a one-person mesh hammock with soft-webbing straps. Loop them around root arches or horizontal pipes at mid-level—high enough to escape the breathing water table, low enough that you are not entangled in the canopy vault. Do not use constrictor knots; the roots are growing, and strangulation will damage them.

Sleep will not come from silence, for there is none. The flywheel grinds, the standpipe thuds, the roots creak against girders like rigging. Let it become a lullaby. If you fight the noise, exhaustion will claim you. If you accept it, the low frequencies will rock you in a humid, geological cradle.

---

### **Health and the Slow Dangers**

*   **Any cut is a crisis.** Lacerations from rust, roots, or iron grating must be immediately flushed with sterile saline and sealed with tissue adhesive (liquid stitch). The black water carries anaerobic organisms that do not forgive delayed care.
*   **Immersion foot is inevitable.** Inspect your soles during every halt. If the skin turns white and puffy, elevate above the waterline, apply silver sulfadiazine or a strong topical antifungal, and wrap in a dry (yes, impossible, but *less wet*) bandage from your sealed kit.
*   **Green narcosis:** The combination of heat, spores, vibration, and lethargic light induces a profound torpor. Set a mental alarm. Force movement every hour. If you sit too long on a buttress of wood, you will stop distinguishing yourself from the architecture.

---

### **Crossing the Boundary**

Choose your breach carefully. There are three. Memorize the curtain of aerial roots you entered by—the way it hangs, the angle of the light behind it. 

Outside, beyond the rotted cofferdam, leave a sealed cache: dry clothes, dry shoes, a towel, and fresh water for rinsing. When you emerge, the outside swamp will feel shockingly cold and vertiginously open. You will shiver despite the mild air, because your body has attuned to the +5 degree greenhouse within. You will be sheathed in a film of algae, rust, and root sap. 

Wash immediately with fresh water. Burn or bury the garments you wore inside; they are colonized by spores and the chemistry of the Works, and they will continue to compost in your pack long after you have left. 

You are a traveler, not a settler. The Saturated Works will continue its patient, photosynthetic, industrial breath—converting water for sugar, iron for acid, sunlight for torque—hour by hour, year by year. You are privileged to have stood in the humid roar of its society. Leave no trace, disturb no root, and trust that if you move slowly enough, the city will let you pass through its lignin streets unswallowed.
