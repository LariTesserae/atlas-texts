**Preparatory Protocols for the Shelf of Held Equilibrium**

### I. The Inner Work: Unmaking the Narrative

The most lethal thing you can bring to the Shelf is a sense of *tomorrow*. Before you even think about your pack or your boots, you must conduct surgery on your own mind. Humans are creatures of entropy in the emotional sense: we require arcs, resolutions, progress, decay. We need breakfast to end so that lunch can arrive. We need conversations to conclude, wounds to close, and years to turn us into different people. The Shelf does not participate in this grammar. It has no future tense, and you must practice its language until your thoughts lose their forward momentum.

Spend two weeks—by your own homeworld reckoning—in absolute present-tense silence. Remove *will, soon, before, after, until,* and *because* from your vocabulary. Speak only in declaratives of immediate condition: *The kettle is hot. The floor is stone. I am here.* You will feel infantile, then insane, then strangely translucent. This is correct. If you arrive on the Shelf still carrying the psychological expectation that your visit will *build toward something*—a discovery, a departure, a change in your own understanding—the equilibrium will not harm you. It will simply not see you. And invisibility within a closed system is far worse than hostility; it means you will stand at the edge of the cistern, calling for help, while the inhabitants maintain their patterns around your frozen scream.

Grieve in advance for the self you are now. The Shelf does not permit becoming. It permits only being. If you have not accepted that you are, in this moment, sufficient, the plateau will teach you that lesson by erasing every other lesson you have ever learned.

### II. The Body in Equilibrium: Medicine and Material

At two thousand metres, the air is thin but not cruel. The cold is ceramic, not blade. Dress not for survival but for *inclusion*. The inhabitants wrap themselves in rust-coloured wool, woven to a density that accepts the eternal damp without mildewing or drying. Wool is the correct fiber; it breathes without changing state, and the locals’ weft has apparently synchronized with the equilibrium’s preference for stasis. Bring wool in natural or rust dyes. Synthetic fabrics that resist water too aggressively may chafe the air, which is already uncertain about newcomers. Cotton rots; linen stiffens; wool simply is.

**Do not bring iron.** This is not negotiable. The inhabitants built this city with bone spades, obsidian chisels, and antler mallets—not due to cultural innocence, but because the physics of the Shelf responds poorly to oxidation. Iron wants to become rust; rust is change. On the Shelf, change is a discourtesy. Your steel knife will not necessarily crumble, but it will suffer a kind of ontological nausea, weeping orange tears that never fully dry and never progress to flakes. Worse, the equilibrium may attempt to *hold* the iron in its original shape by force, creating a low-grade field of resistance that will migrate up your arm as a persistent ache. Use tools of stone, fired ceramic, bone, or volcanic glass. If you must cut, carry an obsidian blade. If you must dig, use a trowel of kiln-hardened clay.

Footwear is critical. The basalt avenues are cambered into troughs that channel condensation, and the stone carries the sheen of recent compression. The surface is mathematically precise but perpetually slick. Bring soft leather boots or fiber sandals—something that molds rather than grips. Rubber soles squeal against silence; their friction is too insistent, too modern. You want to move like the mist: present, but without pressure.

**Medical preparations:** Consult a chronobiologist or entropic physician before departure. Healing is a change of state; on the Shelf, wounds may refuse to close. Bring an aggressive suture kit, topical cellular accelerants from a high-momentum timeline, and coagulants that do not rely on your body’s own inflammatory response (which the local physics may interpret as unnecessary turbulence). If you are injured, expect the cut to hold its shape indefinitely—neither festering nor resolving—like a statement of fact carved into skin. Similarly, do not rely on the local water to facilitate normal digestion. The moisture here does not run; it condenses, beads, exhales. It participates without forcing issues. Swallowed, it may make your gut hesitate at the moment of absorption, leaving you full but not nourished, or nourished in a loop that your metabolism cannot exit. Seal your drinking water in thick-walled ceramic canteens—the text mentions water held in a ceramic cup, and this is the only container the physics seems to understand.

### III. The Pack: What to Carry and What to Leave

Pack light. The city was built for multitudes and is now terribly empty; the silence of the engineered space will make every object on your person feel loud and presumptuous. 

*Bring:*
- **A ceramic bowl and cup.** Unglazed is acceptable, glazed better. The local physics already understands ceramic; it will not behave strangely in your hands.
- **A spool of waxed thread or sinew.** Distance on the Shelf is deceiving. The grey, equal light and the absence of moving reference points will destroy your depth perception. If you need to find your way back to the seismic stair, lay a physical line. Maps are useless because nothing shifts; but because nothing shifts, the sameness becomes hallucinatory, and you will walk past your own trail without recognizing it.
- **A sand-timer from a chaotic realm.** Specifically, one that runs erratically. This is not decorative. It is a diagnostic.
- **A narrative anchor.** A long poem or song from your home, aggressively historical, full of battles and seasons and deaths. Something that celebrates becoming and fading. Carve it into a wooden tablet. Recite it twice daily. If the words begin to feel like nonsense, you are synchronizing. Leave immediately.
- **Non-consumptive light.** Do not bring flame. Fire on the Shelf warms without consuming beyond ritual replacement; your portable stove or torch may malfunction in ways that violate thermodynamics—burning endlessly without fuel, or refusing to ignite because the equilibrium perceives combustion as an unfinished negotiation between fuel and air. Use bioluminescence or cold phosphorescence.
- **Wool wraps in rust.** If you do not wear them, the inhabitants will see you as an unresolved equation.

*Do not bring:*
- **Cameras or digital recording devices.** Images here may refuse to complete. The shutter will click, but the data will hesitate, trapped in a state of unresolved potential. Worse, the act of trying to record the statues or the moths may trap *you* in the viewfinder’s logic: a single held moment that cannot progress to memory.
- **Wheeled luggage.** The text tells you the inhabitants had no wheel for hauling. This is not mere technological limitation; wheels imply destination, momentum, arrival. The Shelf has arrived. Wheels turn; turning is change. Carry your load on your back or balanced on your shoulder.
- **A watch with a second hand.** Timepieces with smooth motion may synchronize with the moth-wing pulse and stop registering anything but the plateau’s own breath.

### IV. Arrival: The Seismic Stair and the Standing Hour

You do not ascend the Shelf gradually. The land breaks along a clean fault, and you are suddenly two thousand metres above the lowlands, standing on stone that remembers compression. Your ears will pop. Your knees will object. Spend a full day at the base of the fault before taking the final step. Let your body understand that uplift here is perpetual and settling is forbidden.

When you step onto the plateau, do not move for one hour. Stand still. Let the mist condense on your lips and eyelashes. Let the cool air sit against your neck like water in a cup. Let the moth-winged creatures settle. If they do not settle—if they hang in the air, pulsing in perfect synchrony, but remain three centimetres from your skin—you are carrying too much intention. Descend. Try again in a season. If they settle, you have been tentatively tolerated.

Do not look directly at the sharp, unswaddled peaks for too long. They are infant mountains, a crown of broken black glass, and their youth is aggressive. They have not yet learned仁慈. Prolonged staring induces a kind of mental scree: the sensation that your own edges are too soft, too worn, and will be sheared off.

### V. Conduct on the Shelf: The Etiquette of Stasis

**Walk softly.** Sound is dampened by the saturated air, which means any noise you make is an intrusion. Your footsteps on the basalt processional will ring like bells in a library. Walk on the outer edges of the cambered avenues where the condensation troughs run; the gutter stones are softer tufa, and they accept weight with less protest.

**Speak in whispers, and only in the present tense.** Do not ask what was here before. Do not speculate on what will happen when the mist lifts—it does not lift; it only relocates according to memory. If an inhabitant approaches, perhaps a woman in rust wool re-seating a coping stone with an antler mallet, do not offer help. She is not repairing decay; she is maintaining the pattern. Your assistance would introduce deviation. If she hands you a tool, receive it with both hands. Use it exactly as she has used it, then return it before your palm memorizes its weight. Tools here have a gravity of their own; hold them too long and they will feel like extensions of the city, and your hand with them.

**Do not test the physics.** I cannot stress this enough. Do not drop a pebble into a cistern to see the ripples. The water will receive your offering, but the rings will hesitate, hold their shape, and subside into the same dark watermark that existed before your arrogance. The equilibrium tolerates your presence, but it does not need your curiosity. Do not stir the mist with your hand. Do not pluck a fern from the coping to watch it wilt; it will not wilt, and the act will mark you as a bringer of unresolved narratives.

**Do not build a fire.** The inhabitants warm themselves by flames that do not consume beyond ritual replacement. Your fire, fed by alien fuel and alien intent, may bind you to an endless loop of tending. You will sit before it, eternally replacing what it refuses to devour, until you realize you have become the pattern.

### VI. The Meal That Is Not a Meal

The grain is abundant. The goat-like browsers are numerous. The food satisfies. This is the trap.

Eat your own sealed rations. Cold. The local abundance participates in a cycle that does not diminish; to eat it is to enter the cycle. You may find, after a single meal of local bread, that you no longer hunger for home-food. You may find that your stomach has learned the local trick of eternal satiety without nourishment. The first symptom of capture is the loss of appetite for change. If you must drink local water, do so from your hands, not from the cisterns; the cisterns are too perfectly level, and to drink from them is to mirror their stasis inside your own blood.

### VII. The Creatures and the Green: Fauna, Flora, and the Risk of Entrainment

The fauna are abundant but silent. The moth-winged slate creatures pulse in synchrony. Avoid letting your breathing fall into their rhythm. If your blinks begin to match their wing-beats, close your eyes and recite your narrative anchor—loudly, if you must, though you will hate breaking the silence.

The ape-like things with spectacled eyes watch from the eaves. Treat them as furniture. Do not meet their gaze for long stretches. My intuition—and you asked for intuition—is that they are not fauna at all, or not originally. They are too still, too proprietary. They may be travelers who sat down to rest and found the equilibrium willing to hold them exactly as they were. If one extends a soft leather palm to you, do not touch it. Politely lower your eyes and step away.

The beetles are living jewels. Let them crawl on you. Brushing them off is a kind of rejection the physics does not perform here.

The vegetation rests in its own fullness. The sward does not yellow. The moss does not advance. Do not walk through the alpine meadows if you can walk around them. Your passage is a kind of narrative; the grass may hold your footprints indefinitely, neither springing back nor crushing, turning your path into a permanent scar of intrusion.

### VIII. Temporal Anchors and the Thread Home

You must prepare for the fact that the Shelf is seductive. The silence is profound. The absence of decay is a relief at first—no rot, no rust, no urgency. Then it becomes an oppression. Then it becomes a comfort. Then you stop noticing it. This is the progression of capture.

Before you arrive, arrange your extraction. If you travel with a companion, let them wait below the fault line, holding the end of a long line tied to your waist. Not a safety line—an *entropy line.* It should be made of something that degrades slightly, visibly, even in an hour: unbleached linen, raw hemp. If the line stops showing wear, if the fibers suddenly look freshly spun and newly tensioned, the equilibrium has extended upward to include you. Your partner must pull you back immediately. Do not argue. You will not want to come.

Set a hard limit. Decide, in the mutable world below, exactly how many cycles of grey-gold light you will endure. Do not trust your judgment once you are there. The light is the light of a held breath; it will convince you that deadlines are a form of violence against the present moment. They are not. They are the only thing keeping you human.

If you possess a sand-timer from a chaotic realm—one that runs too fast, too slow, sideways—check it daily. If the sand begins to fall in steady, identical intervals, in time with the moth-wings, in time with your own heartbeat, you are being synchronized. Descend the seismic stair at once. Do not pack. Just walk. Walk softly, but do not linger.

### IX. The Final Warning: Signs You Have Stayed Too Long

You will know you are becoming part of the Shelf when:

- You stop checking your sand-timer because *now is sufficient.*
- You wrap your cloak in the same rusty wool and cannot remember what colour you wore before.
- You see the generic statues lifting stone, and their faces begin to look like people you once knew, frozen in the act of building something they will never finish.
- You feel no grief for the wind that does not shift, but instead a profound relief.
- You open your mouth to speak, and no tense comes out but the present.

If any of these occur, descend. Do not run—running implies destination, and destination is change. Walk quickly and softly down the processional. Do not look back. Do not take a souvenir. A stone chipped from a wall here will not chip; it will remain a wound, and the equilibrium, which tolerates all things, may choose at last to hold you responsible.

The Shelf is not cruel. It is simply complete. It does not want you. It does not reject you. It offers, with terrible generosity, the possibility of a single moment extended forever.
