**Preparing for the Pocket Beneath the Dry Country**

You cannot plan your way into this place. That is the first thing to accept. The Pocket is not a destination; it is a *consequence*—the geological equivalent of a sigh caught in the throat of the world. You must first commit to being lost in the outer desert, to walking so far past the limits of markers and reason that your own footprints begin to seem like threats. Only then, perhaps, will the vitrified crust betray you, and you will fall *inward* rather than downward, through a flaw no thicker than a finger, into a room the earth forgot it was keeping.

Because arrival is accidental, preparation must be worn, not carried. Everything you bring must be distributed across your body, low-profile and shock-mounted, because the moment of entry is a violent stumble into geometry. You will land low, in a bowl of compressed ceramic and heat-sealed clay that has been settling for eons. Pack nothing that requires two hands to deploy; you will need them to break your fall and to catch yourself on the terraced slope below.

---

### **The Thermal Regime: Becoming Unappetizing to Heat**

The outer desert is merely dry. The Pocket is *dense*. The air here does not sting; it presses. It has mass, a muffling warmth that wraps the lungs and seems to push back against your eyeballs. The temperature hovers in that range where stone becomes feverish, where the boundary between your body and the atmosphere begins to dissolve into a shared, uncomfortable glow. Ordinary desert gear will cook you alive.

You will need an **active cooling underlayer**—a liquid-circulation garment, or at minimum a phase-change vest with replaceable packs rated for extreme radiant heat. Your outer shell must be **insulated against infrared**, not just reflective. Remember: the vegetation here does not photosynthesize from sunlight. It drinks the infrared wash seeping from the cracks below. If you present as a warm mammalian silhouette against the terraces, the crimson mats will notice. Over hours, they will creep toward you, extending enzyme-heavy tendrils across the ceramic floor. You do not want to wake from a rest break to find yourself being slowly digested by velvet.

Your boots must have **aerogel-ceramic soles** thick enough to let you stand on a frying pan. The floor is not merely hot; it is a radiant organ, pulsing in slow burgundy cycles. Standard rubber will soften and bond to the vitrified clay. Gloves, likewise, must be heat-rated and non-porous, because you will need to steady yourself against walls that expand and contract with the thermal tide.

Hydration cannot be overstated. There is no water in the Pocket. The cisterns hold dust or perhaps air; the channels manage temperature, not moisture. Carry a **closed-loop hydration system** with an insulated bladder and tubing—standard plastic hoses will leach and may soften in the ambient heat. Double your water estimate. Then double it again. The air is stewing in its own minerals, and every breath you take is a transaction of moisture for iron-heavy dust.

### **Breathing the Pocket**

The atmosphere is ancient. It has been scrubbed by mineral winds and baked until it is more a broth of particulate than proper air. A **full-face respirator with HEPA and mineral-fume filters** is non-negotiable. Silicate dust, mica flakes from the stratified walls, and the fine inert powder shed by the digesting mats will otherwise settle in your lungs with the patience of geology. The heat will try to warp your mask seals; use high-temperature silicone and check them constantly. Do not rely on passive cloth. The Pocket has no mold, no rot, no pollen, but it has *stone* in the air, and stone, in sufficient quantity, is a suffocating agent.

---

### **Seeing by the Planet’s Blood**

Light here is starved. What illumination exists seeps upward from hairline cracks in a palette of ember-red and slow-pulsing burgundy. Human eyes adapt poorly to this submarine glow; depth perception falters, and the shadows pool like old wine, swallowing the open frames of buildings and the edges of terraces.

Bring **low-light amplification** tuned to preserve red-spectrum sensitivity. Do not use white light. A bright torch in the Pocket is an act of vandalism—it will bleach your vision for hours and disrupt the subtle infrared ecology you are trespassing within. If you must supplement the glow, use a dim, narrow-beam red lamp. Better yet, learn to navigate by the cracks themselves: the brightest lines indicate the lowest, hottest terraces; the dimmer zones are safer for rest, though nowhere is cool.

### **The Symphony of Vacancy: Auditory Survival**

It is loud. This surprises everyone. The Pocket is not a tomb; it is an instrument. The thermal tide forces air through ancient clay channels, hollow pillars, and the narrow throats of vent systems built by hands that never knew metal. The result is a resonant drone, somewhere between an Aeolian organ and a moan, that fills the avenues without cease. Against this, the dark fronds clatter with the sound of heavy fabric tearing, and ceramic pipes, heated to their limit, split along hidden flaws with the sharp clarity of a bell struck underwater.

You must protect your hearing without isolating yourself. Use **filtered acoustic earplugs** that dampen the low-frequency drone but preserve the high-end crack of splitting ceramic. That sharp note is structural warning. A pipe splitting overhead may shower the avenues with vitrified shards, or signal that a nearby wall is about to shed a layer of compressed ash. The hum will also disorient your sense of space; it has no source and no direction. Practice navigating by touch and gyrocompass in loud, featureless environments before you arrive.

### **Biological Protocol: The Monolith**

There is only one kingdom of life here, and it has filled every niche. There are no insects to sting you, no predators to hunt you, no rot to infect your wounds. The danger is slower and more total. The crimson mats and the carbon-dark stalks with their rust-colored scales are a single continuum of being. They do not compete; they *occupy*.

**Do not touch the vegetation.** The mats secrete enzymes that digest ancient clay to extract minerals. They will not distinguish between the terraced wall and the clay-dust coating on your gear, or the keratin of your boots, or the dead skin on your knuckles. The fronds are heavy with accumulated mineral dust and will abrade skin on contact. Worse, they are thermotropic: they move toward heat sources over the course of hours. If you sleep on the floor, you may wake to find yourself partially enveloped, your body heat having drawn the garden like a tide.

Set camp on an **elevated, insulated platform**—a folding cot with ceramic-composite legs, placed on the most stable avenue you can find. Shake out your boots before putting them on. The mats creep silently.

### **Structural Etiquette in a Skeleton City**

The settlement is urban in logic but hollow in fact. The builders used rammed earth vitrified to ceramic, blocks of compressed ash, and strata of clay, sand, and crushed mica. Time and thermal cycling have polished every broken edge to a satin smoothness, but the bones are brittle. The wide avenues are safe; the open frames of buildings are not. Do not climb. Do not trust a wall to bear your weight simply because it looks thick. The infrastructure was designed to manage the exhalations of the Pocket, not to support a human interloper.

The city breathes. As the glow from below waxes, the clay walls expand minutely; as it wanes, they contract. You will feel this if you lean against a surface. Do not wedge yourself into crevices for rest, and do not block the narrow clay channels that thread the terraces. They are the Pocket’s arteries. Obstructing them could cause a pressure spike, a split pipe, or a localized surge of superheated air.

Watch the ceiling. It is close, gnarled with semi-molten drips weathered into soft stalactites that almost brush the upper roofs of the hollow buildings. Mineral deposits of verdigris and sulfur-flower mark the places where heat escapes upward. These patterns can guide you, but the stalactites themselves are not stable. A helmet is advisable—not for impact, but for the slow, patient rain of mineral grit dislodged by the thermal pulse.

### **Navigation and the Problem of Exit**

The Pocket is small—no larger than a collapsed amphitheater—but it is a bowl, and bowls disorient. The entry flaw is a puckered wound in the ceiling, invisible against the gnarled stone and the ruddy glow. Once inside, you will not see it unless you know where to look.

Before you move from your landing point, **mark it**. Use a heat-resistant, non-biological marker: a strip of reflective metal foil, a spool of high-temperature wire, something that will not be digested by the mats or degraded by the heat. An inertial gyrocompass or a simple pendulum mapping device is more reliable than visual landmarks, because the vegetation moves. The straight avenues are mathematically precise but the shadows shift with the pulse below, and the creeping crimson carpets will alter the silhouette of a terrace within a single thermal cycle.

Your exit is that single flaw in the crust. There are no other doors. The Pocket is sealed by a law of contained recursion; nothing leaves. You must be prepared to climb, to chimney up between a stalactite and a building frame, or to rig a grapple and line to the ceiling if the geometry allows. Bring **lightweight, heat-rated climbing gear** and a rope that will not off-gas in the mineral-laden air.

### **Psychological Inoculation**

This is the hardest preparation. The Pocket is not alien enough to be unrecognizable, which makes it worse. It is a town square, a garden, a civic space—but all the wrong things have flourished. You will stand in a plaza wide enough for markets, and you will be the only animal. There are no voices, no insect threads in the air, no scent but the flat, hot smell of old ceramic. The red light will leach the color from your skin. The constant, loud hum will make your own heartbeat seem like an intrusion.

You must prepare for **profound acoustic and ecological loneliness**. The symphony is ceaseless and utterly without voice. Do not shout; your voice will die in the dense air, absorbed by the heat, and the silence that swallows it will feel like an insult. Move slowly. The Pocket has been patient for eons; your haste is profane. Keep a physical journal—ink on paper, as electronics may fail in the sustained heat—and record your thoughts before the hum erases them.

Accept that you are a contaminant. The ecology runs on heat and the slow digestion of the architecture itself. Introduce nothing: no shed skin if you can help it, no spilled water, no foreign seeds. Pack out every thread and drop. You are visiting a secret that the earth has kept not by hiding it deeply, but by sealing it *perfectly*. Behave as though you have no right to be there, because you do not. The Pocket will tolerate your presence, but it will not welcome you.
