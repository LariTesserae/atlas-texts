To prepare for the Altiplant is to accept that you are not a visitor but a *temporary anomaly* in a system that has industrially perfected the art of not falling apart. It does not want you dead; it simply has no protocol for hosting. Your goal is to arrive already corrected—for the Floor, the damp, the light, and the silence to pass over you as if you were merely another maintenance interval, brief and mechanically inevitable.

**First: Make Your Breath Quiet**

The air is thin at that elevation, cool and insufficient, yet you cannot compensate with the usual human noisy gasping. Gasping is sound, and sound here is heresy against the suture. Before you transit, spend weeks in altitude chambers until hypoxia feels like a default state rather than an emergency. Train yourself to take shallow, glacial sips of air—almost nothing, just enough to keep the lamp lit. If you carry supplemental oxygen, route it through a rebreather that muffles your exhalations. Every breath must be interior. The fauna are silent; the moth does not pant. You may not either.

Bring chewing apparatus—gum, a silicone bite guard, dried leather to work with your molars. The subsonic pressure will bloom behind your jaw like a second heartbeat, a tectonic suggestion that your skull is being gently pressed from the inside. You must have a way to meet that pressure without screaming into the fog.

**Second: Dress for Aggression, Not Comfort**

The Floor is vitreous ceramic and cordcrete, recent enough that its blueprint still aches. It has not learned mercy. Its grit carries creation-static; its chamfered edges retain the exact cut of their intention. You will want boots that are puncture-resistant and, crucially, *sound-dead*. Soft polymer soles, felt underlayers, something that grips without grinding. No clicking heels. No ringing tread. You should sound, when you walk, like a shadow deciding whether to fall.

Wear greys, oxidized silvers, the pale blue of transformer housing. Do not wear green unless you are prepared to be mistaken for the green-glass staircase. Do not wear black; black is not a color here, only an absence that the actinic light erases. Your silhouette should be tubular and narrow. In the lower troughs, shoulders brush three alloys at once; a backpack that protrudes six inches will snag, clang, and reverberate through the mist like a struck bell. Compress everything against your body. Become a conduit yourself, smooth and continuous.

The damp is constant and scheduled. It does not dry. Waterproof yourself head to toe, but ensure your outer layer does not *squeak*. Synthetic shells that remain silent when folded are preferable to waxed canvas that whispers with every joint bend. The water is not weather; it is accounting. Do not let your perspiration or your gear’s exhalations add unauthorized moisture to the closed loop. If you must carry water, do so in vacuum-sealed bladders that cannot sweat.

**Third: Prepare for a Brightness That Has Never Slept**

There is no sun, only the waste-light of engines that smelt causality. The illumination is sterilizing, recursive, and absolute. It will strip your circadian rhythm to a bare wire. Before arrival, accustom your eyes to permanent noon. Stop using darkness to rest. Carry a piece of absolute opacity—a velvet blindfold, a thick scrap of felt from a place that still has nights—and learn to sleep beneath it while the world around you screams with grey photons. Photochromic lenses will help, but they cannot darken the inside of your skull. You must build your own night and carry it against your face.

Shadows here are merely thinner grades of grey, not refuge. You cannot hide. Do not bring camouflage that depends on contrast or concealment; there is no shrub, no gully, no umbra in which to vanish. Instead, bring *congruence*. Become the color of ducts and retaining walls. Pass not by hiding, but by aligning.

**Fourth: Silence Is Not A Suggestion; It Is Load-Bearing Structure**

This is the most critical preparation. The Altiplant’s silence is not eerie mood; it is engineering. If the contradictions heard themselves, they would unravel. You must therefore become structurally silent. Pad every carabiner, every tool handle, every zipper pull with felt or surgical tubing. Wrap your cookware, if you must cook, in silicone. Do not speak. Do not whisper. Do not hum. Practice anechoic living for a month prior: learn to communicate by pressure on the wrist, by gesture, by the exchange of written notes on soft, uncrinkling paper.

If you are injured—and between the sharp edges and the gravity shifts, you may well be—you must not cry out. Pain in the Altiplant must be swallowed. A scream is not merely noise; it is a resonant frequency that could loosen a paradox rivet half a valley away. Train now to suffer quietly. Become someone who could be impaled on an I-beam of impossible geometry and only exhale, slowly, through the nose.

**Fifth: Navigate By Inertia and Faith, Not Maps**

Cartography is useless. The foundry outside is a broom closet; the staircase a cathedral. Your reflection greets you three seconds before you arrive. Gravity in the alleys negotiates with walls. To prepare, abandon the compass. Abandon GPS. Instead, cultivate *propriohemianism*—the discipline of trusting your inner ear more than your eyes. Practice falling sideways in your mind’s gymnasium. Learn to read local down by the bead of condensation on a duct: if the water clings horizontally, that surface is the new floor.

Carry an ontological anchor. This is not mystical; it is a dense object from your home continuum, something with an unbroken causal chain—a river stone from your childhood, a bolt from the machine that brought you here, anything that remembers what linearity feels like. When your reflection precedes you, or when time inside a pylon seems to run counter to your heartbeat, grip this anchor. It will not fix the Altiplant, but it will fix *you* relative to your own story.

A simple gyroscopic level may help, but only if you are willing to accept its madness: when the horizon tilts ninety degrees, trust the level and let your body reorient. Do not cling to verticality. The Altiplant was built without regard for an underlying planet’s curve; there is no true up, only local specification.

**Sixth: Eat Your Own Causality; Drink Nothing Local**

Do not eat the rats, though they are plump. Do not eat the moths, though they are bright. Do not drink the mist, though it beads temptingly on every bright lip of metal. The rats dine on synthetic detritus and *leaked potential*; their flesh is woven with stabilized paradox. The mist is not water in the hydrological sense but an ontological ground, a closed-loop respiration that keeps the reality-weave from flaring. To drink it is to risk grounding your own potential out of your body. You may become very structurally stable, and very difficult to leave.

Bring all your food. Dry, dense, silent food. Nut-butter in soft pouches. Compressed bricks of grain. Nothing that needs to be boiled, because boiling is noisy and steam is unauthorized moisture. Seal your waste; the Altiplant does not digest foreign biology, and your sweat and urine are not part of the scheduled damp. You are a closed system visiting a closed system. Respect the boundary.

**Seventh: Learn the Etiquette of Maintenance Fauna**

You are not the only animal present, though you are the least adapted. The moths are photovoltaic citizens. If one lands on your wrist and folds its wings, freeze. It is not resting; it is sampling your luminescence. Wait until it departs of its own accord. The translucent, boneless mammals slipping through gaps in the kinematic housing are harmless inspectors of condensation and hum. Do not reach for them; your hand will not fit where their bodies go, and your reaching constitutes an event.

The rats with too many elbows have more axes of movement than you. They are maintenance heirs. If you encounter their gaze from within a baffled housing, do not threaten them. They are not afraid of you, and in their environment, spatially, they hold the advantage. Step back. Narrow your profile. Let them inherit the corridor.

**Eighth: Slow Until You Approach Geological Stillness**

The great flywheel turns once per decade. The conveyor of half-objects advances at the speed of ice shelving. Your metabolism, your anxiety, your human urgency—these are errors in this context. Before arriving, practice slowness until it feels like dignity rather than restraint. Move as if you are compressing yourself through a viscous causal medium. Pause after every third step. Not to listen—there is nothing to hear—but to allow the machinery to register you as non-anomalous.

**Ninth: Accept That There Is No Nature to Console You**

There is no weather. There is no earth. There is no biological soil offering its forgiveness. Prepare your mind for a world without mercy or cruelty, only *specification*. The silver lichen and the hesitant fibre-moss are not nature reclaiming a space; they are permitted oxidation, tolerated humidity stains. They do not grow; they are resulted.

Grieve this before you arrive. Leave your ecological nostalgia at the departure gate. If you enter the Altiplant still hoping for a tree, a breeze, or a bird song, your disappointment will make you loud. You must become temporary infrastructure: functional, deliberate, unhurried, and entirely made.

**Tenth: Do Not Trust the Terraces to Lead You Home**

The Floor steps down in measured terraces toward a sourceless, bright horizon. You will feel the instinct to follow them, to descend, to find the mountain that must be below this plateau. There is no mountain. There is no below. The curvature of the underlying world was deliberately ignored during installation. The terraces are aesthetic or functional, not geographical.

Your exit must be *your* mechanism. Keep your transit device, your return key, your multiverse pickup signal ready and charged. Do not make it dependent on local conditions, for the local conditions are a wound held shut by density and intent. The Altiplant is in no hurry to unmake, and it is equally in no hurry to release you. Approach it as a maintenance form approaches a turbine: with knowledge, with silence, and with an exit strategy that does not require the machine’s permission.

Arrive soft-soled, soft-breathed, densely anchored, and interior. Let your presence be negligible. Let the moth depart of its own accord. Let the moisture bead and fall according to schedule. And when you stand still, as you must, let your stillness be indistinguishable from the Floor’s own remembered making.
