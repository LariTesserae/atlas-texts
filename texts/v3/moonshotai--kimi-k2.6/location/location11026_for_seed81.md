You do not arrive at the place so much as you notice the distance beginning to win. The road, if it can be called that, is a causeway of vitrified stone that has softened over centuries into something like worn enamel. It ends at a railing, and beyond the railing is not a view but a *volume*—an immense, regulated hollowness that extends for miles in every direction, high on a broken-backed plateau where the air is thin but somehow does not cool the lungs. It is very hot here. The heat does not strike; it accumulates, a debt paid by the body to the stone. The rock beneath the causeway radiates upward with a dull, steady glow, a fever that has lasted longer than memory.

This is a city. You must keep reminding yourself of that.

Its urban character is undeniable if you look at any single part. There is infrastructure here of profound sophistication and ambition. But the defining feature is the *arrangement of absence*. Between the structures lies nothing—vast, calculated volumes of clear, steaming air that have been mapped, titled, and serviced as diligently as any crowded avenue. The built environment occupies perhaps one part in ten thousand of the total space, yet it is unmistakably a metropolis: towers of open lattice the width of cathedrals, suspension bridges broad enough for armies, aqueducts that arch from mesa to mesa and terminate in empty plazas suspended over chasms. The engineering is layered, recursive, dense with intent. But the population, the filling, the *matter*—all of it is missing. You are standing in the bones of a whale that was hollow to begin with.

The age of the place is evident in the weathering. The metal—an alloy the color of dried blood and tarnished bronze—has not rusted through so much as *settled*, its edges becoming pillowy, its rivets sinking like old teeth into gum-iron. Stone has been licked smooth by a million years of condensate. This is not a ruin; it is an established form, long ago having reached equilibrium with whatever forces shaped it. It is simply doing what it was made to do, but without an audience.

Water is the true citizen here. The atmosphere is saturated, a perpetual hot-bath exhalation that never quite becomes rain. Instead, it weeps. Every surface sweats. Hot droplets collect on the undersides of catwalks, growing fat and pendulous before they fall in slow, silver arcs to decks hundreds of feet below. The sound is distinct in the hush: a scattered, arrhythmic percussion, *plink*, *tock*, *plish*, like a clock dismantling itself one second at a time. It condenses on your skin before you even feel the moisture in the air. In the deeper hollows, where the heat pools and the crosswinds die, there are marshes of a kind—not of peat and reed, but of sinter and mineral crust, where hot springs percolate upward through fissures and form mirror-flat pools the color of verdigris and iodine. The water table breathes just beneath the surface. Step heavily, and the stone sighs back.

There are no plants. None. The palette is strictly mineral: the rust-red of oxidized infrastructure, the chalk-white of precipitated salts, the obsidian gleam of ancient slag, the sulfur-yellow of vent-stains. It is a pre-life or post-life aesthetic, a chemistry-set world too hot and too laden with metallic vapors for cellulose to bother. If there is biology below the scale of seeing—anaerobic films, chemosynthetic lace—it does not announce itself. The landscape is a conversation between rock, water, and heat.

And yet the city moves. It is active. Somewhere in the thorax of a distant tower, a piston the height of a house cycles with a rhythm slower than a heartbeat, compressing steam whose purpose is now entirely its own. Vents open and close on thermal timers, exhaling columns of white vapor that climb until they hit the dim ceiling of cloud and spread into a roof of false weather. The entire plateau generates its own climate: evaporation, condensation, convection cells that track like migrating herds through the lattice. It is an ecosystem of pressure-valves and expansion joints, a predator-prey dynamic played out between temperature differentials. You can feel the place *metabolizing*.

The light is poor. A dim, bronze twilight persists regardless of the hour. The sky, if there is a sky above the haze, is occluded by a permanent thermal lid of steam and ash-cloud that diffuses what illumination exists into a directionless glow. Shadows are shallow, uncertain, sometimes doubled. The brightest things are the deep furnaces still burning in the cores of certain towers—round, red eyes that do not blink, casting no beams, only a smoldering presence. In this half-light, the empty spans between buildings look solid. The void gains a velvet density. It becomes easy to believe that the city was built not to house bodies, but to shape and contain the emptiness itself—that the architecture is merely a mold, and the negative space is the true product.

It is quiet. Not silent, but *hushed*, as if the vastness swallows edges off every sound. What reaches you is low, subsonic, felt in the molars and the sternum: the groan of cooling metal, the subterranean gulp of water draining through pipes wide as subway tunnels, the rare, crystal chime of a thermal fracture opening in glassy stone. Sound travels strangely in the heat. A drip from a nearby rail arrives sharp and immediate, while the groan of a distant engine seems to emanate from the air beside your ear.

Life—complex, macroscopic life—is rare to the point of myth. You may walk for hours and see nothing move but steam. Then, perhaps, a flicker at the edge of vision: a thing like a kite made of mica and chitin, riding an updraft between two chimneys, filtering condensate through filamented wings. Or the slow, jointed traverse of a beetle the size of a dog, its shell an alloy of native copper and carapace, picking its way along a conduit in search of heat-blooms. They are punctuation marks in an otherwise blank text. You learn to treasure them not for companionship, but for the proof they offer that scale and emptiness have not entirely won.

The strangeness of the place is cumulative. It accrues. You begin to notice that the largest plazas are not floored—they are sheer drops into fog, edged only by low rails, as if crowds were expected to hover. You notice that some staircases ascend into blank walls, and some doorways open onto the outside of the structure, facing a hundred-foot fall. The city obeys a logic of perspective and pressure rather than utility. Water, at times, does not fall. In certain shafts, the thermal gradient is so precise that droplets hang, trembling, or drift sideways, collecting in mid-air pools that float like jellyfish before they collapse into steam. Gravity here is not so much weaker as it is *negotiable*, a courtesy extended differently to solids than to vapors.

After a time, you understand the rule of the place: it is a machine for waiting. It is industrial, yes—there are the gears, the vents, the rails, the vast sedimentation tanks that once separated ore from brine—but the product was always a kind of patience. It was built to outlast whatever filled it. Now it simply continues, hot and wet and mostly empty, refining the atmosphere, dripping into its own hollows, keeping the shape of a civilization as a glass keeps the shape of its missing wine. It does not need you. It does not need anything. It holds its breath, and the breath is steam, and the steam is all there is.
