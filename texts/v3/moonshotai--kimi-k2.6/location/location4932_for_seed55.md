There is no ground here, only the bottom of making. 

You descend through leagues of cold, still water, past the last faint scrim of borrowed light from above, into a darkness that has structure. The darkness is not empty. It is inhabited by the oldest-stable-state of architecture, a continental plain where every square span is artifact. No basalt bubbled up from a mantle here, no limestone settled from ancient seas. The seafloor is entirely built: a drowned tessellation of hand-dressed stone, petrified timber, compacted bone-ceramic, and mineralized fiber, packed so densely that the concept of “street” or “chamber” has long since dissolved into something more like geology. Over a vastness large enough to birth its own slow weather systems, the elevation never rises more than a few dozen meters. It is a low place, sunken not dramatically but terminally, as if a world of low walls and causeways chose to lie down and let the water settle over it like a final blanket.

The materials speak of patient, pre-metallic hands. Blocks the size of hay-carts sit fitted one against another without mortar, their joinings still tight after eons of compression. The stone is not quarried in any modern sense; it was persuaded loose with wedges and antler, then dragged and abraded until the corners, now, are soft as fat thumbs. Petrified beams—oak or some analog rendered gray and iron-heavy with permineralization—bridge gaps between the masonry, their grain still visible under crusts of deposited silica. In places, compressed strata of woven reed and clay tile have fused into a kind of brittle, stratified shale, flaking off in sheets that drift downward through the water column like pages torn from a book. There is no iron, no bronze, no alloy to stain the stone green. If tools were left behind, they were stone, and they have become indistinguishable from the construction itself.

Temperature hovers near the freezing point, but the water will not ice. The salinity, or the pressure, or some slow chemical exhale from the ruins themselves keeps it liquid and thick. It moves not in currents but in breaths. A column of water might take a decade to migrate from the northern basin to the southern rim. Thermal layers stack like blankets of different weights, and when they slip, whole districts shiver with a displacement so gradual that a visiting body—if any existed—would feel only a sudden, deep chill, then nothing for years.

Light is practically absent. At the surface, far above, there may be days and suns, but down here the darkness is 0.2 of nothing: a blackness relieved only by the occasional phosphorescent punctuation of the flora. No animal life stirs to make light of its own. There are no fish to dart, no worms to burrow, no crustaceans to pick. The fauna is absent, and so the silence is nearly total. Sound, when it comes, is structural: the *tock* of a beam settling another micrometer into its socket, the *shush* of a mudslide accumulating against a retaining wall, the slow, infrasonic groan of two continental-scale weight-distributions adjusting their conversation across the millennia. It is a place that has finished speaking and now merely breathes.

Yet it is lush. Without herbivores, without predators, without the churn of animal motility, the vegetation has claimed this constructed world as a single, undivided garden. The plants here are not the green things of sunlit topsoil. They are the chemotropic and osmotrophic heirs to a planet of marine snow: broad, rubbery fronds the color of dried blood that unspool from the mouths of sunken cisterns; curtains of filamentous algae draped between lintels like rotting tapestries; tuberous growths that swell from the mortar-lines, their surfaces beaded with cold bioluminescent dew that is their waste, their respiration, their only comment on the dark. Where a roof has fallen in—or perhaps was built open—these growths mass especially thick, creating submarine groves so dense that to push through them would be to force your way through a jungle of wet felt and pemmican. They feed on the leaching minerals of the ruins, on the slow rain of organic detritus from waters impossibly far above, and on each other. Roots, if they can be called roots, have prized apart some of the smaller fittings, but so slowly that the architecture has had time to settle around the insult. The plants do not destroy here. They inherit.

The density of the built environment is absolute. In the most ancient sectors, the original plan—if there was one—has been forgotten under accretion. Walls were built against walls until the alleyways became slots, then seams, then mere hydraulic passages. Domes of compressed clay tile sag against one another in ranks like eggs in a crate. You could travel from one edge of this drowned continent to the other without ever touching an open plain; there is only the next room, the next threshold, the next low vault. It is a three-dimensional agglomeration, a hive of negative space carved from positive material by nothing more than the settling of weights and angles over millions of years.

And it is old. The erosion is not the violence of cliff-fall or flood-scour but the deep, democratic patience of water that has agreed to a truce. Corners are not broken but eroded into consensus. Once-sharp arrises are now ellipses. The entire landscape has achieved a kind of final form, a near-flattened state where nothing will change quickly anymore. A block may descend another finger-width in ten thousand years. An algal curtain may thicken and then die and mineralize into a new stratum of pseudo-stone. The weather systems of this place—thermal halos, turbidity currents, pressure differentials between dense groves and open crypts—move with the same cadence. It has outlived the distinction between ruin and landscape.

The rules of the world are simple and severe. Everything is substrate. Everything is salvage. The only metabolism is vegetable, the only history geological, and the only time scale glacial. Water does not enter here; it *is* here, the eternal medium. The built environment, for all its evident intentionality, has become a kind of natural formation—except that nature was never allowed to start the job. What you are seeing is a world where a civilization, working with flakes and mallets and cordage, completed a continent. Then the water came, or perhaps had always been coming, and they left, or they never existed in any form we would recognize as life, or they became the bone-ceramic in the walls. Now the place waits, cold, dark, verdant, silent, finished. It requires nothing. It offers only the spectacle of matter at the end of its first long thought, resting in the dark while the fronds fatten and the stones decide, with infinite leisure, where exactly they wish to lie for the next million years.
