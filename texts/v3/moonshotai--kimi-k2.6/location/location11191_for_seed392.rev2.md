The floor of the world is a shallow hand-basin pressed into being. Not excavated—there is no natural earth beneath it, no bedrock, no magma. The material is a pale, porous composite, something between unfired clay and compacted bone-meal, extending downward until the mind loses interest in depth. It was laid while still soft, shaped by patience rather than machinery, and it has since petrified in place. The erosion is infantile. Corners are knife-sharp. Ridges and furrows carry the unmistakable signatures of simple tools: the broad, tearing striations of obsidian adzes; the jittered stippling of antler picks; the dragging comet-trails of fingers working material that yielded like damp dough. Nothing has been worn smooth. Nothing has been rounded by time. The landscape has only just arrived, and it has not yet been touched by a single tomorrow.

Heat sits here like a solid object. The temperature is not weather; it is the basin’s own fever, a low, constant exhalation from the substance of the place. The air is a hot broth, thick enough to chew. Moisture is not an event but a permanent condition. It does not rain—rain would require a sky capable of change, and change does not occur here. Instead, water simply exists, condensing on every surface in beads too heavy for mist and too fine for rain. The humidity weeps from the tool-marks in the basin walls, filling the carved grooves with living silver. Capillary action pulls it in impossible trajectories, laterally across vertical faces, spiraling up the stepped terraces, as if the water itself has not yet agreed on which way to fall. It gathers in broad, lens-shaped pools across the pale floor, pools with no inflow and no outlet, their surfaces taut as drum-skins, perfectly still.

And perfectly silent.

You can stand at the basin’s center and hear nothing but the blood in your own ears. No drip of water. No rustle of vegetation. No insect buzz, though the air is thick with life. The wetness makes no sound. A stone dropped into a pool sinks, but the surface seals behind it without a splash, leaving only a bruise that fades slowly, like ink dispersing in gel.

Vegetation erupts in deliberate pockets, as if the makers left behind vast dishes, troughs, and stepped planters intended for some nursery or garden. Now they are buckling under lushness. Enormous leaves—waxy, heart-shaped, deep viridian shading into black—press against each other in a competition for space that reached its crescendo and was frozen there. Vines hang in veils but do not sway. Flowers hang open, perpetually dripping nectar in amber threads that never quite break, connecting bloom to manufactured soil in eternal, sagging catenaries. The soil itself is built: layers of matted fiber, crushed shell, pulverized ceramic, and something like composted hair, all held in beds framed by rough monoliths and lashed with braids of dried sinew that have not tightened or loosened since they were tied. The plants feed on heat and moisture alone, rooted in the constructed loam, growing riotously but never completing their growth. Fruit is always ripe and never falls. Leaves that should have yellowed hang suspended at the moment of their deepest green. The gardens are lush to the point of pathology, a verdant seizure held in equilibrium.

Between these enclosures, the basin is naked. Sparse. You can walk for hours across the pale floor and encounter only the occasional presence: a cairn of knapped chert, a pole driven into the substrate and hung with cordage that might be language or might be tally-marks, a low wall of mortared shell grit that begins nowhere and ends nowhere. The emptiness is architectural. It has been balanced, with tremendous care, against the fullness of the gardens. The scale is regional, open, a valley of mid-sized features scattered across a floor that extends beyond the immediate and vanishes into the white. Distance feels negotiable. A terrace that appears an hour’s walk away might arrive in twenty minutes; a nearby cairn can take half a day to reach, as if the space between things has not been fully measured yet.

Animals are abundant in this emptiness, and they are wrong. Pale moths settle on the wet floor in such numbers that the pale substrate appears furred, each drinking mineral water through an uncoiled tongue, but their wings beat without sound, the air too thick or too indifferent to carry the frequencies. Bright lizards, turquoise and old-gold, carpet the carved ridges, yet their claws produce no skitter. Birds with oily black eyes perch on the sinew-bound poles in pairs, beaks gaping as if mid-song, but no song emerges; they are frozen in the act of singing, throats motionless. There are grazers too, things with too many joints in their legs, lowering narrow heads to the silent pools. When they drink, their reflections drink a half-second behind them, and sometimes a second reflection drinks behind the first, lagging, trailing reality like a slipped stitch.

This is the texture of the weirdness. Reality here has not fully set.

The light is absolute. It has no discernible source, no sun, no flame. It bleeds from the white dome of the sky—or from the sky itself, if that kiln-white vault is not a ceiling but an atmospheric condition. Shadows pool beneath objects like spilled ink, but they do not always agree on direction. A standing stone might cast its shade to the north, while a moth upon it casts its shade inward, toward the stone’s heart. The brightness is so total that looking toward the horizon induces a snow-blindness, and in the afterimages that bloom across the retina, you see the scaffolding. Ghost-geometries of wooden poles, ladders, and framing loom faintly in the air, suggesting that the world is still under construction just beyond the edge of perception, or that it was unmade and remade so recently that its previous iterations have not finished fading.

Water occasionally runs upward, returning to the weeping walls in reverse along the adze-marks. In certain gardens, gravity has local opinions: fruit hangs at forty-five-degree angles, perfectly motionless, as if that section of the world was tilted and nobody corrected it. Sound does not propagate normally; a shout dies in the mouth, absorbed by the hot, motionless air. The mist, where it gathers, is a solid mass at chest height, a cloud that has forgotten to drift, its edges as sharp as the tool-marks in the stone. If you look away from a lizard and look back, there may be two, occupying the same sun-warmed spot, slightly out of phase, their colors overlapping like double-exposed film. They do not interact with their twins. They simply exist, in duplicate, in triplicate, in the abundant silence.

Nothing changes. Nothing completes. The moth does not finish its drink; the lizard does not finish its bask; the nectar-thread does not snap. The place exists in a single sustained inhalation. The hot wet air does not circulate. The tool-marks are fresh because time has not been permitted to dull them. The world is a maquette, a full-scale prototype assembled before nature existed, built from what was at hand—clay, bone, heat, water, and an abundance of crude, patient labor. The makers are absent, but their fingerprints are the landscape. And under the white, unwavering light, in the sweltering, weeping hush, the abundant life of the place proceeds without breath, waiting for the instruction that never comes, lush and frozen and utterly constructed.
