**The Hold of Unfinished Cleaving**

You do not enter it so much as you discover that you have always been within it. The ceiling is too high to sense and the floor too broad to curve underfoot, yet every direction confirms the truth: you are *inside*, somewhere far below any surface that has ever known wind or rain. This is a void the size of continents, a planetary wound that did not heal but also did not bleed. It generates its own horizon, its own laws of perspective, its own silent meteorology of pressure and thermal layer. It is warm here—unnaturally, uniformly warm, like the space between a sleeping body and its blanket. The heat has no source you can find, only a sourceless ubiquity, the breath of the rock itself.

The terrain is a tantrum of youth. Every edge is new. Every plane has the gloss of having just been split. Great blades of olivine and pyroxene jut at angles that suggest they were frozen in the act of explosion. Basaltic glass fans out in sheets sharp enough to shave atoms. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is worn. In the absolute absence of weather—no wind, no water, no temperature flux, no cycle of day or night—the rock remains eternally adolescent. It has never been scolded into smoothness by time. The dynamic of the place is a held breath: a frozen equilibrium where even the concept of erosion is theoretical. A chip knocked from a spire five thousand years ago (if years meant anything here) still lies at its base, retaining the same kinetic heat it had when it fell, neither cooling further nor warming, neither embedding itself nor rolling away.

And there is no water. None. Not as vapor, not as ice, not as trace dampness. Chemistry here is a desert of aqueous possibility. The atmosphere—if it can be called that—is a dense, warm soup of heavier-than-air volatiles: carbonyl sulfides, noble gases in suspension, metallic vapors cooled to a syrupy phase. It is thick enough to swim through, though you would not want to. It presses against the skin with the insistence of deep water, supporting life that has never conceived of buoyancy in H₂O. The density is not merely physical but biological. The air—or *plenitude*, as it might better be termed—is saturated with fauna.

They are everywhere. Abundant does not approach the truth. They crowd the warm darkness in layers, strata of animation from the microscopic to the macabrely vast, near-touching, a high-saturation ecology that leaves almost no cubic meter empty. The smallest are filaments of copper-silicate colloid that drift in clouds, metabolizing the thermal radiation of the walls. The largest are wall-clinging things the size of cattle, with bodies like nested geodes, articulated by piezoelectric muscle analogues. There are flyers, too, though flight is a misnomer; they are neutrally buoyant in the dense medium, undulating through the dark like rays through an ocean that forgot to be wet. Reach out your hand and you will graze three, four, a half-dozen—velvet, chitin, liquid stone—before your fingers close on empty plenitude. They make no sound. The thickness of the medium swallows vibration. The fauna are silent not by restraint but by physics; their movements are too slow, too frictionless, or too perfectly harmonized to generate acoustic disturbance. What you hear is your own pulse, and even that seems muted, as if the Hold politely asks all things to keep their voices down.

They are strange. Deeply, systemically strange. They do not eat and grow; they accrete and partition. A grazer fastens to a mineral outcropping, dissolves a filament of pyrite, and does not enlarge—instead, it splits into two identical halves, each half the size of the original. The population remains constant, a fixed integer of biomass. Death, when it occurs (if it can be called death), is a liquefaction that immediately seeds a new lith-node, which in three days or three centuries—the interval is unmeasurable without change—will bud the same creature again. It is an ecology of eternal recurrence, a food web looped into a Möbius strip. The fauna move, yes, but they move like hands on a clock that has already completed its revolution and now only demonstrates the idea of motion. Their paths are exact. Their interactions are predictable down to the millimeter. The entire ecosystem is a single, solved equation, time-invariant and complete.

There is no vegetation. No moss, no mold, no bacterial film as we would know it. Instead, there are the *liths*: sessile precipitations of mineral that grow upward in geometries too regular to be accidental. They are not alive, yet they are the foundation of life here, the substrate upon which the chemosilicate fauna graze. They are pale, warm to the touch, and vaguely luminescent in infrared, though to normal sight the world is absolute darkness. Light is a forgotten sense. The only illumination is the faint, ambient thermal ghost of rock that is merely warm rather than warm*er*. It is a darkness so total it feels textile, like velvet pushed against the eyeballs.

Scattered across this impossible floor, rare and lonely, are the signs of hands. Not many. A cairn of three black stones balanced to a point that defies the tremorless air. A circle of obsidian flakes, each knapped with the telltale ripples of conchoidal fracture. A length of native copper, hammered flat and wrapped around a crystal spire, now fixed there for millennia because oxidation does not occur and the metal has no reason to let go. The builders used no fire, no smelt; their technology was percussion and leverage, the simplest augmentation of fist and nail. Who they were is irrelevant—they left no graves, no middens, only these quiet assertions that *someone noticed*. The structures do not decay. They simply are, as static as the rock, as eternal as the equilibrium.

The scale of it dwarfs intention. You could walk toward a distant ridge for months, and the ridge would grow no closer in any way that matters, because the Hold generates its own curvature, its own continental weather of dense, sluggish pressure fronts that never break. It has climates that shift with geological slowness, zones of warmth that feel like seasons, but the weather never turns. It is a climate of permanent, indifferent coziness.

The strangeness seeps in slowly. You realize, after long enough, that your footprints behind you are already half-erased, not by wind but by the *plenitude* itself, which flows back into stillness the moment you lift your boot. You realize that the fauna avoid you not out of fear but because your path was not part of the solved equation, and their avoidance is a correction, a restitching of reality's hem. You realize that the warmth is the temperature of metabolism without decay, of birth without growth, of a world that started its exhale and will never need to inhale.

It is quiet. It is full. It is sharp. It is dark. It is young, and it will never grow old.
