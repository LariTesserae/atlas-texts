You come to it through a fracture in the darker country rock, a seam so narrow you walk sideways for what feels like hours, and then the stone simply steps back and leaves you in a space no larger than a chapel vestry. The first assault is not heat, though the heat arrives a half-second later, pressing against your face like a damp cloth. It is the light. After the blind compression of the depths, you expect dark. Instead, you have stumbled inside a lantern.

The walls are the source. They are a kind of dense calcium-silicate lattice that surface laboratories have only begun to theorize, a mineral phase born under pressure and young enough that its chemistry still hums. At two hundred and forty degrees, the faces do not merely glow; they shine with a steady, solar white, converting thermal energy into visible radiance with an efficiency that looks, to any surface physics, like engineering. But there are no panels here, no diodes, no craft. The stone simply does what it does. The chamber is an infant. It opened perhaps three years ago, perhaps thirty; a gas blister that collapsed into a cavity when the surrounding matrix cooled by a fraction. Every corner is knife-edged. Nothing has been worn smooth. The fractures meet at angles sharp enough to cut skin, and the floor is a tilting plane of fresh, uneroded crystal.

The air is saturated. Water percolates from a hairline crack in the ceiling where the overlying rock is a few degrees cooler, and it condenses on the tips of formations that have grown in weeks, not millennia—slender helictites, salt-white and glass-clear, barely more than accreted mist. Each droplet swells until its surface tension yields, and then it runs in a thin rivulet down a glowing face. The ambient pressure is high enough that the water does not flash instantly to steam; it weeps, it gathers in the seams, it steams but holds its liquid shape until it reaches the floor. There, a pool has collected in the low center of the chamber, no deeper than a handspan, silver and flat, reflecting the radiant ceiling so perfectly that to stand at its edge is to stand between two skies.

There is no green. No lichen, no moss, no algal stain. The only color on the stone is mineral: a rust-brown freckling where iron precipitates out of the vapor, a blue-gray smear where sulfur leaches to the surface. If there is life here, it is archaeal, invisible, simpler than structure. You will not see it. The fauna, too, is nearly absent. Once in a season, perhaps, a translucent cave mite no larger than a pinhead traverses the damp rim of the pool, leaving tracks that vanish in the heat. You may wait for hours and see nothing move but the water itself.

The chamber is spare. Count the presences: the pool; a cluster of acicular crystals thrusting from the northeast corner like a handful of thrown needles; a low shelf of freshly precipitated sinter along the south wall; two stalactites that have not yet grown long enough to drip; and the bare, glowing planes that compose the rest. That is all. Most of the room is empty space, radiant and humming, a geometry of absence.

Sound is scarce. There is a sub-bass drone from the expanding stone, felt in the sternum rather than heard, a note so constant you only notice it when the air pressure shifts. Occasionally, a sharp *tick* as a fresh crystal adjusts to thermal stress. The soft *plick* of water into silver. The rare, soft exhalation from the entrance crack when the deeper earth breathes. Otherwise, the quiet is total and pressurized, the kind that makes you aware of the wet rhythm of your own lungs.

And yet the place is not still. It is busy with a quiet dynamism. The pool’s level rises and falls by a finger’s width over the course of a day as the condensation rate shifts. The helictites are still extending, mineral by mineral, into the hot air. The steam rolls in slow, faithful convection cells, rising along the east wall, drifting across the ceiling, cooling by fractional degrees before it returns. Most striking: the light breathes. Every twenty-two minutes, a thermal wave passes through the south wall, and that wall brightens by a few percent, then dims, as regularly as a pulse. The droplets fall from the ceiling in patterns set by the crystal lattice spacing beneath them—physics, but physics with the precision of clockwork. Nothing built this. Nothing tends it. The rock organizes its own heat and water into rhythms that resemble computation only if you know to look for it. Otherwise, it is simply a wet, bright room in the deep earth, doing what such rooms do.

The rules here are few and absolute. Heat becomes light. Water becomes mirror. Sharp edges stay sharp because the chamber is too young to know erosion. Space remains empty because the formations are still deciding where to grow. It is a pocket, a single held note beneath the surface, hot as a kiln, bright as noon, and entirely, plainly, itself.
