There is a bowl in the mountain, not much wider than a cathedral nave, and it is closed.

You enter through a wind-tunnel couloir that suddenly releases you onto a floor of warm, polished obsidian-porphyry, a hollow burnished by ages of carrying wind until it gleams like the inside of a cast-iron bell. The space is small enough to cross in fifty breaths, yet the walls rise in a near-perfect amphitheater, so that standing on the floor you feel neither entirely outside nor inside, but suspended in a geological lung. Here, at this altitude, the air ought to bite; instead it exhales against your skin with a damp, botanical heat, thick and faintly metallic, as though the stone itself were running a low fever.

There is no water. None. No rivulet has ever cut this stone, no snow rests in the lee of the rim, no dew condenses on the glossy surfaces. Liquid water is a forgotten chemistry here, incompatible with the atmosphere; were a droplet to fall it would boil or combust or simply be rejected, sizzling into a phantom. What exists instead is a lushness that has never needed it.

Seven pillars rise from the polished floor. That is all. The density is sparse—vast arenas of emptiness between each presence—but each presence is a world. They are not trees. They are living columns of translucent, amber-pink tissue, each trunk broad as a cottage, walled with integument like steamed abalone shell, shot through with slow peristaltic rivers of dense, ochre fluid. Heavy silicone nectars, hydrophobic and glossy, not water but a substitute chemistry that conducts nutrients and memory. Vesicles of the stuff swell from their flanks like war-drums, some twice the height of a person, membranes drum-tight and iridescent. Between the pillars hang curtains of filament—lush, dripping, opulent veils that bridge the gap as if the grove were weaving itself shut, slowly, lazily. The vegetation is luxuriant in defiance of every hydrophilic law, a garden of deep tissue and pneumatic bladders, warm to the touch, exhaling sweet, cloying vapors heavy with esters and benzenes.

And it is loud. Overwhelmingly, pressingly loud. A ceaseless, multi-layered roar that makes the jaw ache and turns thought into something felt rather than heard. The pillars vent gases from basal pores in rhythmic, thundering exhalations—*whoomph, whoomph*—each release a forty-second cycle that rattles the sternum. The membranes snap in the constant thermal churn, cracking like wet sails in a hurricane. The air is black with an abundant fauna: millions of thumb-sized, articulated creatures, not quite insects, not quite seeds, their bodies banded with glossy plates, their wings made of a resonant material that does not buzz but clangs, thousands upon thousands of them in a swarm that never lands, harvesting leaked vapors with obsidian beaks, their mass flight a demented orchestra of cymbal-timbres and glassy drones. Larger shapes move inside the translucent trunks, jointed and many-limbed, never emerging, only silhouettes drifting through amber peristalsis. At the roots, scuttling things like compressed bundles of fiber tend the flares where the pillars meet stone, plugging micro-leaks with resinous spit. Life is everywhere, frantic, uncountable, a circuitry of motion.

The light is dim. A tarnished copper gloom filters from above, where the sky is occluded by a permanent reef of high metallic dust, a twilight held in stasis. What illumination exists leaks from the pillars themselves—soft violet and sick-green phosphorescence from nodal organs beneath their skin, casting long, swimming shadows across the polished floor. It is just enough to see the constant turbulence, the reason the place feels more like a cauldron than a grove.

For nothing here is still. The dynamic is violent, intimate, rapid as an anthill poured into a storm. The pillars inflate, their girth broadening visibly, stomachs of pressured chemistry, then deflate with whistling shrieks. Vesicles burst without warning—the gunshot cracks of their rupture echoing off the rim—and the fiber-crabs swarm the wound, weaving patches that seal over in minutes. New bladders bud in real time, bulging and ripening before your eyes. Spore-clouds spin into miniature cyclones five paces wide, dancing between the pillars, carrying sprout-clusters that root in mid-air upon the gauzy curtains, only to be torn free seconds later by the next surge of vented warmth. The floor’s skin of powdered oxide shifts in ripples, drawn into spiral patterns by the air’s insistent choreography. Shelters are built and unbuilt by the weather here; equilibrium is not stillness but a managed hysteria.

Yet the erosion is established. This chaos is ancient. The stone has been worn smooth by this exact behaviour for geological ages; the pillars are not invaders but citizens of long standing, their roots anchored in wind-hollowed cups that fit them like sockets in a joint. The violence is ritual, a weathered dance that has found its form. Balance, here, looks like frenzy.

Against the northeast curve of the rim, scattered evidence of primitive habitation clings to the stone. A cairn of vent-scorched rocks, stacked knee-high, marks a windbreak. Within it, a half-finished shelter: ribs of cured stalk-pith, arched and sagging, draped with shed integument that still faintly glows. The tools lie about in the open as if their owner stepped away moments ago: a chisel of volcanic glass, scrapers fashioned from the shed wing-casings of the clangorous fliers, mallets of compressed root-fiber. Nothing that required smelting, nothing complex. Just hands, and patience, and an understanding of which membranes could be cut and which would retaliate with a spurt of searing nectar.

This is a sealed chamber, a warm, dry, lunging grotto above the world. It breathes without water, thinks without silence, and builds without permanence. To stand in it is to stand inside a heartbeat that has forgotten its body, loud, lush, and terribly, churningly alive.
