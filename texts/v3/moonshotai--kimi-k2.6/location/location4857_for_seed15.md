**The Cistern at Noon**

At first it seems less a place than a solidification of light: a pale, burning cube dropped beneath the surface of the world. Stand in the shallow lagoon above—where the water is itself warm as fresh blood, bright as molten glass—and you would never know the chamber is there, hidden under a skim of white sand and a capstone carved to look like seafloor. But descend two body-lengths through the stone throat of the shaft, and the water closes around you not into darkness but into an almost unbearable radiance. The sun, directly overhead, fires straight down the shaft’s white-lined bore, scattering off every wall until the whole room seems cast from luminous milk. There are no shadows here, only degrees of glare.

The chamber is a single space, intimate, no wider than a sleeping hall and barely tall enough for a tall person to stand upright with raised hands. Its scale is pocket-deliberate: a concealed cell, a swallowed room. Yet within this modest volume everything has been made. There is no natural substrate to touch, no bare reef or earth. The floor is paved with hand-split limestone slabs, pale gray, laid without metal—dry-stacked with the obsessive patience of primitive craft, then sealed from within with a slurry of baked lime and crushed shell that has not yet had time to dull or flake. Young. The erosion score of the place is measured not in centuries but in seasons. Chisel marks still bite the stone like frozen ripples. The edges of the pavers are knife-sharp; no current has ground them round, no biological film has softened their lines. The walls rise in four precise planes to a low vault, and in the center of that vault gapes the circular shaft, the oculus, the wound through which the midday world pours its light.

The water inside is warm—nearly blood temperature—and this warmth is not passive. It arrives actively, dynamically. Through a low slot in the southern wall, cooler water from the lagoon above seeps in with a constant, sibilant *hiss*, heavier and saltier, sliding along the floor in a visible current. Meanwhile, from the very center of the chamber, up through a circular grill of carved porite stone, bubbles groan and rattle in a ceaseless upward boil. A spring. The floor here is thin crust over some geothermal throat, and the water it exhales is hot enough to shimmer, rich with dissolved minerals that have already begun to etch pale fans onto the surrounding pavers—new geology already in its infancy. The two waters meet and turn, corkscrewing slowly around each other in a cage of stone. The convection keeps the water lucid, polishes the light, suspends the few motes of limestone dust that still drift from the last striking of the mallet.

Sound here is a physical weight. Underwater, in this hard, empty box, noise does not fade; it accumulates. The spring’s boiling is a low, tectonic *thrum*, felt in the sternum before it is heard. The inflow from the wall adds a higher, tearing note, like silk being continuously ripped. Together they reverberate between the stone faces, amplifying into a blurred, aqueous roar that fills the pocket completely. It is loud enough, constant enough, to erase the memory of quiet. If you pressed an ear to the wall, you would hear the stone itself singing with the vibration.

And yet there is almost nothing living to make a sound of its own. Fauna is absent—not scarce, but truly *not there*. No fish slip through the oculus to investigate; no crustaceans have colonized the sharp new corners; no worms thread the seams. The space is too fresh, too sealed in its sterility, perhaps too hot. What biology exists is vegetable and sparse. Along the upper course of the eastern wall, where the light is most searing, a few filaments of green algae have taken hold, no thicker than thread, swaying in the slow interior weather. A single aquatic plant, something with narrow strap-leaves and no name you know, has rooted in a crack between two floor stones near the spring, its pale roots visible against the white lime, drinking the heat. That is all. It is enough to prove the water is not dead, merely waiting.

Above you, the shaft is a column of silver incandescence. Bubbles from the spring rush up into it, spinning, catching the sun, becoming drops of mercury shooting toward a distant, dancing roof of water-surface and sky. If you looked straight up, you would see the round light of the world framed in a perfect circle of stone, as if the chamber were an eye looking out, or the world an eye looking in.

What rules govern such a place? They are the rules of manual intention, of total making. Nothing here grew; everything was set. The angle of the shaft tracks the sun at its zenith. The spring was not capped but *framed*, not diverted but *directed*. The chamber is too small to be purely utilitarian, too bright and warm to be a tomb, too sharply crafted to be accidental. It feels like a beginning: the first room of a submerged world, recently finished, flooded, and sealed under the brilliant water by hands that knew copper, bone, stone, and patience, but not yet iron or engines. A room for something that has not yet arrived. A prepared space, humming with heat and noise, patient in its absolute, empty brightness, deep below the dancing surface.
