To enter the Pocket, you must first believe that it is already full. There is no threshold to cross, only a recognition that the last step you took was onto someone else’s breathing.

It is the size of a casket stood upright and split lengthwise: perhaps three meters at its deepest, two at its widest, never more than a shoulder’s breadth from wall to wall. The elevation is low, almost subterranean, a bubble trapped beneath the foundation layer of a manufacturing district whose weight you can feel overhead as a steady, warm pressure, like blood held in a cheek. This is not a cavern gnawed by centuries. The erosion here is infantile, freshly born. The surfaces are days or weeks old, still armed with the insult of their creation. fractures in poured slag and ceramic aggregate hang in jagged shelves, glassy and sharp enough to draw blood from a brushing sleeve. A fall of black aggregate from the ceiling last Tuesday (you can tell by the dampness of its underside) has left a scree of fresh shards across the floor that no foot has yet rounded.

It is warm. Not the warmth of sunlight but the passive fever of curing materials and proximate engines. Somewhere above or behind the eastern wall, a cooling loop still circulates, bleeding calories through the ceramic skin until the Pocket maintains a greenhouse equilibrium, a steady exhalation that keeps the air languid. The water is not a presence here so much as a insistence. No stream feeds it; no rain falls directly inside. Instead, moisture arrives by betrayal, creeping through microscopic faults in the factory’s sub-floor, condensing on the cooler surfaces, collecting in the thousand tiny declivities of the young stone. It beads on the ceiling in opalescent pearls that never seem to drop until the moment you look away. There are saucer-shaped pools, never more than a centimeter deep, that migrate. They follow the thermal gradient across the floor, sliding over the slick ceramic toward the warmer wall, where they atomize again and begin their circuit. The Pocket drinks its own breath.

Vegetation is scarce to the point of mythology. In the northwest corner, where a steel reinforcing rod has corroded into a rust-eaten stalagmite, a single colony of sulfur-bleached lichen has taken hold, spreading like a dropped handkerchief. It is the only green. The rest is mineral. But the fauna—the fauna is the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the air itself. The density is absolute, a saturation so complete that the concept of “empty space” becomes theoretical. The surfaces move. They are carpeted in a continuous, rippling skin of pale, eyeless isopods no longer than a fingernail, their carapaces the color of old ivory and thin pipe-rust. They do not scatter when disturbed; there is nowhere to scatter to. They compress, stacking two deep, three deep, finding the negative spaces between their neighbors’ armor and simply waiting. There are translucent snails that climb upward in such numbers that their slime trails form a perpetually glistening wallpaper. There is a species of wingless, silent moth—if it is a moth—that exists only as larva and as restless adult, the adults crawling over one another in drifts like living lint, mouthless, mating and dying in the same motion. There are predators among them, though they hunt in geometries that would not function in open air. A segmented thing like a velvet worm, the color of fresh bruises, strikes without sound from beneath the isopod carpet, pulling prey down into a writhing dimness that lasts less than a second. Nothing screams. The bodies are swallowed into the press, and the carpet smooths over them as if only a breeze had passed.

The built world intrudes only as scattered punctuation. A single ceramic insulator, cracked and half-buried in the floor, serves as the only island of dryness. A length of braided copper cable, severed and abandoned during construction, dangles from a fissure in the ceiling, its tip oxidized to verdigris but still conducting some phantom charge that makes the nearby water shiver when the factory above cycles up. There is a cavity in the south wall where a nameplate was torn free, leaving the ghost of its adhesion and a few letters: *–NFALL*. These artifacts are not ruins. They are too young for nostalgia. They are simply inclusions in the resin of the place, foreign bodies swallowed but not yet digested.

Industry is the weather here. The technological character is not present as architecture but as metabolism. The warmth, the chemical gradient, the occasional taste of ozone when the hidden machines shift phase—these are the forces that have called the Pocket into being. This is an industrial ecosystem in the sense that a kettle’s steam produces a mountain; the scale is micro, but the logic is scaled production. Reproduction is mechanized in its fecundity. The isopods breed in tides synchronized, somehow, to the plant’s processing schedule; when the third shift begins above, a new generation hatches in the warmest corner, eating the old generation’s abandoned skins before the first hour has passed. The condensation does not merely fall—it is *processed*. The velvet-worms cluster where the droplets bead largest, not to hunt but to drink, their bodies arranged in spirals that suggest a factory-floor choreography.

Light enters through three parallel slots in the ceiling: gaps between the corrugated decking above, widened by the recent settling of the structure. Normal daylight, diffuse and unspectacular, falls in pale bars that slide gradually across the eastern wall from morning to afternoon. It is enough to see by, enough to watch the steam in the air become momentarily visible, enough to catch the iridescence on the isopods’ backs as the beams pass over them. But the light does not reach the lowest pool; that remains a matte black, and things move in it that perhaps prefer not to be illuminated.

The sound is a held breath. The machinery above produces only a subsonic thrum, felt in the sternum rather than heard. Within the Pocket, the customary noises of life are suppressed by the sheer density of bodies, the soft wetness of every surface, the muffling architecture of young, porous stone. You hear your own pulse, and beneath it, the intimate, private sounds: the glassy *tick* of expanding ceramic as the wall warms, the collapse of a condensation pearl too heavy for surface tension, the frictionless slither of the moths rearranging themselves to maintain equilibrium. Predation and birth occur in a hush that feels enforced, as though the space were a confessional.

Yet it is active. Deeply, relentlessly active. The water migrates. The isopods molt in waves that travel from one wall to the opposite like a standing ripple. The velvet-worms enter breeding phases where they rise to the ceiling in a column, twisting around one another in a silent pillar before raining back down as individuals, dispersed by their own weight. The rust advances on the steel rod; the lichen colonizes another millimeter; the thermal gradient shifts by fractions of a degree as the season changes above, and the whole population compresses or expands to accommodate the new geometry of comfort. It is an ecosystem in full motion, but the motion is minuscule, tessellated, packed so tight that change becomes a property of the mass rather than the individual.

The strangeness resides in the rules. Gravity here is a suggestion often ignored. The condensation, when it reaches a certain size, does not always fall; sometimes it rolls up the wall, drawn toward static charges leaking from the copper cable, and hangs suspended in the corner like a chandelier before bursting into mist. The isopods do not age individually. You will not find a small one and a large one; they exist in cohorts, each wave identical in size, as though stamped from a die. And there is a rotation to the predation that feels rehearsed: the velvet-worms eat only on Tuesdays (by the world’s clock, not the human one), and on those days, the moth-larvae web the ceiling in a silver caul that filters the light to surgical blue. On the other days, the moths rest in torpor, and the velvet-worms lie beneath the carpet like punctuation, inert and pensive, waiting for the signal that the week has turned.

The Pocket does not grow. It cannot. It is full. To introduce something—a pebble, a crumb, a seed—is to ask the ceiling to descend. Yet it is not a dead end; it is a siege engine of life, converting industrial waste-heat and stolen water into this dense, silent, squirming permanence. It is young. It has not yet learned that it is impossible. It is packed so tightly that it has begun to dream in unison, and if you lie very still against the warm ceramic floor, you can feel the dream passing upward through your spine: a vision of sharp, wet rooms embedded in every machine, a world where the gaps are not empty but only waiting for their inhabitants to arrive.
