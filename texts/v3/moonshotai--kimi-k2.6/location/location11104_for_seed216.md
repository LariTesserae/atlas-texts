There is a world that is mostly absence. It takes the shape of a woven thing, a basket blown to the size of a planet, its weave tightened across an abyss so wide that storms gestate inside it and die without ever touching the walls. The walls themselves are the ground. Every surface, every arch, every load-bearing sky is plant-stuff: lignin, cellulose, petrified sap, dead fiber compressed to the hardness of stone and live fiber still drinking, still growing. There is no stone here, no raw geology, no original matter that was not first a seed persuaded by patient hands. Everything is made. But the making was done with tools that would not impress an ant. Bone needles, fire-hardened digging sticks, obsidian flakes no broader than a thumb. The making took long enough for oceans to forget their beds.

You stand on a cable. It is a root-cathedral in cross-section, its diameter that of a mountain range, its surface ridged and fluted by centuries of weather until it resembles topography: hillocks of amber, valleys where the grain runs soft, cliffs of varnished bark. But this is only one cord in an infinite lattice. Look up, look down, look across the warm, dim gulf, and you see others. Some near enough to bridge with rope and courage. Some so distant they are only smudged threads against the central glow. Between them: nothing. Miles upon continental miles of warm, damp, pressurized nothing. The average density of the world is that of a dried sponge. It is vast enough to own its own coriolis, its own tectonics of torque and tension, and yet it is hollow, skeletal, a lung.

The light comes from the center. A coal-heart. A star in the last sullen phase of its burning, or perhaps something more like a furnace left by the unknown original cultivators—regardless, it is dim. You could stare at it openly. It paints everything in shades of umber and wet rust. But it is warm. The woven sphere is a greenhouse of impossible scale. Heat radiates inward, reflects, accumulates in the cellulose mass. The air is thick, tropical, suffused with a feverish humidity that never quite breaks into rain. Water does not fall here; it perspires. It condenses on the cooler root-faces in the upper reaches of the weave, beads into slow, slug-heavy droplets, and migrates through capillary action downward, inward, sideways—following the logic of the fibers rather than gravity. There are no rivers. There are weeping walls. There are spongy bladders inside the larger trunks that pulse with stored moisture. There is mist that fills the void-chambers like breath on a mirror, then vanishes as the weave inhales.

And the weave is alive. Totally. Vegetation is not a feature of this world; it is the substrate, the architecture, the soil, the sky. Where the cables cross, they have been trained to fuse, forming platforms, vaults, knuckles of hardened heartwood that serve as continents. Between these nodes, aerial soils have accumulated over epochs—pockets of compost caught in the mesh, no deeper than a grave but wide as counties. Here the plant-life has diversified into grotesques: black-leaved lianas that harvest the dim infrared with leaves like satellite dishes; gills of pale fungus that exhale oxygen in soft, visible jets; pitcher-shaped blooms that collect condensation and drown the insects that never evolved here, but came in on spore-ships of air. The entire structure respires. You can feel it if you press your ear to the grain—a subsonic systole, a century-long intake and a century-long sigh.

Because it is old. The erosion here is not of breaking, but of settling. The weave has found its form. It creaks, it flexes, but it knows its shape. The surfaces are polished by the passage of condensation, by the friction of climbing things, by the patient burnishing of airborne grit. There is no chaos. Only the long, dynamic equilibrium of a structure that has existed long enough to develop its own second nature.

Sound dies. The weave drinks it. The voids between cables are anechoic cathedrals. A voice thrown across the gap falls as a leaf falls—slow, and then not at all. The fauna have adapted to this silence, or perhaps they made it. They are abundant, but quiet. In the tangles, in the humus-crotches where cables marry, there is teeming life: mites of chitin and wax that graze on dead bark; root-clams that burrow into the lignin and filter nutrients; ribbon-eels that emerge from boreholes to snap at spore-bats. The spore-bats themselves are nearly silent, navigating by the vibration of the cables, their wings membranes of black leaf. And then there are the drifters. Megafauna of the middle distance—bladder-bodied, translucent things that swim the warm air between cords, grazing on aerial plankton, pulsing with internal bioluminescence that is too faint to read by but bright enough to break your heart. They make no cry. They are wet, warm shapes passing in the brown twilight.

But the quiet is not empty. It is held. It is the quiet of a held breath, and sometimes, in the woven dark, you hear the world thinking.

For this is where the reality breaks down.

The builders—those patient, primitive ones, the ones with their bone hooks and their songs that took a generation to sing—did not merely weave a cage around a star. They braided something into the geometry. The cables do not respect distance the way they should. Two platforms grown from cuttings taken from the same parent-root remain kin in a way that physics cannot police. Walk from one to the other along the weave, and the journey is what it is: months, years. Time, too, is perforated here. In the deeper hollows, where the light of the coal-sun barely warms the nap of the moss, you find places that are older than the weave that contains them, pockets of weathered wood that remember being a forest on a world that may never have existed. The tools that built this place were primitive, yes, but the design was not. The design was inherited from something that understood the grain of reality well enough to weave it.

So the elevation is high, unspeakably high. Not merely above a sea, but above all certainties. You are suspended on the inside of a sphere that stretches from one silent horizon to the next, the false horizons of the cable-continents curving away. Beneath your feet, if you stand near the edge of your woven platform and look inward, there is the drop: thousands upon thousands of miles of warm air, leading down to the sullen red ember that heats your skin. You are high because there is nothing below you but fall.

And yet the fall is not fatal here, not always. The air is thick. The gravity, strange. Some say the weave warps mass itself; a body released from a cable does not plummet as it should, but drifts, dream-slow, tumbling through the mist toward the center, where the rules fray entirely. Travelers who have descended—on ropes of braided silk, lowering themselves for decades—report that the lower cables are stranger. The wood there is younger and older simultaneously. The coal-star, seen from below the weave’s equator, is sometimes blue, sometimes a sound, sometimes an eye. The reality breakdown intensifies as you approach the locus. The world is not only hollow; it is uncertain.

Still, life abides. Because the structure is dynamic. Seasons are marked not by light—so little light, so dim—but by the pulse of condensation, the swelling of the bladder-roots, the migration of the drifters from the upper latitudes of the weave toward the central warmth. There are predations. A root-clam yanked open by a bore-worm, the worm seized by a wire-snake that dropped from a higher cable, the snake starved because the spore-bats altered their flight-path this century due to a shift in the spore-winds. It is an ecosystem, slow and quiet and vast, turning on an axis of cellulose and time.

What does it sound like? The pressure of water moving upward against gravity in a xylem tube. The creak of two continent-cables adjusting their embrace by a millimeter. The soft, wet pop of a seed germinating in compost older than nations. The subsonic groan of the structure settling into its shape, a sound felt in the marrow, never reaching the ear. A silence so complete it has texture, like velvet, like the inside of a seed.

You could walk this world for a lifetime and never reach a natural thing. Every surface has been touched, encouraged, pruned. Even the decay is cultivated. Even the void is shaped. It is a made place, warm and weeping, dim as a childhood memory, held together by the patient, animal repetition of hands that knew only simple tools but knew also that given enough time, enough breath, enough repetition, the world itself could be taught to grow into a basket to hold the failing light.

And in the silence, it holds.
