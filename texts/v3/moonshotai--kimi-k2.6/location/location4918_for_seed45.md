The darkness is not cold. That is the first thing you notice, and it stays with you. You stand at an elevation that should be frozen, where the atmosphere has bled away to a thin, metallic suggestion, yet the stone beneath your feet radiates a steady, omnidirectional heat. The sky overhead is a lid of absolute matte black—not starless, but star-occluded, choked by a permanent estratosphere of silicate dust that has never parted. No stellar light has ever touched this ground. The warmth here is endogenic, rising from a planet whose interior is still deciding what kind of world it wants to be. You are warm because the crust is a feverish membrane, not a shield.

This is a landscape in active gestation. Erosion has not yet been invented; there are no rivers to carve, no wind to blunt, no rain to dissolve. Every surface is newborn, raw, unweathered. To the east, a seam of obsidian splits with a sound like a drawn breath, and black glass pushes upward, sharp enough to part atoms, rising three meters in the time it takes to exhale. To the west, a tower that has stood for perhaps a decade—a mere infant—shivers and sags, not into rubble, but into a viscous reclamation, as the stone remembers it was recently magma. The dynamic is ceaseless, a solid storm. The lithosphere is hyperactive, a bazaar of mineral exchange where creation and destruction are indistinguishable. A ridge inflates. A dome cracks and oozes a slow ribbon of orange-gray melt. A plain of scoria buckles into a new mountain range over the course of an afternoon. Nothing is permitted to settle into the shape of peace.

And yet the world is mostly quiet. The violence happens in a register too low for the thin air to carry. The collapse of the tower made no audible crash; it arrived instead as a pressure in the sternum, a subsonic thrum felt in the teeth. What reaches the ear is sparse and precious: the ticking of fresh surfaces cooling and contracting, the infrasonic groan of a plateau swelling with new magma, and sometimes—rarely—the pure crystalline chime of a mineral splitting along a perfect, fresh plane. Between these events, the silence is total. Without water, there is no river song, no trickle, no whisper of liquidity. Without vegetation, no rustle. Without fauna, no cry, no skitter, no wingbeat. Without sustained wind, no howl. The quiet is architectural, built from the absolute absence of biology and the poverty of weather. It is a silence so complete that your own heartbeat seems like an intrusion.

But the landscape looks *built*.

Scattered across the open expanse—vast thermal plains extending past the immediate horizon into a haze-darkened distance—are structures that suggest a civilization of geometry. A colonnade of hexagonal pillars, each sixty meters tall, their capitals interlocking to form a vaulted canopy of stone lace. A ziggurat of layered pyroxene, its steps too regular to be accidental, standing alone on a sheet of cooling slag. A ring of arches, each identical in span and thickness, as if drafted by a geometer’s compass and then abandoned. You might walk for twenty kilometers across an undulating purple-gray scoria field and find only one such feature; then walk another twenty and find a single dome, half-bubble of solidified foam-stone, pierced by a vent at its apex. The density is sparse. Emptiness is the dominant presence, and the eye learns to read the landscape by its rare vertical exceptions.

These are not ruins. They are not artifacts. There is no technology here, no tool mark, no residue of intent. The world is pure nature, pre-tool, its physics working in a realm that has not yet learned imperfection. The structures are tectonic fruits, the crystallization paths of cooling plutons forced into regularity by deep-pressure geometry, by the way certain magmas fracture under specific thermal gradients. Basalt wants to be hexagonal. Foam-stone wants to be domed. The planet, in its infancy, doodles architecture in the margins of its own creation. They stand as scattered monuments to a mathematics of cooling. You cannot enter them; there are no rooms, only the hollow luck of bubbles and joints. They are the built quality of a world that has not yet met life.

The scale is open, regional, multi-featured. From any vantage, you see a vocabulary of forms: the fissure garden of needle spires to the north, the slow-motion lava coils to the south, the vast tilted plateau that is currently splitting into two new plateaus. It is a town-sized valley of geology, but the town is in violent, slow-motion rearrangement.

There is no water. Not a drop of liquid, not a vapor that could condense. The chemistry here is non-aqueous, dry to the bone. Yet in the glassy vesicles of the newest stone, there is a ghost—a trace of hydrated minerals, a fossil of wetness locked in permineralized vapor, as if the planet remembers a chemistry it has not yet chosen to use. The dryness is absolute, but it is an absolute with a memory.

The weirdness is subtle, not overwhelming. It is in the way you see. Despite the darkness—despite the dust-lid sky and the absence of sun, moon, or star—you can perceive your surroundings. Not brightly. Shapes resolve at twenty meters, mass and silhouette at fifty. But there is a sourceless visibility, a faint luminescence that clings to the edges of things. The rocks do not glow; the darkness itself seems faintly reflective, as if the absence of light has acquired texture. Or perhaps the constant warmth and the constant dark together generate a confusion of sense, a thermal vision that prints itself on the retina without photons. You see by heat, but not with infrared—rather with some wrong intermediate, a grey visibility that makes the warm stone stand out against the slightly cooler air. It is a world that defines itself by thermal outline, a palpable dark where shadows are warm and the only light is the outline of warmth.

The rules of this place are austere and singular. Nothing grows; nothing rots. There is no cycle of nutrients, only a cycle of states: melt, extrude, crystallize, fracture, subside, melt again. The only succession is mineral—olivine yields to pyroxene yields to feldspar as the body cools from the outside in, a lithology of thermal descent. What appears solid is merely slow. What appears still is merely between motions. The ecology is one of crystal grain competing with crystal grain for cooling space.

You are not in danger of freezing. You are in danger of becoming irrelevant. The world is too busy becoming itself to notice you. The scattered architectural stones stand empty because there is no one to fill them, and no reason to. They are the byproduct of a planet teaching itself how to stand. The turbulence will continue—in millions of years it may settle into hills and valleys, the dust may part, the darkness may crack open to some distant star. But here, now, in this warm, high, hollow dark, the ground is still sketching its own face in sharp, raw, mineral lines, and the silence is the sound of a world that has not yet learned to speak.
