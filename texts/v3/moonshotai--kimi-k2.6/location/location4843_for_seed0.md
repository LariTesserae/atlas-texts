The basin does not sleep; it has not yet learned how. It is a wide, low sump in the crust of the world, recently arrived, still cooling from the argument that birthed it, and the first thing that meets you—before the heat, before the smell—is the sound. It is not a sound you hear so much as a pressure you wear. Ten million chitinous rattles, an uncountable chorus of air sacs and vibrating membranes, the endless percussion of claw on glass and mandible on pulp, all layered into a single, sustained, deafening roar that makes the molars ache and turns the air into something almost solid. It never drops below this threshold. There is no dawn chorus, no evening hush, only the permanent white-noise storm of a biology that has discovered urgency.

And because the sound is so total, it takes longer than it should to notice the darkness.

Light down here is a rumor. The basin is low, sunk below the knees of the surrounding world, and what little sky might be visible is smothered by a perpetual thermal haze rising from the warm floor. But even that is insufficient explanation; the gloom feels older than the steam. It is a bruise-colored, submarine darkness in which your hand vanishes before your elbow, a darkness so complete that the lushness of the vegetation seems like an act of defiance against physics. Yet the plants do not merely survive; they riot. They have abandoned photosynthesis as a luxury they cannot afford. These are heat-eaters, sulfur-drinkers, chemotropic and thermotropic organisms with thick, rubbery leaves the color of bruised plums and old blood. Towering cycad-like ferns pulse with internal warmth, their fronds uncurling not toward any sun but toward the nearest geothermal exhalation. Vines thick as a man’s wrist braid between the raw stones, dripping condensation from waxy bulbs that store chemical energy. The understory is impenetrable, a mesh of living tissue that fuses at the margins—stem grafting to stem, leaf drowning leaf—so that walking through it is less like moving through a forest and more like pushing through the wall of a spongy, sweating lung. Every cubic foot is occupied. The density is not merely visual but tactile; the air itself feels packed, swarming with spores and shed skin and the microscopic freight of decay.

The terrain beneath this dense, pressing life is absurdly young. Geologically, it is an infant. There has been no time for water or wind to soften the edges. The floor is carpeted with fresh basalt and obsidian, sharp enough to part hide like paper. The stones lie in fractured geometries that mimic the toolkit of an earlier world: conchoidal hand-axes, scrapers, awls, blades—all perfectly accidental, all completely virgin. No hand struck them. They are the byproduct of the earth’s recent convulsion, a lithic scatter so naturally precise that to pick one up is to feel the phantom grip of a million years of unlived prehistory. And something here has noticed.
