To reach the floor, you descend through a breach in the earth that has not yet learned to be a valley. The rim is raw permafrost and shattered Silurian stone, still sharp from the collapse that opened this place only decades ago—geologically, it happened yesterday. Thermokarst on a massive scale: an ice wedge melted, a dome of frozen earth lost its spine, and a whole section of boreal plain folded inward, leaving a subsided bowl a mile and a half across. From the surrounding tundra, the land simply falls away, and you drop below the surface level of the world into a space that feels less like a cave than like an open wound held open by the sky.

It is dim here. The sun, already terminal-low on the horizon at this latitude, must fight through a persistent ground-haze of sublimating ice and the overhanging lip of the collapse. What arrives is not light so much as a gray-green phosphor, a twilight that seems to emanate from the mist itself rather than from any celestial source. Shadows do not move; they merely settle, inches deep, in the lee of every object.

The floor is not soil. It is plant matter. Centuries of undecayed moss, sedge, and larch needles—compressed into a thick, rubbery peat—form the only substrate. You stand on vegetation. You walk on a mattress of organic history that yields slightly underfoot, exhaling cold, acidic breath. This is a pure plant ecosystem; no animal has left any meaningful mark. The fauna, what little there is, consists of the occasional midge too sluggish to fly in the cold, and the rare arctic fox that appears at the rim, looks down, and thinks better of descending. The ground itself is alive in places—pockets of Sphagnum still rooting in the upper layers, dwarf birch sending hair-fine roots laterally, forming low hummocks—but the presences are scattered. Between the hummocks stretch wide, flat pans of bare peat, rime-crusted, gray-brown, empty. It is sparse. You can walk for twenty minutes and encounter nothing taller than your ankle, then come upon a stunted larch, forty years old and only waist-high, holding its needles like a clenched fist.

The air is damp, not wet. There are no streams. Water here arrives as condensation, as a cold breath that beads on the rusted flanks of machinery and drips from the fractured walls in slow, irregular intervals. Occasional seep lines darken the raw sediments, but they freeze before they can pool. The humidity is constant, a cold sweat on every surface, turning the dusting of snow that blows in from the rim into a slush that never quite melts.

It is cold. The temperature sits at the threshold of freezing and seems to prefer the side of ice. Exhaled breath joins the fog immediately
