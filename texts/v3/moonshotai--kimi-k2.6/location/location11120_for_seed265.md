There is no light here, not merely an absence but a settled priority, as if the atmosphere long ago reached a consensus that photons are unnecessary. Bring a lantern, and the flame will cower; it does not sputter for lack of air—there is air, thick and warm and tidal in the lungs—but because the darkness here is structural, a root-condition of the place. It is a darkness with weight and nap, like velvet laid over the eyes, and beneath it the world proceeds by touch, by smell, by the muffled pressure of distant mass against the eardrums.

You stand in a basin so newly born that the crust has not yet finished sighing. The elevation is low, a sag in the world where the mantle recently shrugged and left a floor of black glass and green, unweathered basalt. Nothing has been rounded. Every ridge is a knife-edge, every fissure a fresh surgical slit. The rocks are young and arrogant, still holding the heat of their making, and when the wetness touches them, it steams. The air temperature hovers in the swelter, a greenhouse without a sun, warmed from below by geological memory and from beside by the leaking machinery that dots the open expanse.

For it is open here. The scale is regional, a valley-or-town-sized hollow that extends beyond any sense of boundary. In the absolute dark, the horizon is not seen but inferred by the way sounds die before they echo, by the way the warm wet wind moves in broad, lazy sheets across your face. It is an openness that should feel liberating, yet it does not, because the place is also dense. Density is not a contradiction here; it is a layering. The air itself is crowded, saturated with water to the point where breathing feels like drinking. Condensation beads on every surface—weeping rocks, sweating pipes, the slick flanks of unseen things—then drips, not in a rhythm but in a continuous, communal exhale. The darkness collects moisture and holds it, refusing evaporation, so that every cubic meter of the atmosphere is a near-liquid soup of warmth and spore and mineral tang.

Vegetation has scarcely arrived. Here and there, pale rootless stalks thread up from fissures in the fresh stone, drawing nothing from photosynthesis, tentative and sparse. But if plants are few, fauna is overwhelming in its abundance. They own this place. Blind, unpigmented, dense beyond counting, they occupy every stratum. In the mud, soft bodies pulse in congregations of thousands per square meter. Above them, mid-weight things drift or cling, and higher still the air carries its own traffic of silent, membranous life. They do not call. They do not buzz. The sound rating here is low, a hush enforced by the wetness and the dark. Movement is lubricated by condensation, so the creatures glide rather than scuttle, ooze rather than stride. You might feel the brush of a thousand tiny feet, the warmth of clustered metabolisms, but you will hear only the faint, biological susurrus of unseen breathing, and the occasional soft *pluck* as something drops from a wet surface into deeper wetness.

Scattered across this crowded void are the remains—or perhaps the continuations—of an industrial apparatus that operates without purpose and without audience. A single foundry, chapel-sized, squats in the glassy grit, its furnace still hot to the touch, though no flame has ever burned here; the heat is the byproduct of a process that has forgotten its product. A conveyor rises from the muck, vanishes into the dark, its belt stiff with rust and mineral scale, advancing one peristaltic inch each hour, carrying toward nowhere a load of identical, dark pellets that may be ore, may be excrement, may be compressed shadow. Smokestacks vent warm vapor into the already drowning air. Pipes, fractal and over-scaled, run from structures that are half-buried, half-emerged, their joints weeping petroleum-scented brine. There is a sense that production is still occurring, but on timescales too broad for a human life to witness the completion of a single unit. The technology is industrial, mechanized, electrified—if you press your palm to certain hatches, you feel a subsonic thrum, a current in the wet ground—but it is industrial in the manner of a heart that continues to beat after the body has been forgotten.

And then there is the weirdness, the 2.7 of reality breakdown, which here is not violent but patient, a slow fraying at the hem of cause and effect. The darkness makes it easier; without light, the eye cannot catch the world in its inconsistencies. Walk toward a smokestack for what feels like an hour, and you may arrive at its base to find you have traveled either ten feet or ten miles—the distance is negotiable. Gravity gathers in certain depressions like water in a hoofprint, so that in one spot you might weigh twice yourself, while three paces away you are buoyant, treading carefully lest you drift. The sharp young rocks remember their molten state and, in the slowest of cycles, occasionally soften and flow for a decade or two before snapping back to obsidian rigidity, leaving behind rippled stone where there should be solid ridge. The industrial machinery participates in this dream-logic: a gear may turn before it is oiled, the oil arriving years later; a boiler’s pressure gauge reads not the present but a Tuesday that has not yet occurred. The place is young, geologically infantile, and like all infants it has not yet learned which rules are non-negotiable. Physics here is a suggestion, a habit it is still considering whether to adopt.

The ecology, if it can be called that, is a collaboration between the wet, the warm, the fauna, and the machine. The creatures feed on the condensation, on the mineral Sweat of the rocks, and perhaps on the byproducts of those ancient engines. The engines, in turn, seem to feed on attention, or on time, or on the slow rot of the darkness itself. It is a closed loop of tremendous scale and minimal flux. Rivers do not run here; water arrives and joins the atmosphere, seeps downward, condenses upward, a patient, breath-like cycle without urgency. The fauna breed slowly, live long, move seldom. The foundries thrum at frequencies below hearing. Everything is gradual, patient, a held breath.

If you stay, you will notice that the darkness begins to feel populated. Not just by the abundant blind life that brushes your ankles and clusters overhead, but by the presences of the place itself—the dense, near-touching layers of entity. The water droplets suspended in the air seem to hold intention. The industrial ruins exert a presence like sleeping giants. Adjacent possibilities press close, realities that chose slightly different paths, layering atop one another so that in certain patches the air is twice as thick, twice as warm, crowded with *almosts*. This is the density: not merely biological, but existential. An open, vast, valley-sized dark that is nonetheless packed to suffocation with what is, what was, and what is slowly, slowly manufacturing itself out of the wet and the heat and the unrelieved black.

It is a newborn place. It does not need light. It is busy becoming, at the speed of cooling magma, at the speed of rust accumulating on a motionless piston, in the warm, dripping silence of a world that has only recently learned to hold itself together.
