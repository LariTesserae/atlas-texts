There is a place where the ocean forgot to move. Not a breath of current, not a thermal whisper disturbs the water column. The world is drowned so utterly that *shore* is a forgotten theoretical state, yet you stand—or rather, suspend—at high elevation, poised on the jagged spinal ridge of a range that has only lately torn upward from the abyss. These mountains are infants. Their basalt is black volcanic glass, unsanded by grain or tide, bearing edges sharp enough to part a passing limb without resistance. They rise in serrated ranks across a vista that devours perspective, and because the water here is almost perfectly empty, you can see them do it.

The density of matter in this ocean is nearly zero. No plankton clouds bloom; no sediment drifts down from a distant surface; no organic snow ever falls. The liquid itself is mineral-pure, pressed near to its freezing point, and thus as transparent as polished crystal. On the highest ridges, visibility is measured not in meters but in hundreds of kilometers, as if you were breathing thin mountain air rather than submerged in crushing depth. What light there is arrives diffuse and neutral—a perpetual gray-blue twilight, sourceless and steady. Far above, a shell of permanent ice or cloud-cap filters whatever illumination exists beyond, and because the dynamic of this world is frozen in equilibrium, it never brightens toward noon or dims toward dark. The shadows are soft, static, eternal.

The sound is the first thing that would kill you, if killing were an active process here. It is not a crash, not a tempest’s howl, but a continuous subterranean drone so loud it ceases to be heard and becomes a physical weight against the sternum, a vibration in the aqueous humor of the eye. It issues from the bones of the world. The young mountains are still trying to grow; tectonic forces thrust them upward in a gesture arrested mid-motion by the planet’s frozen equilibrium. The plates strain against one another and cannot slip, cannot release. The resulting resonance is a single, standing note—lower than thunder, steadier than any machine—propagating through the crust and into the entire oceanic body. It never swells in crescendo. It never hollows into silence. It is simply *on*, a deafening infrastructure of acoustic pressure that has outlasted every purpose it might once have served.

Scale is hallucinatory. The ridgelines run until they curve with the world. Because there is no haze, no particulate veil, the curvature itself is visible: distant peaks diminish not into blur but into perfect miniatures, stacked like paper silhouettes against blue-gray emptiness. This ocean is vast enough to generate its own weather systems, its own gyres and storm fronts, but weather is a process and this place has abolished process. Instead, you sense the *ghost* of weather—layers of thermal and saline gradient suspended in the water column like strata in amber, unmoving, potent, and asleep. It is a planet unto itself, yet nothing happens on it.

What structures exist are scattered, angular, and ambiguous. Every several hundred kilometers along a ridge crest, the bare rock is interrupted by the artifacts of industry. Stepped towers of heat-scored ceramic and oxidized steel rise from fissures, not ruined but suspended in function. There is no current to scour them smooth, no storm to unseat their rivets, only the slow molecular conversation of cold water on metal. Their furnaces still glow; their pistons still cycle in a rhythm synchronized, perhaps accidentally, with the planetary hum. Vent pipes exhale mineral precipitates and trapped gases in silver columns that rise toward the distant ceiling in perfectly straight lines, never wavering. Over decades, these exhalations have accreted into hollow salt-spires—industrial chimneys growing like stalagmites—because in a medium without drift, nothing disperses. A bubble released from a fracture will rise to its equilibrium depth and hang there, a sphere of metallic gas locked in place, unchanged since the day it formed.

Fauna is abundant, though the contradiction resolves only when you remember the volume. Millions upon millions of creatures occupy this continuum, but the space between any two of them may be measured in kilometers. They move along invisible corridors as if the ocean were an orrery and they its ponderous planets. Vast, translucent crustaceans stroke the water with limbs like fractured church glass, their metabolisms slowed to the tempo of the cold. Schools of bone-white ribbon-eels, blind and delicate, pass in formations that never tangle or alter. Armored isopods cling to the industrial pipes, pulsing in metronomic unison as they filter metallic oxides from the tower-exhalations. None of them feed on plants, for there are none; there is no vegetation here, no algal film, no bacterial mat vast enough to color a stone. The rock is bare. The animals live instead on chemistry, on the slow ripeness of the young stone itself, and on the synthetic rains of the scattered refineries. They do not evolve, and they do not age in any meaningful sense. They circulate, return, repeat.

It is the youth of the terrain that makes the stillness so uncanny. These features have not had time to soften. No canyon has been widened by persistence; no slope has been gentled by collapse. The glass-sharp ridges exist in a state of permanent prelude, as though the world has been filmed at the exact frame of its birth and the reel has jammed. The water is cold and clear and endless. The light does not shift. The deafening resonance of the straining earth continues without measure. You are suspended in an ocean that has achieved, against all entropy, a perfect and terrible equilibrium—vast, empty, loud, and forever holding its breath.
