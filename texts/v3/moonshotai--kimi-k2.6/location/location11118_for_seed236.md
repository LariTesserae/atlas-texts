At first, from the ridgeline, you mistake it for a town.

The peaks around it are infantile—raw, livid stone, still keening from the recent violence of their upthrust. Black flanks exhibit no gentleness; erosion has not yet visited to file away the knife-edges and soften the spurs. This is a freshly wounded range, and the hollow cradled between two serrated shoulders appears, against all reason, to contain architecture. Domes. Cloisters. The suggestion of alleys and small plazas. The scale is wrong for a wild thing: it is the size of a modest settlement, a single bounded amphitheater of life cupped in stone the color of scorched bone and cooling iron.

Then you descend, and the error persists, but its nature inverts. It *is* a town, but nothing here has ever been built.

The silence is the first thing that enters your body. Not the vacancy of a desert—this is a *full* silence, the density of a crowded room where every occupant has simultaneously agreed to hold its breath. Sound dies here. The material of the hollow, accreted over only a few centuries by organisms that precipitate mineral as coral precipitates lime, is profoundly absorbent. It is porous, warm, and damp-looking, yet desert-dry to the touch. There is no water here as you understand it. No creek hisses; no dew beads. Moisture is a trace element, locked in crystalline hydrates and internal biological gels. The life of this place has bypassed the aqueous phase and runs instead on a chemistry of metal-sulfur complexes and siloxane polymers, but the result, paradoxically, is lush. The biomass is overwhelming. Every vertical face is furred with a nap so thick and trembling it resembles velvet upholstery. The colors are the colors of oxidation: verdigris, burnt sienna, the bronzed green of weathered copper. Succulent rosettes, glassy and hard as cabochons, crowd every cornice. Hanging curtains of filamentous growth drape between the spires like tapestries in a merchant’s quarter. It is a garden of mineral verdancy, wet in appearance, dry in essence, dripping with a chemistry that has never known rain.

And the spires themselves—these are the builders, the citizenry, the infrastructure. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder, nearly touching, layered in vertical strata from the floor of the hollow to the lip of the rim. Density is the governing law. The gaps between them have been narrowed by slow competition to widths that admit only a turned body, a sideways shuffle; the alleys breathe a trapped warmth that never dissipates. The stone of the young mountains radiates upward from below, and the metabolic blanket of living matter seals it in, creating a greenhouse of oddly municipal character. You walk not through wilderness but through *arrangement*. Turn left and the passage opens into a rough square where the pillars have, by mutual exclusion zones grown in chemical dialect, cleared a space the size of a chapel nave. Dead-ends are rare; the plan seems to funnel in spirals. Windows appear—dark, kidney-shaped apertures where symbiotic fauna nest in stacked galleries. A bell-tower rises, but there is no bell. There is no bronze, no timber, no fired brick, no fiber, no flaked stone. No tool has ever struck this place. The urban form is a convergent accident of tropism and geometry, of shadow-seeking growth and load-bearing crystallization. It is a city in the same way a termitarium is a cathedral: organized, divisible into districts, dense with habitation, and entirely innocent of intention.

The fauna are everywhere, and they are mute. Life is abundant to the point of congestion. Flat-winged creatures the texture of dried parchment adhere by the hundreds to the warm walls, pulsing gently as they drink mineral vapor through abdominal spiracles. Worms of ocher felt thread the deeper grottoes. Mites like mobile jewels negotiate the valleys of the velvet mosses. None of them speak. None click, chirp, hiss, or buzz. They are too soft, or the medium is too viscous with suspended salts, or perhaps in a world so densely packed, sound is simply too expensive—a disruptive wavefront in a brittle equilibrium. Communication proceeds instead by chemical bloom. The air tastes of ozone and clove, of struck flint and coriander. When a creature wishes to declare itself, it vents a molecule; the message lingers in the warm stillness for hours, a graffiti of scent.

The light is normal, fierce and high-altitude clear. The sun at this elevation has a clinical edge, but the densely packed verticality shatters it. Beams enter the hollow in warm, dusty lances, partitioning the space into wedges of amber and cobalt shadow. The normalcy of the light makes the place stranger. You expect a green world beneath such illumination, a water-brimming dell. Instead the glow falls on this dry, breathing mineral metropolis, highlighting the thready texture of the biological pelts and the newborn sharpness of the young extrusions. Everything gleams with the unweathered freshness of a thing just emerged. There are no rounded cobbles, no softened stair-treads. Edges are acute. Surfaces are matte and tooth-colored, unsoiled by the long centuries that usually grime a city into comfort. This place is still hot from its own becoming.

It is growing. That is the slow dynamic, the final rule. The hollow was not here five hundred years ago. It began as a crack in fresh uplift, a stress fracture where warm mineral vapors vented and a pioneer colonial organism found purchase. Since then the vertical alleys have narrowed finger by finger, decade by decade. The tallest spires continue to sweat upward, accreting laminae of carbonate and phosphate biopolymers. Eventually the passages will seal. The forum will close. The breathable space will be consumed by its own inhabitants, and the town will become a solid, stratified plug—a frozen instant of urban desire. But the colony is not dying; it is ripening. Its spores, light as glass dust, will ride the high winds to other young wounds in the infant mountain range, there to found another hollow, another silent, waterless, lush and crowded city in the cupped palms of raw stone. Patient beyond any human metric, the landscape breeds municipalities that neither know nor need the hand that built them.
