You do not see this place. You enter it as you might enter the throat of something sleeping underwater: the cool, the pressure, the absolute insistence of dark. Light here measures itself at a vanishing fraction—less than starlight behind a moon—because the bowl of this world is set low, sunk into a weathered continental depression so vast that its walls, if walls still exist, are many days’ travel away, lost to both sight and to the curvature of the stone itself. The elevation is a long exhale of geology; everything has settled, sagged, flowed downward until the ancient karst behaves like a sea floor stranded in the open air, or what passes for air. There is no architecture. There is no tool, no made line, but there is an overwhelming presence of the living.

The first thing that strikes is the noise. It is not a background. It is foreground in all directions, a physical viscosity that fills the ears until the jaw aches and the ribs seem to rattle in sympathy. The sound is deafening not by violence but by mass. It is the friction of uncountable bodies—trillions, perhaps quadrillions—wearing their existence against the wet rock and against one another. A constant scouring hiss. A percussive clicking, as if the basin is filled with hail that never falls, only taps and scrabbles and devours. Low beneath that, a sub-bass drone: the resonance of the hollow stone itself, the chest-cavity of the world amplifying every chitinous step into a sustained, geological note. To think clearly here is difficult. The sound demands participation; even silence would be shouted down.

The temperature sits in the cool margin, neither freezing nor merciful—perhaps ten degrees Celsius, a damp cellar chill that never warms and never breaks into frost. The cold is married to saturation. Water does not arrive here as a gift of rivers alone, though there are channels, black and slow, that wander the basin floor and vanish into swallow-holes with a patient, sucking patience. Mostly it arrives as breath. The air is a sponge pressed against the stone; condensation beads on every surface and trickles in silver peristalsis down the calcified flows. When it rains, it rains without announcement—a sparse, cold application of heavier drops from a ceiling of cloud or stone or permanent night that never shows its face. The moisture never dries. It is the medium, the blood substitute, the only glue that holds the emptiness together.

And indeed it is nearly empty of vegetable life. Vegetation here is a rumor, a statistical error. Perhaps a stain of oxidized mineral that mirrors the shape of a leaf; perhaps a faint slick of chemosynthetic film in a grotto corner, so marginal it is consumed faster than it can be named. No root holds the ground. No stem reaches. The eroded stone—established, balanced, worn to the smoothness of old bone—has nothing to grip except the damp. What forms the base of the food chain is not rooted but fallen: organic snow. From some distant, sunlit plenum above the rim of this sunken country, matter dies and drifts downward. Dissolved, particulate, gelatinous—marine snow, terrestrial sporefall, the liquefying effluvia of a brighter world. The basin catches it in its cool, wet lung. By the time it arrives, it is ghost-flakes of sustenance, a thin soup in the condensation, and the only economy is the scavenging of sky-catch.

Yet from this impoverished rain, the fauna have built an empire of overwhelming density. There is no empty volume. The scale of the place is open, regional, valley-and-town-sized in its repetition, but every meter of it is crowded near to bursting. The floor is not floor but a living nap, a carpet of translucent, eyeless things whose segmentation is uncertain—some with too many legs, some with none, all with skin or chitin that has gone the color of cave milk, of old limestone, of the water that films their backs. They are wet. Their bodies are held together by surface tension and glycoprotein slime that prevents desiccation in the saturated chill. They move in tides, not by season—which does not exist in the dark—but by condensation fronts. When the air thickens, the living carpet surges toward the moisture like iron filings to a magnet, and the sound swells accordingly, a crescendo of need.

Layers of them. A ground stratum of crawlers and suckers, bellies pressed to stone, feeding on the organic mist and the particulate fallout. Above them, a middle realm of larger forms, jointed or boneless, navigating not by sight—eyes are a forgotten luxury here—but by vibration, by the acoustic signature each exudes into the deafening roar. They hunt by hunting silence, seeking the dead spot in the tapestry of clicks where prey has paused to breathe. Higher still, the air itself carries weight: floating larval stages, ballooning things trailing sticky filaments to snag descending matter, winged or wingless fliers that never see the sky they inhabit. The density is such that to wave an arm is to strike meat. To fall is to be caught by bodies. The basin has no open space; it has only interstitial stone, wet intervals between the pressing fact of animal presence.

They are strange, but anchored to the believable. Their mouths are often not mouths at all but ventral fields of palps, absorption surfaces that kiss the slick rock and strip the water of its nutrient load. Some bear their nervous systems in braids along their spines, external, twitching in sympathetic rhythm with the sub-bass drones of the stone. Predation is horizontal and immediate; with no vertical architecture of root or branch, there is nowhere to flee except into the mass. Safety is statistical. A million are crushed or devoured every hour; a million million hatch or bud or split from gelatinous holding-sacs flushed by the black streams. The ecosystem wastes nothing. No midden rises. No structure accrues. Carapaces, when shed, dissolve in the acidic film of the condensation before dawn—which is a meaningless word here—can arrive. The rules of the world forbid accumulation. What is dead is eaten; what is eaten is shat; what is shat is leached into the stone, and the stone, old and porous and in balance, drinks it without comment.

The dynamic is restless, mid-paced, a biological weather. Cold fronts of migration move across the open floor, visible only as shifts in the sound’s texture: a region of lower clicks where the crawlers have drained away, an onslaught of higher-pitched friction where a new wave arrives. The black pools rise slightly with the rains and lower as the karst sips them away, and each fluctuation causes another turn in the living clock. The only stillness belongs to the rock itself, the long-eroded, low-lying stone that has accepted its role as host. It does not judge. It has been here longer than light mattered, and it will remain when the last moist body has finished its screaming.

You stand, or imagine standing, in the center of this country of darkness, and the cool air presses your face with damp fingers. You cannot see your own hand, but you know it would be rimed with the condensation of so many breaths, so many exhalations from the trillion throats that are the only canopy here. The sound does not diminish. It has nowhere to go in this open, sunken bowl; it circles, accumulates, compounds. It is the sunlight of this place, the mapping system, the calendar, the warning, and the inevitable end. A world without plant, without light, without built thing or tool—only the wet, the chill, the worn and weathered hollow, and an animal density so complete that the earth itself seems to have been replaced by their hunger,
