The climb ends not at a summit but at a hole: a crevasse punched into the mountain’s final skull of weathered granite, some eight thousand feet above the last whisper of treeline. The air outside is thin enough to sharpen stars to ice, yet the breath that rises from the fissure is warm, wet, and heavy, carrying the mineral tang of long-buried water. You descend not into cold but into a sultriness that belongs to the guts of the earth, a pocket trapped between the mountain’s ancient bones and a last, dying pulse of geothermal heat.

It is the size of a cathedral nave, but no human hand ever shaped it. Erosion—patient, grinding, millions of years in the making—has done all the work. The walls are flowstone and basalt worn to the curves of a body’s interior: bellied ceilings, thighs of rounded rock, a floor cupped like palms pressed together. There are no corners here, no scaffolding, no axe-mark or chisel-scratch. Only the slow sculpture of vapor and gravity, settled into its own state so completely that the chamber seems less excavated than grown.

And at first, because the light is nearly absolute—0.3 on any scale you might carry—you think the space is empty.

It is not.

Your foot comes down on something that gives, slightly, a cool rubberiness between sole and stone. You freeze. The darkness is so profound that your eyes invent phantoms; but the sounds do not lie. Around you, the wet air is *thick* with presence. A constant, low-grade *shushing*, like sand dragged across satin, rises from every direction. Thousands upon thousands of bodies, shifting against one another and the walls.

As your senses adjust, the density reveals itself. The chamber is packed near to saturation. Every surface—floor, ceiling, the bulbous protrusions of mid-wall ledges—is layered with fauna, stacked in strata of warm, slick weight. They are the size of housecats, or slugs, or something caught between salamander and cuttlefish: pale, translucent, their bodies threaded with slow pulses of amber fluid that you can only see when your eyes brush against the rarest photon. They have no visible eyes themselves, no clear front or back, just a series of cartilaginous ridges down their lengths and dozens of tiny, stubby paddles that grip the stone with a wet *click*. Where one ends, another begins. They cling. They drape. They fill the room like a living mortar, five deep in the hollows, draped from the ceiling in stalactitic clumps that sway with a motion too patient to be called swaying.

The overwhelmingness of them is not in aggression but in weight of numbers. There is nowhere to look without finding their slick pallor, their peristaltic breathing, the translucent skin stretched over organs you can almost name before remembering they belong to no taxonomy you know. They are primitive in the truest sense: pre-complexity, pre-specialization, ancient as the softened stone they cling to. They do not build. They do not hunt in any way you can witness. They filter the damp air, scrape microscopic sustenance from the condensation that beads on the ceiling and trickles in a barely perceptible rhythm into the warm pools scattered across the floor.

Water does not rush here; it accumulates. The dampness is existential, a floating mist that never quite becomes rain. Occasionally a fat drop breaks free from above and falls through the strata of bodies, each creature shifting minutely to let it pass, a wave of slow undulations that takes minutes to settle. The pools are no larger than offering bowls, warm as blood, and they reflect nothing because there is nothing to reflect.

The sound is loud, but strangely without urgency. It is the aggregated noise of near-ten thousand individuals existing in proximity: the wet *click* of chitinous paddles finding purchase, the *slop* of mucus as one repositions over another, a constant infrasonic hum from their communal breathing that vibrates in your molars. It does not spike. It does not ebb. It is a sustained chord of animal presence, patient and eternal.

Temperature holds steady at the low end of fever. The warmth keeps the condensation coming, keeps the creatures active, keeps the cycle turning at a speed almost too slow to watch. They breed, if that is the word, in glacial pulses—egg sacs like opalescent grapes that will gestate for years before releasing pale young already equipped to cling. The chamber has looked exactly like this, give or take a generation, for centuries. Erosion has not broken the balance; it has only cupped the space more deeply, made more room for the layers.

There is no vegetation to speak of. A single albino tendril, no thicker than a thread, pales its way down from a hairline crack in the ceiling, root-hunting in a place where no sun has ever fallen. Otherwise, life here skips the plant entirely, moving from mineral and vapor straight into animal, dense and unapologetic.

You stand, and the darkness presses your skin like a palm. The creatures do not fear you. They have never learned to. They adjust around your ankles, slow as cooling wax, and the clicking continues, and the warm mist settles on your lips with the taste of copper and time. The mountain breathes out, and they breathe with it, a primitive, overflowing congress in the dark at the top of the world.
