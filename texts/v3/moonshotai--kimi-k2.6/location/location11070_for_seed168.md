You stand on a plateau so high that the horizon shows a faint curvature, a brass-colored world floating above any conceivable sea level. The air is warm—not from trapped greenhouse breath, but from the ground itself, which radiates a steady, metabolic heat upward through whatever touches it. It is anhydrous here. There is no water cycle, no rain, no rivers carving channels, no ice gnawing at the heights. What little vapor exists is metallic: copper chloride, volatilized silicates, a faint haze of zinc and cadmium that turns the sunlight into something normal yet strangely weighted, like afternoon light through the high windows of a cathedral built to machines.

The scale is enormous. You can see for fifty miles in every direction across a basin that might be the size of a small continent, yet the density of things is so low that each presence exists in sovereign isolation. A grove. A vent. A single towering formation. Between them, nothing but the raw, emerging stone.

Erosion has not begun. This is a pre-landscape, a geology still in its first hour. The stone is not rounded; it is accusatory, sharp, half-cooled. Slag-piles of green peridotite rise in angles too steep to survive a single season of weathering, yet they have stood since always. Fissures open with the fresh black lips of recent tearing, though nothing has moved here since the beginning. The crust is embryonic, a foetal lithosphere that has not yet learned to age. It is all birth and no decay.

And on this warm, raw, empty stone grows a vegetation so lush it defies the absence of water. It is not biology. It is chemistry performing the imitation of life. Across the sheltered lees of thermal vents, verdigris mosses of copper sulfate spread in velvet carpets two feet deep, soft and damp with condensed metal vapors. In the open plains, forests of potassium dendrites rise like pines, branching in perfect industrial fractals, their needles glittering with electroplated silver. Meadows of tin whiskers sway in magnetic breezes no animal will ever feel, and great fans of aluminum sulfate unfold like tree-ferns, their surfaces etched with the precise, repetitive patterns of circuit boards. Wherever the subterranean fractionation columns vent their purified products, the ground blooms into gardens of oxide and salt—lush, profuse, riotous—but each grove is an island separated by hours of walking across bare, warming rock.

There is no fauna. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. Nothing buzzes between the metallic blossoms. The crystal ferns do not need pollinators; they precipitate. The dendrite trees do not drop seeds; they accrete from vapor phase. There is no hum of insects, no cry of birds, no scuttling in the undergrowth because there is no undergrowth in the organic sense, and nothing to scuttle. The ecology is a monoculture of process: deposition, crystallization, sublimation, recrystallization. It is lush, but it is silent.

And it is industrial. The entire plateau operates with the logic of a scaled refinery, though no hand has ever built a thing here. Deep beneath the raw crust, silent convection cells sort molten material by density and conductivity, functioning as blast furnaces without walls. Up through the fissures come pure streams of metal vapor, rising into cooler strata where they condense according to their boiling points—natural distillation. The ground is perforated with vent-holes that act as extrusion dies, producing hexagonal rods of basaltic glass with the regularity of manufactured rebar. Fault lines creep at exactly the speed required to sort ore from slag. A canyon opens not by random catastrophe, but by the slow, relentless pressure of a tectonic press, forging new sheet-metal planes of mica. The landscape is a factory that manufactures itself, and its product is more landscape.

Yet nothing changes. This is the central mystery, the frozen equilibrium. The crystal pines grow, but they never reach maturity; each is eternally half-formed, caught in the act of branching. The vents exhale, but never weaken; the fissures widen by microns, then seal by microns, locked in a standing wave of emergence. The warm stone stays warm. The brass light stays brass. If there is a sun, it does not move, or if it moves, the shadows do not matter, because the landscape is temporally invariant—a photograph of the moment after creation, held in suspension forever. It is emerging, always emerging, and therefore never arriving.

The sound is almost nothing. The industrial processes are thermodynamic, electrochemical, silent. No grinding of gears, no hiss of steam. The air is too thin at this altitude to carry the low frequencies of tectonic movement, and the metallic vegetation grows without friction. Occasionally a tin whisker, grown too tall, shears off in a breeze of ionized gas, but it falls into the warm dust without a sound. The silence is total, profound, the hush of a machine room where every motor has been perfectly dampened, every belt runs in vacuum.

This is a world where geology achieved industry without ever inventing life or architecture. A warm, high, empty anvil of a place, forever being hammered into shape but never struck a second time. Lush and silent, raw and eternal, industrial and untouched. A garden of precipitates. A nursery of ore. A place that is always beginning.
