You do not walk on stone. The ground beneath your feet is lignin turned to something like horn or chert — warm, faintly yielding, with a grain that flows in great liquid curves, evidence that this was once alive. It is the skin of an astonished organism, a peneplain of organic matter planed flat by eons of wind and mild, enzymatic dissolution until the horizon is a true line, amber-brown and sage-gray, dividing a pale sky from its own sun-warmed hide. You are high, extraordinarily high; the air carries that thin, clarifying sharpness that makes distant ridges seem close enough to touch, yet the temperature clings to your skin with a humid, almost tropical weight. The plateau is a thermal battery, hoarding warmth in its compressed cellulose body and exhaling it through the mist-cool dusk.

This is a world of absolute vegetable continuity. There is no bedrock here. No granite spine underwriting the landscape. Everything — the pavement underfoot, the low undulating walls that rise like frozen swells, the “soil” in the crevices — is plant, was plant, dreams of being plant again. The surface is a necropolis and a nursery at once: peat compressed to the density of leather, heartwood mineralized to the hardness of tooth, and, colonizing every fissure, tentative new growths. Air-roots dangle in bony curtains from the lips of sinkholes. Lichens the color of tarnished copper spell out their slow geometries across the slopes. Even the dust is spore and milled cellulose. It is not lush in the way of a jungle; there is no dense canopy, no closed green chamber overhead. But every material presence is botanical. The architecture of the place is vegetal in origin — vast buttresses of fused root-matter, tubes the diameter of towers where xylem once rose toward a sun now dead, now empty and open to the sky. The erosion has revealed an anatomy lesson on a continental scale.

And yet, for all this material plenitude, the plateau is hollow.
