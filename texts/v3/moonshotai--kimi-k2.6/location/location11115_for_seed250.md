The water does not obscure; it reveals. You are submerged in it utterly, held inside a liquid so stripped of grain and body that sight lines reach out not for meters but for hundreds of kilometers, dissolving only at the curvature of the world itself. It is an ocean, yes, but an ocean trained into the habits of vacuum. No plankton bloom drifts across your field of view, no snow of marine detritus falls from above, no skin of organic film clouds the surface far overhead. The water is a cold, patient glass, swallowing definition but somehow never dimming it. Light arrives from the distant roof of the world not as a bruised blue twilight, but as something closer to normal: a thin, overcast afternoon that has simply been lowered into the deep and left there, still functioning, still ordinary, though it illuminates nothing like an Earthling sea.

You are high here. Sound paradoxical as it is, elevation holds meaning even beneath the weight of a global hydrosphere. Beneath you, the floor falls away into unmeasured trenches, but where you drift, the terrain thrusts upward in a mountain range that would shame any continental spine—if only continents had been allowed to form. These peaks are raw, embryonic, days or weeks old in geological time, still weeping from their own creation. The basalt has not learned the softening of erosion; there are no rounded boulders, no smoothed riverstones, no valleys carved by patient retreat. Instead, angles dominate. Hyperbolic shards of volcanic glass interlock like the broken facets of an expanding geode. Pillars of chill black stone rise in geometries that seem fractionally too precise, their hexagonal joints aligned with a regularity that suggests calibration rather than cooling. The rock is under construction. The planet is still pouring itself, and you have arrived at the workshop before the scaffolding was removed.

It is cool. The ambient water carries the chill of a deep cellar, hovering just above the threshold where pressure and salinity would force it into stranger states. Against this baseline frigidity, the embryonic range seethes. Fissures slit open along the ridgeline—bright, pneumatic wounds—and exhale supercritical plumes that boil upward in silver columns before being gutted by the surrounding freeze. The temperature never averages out. The water here exists in a permanent state of thermal argument: arctic currents slamming into magma-borngonne superheat, generating accelerating vortices that spin off into the photic haze and die. Because the scale is vast—not merely a rift but a planetary seam, a belt of mountains that girdles the entire sphere—these thermal collisions birth weather systems underwater. Internal tsunamis propagate along density gradients and never fully exhaust themselves. Standing waves taller than skyscrapers petrify against thermal shelves, collapsing and reforming with the rhythm of a respiring lung.

The sound is inescapable. Water, for all its apparent softness, is merciless with vibration. The constant drone of the range’s expansion lives in your bones before it reaches your ears: a subsonic note generated by two tectonic plates drawing apart at speeds that feel hurried, almost anxious. Over this basso profundo comes the percussion—sharp reports as magma chambers depressurize, the crystalline *tick* of basalt skin cracking under cold duress, the jet-engine scream of hydrothermal vents vomiting mineral liquor into the blue. It is loud enough that directionality fails. The sound does not come from left or right, above or below; it comes from the medium itself, from the architecture of the place, as if the water were a membrane being struck ceaselessly from inside and out.

And the place is empty. Between the volcanic highlands, the water column extends in distances normally reserved for desert skies. You can perceive the loneliness as a physical property. The density of solid matter is so vanishingly low that the mountains seem like afterthoughts suspended in a void that happens to be wet. Even the stone itself is hollowed. The newly extruded lava is frothied through with chambers, tunnels, vesicular bubbles the size of cathedrals. The ridge is riddled with negative space, as though the geology were trying to decide whether to become matter or void, and has compromised by becoming a lattice. You could swim through the flanks of these mountains for hours, navigating caverns that have never been mapped because they did not exist last month, their walls still slick with the vitreous sweat of first cooling.

There are no animals. None. Not even the theoretical memory of them. The fish-shaped silhouette that haunts the corner of an Earthling diver’s eye never forms here. No eye, no jaw, no flick of tail disturbs the crystalline equilibrium. The ocean has not invented fauna yet, or perhaps it has declined to. What little life exists is sparse to the point of austerity: brittle veils of pale vegetation, more mineral than plant, clinging to the thermal vents in charts and whorls. They look like verdigris growing on a circuit board, or lichen colonizing the lip of a forge. They do not sway with any pleasant organic rhythm; they endure, extracting chemistry from the rock’s exhalations, keeping their colonies small and far between. In the absence of herbivores, predators, and scavengers, there is no web, no pulse of hunger threading through the dark. Motion is reserved for the minerals and the currents.

The turbulence is the true inhabitant. This is not a landscape that sleeps. The ridge is not a *thing* but a *process* caught mid-sentence. Floor becomes wall; wall becomes caldera; caldera becomes chimney; chimney becomes rubble, and the rubble is already being ingested by the next fissure. You can watch a spire accrete in realtime, molecule by molecule, its mineral skin accumulating from the vented fluids with the brisk certainty of a 3D printer fulfilling a final blueprint. The water itself rebels against stillness. Upwellings corkscrew sideways; supercooled brine sinks in silver curtains that shatter upon contact with warmer strata; pressure fronts collide and birth transient pockets where the laws of buoyancy stutter.

And yet, for all its violence, there is a strangeness here that exceeds mere geology. The world behaves as though it were engineered. Not built—there is no artifact, no beam nor bolt, nothing that was *made*—but organized with a precision that mocks accident. The hollow chambers in the rock align in networks that channel heat with the efficiency of thermal superconductors. The vent orifices pulse in phased rhythms, distributing mineral load not chaotically but in oscillating waves, as if the planet were regulating its own metabolism. The clarity of the water, the exactitude of the basalt’s fracture patterns, the standing-wave climate—it all operates with the slick economy of a technology that has outstripped the need for hands. It is nanoscale engineering performed at a radius of thousands of kilometers, cosmic infrastructure wearing the mask of raw nature.

You hang in the blue, and the blue hangs in the world. There is no horizon that resolves into land, no promise of shore. The range simply continues, elevation without summit, high enough to snag the last ordinary light from the distant surface but too young to know what it is reaching for. It is loud, vast, empty, cool, and still being born—a submerged, unfinished continent ringing a planet still teaching its oceans how to be wet.
