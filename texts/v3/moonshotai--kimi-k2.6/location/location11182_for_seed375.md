There is a bowl in the earth where no water has ever gathered—not as dew, not as ice, not as the faintest blush of condensation on a cooled surface. The plain that surrounds it is a single, fused sheet of desiccated mineral dust, so dry that the lips crack merely to breathe its air. The bowl itself is low: you cross its rim not by descending a cliff but by stepping over a threshold no higher than a curb, into a sudden depression barely below the surrounding deadlands. Yet within that shallow dish, compressed to the scale of a cathedral close or a hamlet’s single clustered street, stands a city.

Or something that wears the corpse of a city.

The silence is the first thing. It is not the silence of emptiness, but of absorption. The very atmosphere here is an anechoic soup, thick with microscopic particulates that drink vibration before it can travel. A footfall on the plaza floor makes no report; a voice dies in the mouth, flattened to a tactile hum in the speaker’s own chest. Struck, the walls give back no ring. They seem to swallow sound as sustenance.

And the walls are everywhere. They rise in a layered density that would suggest a thriving municipality were it not for the hollowness—the profound, echoless vacancy—within each structure. Streets run between them, too wide for the sparse traffic they receive, vast corridors of motionless air. The architecture is organized with unsettling specificity: there are arcades, staircases spiraling around cores that hold no floors above, domed chambers with oculi open to the dim sky, and balconies that overhang the boulevards at heights suited to beings that do not exist here. It is urban in the truest formal sense—settlement-scale, infrastructure-dense, tiered in construction—but there is no technology. Nothing has been cut, or carved, or lifted by device. There are no marks of tools, no ash from fire, no metal, no joints. The material is uniform: a pale, chalky polymer-like substance the color of old bone left in moonlight, warm to the touch yet somehow drinking heat, so that the air within the bowl remains perennially cool. Geologically, it is newborn. The edges of every cornice, every window-mullion, every thin fluting on the colonnades are sharp as broken glass, unsoftened by any weathering. This place has only recently emerged, or recently decided to begin.

It is still deciding.

Watch a single block for an afternoon and you will witness the dynamic processes that belie its stillness. From hairline fissures in the plaza floor, a dry mist vents upward—an exhalation of non-aqueous chemistry, silicon and sulfur compounds in a suspension that owes nothing to water. Where this mist contacts the cool air, it accretes. Not slowly, not imperceptibly, but visibly, in a silence so complete one might imagine oneself deaf. A balcony extends by the width of a finger. A new wall buds from the flank of an existing façade, its surface already bearing the impressed texture of door-hinges and latch-plates, architectural details that serve no purpose and imply a memory no one here possesses. On the northern edge, a tower split this morning along a vertical seam; by evening, the two halves will have eased apart to form an archway, the fracture surfaces already bearing decorative geometric reliefs. The city is in motion. It breathes, expands, reconfigures. It is an ecosystem of mineral elaboration, a predator-prey cycle in which form devours empty space and excretes ever-more-refined infrastructure.

Vegetation, such as it is, exists only in the most tenacious sparse fashion: a few wire-thin stalks of metallic grey, rooted in the lee of walls where heavier particulates settle. They have no leaves, only translucent sheaths that seem to drink the dim light—a light that filters down from a sky occluded by the same pale dust that lifts perpetually from the fissures. The illumination is twilight made permanent, sufficient to navigate by, insufficient to cast a sharp shadow. The blue-grey stalks glow faintly at their tips with a bioluminescence or chemiluminescence of their own, drawing slow lines of faint phosphor along the lower thoroughfares.

And the fauna. They are rare. In an hour’s slow walk from the rim to the center and back, you may see none. But they are present. They appear as low, pale shapes the size of large tortoises, many-segmented and translucent, gliding across the plaza floors on hundreds of microscopic cilia that generate no friction-sound. They graze on the fresh accretions, rasping away thin layers of the bone-white material with radulae that leave polished trails. They are not builders. They do not plan. They are, instead, gardeners of happenstance, their grazing subtly altering the growth patterns, ensuring that the streets do not entirely close over, that the oculi in the domes remain open to the haze. Without them, the city might petrify into a solid mass. With them, it remains a habitable hollow—a thing that is empty by necessity, maintained in its vacancy by the rare traffic of these quiet beasts.

There is a fountain in the central plaza. It has basins, and tiers, and a dry spout shaped like a coiled serpent. It has never held liquid, and was never meant to. It is simply what this place understands a center to require. Nearby, an arcade of shops fronts the square, each niche complete with a counter ledge and a recessed back wall where merchandise would stand, but the counters are fused to the floor and the openings are sealed by nothing, leading nowhere. The place insists upon the grammar of civilization—the street, the square, the fountain, the watchtower—but it possesses no vocabulary of use. Here, the world has learned to build without ever learning why.

To stay here is to understand a rule fundamental to this landscape: that all the arrangements of intelligent life may be a chemical eventuality, no more intentional than the growth of crystals in a supersaturated solution. The city has no inhabitants because it does not need them. It hums with activity—accretion, respiration, the slow capillary creep of mineral through its upper stories, the grazing of the pale custodians—and yet it is a vacancy dressed in the ornate costume of society. It is young. It is sharp. It is cool and dim and catastrophically, radiantly silent. And it grows, building more rooms for no one, more stairways that lead to blank walls, more balconies from which nothing watches the dust-choked twilight, composing its endless, empty, architectural song.
