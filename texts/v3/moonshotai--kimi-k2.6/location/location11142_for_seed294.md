The silence here has weight. It does not arrive from absence but from saturation: the cold water is so dense with dissolved minerals, so viscous with nearness to freezing, that sound seems to curdle in it rather than travel. You are suspended above a submerged plateau that rises two kilometers from the abyssal plain, a drowned highland where the pressure is immense and the temperature hovers at the threshold where water forgets its willingness to flow. Yet flow it does. The plateau sits at the collision of ancient thermohaline rivers, and the currents that sweep its ridgelines are invisible, catastrophic, tireless—internal waves a thousand meters tall that shear past one another in opposing layers, carrying black snows of silt and pulverized shell. The turbulence is total, a three-dimensional storm that never breaks because there is no surface to break against. Only your own hollow bones and the thud of your heartbeat tell you that the world is not standing still.

Everything here is constructed. There is no bare rock, no outcropping of natal geology. Even the seafloor beneath the plateau is paved with forty meters of compacted detritus: the ossified architecture of the *Fabricators*, a kingdom of fauna that solved the problem of shelter not with tools—that concept never took root in this cold crucible—but with secretion, accretion, and geometric instinct. Over four hundred million years, they converted the seamount into a necropolis of calcium phosphate and chitin, a regional-scale city grown rather than built. You drift between colossal buttresses that look too regular for limestone, too alive for masonry. They are neither. They are the empty shells of continent-sized colonial organisms, their growth rings forming archways, their respiratory pores aligned into galleries and clerestories, their abandoned digestive chambers yawning as amphitheaters that could swallow cathedrals.

The erosion is ancient, settled into final form. The sharpest edges have been nursed smooth by the perpetual benthic storms until the surfaces resemble soapstone handled for centuries. The place has achieved its last shape; it will not change again except to slowly, molecularly, dissolve back into the hungry water over epochs you cannot compass. Yet the dynamic never rests. A horizontal cyclone of supercooled brine may sweep through the plaza before you, tugging at your body with the polite but irresistible insistence of a rising tide, and then a counter-current above it will snatch the sediment away in the opposite direction, leaving the stone briefly clean as polished ivory. The turbulence is chaotic, but it has chaoticked for so long that the architecture has learned its habits, wearing grooves in the floor where the water prefers to run.

There is no green. No plant life, no frond, no diatom bloom to stain the slopes ochre. The palette is mineral and funeral: greys irradiated with a faint nacreous sheen, the mauve of old bone, the black of cold anoxic silt. In this pre-photosynthetic economy, energy seeps upward from chemolithic pores in the deepest pavement, but it is captured not by leaves but by mats of slow-living bacteria and the fauna that graze upon them. That fauna is abundant, though the sparse density of the landscape keeps them scattered across the vastness like votive offerings left in a ruin. Ghostly tunicates hang in the vaulted ceilings by the thousands, pulsing in arrhythmic unison. Armored polychaetes, pale as temple candles, inch along the grooves cut by currents. Bioluminescence is the only light, and it is dim: cold blue sparks that gutter behind translucent inches of calcite walling, or the gentle headlamps of solitary leviathans moving between spires, their bodies too soft for the mechanized world but perfectly suited to the hollows left by their predecessors.

The weirdness is subtle enough that you might almost miss it. It is in the staircases. There are stairs everywhere, ascending the exterior of chimney-towers and corkscrewing down into wells that terminate against solid stone. They were never used; the creatures who made them had no legs, no need for elevation within a fluid medium. The steps are vestigial ornament, an architectural gene expressing itself without purpose, like the dream of walking in a species that has never known land. Balconies open onto sheer walls with no adjoining bridges. Arches frame other arches framing nothing. It is a total environment, fully constructed, and yet there is not a single right angle drawn by intention or tool. The geometry is biological: fractal, over-detailed, slightly nauseating in its repetition of structures that resemble ribs, ear-canals, and teeth.

It is mostly quiet. The water swallows the groans of the moving world. Occasionally a structure shifts—some vast buried hinge settling another micrometer into its final closure—and the sound is a sub-bass note felt in the viscera rather than heard. The fauna do not call to one another in frequencies that carry; they thrum against the bone-stone, sending messages through the substrate itself. You are left with the intimate acoustics of your own body while, all around you, a silent hurricane reshuffles the silt between the abandoned palaces of a civilization that was never civilized, only grown.

To move through this place is to understand that construction does not require technology, only time—unimaginable, insulting quantities of time—and that a landscape can be at once violently unstable and finished forever. The storm rages on, invisible and mute, wearing a city that was already dead before the first seed of a plant ever contemplated the light.
