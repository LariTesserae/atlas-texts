The sound is the first thing. Not the sight—though the light here is viciously bright, a white, depthless glare that scours every surface—but the *pressure* of the noise, a subsonic weight against the sternum that arrives before any particular object resolves out of the haze. It is the accumulated roar of a continent in perpetual motion: the bass thrum of tons of lignin shifting under torsion, the shriek of crystalline pollen shearing against itself in atmospheric rivers, the pneumatic *chuff* of ten thousand woody pistons compressing air in trunks wide as city blocks. It is deafening not because it is close, but because it is everywhere, transmitted through the compacted substrate of the plateau itself, a vast, high-altitude tableland where the air is too thin to warm under all that brilliance, and the cold seeps in dry as ash.

There is no water. Not as river, not as cloud, not as the binding medium of life. The chemistry here has routed around it entirely, evolving instead around silicate slurries, oxidized metallic aerosols, and a sap that runs thick and copper-green, heavy as mercury, freezing slowly in the permanent cool of the elevation. What looks from orbit like a verdant world is, on the surface, something else entirely: a total vegetative empire that has renounced green for the colors of industry—bone white, rust orange, galvanic blue, the baked-grey of compressed cellulose armored with silica nodes. The living surface is not lush. It is *machined*.

They stand kilometers apart, these growths, sparse as watchtowers on a sea of compacted fallout. To walk from one to the next takes days across the interstitial wastes, yet they dominate the horizon utterly, each one a vertical refinery evolved over epochs into weathered, settled form. Their trunks are ribbed like cooling towers, skinned in plates of petrified bark polished to a dull gleam by centuries of their own abrasion. They are old. The erosion parameter here is not of water wearing stone but of motion wearing motion—ball-joints of compressed heartwood grinding in their sockets, polishing themselves smoother, finding their eternal posture in constant flux. They are established, balanced, ancient despite their violence.

And they are violent. The plateau is a turbulent thing, hyperactive, storm-bright with internal weather. Each autoclave-tree operates on a metabolic calendar of seconds and minutes, not seasons. You can watch them grow. The cambium layers crack like gunshots as they expand. Branches articulate on hydraulic woody muscles, tracking the hard white sun with reflector-leaf arrays that whir and ticking like escapements. Deep in their cores, anoxic furnaces smelt the ores their taproots—drill-shafts sinking miles through bedrock—have drawn up. They forge their seeds in crucibles of compressed air and chemical fire, then launch them with cataracts of vented gas that leave spiraling contrails in the thin atmosphere. The ground trembles. The air shudders. Between the trees, the emptiness is not stillness; it is the space where competing shockwaves overlap, where dry thunderstorms breed in the thermal chaos of their crowns, and where dust-devils of metallic spore-fall turn day into a strobing, roaring twilight.

There is nothing built. No wall, no road, no artifact of intention. What might look like pipeline is root; what might sound like a forge is stomach; what might resemble a smokestack is merely exhalation. The technology of the place is entirely organic, an industrial ecology that arrived at combustion, compression, and electrification without ever passing through a hand or an eye. The trees do not think. They *produce*.

And they host. The fauna here is abundant, riotous, evolved to the niche of a living factory floor. Hordes of enamel-shelled beetles the size of fists clamber through the ribbed fluting of the trunks, cleaning vents of slag, drinking the excess heat in their blood. Tube-worms of articulated chitin nest in exhaust orifices, feeding on the precipitated minerals. Between the great trees, migratory herds of ash-grey ungulates move like shifting weather systems, grazing on the shed bark-plates and compressed nodules that litter the ground, their multiple stomachs fermenting cellulose into volatile compounds they ignite in flash-pods along their spines to ward off the pack-hunters that ride the thermal columns. It is a bazaar of appetite, loud with clicks and venting and the percussive mating calls of creatures whose vocal cords have evolved into industrial whistles.

The scale warps perception. A storm here does not pass; it *transits*, a continental front generated by the aggregate exhalations of the forest-floor, carrying lightning that arcs from crown to crown in dendritic webs visible from orbit. The plateau is vast enough to have its own gravity of phenomena, its own physics of noise and motion. You are not standing on a landscape. You are standing inside a single, distributed organism the size of a subcontinent, cool and bright and mercilessly dry, listening to the unending grind of its digestion.

After enough time, the deafening becomes a kind of silence, a tinnitus of the world itself. You realize the erosion, the balance, is not in spite of the turbulence but because of it. This is a place that has settled into chaos as another world might settle into sleep. The grinding never stops. The trees never rest. The ash never ceases to fall, layering, compressing, becoming the mineral floor upon which the next generation of refinery-roots will drill. There is no water to soften the angle of things. Only the hard, bright cold, the roar, and the patient, thrashing industry of biology that learned to forge before it learned to flower.
