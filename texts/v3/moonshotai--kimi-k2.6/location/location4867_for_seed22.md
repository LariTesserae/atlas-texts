You emerge onto a shelf of basalt so fresh it still smells of breached magma, though the flow cooled decades ago at most. The valley below is not old enough to have earned a river. It is a raw declivity, low-slung and broad, perhaps sixty kilometers from rim to rim, and the stone has not yet been taught patience. Every edge stands at its original angle; fracture-planes gleam like broken crockery. No gravel has accumulated in the joints. The scree is still sharp enough to draw blood with a glance. This is terrain in its infancy, a geological nursery, but the midwife was not erosion—it was intention.

Across this openness, the density of matter is scandalously low. You can stare for minutes and see nothing but air, distance, and the bruised pewter glow of a sky that never quite brightens. Yet what does exist is urban. Not dense—never dense—but organized at a scale that implies decisiveness. Eleven primary structures rise from the valley floor. Call them towers, though they refuse the logic of buildings. Each one is a spindle of hypermaterial, somewhere between ceramic and coherent light, rising two or three kilometers before branching into cantilevers that do not so much support as *suggest* architecture. They are spaced far apart, too far for any conventional city, connected only by cables no thicker than your wrist that sag across distances of ten or fifteen kilometers. The volume between them is overwhelmingly empty. A town’s worth of planning stretched across a province of void.

The air is warm, nearly blood temperature, and moist in the manner of exhalation rather than rain. Water does not fall here so much as precipitate in place. The warm atmosphere meets the cooler surfaces of the spires and condenses in a perpetual, fine sweat. Every vertical face weeps. Droplets gather, fatten, and run in intermittent rivulets down the unweathered flanks, forming no streams, only momentary gleams that evaporate before they reach the ground. It is a dampness of surfaces, not depths. Puddles are brief affairs; moisture is inhaled almost as fast as it bleeds.

Because of this, the vegetation is lush without being grounded. There is almost no soil on the young stone—nowhere for roots to earn a living in the traditional manner—so the plants have gone aerial. They cluster along the cables and the lower arms of the spires in massive, tumorous mats of deep violet and wet black. Their leaves are not green; chlorophyll would starve in this dimness. Instead they drink the warm infrared radiating from the structures themselves, or perhaps they metabolize the condensation, or feed on the sound. They are certainly thick enough to be called jungle, but it is a jungle suspended in midair, a riot of biomass clinging to the thinnest pretext of support. Great curtains of moss and vine drape from cable to cable, forming ephemeral bridges that sag and tear and regrow within seasons. Orchids with blossoms the size of skulls open their damp mouths to the murk. Fungi erupt in shelving colonies along the condensation lines, glowing faintly with a light that does not illuminate so much as testify to chemistry.

And the fauna. God, the fauna. They are abundant in the way that stars are abundant—not necessarily clustered, but numbering in the uncountable. Mostly they are airborne. Membrane-winged creatures, neither bat nor insect but something that whirs on four asynchronous wings, fill the gulfs between the spires. They move in loose flocks millions strong, filtering the air for spores and condensed organic mist. Their bodies are translucent, the color of amber held to a candle, and their wings beat at frequencies that blur the eye. When a flock passes between you and the dim sky, it eclipses what little light there is, a living eclipse, a river of glass and chitin. Others cling to the vegetation: trilobite-shaped things with too many legs, crab-like symbiots that prune the fungal shelves, worm-bodied climbers that leave phosphorescent slime-trails across the hypermaterial. Life here has not colonized the land; it has colonized the interval.

The sound is the first thing that would kill a visitor, if the fall did not. It is deafening in a way that compresses the chest and blurs the vision. The spires are singing. That is the only word for it, though the mechanism is technological—post-modern in a direction physics has not publicly discovered. The cables vibrate with a fundamental tone somewhere between a pipe organ’s lowest register and the subsonic rumble of tectonic plates shifting. Each spire emits a different note, and the interference patterns between them create standing waves of pressure that make the warm air shiver against your skin. It is infrastructure-as-instrument, a power grid that transmits energy through resonant compression rather than wire. Or perhaps the sound is structural, a necessity to keep the hypermaterial coherent. The megafauna add their frequencies: the whir of billions of wings, the mating drones of the canopy climbers, the percussive clicking of the filter-feeders as they strain the damp air. Together it is not a cacophony but a single, titanic chord that never resolves. You feel it in your teeth, your sternum, the fluid of your inner ear. Conversation is impossible. Thought becomes slippery.

Yet for all this biological and sonic abundance, the place remains hollow. The weirdness lives in that paradox. You can stand on a cable-mat thick with fern and jittering with life, look out across fifteen kilometers of open air to the next structure, and see nothing but a slender needle weeping into the dusk. The valley functions as a single machine, but most of it is absent. The city is a diagram of potential space. The rules here seem to be that existence prefers the interstitial: life boils in the gaps, moisture clings to thresholds, sound occupies the emptiness, and the architecture provides only the faintest scaffolding for a world that would rather be vapor, vibration, and vine.

It is active, always. The temperature differential between the warm basin air and the cooler upper atmosphere drives a slow, constant circulation, a breath that never stops. The condensation patterns shift with the hour, sending water down different spire-faces, which causes the vegetation to lean, to send root-tendrils questing after new moisture, which stirs the fauna to migration, which alters the flock-densities and therefore the acoustic dampening, which changes the resonant frequencies of the cables, which slightly alters the condensation points. It is an ecosystem locked in feedback with its own infrastructure, a city learning to be a forest, a forest learning to be an instrument, and the valley, young as it is, already thrumming toward some future equilibrium that none of its absent builders will return to witness.
