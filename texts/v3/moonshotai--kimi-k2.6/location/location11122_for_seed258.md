The first thing is the drone. It does not rise and fall; it does not approach or fade. It is simply *there*, a low, tectonic exhalation that fills the warm air like water fills a jar, pressing against the eardrums with the constancy of gravity itself. It is the sound of sap moving through trunks the width of cathedral naves, of millions of stoma breathing in unison, of condensation sliding down cellulose channels and falling in unvarying cataracts into resonance chambers far below. It is loud—not the urgent loudness of a storm, but the architectural loudness of a structure so vast that its mere operation becomes a roar.

You are high up. The atmosphere should be thin here, sharp in the throat, but it is not. The air is warm, thick, and wet, pressing against the skin like a heated compress. Mist does not drift; it hangs in fixed sheets, as if the very idea of weather has been paused. Droplets bead on every surface—bark, petiole, the broad concave leaves that form the walls—and they weep, slowly, always, into catchments that never overflow. The water table sits just beneath the world’s skin. Lift any panel of root-mat flooring and you would find it spongy, saturated, a dark mirror trembling against the underside of the plant-made world.

And it is all made. There is no raw meadow, no naked scree, no stone left to the mercy of wind. Everything has been constructed. Not built—there are no tools here, no ax-marks, no fired brick, no alloy—but *grown*. The technology score is zero. This is pure, pre-tool biology executing the ambitions of architecture. Load-bearing arches of lignin and heartwood span distances that would demand steel elsewhere. Vaulted ceilings of woven lamina filter the light. Staircases spiral up trunks in geometries too precise for chance, leading to platforms and galleries and hollows that resemble rooms. Bridges of braided vine, already hard as oak, cross the wide spaces between. It is a city, a infrastructure, a total environment, and every inch of it is alive. Or rather, *living*.

Yet the landscape is sparse. The constructions are enormous—individual structures the size of hills, the size of towns—but they stand separated by vast, open plazas of woven root-pavement and juvenile soil. You can see for miles across the plateau, the scale extending regionally, valley-sized, town-sized, a mid-range openness that lets the eye travel from one vegetative monument to the next across expanses of emptiness. The density is low. There is so much space between presences. The plants have filled the *concept* of the world without crowding its geography. They are the substrate, the architecture, the floor and wall and roof, but they have left the distances between themselves hollow, balanced perfectly with silence and absence.

Beneath the bio-constructed floor, the geology is newborn. This is the part that feels almost obscene to discover. The rock is embryonic, still sweating the heat of its recent making. It has not been eroded because time has not yet worked on it; there are no rounded river stones, no polished cliffs, no valleys carved by persistence. The stone is raw, angular, and warm to the touch, still settling into its own mass. The plants have colonized it so totally, so immediately, that they seem to have grown *over* the world’s infancy, encasing the pre-formation in halls of green and umber. The land is under construction, and the plants have finished their part before the stone has even cooled.

And nothing changes. The dynamic is frozen in a perfect equilibrium that predates the idea of history. A leaf that yesterday hung at a certain angle hangs there now, and will hang there forever, its cells metabolizing, its water cycling, but its position eternal. The warm mist does not thin or thicken. The drone does not modulate. The light—normal, clear, rendering colors with honest accuracy—does not shift in quality. There is no dusk, no dawn. The sun, or whatever source illuminates this place, seems fixed in a perpetual afternoon, casting shadows that do not lengthen. Seeds hang in the air, caught in the same eddies, never settling, never drifting away. A pool of water at the base of a trunk remains exactly the same volume; it evaporates and is replaced at identical rates. The world is a living photograph, a biological steady-state.

The strangeness seeps in slowly. The architecture is not quite right for human use. Doorways open into solid wood; stairs ascend to nowhere and then stop, mid-air, as if the blueprint had been abandoned, yet the growth continues the pattern faithfully. Arches support no load but themselves. Some trunks grow in perfect squares. Others are hollow, resonant, and from their openings the loud drone takes on chorded, almost choral qualities—notes that do not harmonize in any musical tradition, yet hold steady, eternal. Water, in places, does not fall downward but crawls in sheets along vertical bark, held there by capillary forces that never break, never win, never lose. The leaves are broad and thick, but some are translucent, and looking through them the light splits into faint, regular prisms that cast multiple soft shadows from a single source.

Life, non-vegetative life, is almost absent. Fauna is rare to the point of myth. Perhaps a beetle, its carapace the color of polished amber, sits motionless in a groove of bark, frozen in the equilibrium as surely as the water. Perhaps a moth hangs from the underside of a leaf, wings spread, not dead but suspended, its metabolism barely a whisper. They are not inhabitants; they are punctuation marks in a text written entirely in chlorophyll. There is nothing to hunt, nothing to scavenge, no cycle of predator and prey. The ecosystem is total, closed, and purely botanical.

You walk for hours, and the scene does not alter. Another root-plaza, vast and empty. Another megastructure, its galleries open to the warm wet air. Another catchment of weeping water, falling into itself forever. The ground beneath your feet is a living floor; the walls are living walls; the sky is a living ceiling of transpiration and filtered light. It is a paradise, perhaps. Or a nursery. Or a machine that has mistaken itself for a forest, maintaining a loud, warm, unchanging vigil over a geology that has not yet learned how to be old.
