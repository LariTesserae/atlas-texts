You do not climb to this place so much as survive the mountain’s sudden conviction that it should exist. The peak thrust itself up through the cloud sea only decades ago—perhaps less; the stone still remembers the violence of its own birth. There has been no time for weathering. No patient river has stroked these flanks into softness. Every ledge is a knife-edge. Every grain of scoria retains the furious texture of the magma’s last exhale. You find the entrance not by light but by the change in the wind’s voice: a subterranean hush behind the roar of the high atmosphere, a place where the gale drops to a murmur and then to something deeper.

The chamber is small. A cathedral nave might fit within it, but only just, and only if it agreed to be irregular, broken, humiliated by the organic press of what lives inside. It is a vesicle in the summit’s throat, a bubble of gas trapped during the mountain’s adolescence and now exposed by a fresh shear in the rock. The air here is cool—always cool, the high-altitude cold that does not bother with seasons—and damp, not with rain but with the mountain’s own respiration. Clouds forced up from the lower world collide with this new obscenity of altitude and surrender their moisture directly onto the stone. The walls weep. The ceiling sweats a constant, patient precipitation. What water does not evaporate again collects in the shallow ferrous depressions of the floor, pools no larger than palms, filled with a syrup of condensed mist and dissolved minerals.

There is almost no light. The aperture, a jagged wound in the peak, faces away from the sun’s path and is further choked by the perpetual storm wrack that garlands the summit. What illumination exists arrives by accident: rare bolts of lightning outside, staining the chamber for microseconds in eldritch violet; or the bioluminescent smear of certain bacteria colonizing the joints of the dead. Otherwise, darkness holds the space utterly. You must imagine the place through your skin, through your ears, through the soles of your boots against the floor.

The floor is not merely stone. It is a fresh scree of volcanic glass and vitreous andesite, so recently cooled that thermal contraction has knapped it with preternatural precision. Nature has fabricated a lithic toolkit here—laurel-leaf blades, discoidal scrapers, burins, choppers—better than any hand of the Lower Paleolithic ever shaped. No one made these. The mountain, shivering as it set, chipped them out of itself. They lie scattered among the bodies, unhandled by intelligence, yet they cut all the same.

Because the chamber is filled with bodies.

The fauna is overwhelming, a density that beggars taxonomy. They are called nothing; no human tongue has named them. Each is about the size of a human heart, segmented, pallid, soft-shelled, blind. They hang from the ceiling in stalactitic curtains. They mantle the walls in living whitish mats ten layers deep, each individual adhered to the stone and to its neighbors by a shared slime that has long since become structural. The floor is a tide of them, shoulder to carapace, a carpet that rises and falls with the slow peristalsis of millions. In a space barely large enough to echo, there may be millions. There is no room to step without pressing into one, without feeling the cool, damp resilience of a shell that is less like armor and more like damp parchment stretched over bone.

They move, but the dynamic of the place is glacial, viscous, a world of honey. A limb unfolds over the course of an hour. A mouth opens across the span of a morning. The entire colony shifts like a single dreaming organ, advancing toward the weeping walls, retreating from some minute temperature drop, but so slowly that an observer might think them dead—if not for the sound.

The sound is loud.

Not sharp, not sudden, but a sustained, tectonic roar. Because they are packed so densely, every infinitesimal movement scrapes against another body. A thousand micro-frictions per second aggregate into a sub-bass drone that resonates in your sternum. The obsidian flakes on the floor, disturbed by this endless slow tide, chime and grind against one another and against the rock. The creatures themselves possess chitinous ridges that click, not in rhythm but in stochastic profusion, a hail of infinitesimal castanets. Condensation drips onto the mass from above, each drop striking a shell with a note like a fingernail tapped on porcelain, and in the chamber’s acoustic architecture these notes do not fade—they layer. The result is a soundscape like standing inside a glacier while it calves, like being held in the lung of a god with a fever. It is never silent here. Even the rock, still cooling and flexing, adds low-frequency booms as the mountain settles around its own heart.

Vegetation is almost absent. Only a few species of grey, iron-loving lichen have found purchase, growing in the creases where water runs thickest, feeding on sulfur whispers and the nitrogen of the high air. They are sparse, starveling things, grey as scabs, and they grow chiefly upon the ones who have died—or rather, upon those who have ceased all motion and are in the process of becoming something else.

This is the heart of the strangeness.

The creatures do not die in the manner of ordinary fauna. Their borders are porous. When two press together long enough—and here, pressed together is the only condition—their tissues begin to merge. Nerves entwine. Shells grow into one another. What was a colony becomes a lattice, a single distributed organism dreaming in slow pulses. The boundaries between individual and collective have eroded until neither term applies cleanly. They share nutrients through the communal slime. They react to threats as a skin reacts, not as a herd scatters. And the mountain participates in this biology. Because the stone is young and sharp, the flakes do not merely wound them; the flakes are incorporated. A creature born in the deepest press may spend its first decade with a spine of raw obsidian slowly growing into its cuticle, until shell and volcanic glass become indistinguishable. Some of the eldest, centuries old by any measure of metabolism, have calcified and mineralized entirely, becoming statues of flesh turned to scoria, still anchored in the living mat, still exchanging chemical whispers through porous stone. The rules here do not privilege the organic over the geological. Here, rock is a phase of life, and life is a stage of erosion.

There are no built things. No walls but the mountain’s own. No tools but the flakes the mountain happens to have dropped. No artifacts. Yet the chamber hums with a primitive technological aura, as if the entire ecology were an unconscious atelier. The colony’s slow dragging of the natural blades scores the floor, deepens the catchment pools, alters the flow of condensation in ways that benefit the lichens, which in turn fix the nitrogen that drifts down in the mist, which feeds the young. It is tool-use without a tool-user, a slow accident of physics and biology converging on a strategy.

Outside, the world is high and wild and new. Inside, the darkness is absolute, warm with the friction of a million cool bodies, loud as a waterfall, and patient beyond reckoning. If you stayed—if you stood motionless in the gallery of the vesicle, letting the damp coat your skin—you might eventually feel the tide touch your boot. It would not recoil. It would begin, over the course of hours, to climb. Not as an attack. Simply as a principle: in this place, density is a law. Empty space is an insult to the economics of altitude and cold. Everything that enters becomes, by degrees, part of the Accumulation. Your hair would grow lichen. Your bones would learn the local time, the geology of slowness. Your heartbeat would join the drone, becoming one more frequency in the roar, one more presence in the crowded dark.

The mountain is young. It has not learned to be empty. And so, at the roof of the world, it grows this one crowded, sweating, thunderous room where the only rule is nearness, and the only boundary is the slow, bleeding edge between the living and the stone.
