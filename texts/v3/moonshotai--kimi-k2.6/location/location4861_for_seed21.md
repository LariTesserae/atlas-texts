It is a single bowl, no larger than a cathedral close, and the rim is sharp enough that you feel you have stepped inside something that has only recently finished being made. The rock is new. Not ancient basalt worn satin-smooth by millennia, but obsidian and viridian glass still carrying the stress-fractures of its own abrupt cooling, cleavage planes that catch the light like broken mirrors. You could walk from one wall to the other in perhaps two hundred steps, and yet the elevation is such that clouds move below the jagged lip, slow herds of gray and silver passing at a distance that makes the basin feel like a room hung in the upper air.

It is very hot. The heat does not come from the sun, which is merely ordinary, but from beneath your feet. The floor is warm, in places too hot to touch, and the air is a thick, breathable soup that wraps the lungs and immediately sheens the skin with sweat. Humidity is absolute; water is not so much present as it is unavoidable. There are no streams, no pools, but the walls weep continuously. Moisture percolates through the porous glass, emerging in beads that fatten and run in slow, erratic paths. Condensation collects on the underside of the rim and drips back down in a soft, patient precipitation, so that the place exists in a state of perpetual post-rain, everything gleaming, everything darkened to its deepest color.

Vegetation is scattered but, where it takes hold, almost indecently lush. Great clots of moss and fern occupy the damp shadows, their fronds broad and dripping, a saturated emerald so intense it seems to hum. There are perhaps a dozen such islands in the bowl, separated by yards of bare, wet stone. Between them: emptiness. The emptiness is deliberate, balanced, as though the green has agreed to let the rock have its say. Nothing carpets the ground. Each plant community stands alone, a self-contained shrine to chlorophyll, and the silence between them is architectural.

Sound here is swallowed. The high, thin air should carry every drop of water, every shifting frond, but instead there is a muffled, submarine quality to the quiet. The steam that rises from thermal vents makes no hiss. The drips fall without echo. You hear your own pulse, the creak of your joints, and beyond that, a hush so complete it seems to press inward on the eardrums. There is almost nothing with eyes. If insects crawl, they make no buzz. If birds pass overhead, they do not enter. The ecology is botanical and mineral, a garden without its animals, which makes the lushness feel slightly theoretical, like a model of a world rather than the world itself.

And yet it is active. The dynamics are slow but relentless. Every eleven minutes, the southeastern vent exhales a fresh coil of vapor that does not rise so much as spiral, twisting in a helix that holds its shape for ten, fifteen seconds before finally fraying. Certain sheets of water on the northern wall do not run downward; they creep upward in defiance of expectation, drawn by some capillary action amplified beyond physics, leaving trails of mineral salts that accrete visibly over the course of a day. A fern near the center, broad as an altar cloth, tracks the sun not by the hour but by the minute, its fronds adjusting with a vegetative restlessness. Mineral deposits build collars around the vents like the growth rings of a stone tree. The place is young, unfinished, and still arguing with itself about what shape it will take.

The built things are few. You might find three of them, or four, depending on the light. One is half-melted into the glassy floor, a form like folded origami in a material that resembles titanium laminated with mother-of-pearl, warm to the touch, humming faintly at a frequency felt in the teeth rather than heard. Another hovers, perfectly silent, three handbreadths above a thermal crack, casting a shadow that falls at a slightly wrong angle, as if the light that produced it came from a sun not quite coaxial with your own. They are not ruins. They are artifacts of a technology that does not announce itself with gears or screens but with gentle violations of the expected. They are scattered, small, easy to miss if you are looking for cities or monuments. They are residue. Someone, or something, engineered this pocket—induced the earth to birth exactly this crater, exactly this size, and then stopped, leaving the basin to incubate.

The light is normal. That is the strangest part. Under a gold-white sky, in air thick as a greenhouse, the illumination is perfectly ordinary. It enters through the steam and breaks into visible shafts, turning the water droplets into drifting coins of brightness. But near the hovering structure, photons seem to negotiate an extra dimension; colors desaturate slightly, edges blur, and your own reflection in the wet stone arrives a half-second late. The weirdness is not loud. It is a place where gravity is locally gentle, where water has forgotten which way is down, where the rock is still learning how to be rock, and all of it happens in plain, unremarkable daylight.

The rules of the world are simple: nothing here is final. The basin is a held breath. The heat will not let it cool, the tech will not let it collapse into ordinary geology, and the sparse, intense life will not yet colonize the bare stone between its islands. It is a room-scale experiment, a single hot, wet, gleaming pocket suspended above the weather, quietly steaming, quietly growing, quietly wrong. If you sit on the warm floor and wait, you can feel the slow pulse of it—the vent’s eleven-minute sigh, the upward creep of water, the faint turn of the floating thing as it orients to a pole that does not exist on any compass. It is a place that has not decided whether it is a machine or a garden. Perhaps it is neither. Perhaps it is simply waiting for the next instruction, weeping water into the silence, growing sharper and greener and stranger with each passing hour.
