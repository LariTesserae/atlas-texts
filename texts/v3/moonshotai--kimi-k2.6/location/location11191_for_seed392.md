The heat arrives first, before the place resolves into detail. It is a hotness that seems to have been waiting, patient and propped like a tent, in the interval between one step and the next. You have entered a basin. That much is clear by the way the horizons hold you in a shallow palm. The elevation is low—not below water, but below the ordinary world, sunken in the manner of a vast dish pressed into a table. Yet there is no table. There is no earth. There is only what has been made.

You are standing on a substrate of hand-beaten clay. It extends in every direction, pocked with the regular dimples of wooden paddles, striated where stone scrapers dragged across stubborn lumps. This is not soil. It is material that was carried here, kneaded, wetted, and laid down by hands using tools of bone, flint, and knotted cord. The technology is primitive and honest; every square foot confesses its method. You can see the spiral marks of coiling in the low ridges, the parallel scars of adzes in the harder banks. Nothing has been machined. Everything has been worked. Because the construction is young—so young it has not yet begun to forget its making—the edges are vicious. A cut channel has lips like broken pottery. A raised bank is sharp enough to slice a shadow in half.

And there are shadows. They lie everywhere, black as ink, cast by a light that is total, absolute, and motionless. The sun, or whatever serves as sun, is a white hole burned into the apex of the sky. It does not drift. It glares. The brightness is such that color seems to bleed upward toward white; the clay underfoot reflects the glare like a dull mirror, and the wetness turns it into scattered coins of light.

The wetness. The basin is wet. Not with running rivers—that would require change, gradient, the dynamic passage of water—but with a saturated, stewing humidity that has nowhere to go. Water lives just below the clay skin. Step heavily, and a clear bead surfaces through a paddle-print, as if the ground bruises liquid. The air itself condenses against any surface, though nothing is truly cool here. Droplets hang on the underside of leaves, on the rim of a stone basin, on the whiskers of beasts. They are swollen, pregnant with gravity, but they never drop. They have been hanging for as long as the light has held its position. The water table is a membrane; dig a hand’s breadth down and the clay bleeds into slip, thick and cool, but it does not flow. It waits.

Between the broad, empty stretches of beaten clay, the vegetation erupts in lush, impossible profusion. But these are not growing things. They are built things. A grove stands a quarter-mile off: hundreds of trunks woven from plaited reed and stiffened with daub, each trunk splitting into branches that end in thousands of leaves. Every leaf is a separate artifact. You can see where fingers pinched the greenware into a central vein, where a thumbnail scraped serration into the margin. Some fronds are fired to a terracotta brittleness; others remain soft, unfired, lush in their damp collapse, but they do not collapse. They are caught in their moment of greatest fullness, held there by the same stasis that holds the light. Lushness here is a measure of density and detail, not of life-process. The greenery is abundant, overwhelming, verdant in a way that hurts the eyes after the blank clay flats. It comes in copses and thickets, separated by long reaches of nothing, so the density of the whole remains sparse—scattered presences across an open, regional scale. You can walk for an hour through silence and baking heat and see nothing but the ground’s tool marks, then come suddenly upon a jungle of woven and molded matter so dense it seems to hum with a green, silent frequency.

And the fauna. They are everywhere, if you look. Abundant. A herd of low, heavy creatures—something between tapir and clay—stands frozen mid-stride along a clay ravine. Their bodies are molded over armatures of willow and vine; their skin is loam mixed with something like dung and hair, worked until it has the texture of hide, but you can still find the flake-scars of the chisels that shaped their hipbones. Their eyes are wet stones, agate or obsidian, and they reflect the white sun with a flat, sentient stare. They do not breathe. Their flanks do not rise. Yet they are not statues in the dead sense; they are too precise, too biological. Blood, dark and viscous, sits visible in the veins of their ears, never moving. They are fauna because they have hearts, lungs, stomachs, but time has been canceled around them. They exist in the broken truth of this place: they are alive in every particular except the temporal one.

Further on, a flock of birds—herons with legs too long, with wings of ceramic tile and real feather—hang above a marsh that isn’t a marsh. The water there is a sheet, an inch deep over more clay, and the birds are fixed in attitudes of landing, their shadows frozen beneath them like black rugs. None call. None stir. The silence is so complete it produces a pressure in the inner ear. It is the quiet of a workshop after the workers have vanished. The quiet of a held breath. The quiet of a world that has not yet learned to make sound because it has not yet been allowed its first second.

This is the core rule: nothing changes. The basin is a snapshot, a diorama of enormous, handmade realism, but it is not a model. It is full-scale, regional, valley-sized. You could live in it, if living did not require tomorrow. The temperature stays locked at a sweltering, body-numbing heat. The humidity stays locked at the edge of drowning. The lush vegetation stays in its summer. The fauna stay abundant and still. The clay stays young, unweathered, its ridges sharp enough to cut.

But reality here is badly broken. You notice it first in the shadows. Your own shadow stretches behind you, long and pointed west, while the shadow of a reed-tree five paces to your left falls due north. Both are equally sharp, equally black, equally motionless under the unmoving sun. You walk toward a copse of vegetation on the horizon, and after an hour it is no closer, though your legs ache and the clay rises and falls beneath your feet in the proper way. Later, you look back and see that copse directly behind you, though you never turned. The basin is open, yes—you can see for miles—but the miles do not map correctly.
