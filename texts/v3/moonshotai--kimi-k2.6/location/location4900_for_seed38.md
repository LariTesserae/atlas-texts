You do not climb to the Thrum. You arrive at its edge, and the edge is everywhere.

It is a tableland the size of a minor moon, raised so long ago that the concept of "mountain" has been forgotten by the stone itself. Erosion here did not carve grandeur; it performed ablution. Over epochs measured in the lifespan of stars, wind and water and the patient gnawing of root-fibers wore every precipice into a sigh. What remains is not a landscape but a continuous body: a warm, breathing mat of organic matter hundreds of meters deep, compressed by its own ancient weight into substances that resemble peat, then cork, then something almost like bone. The mineral world is still down there somewhere, buried beneath the sleep of vegetation, but on the Thrum, geology has been digested. The ground is a plant. The hills are plants. The architecture is plant. You walk upon a single, dreaming organism that has forgotten it was ever many.

The light is dim, not because the sun is absent, but because the air is too full. Spores, pollen, and vapor hang in a warm, perpetual suspension, a golden-green twilight that never quite becomes day. What illumination filters through does so as through stained glass made of chlorophyll and condensation. It is always the hour before dusk, warm as a held breath, and the humidity beads on every surface until the world appears to weep. Water does not fall here so much as exude. There are no rivers in the old sense; instead, the plateau perspires. Capillary action draws moisture from reservoirs deep in the compressed corpus, and it bleeds from pores in the lignin-stone, weeping from the cheeks of eroded buttresses, collecting in cupped leaves the size of lakes. The water is warm, slightly sweet, alive with microscopic grazers. It never floods. It only condenses, drips, and is drunk again.

Vegetation is not a feature of the Thrum. It is the medium. The density is such that space itself feels crowded. Above, a canopy of interlocking crowns stretches to every horizon, not a forest but a ceiling, leaves layered upon leaves in translucent strata. Below that, the understory is a vertical maze of aerial roots, hanging gardens, and epiphytes that have never touched the true ground because the true ground is fifty meters below them and is also root. Every cubic meter is tenanted. Orchids the color of bruises nest in the armpits of branches. Fungi like melted candles drip from joints in the wood. Vines as thick as thighs braid themselves into load-bearing columns, creating natural vaults and galleries that resemble the naves of cathedrals—though no hand ever shaped them, and no god was ever worshipped here. The plant fills the space so completely that the air itself feels chewy, textured, as if you could bite it and taste green.

And yet, for all this life, the Thrum is mostly quiet.

Sound is devoured by the porous substrate. The ancient, eroded flesh of the plateau is too soft to ring, too saturated to crack. There is no wind-song; the leaves are thick and waxy, designed to shed water rather than tremble, and the warm air moves in slow, viscous exhalations rather than gusts. What fauna exist—and they are abundant, teeming in the layered volumes—have learned the acoustics of their world. They are soft-bodied, padded, damp. Translucent snails the size of hounds leave silver trails across the bark-flesh. Moth-things with bodies like wet paper drift through the spore-thick air, their wings beating in frequencies below hearing. Creatures that might be worms or might be roots move through the capillary tunnels of the deep substrate, communicating not with calls but with pressure waves, gentle squeezes passed from one living wall to the next. When a predator strikes, it does so in silence, with a sudden glut of sticky tendrils or a falling bladder-trap. Death here is a muffled thing.

The plateau is so vast that it generates its own physics of scale. Weather does not cross it; it is born here. Warm fronts rise from the composting deep, meeting the cooler exhalations of the weeping pores, and in the hollow places—caverns within the root-mass large enough to hold cities—internal storms brew. You can stand in a dry gallery and hear, leagues away, the soft percussion of rain falling inside the world, a storm in the belly of the beast. The cycles are glacial. A flower may take seven years to open. A scar left by a falling limb heals over decades. The only quick thing is the drip of water, and even that seems patient, a metronome marking a tempo too slow for human blood.

Scattered across this vastness, rare and strange, are the built things. Not structures of technology—there are no wheels, no fire, no worked stone—but forms that suggest intention, or at least a moment when growth dreamed of geometry. A tower of fused heartwood, hexagonal in cross-section, rising thirty meters before splitting into five equal spires. A bridge of woven fiber spanning a mist-filled depression, its railings grown with perfect symmetry. A dome of calcified sap, hollow inside, the walls etched with patterns that look almost like script. They are too few and too far between to constitute a civilization, but they are too ordered to be mere accident. Perhaps they are galls, the defensive response of the plateau to some ancient parasite now extinct. Perhaps they are the fruiting bodies of a symbiotic fungus that once needed doors. Whatever they are, they stand in silence, half-swallowed by the patient reclamation of the living substrate, their angles softened by new growth, their hollows occupied by the soft, quiet fauna.

It is an old place. Not merely aged, but finished. The Thrum has reached its final form, a stable state where erosion and growth have achieved perfect equilibrium. The mountains are gone. The rivers are gone. The sharp edges of the world have been rounded into the continuous curve of a body at rest. It is warm, wet, dim, and absolutely, overwhelmingly alive. It does not need to change. It only needs to continue, breathing once every century, weeping constantly, holding its countless soft inhabitants in the crowded, muffled dark.

To stand upon it is to stand upon something that has already lived forever, and intends to live forever more, digesting time itself in the slow, green dark.
