It begins with the floor. Not stone, though it carries stone’s grey patience; not soil in any familiar sense. After epochs of erosion, the mountains have been planed down to this: a vast tableland so high that the sky above it is bruised thin and dark, yet so worn by time that you could roll a marble from one horizon to the other and watch it coast for miles. But press your palm to the ground and you will feel a subsonic thrum, a vascular persistence. The floor is wood. Not a forest floor, but floor-as-forest—compressed, fused, metabolized, polished by the diligent abrasion of millions of years into a single contiguous skin of root-matter. Rock gave up here long ago, dissolved into the patience of biology, and the plants, total and unrelenting, filled every vacancy. Erosion did not end in dust. It ended in substrate. This is the oldest stable state, the final geometry the world could settle into before it forgot the difference between dead matter and living scaffold.

Across this open expanse, the presences rise. They are scattered. The scale is regional, mercilessly broad; you can walk for an hour through the dim, bruised twilight and meet nothing but the mist coiling around your ankles, then crest a subtle ridge and find yourself at the foot of a structure that occupies a valley’s worth of air. No wall was planned. No chimney was laid by hand. There are no artifacts of intention, no constructed things at all, and yet what grows here is industrial in every way that matters. Factories of lignin and cellulose loom against the sagging cloud-base. Corkscrew flues of woven bark, two hundred feet high, vent scalding plumes into the frigid air. Bellows the size of cathedrals, made from leaf-matter blackened by age, pulse with a mechanical rhythm. Articulated arms, bracketed to nothing, suspended by the torque of their own woody tension, end in nozzles that drip not sap but something refined, something processed in chambers where the pressure would crush steel if steel were permitted here. Valve-flowers the color of rust regulate internal torrents. Seed-pistons fall in arboreal galleries, producing a bass-note percussion that travels through the root-floor and emerges elsewhere, transformed, louder.

It is cold. A cold that snaps at the edges of sound, that makes each crack and hiss ring with unnatural clarity. Your breath leaves you in dense clouds that hang, too heavy to rise, joining the general condensate that weeps from every pipe and flange of bark. The wetness is absolute—water does not stay still here. It is a restless, marsh-like element suspended in the atmosphere itself. It condenses on the frozen outriggers of the factory-trees, runs in engineered rivulets down channels that might be drainage or might be deliberate design, pools in the shallow declivities of the ancient wooden plain, then flash-steams when it touches a warm biological junction, rises, drifts as fog, and freezes again into filigree on the next cold surface. Ice marbles rattle in wooden chutes. The air itself is a wetland, a cold distillation column, forever cycling through its phases.

And they are working. They have always been working. This is the industrial age of biology, but it is the heat-death made productive. The machines do not rust because they scar over with cambium. They do not tire because they photosynthesize at efficiencies evolved to mine the dim, filtered light of this altitude—a light the color of a bruise under water, never bright, always sufficient. The plateau is near-flattened, its violent youth eroded into amnesia, yet the dynamic is ferocious. A pressure valve erupts in a tower miles distant, punching a column of warm mist through the fog-banks, which shear and roll like storm surf. Piston-roots the width of subway tunnels heave the wooden floor, not to break it but to circulate nutrients, causing the ground to shudder in rhythms that would register on seismographs if anyone remained to read them. The factory-organisms manufacture at scale. Cellulose is cracked and reformed. Excess is vented. Spore-clouds bloom and collapse. The turbulence is the turbulence of a mill running at full capacity, of a forge that never cools, of a billion pistons firing in a space that geology has long since surrendered.

The fauna does not flee the noise. It revels in it. Life here is abundant, though the density of presences remains sparse across the immensity; the creatures are concentrated in pockets, clouds, processions. Swarms of symbiotic things—neither insect nor machine-tender but somehow both—cling to warm junctions where condensation runs hottest, their chitinous bodies polished to a bearing-sheen by constant motion, secreting lubricants that the living machinery accepts. Great shaggy ungulates wander the leagues between factories, hooves like mallets striking the resonant root-floor, browsing not on grass—there is none—but on the byproduct-fall: waxy kernels dropped from processing heights, resin that hardens into amber in the cold. Birds, or entities with the purposeful density of birds, nest in the upper flues where steam is warmest, their cries sharp and frequent, stitching across the industrial drone. In the hollows, hundreds of small, clicking creatures tend a single root-node as big as a house, their accumulated body heat melting divots in the frost, their abundance so total that the air itself seems to swarm, though the next valley over may offer only silence and mist.

But it is the byproducts that betray the true nature of the place. The factory-trees do not only process carbon and water. They refine something older. At the bases of certain chimneys, in the cold pools where effluent gathers, you find objects. A gear cast from lignin-stone, perfect in its teeth, fitted to no axle. A glassy sphere that refracts not light but a color that has no name, pulsing with a rhythm that matches nothing biological. These are not fruit. They are waste. The trees, in their scaled, mechanized production, process reality itself, and reality does not always survive intact. Sometimes the fog thickens in one spot and refuses to thin, and if you walk through it, the factory you left behind is no longer the one you approach—they have exchanged positions, or years, or states of repair. The sound of a falling seed-piston may reach you first from the left, then from beneath your feet, then from a direction that does not exist. The root-floor remembers paths that no longer align with geography. You stand on a plain that is both utterly stable, worn to its final form, and actively liquefying at the seams of causality.

This is the end of the world’s architecture. The mountains eroded themselves into a stage for roots. The climate froze to slow entropy to a rate that production could match. The biology, total and unstinting, became architecture, became machinery, became the substrate itself, and now it processes forever in the dim, wet cold, loud with the unending din of pistons and sap, sparsely scattered across a tableland too vast to hold in one thought, manufacturing the impossible from the forgotten, grinding on in the oldest, settled state—unbuilt, unplanned, and ceaselessly industrial.
