You come up onto the Shelf not by degrees but by a single seismic stair: the land breaks along a clean fault-line, and suddenly you are standing two thousand metres above the lowlands, on stone that pressed its way into the sky so recently the rock still carries the sheen of compression. The peaks around you are infants—sharp, unswaddled, left raw by a birth that happened yesterday in the language of geology. No river has had time to carve a kindly valley into them. No frost has yet rounded their edges. They stand like a crown of broken black glass, and between them the plateau opens, wide enough to hold a township, wide enough that the eye loses its estimation of distance in the grey, equal light.

It is cool. Always cool. Never the biting cold of snowline, but never the mercy of warmth. The air sits against your skin like water held in a ceramic cup. And water is everywhere, though you will not find a river. The moisture here does not run; it condenses. It beads on the lip of every lintel, hangs in silver collars around the necks of moss-roofed towers, and exhales from the pores of young volcanic stone that has not yet learned how to be dry. Mist lid the hollows in a layer you could stir with a hand, but it does not drift so much as appear—each morning rising from the same fissures, each dusk settling into the same basins, as though the air has memorised its habits and refuses to improvise.

The city occupies the Shelf completely. It is urban in its bones—terraced, layered, infrastructured. Avenues of fitted basalt run for half a league, engineered with a camber that channels the condensation into troughs carved from softer tufa. There are granaries, public cisterns, retaining walls stepping down the escarpment like a geometry lesson, and stacked dwellings that climb four storeys high in corbelled arcs of pale stone. Yet the density of presence is desperately thin. You can walk the main processional for an hour and meet no one. The city was built for multitudes, organised for traffic it no longer receives, and the silence of all that engineered space feels less like abandonment and more like an event that has not yet happened. The stone is dark with damp; the gutters are clean. Nothing erodes, because nothing here is allowed to finish.

This is the revelation of the place: it is young, and it is old, and it is neither. The mountains are freshly torn. The architecture is hand-wrought, breathtakingly primitive. You can see the strike-marks of pounders, the scoring of obsidian chisels, the wear-polish of bone spades in the threshold grooves. They had no iron, no wheel for hauling, no mortar but the perfection of their own patience. And yet the city stands, complete, in a condition of absolute equilibrium.

Nothing changes. The moss on the northern aqueduct is lush—almost obscenely so, velveteen and viridian—but it does not creep further along the stone than it did a year ago, or a century ago. The ferns that sprout from the copings uncurl to the same shape every spring, which is every day, which is no day at all. The temperature neither spikes nor drops. The water drips, but the cisterns never overflow and never empty; the level remains at the dark watermark, perpetually. The young sharp peaks surrounding the plateau do not shed scree. No boulder has shifted since the uplift. No slope has weathered to soil. The world here has found its form and been granted, or has seized, permission to stop.

And so the vegetation is lush not because it is racing, but because it is resting in its own abundance. Alpine meadows fill the spaces between plazas, thick with a sward that does not yellow. Furred lichens drape the statues—there are statues, uncannily generic, of figures in the act of lifting stone—and the lichens do not advance to swallow the faces. They simply live, in their fullness, in stasis.

The fauna are abundant. You will see them immediately if you stand still. Moth-winged creatures the colour of wet slate settle on the terrace walls in hundreds, pulsing their wings in perfect synchrony, making no sound. Ape-like things with spectacled eyes and soft leather palms watch you from the eaves, silent as furniture. Beetles move like living jewels through the meadows, numerous as dewdrops, but none of them click or scratch or chirrup. Sound here is dampened by the saturated air, by the equilibrium that swallows vibration. What little noise exists is human: the soft clink of a stone tool being handed from one person to another, the whisper of fiber sandals on basalt, a single voice murmuring a phrase that has been murmured in that tone, at that hour, in that doorway, since the city reached its current shape.

There are inhabitants. They are sparse. You might find a woman in a wrapped woollen cloak—always wool, always the same weave, plant-dyed to the same rust colour—re-seating a coping stone with an antler-headed mallet. The tool is wood and knapped flint; the technique is exactly what was used to build the wall. She is not repairing decay, because decay does not arrive. She is maintaining the pattern. Nearby, a child—though no children seem to age past a certain season of youth—may be scattering grain for the silent goat-like browsing animals that move through the lower terraces. The grain exists in abundance. The animals exist in abundance. Neither population grows nor diminishes. Meals satisfy without diminishing the larder; fires warm without consuming the fuel beyond what is ritually replaced.

Light here is normal, if permanently filtered. The sun, when it penetrates the high cloud deck, does so with a grey-gold evenness that casts faint shadows to the north-east and no farther. It is never fully day, never fully dusk. It is the light of a held breath. The clouds themselves do not progress; they appear at the rim of the plateau, assume their stations, and dissolve at dusk in the same configuration they dissolved the day before, which is the same as the day that will follow.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute: entropy is locally unwelcome. Energy enters and leaves in perfect correspondence. The city was completed, and completion here is not a static terminus but a stable state, a machine of stone and damp moss whose every gear has found its mate. No person remembers a different configuration of the streets; no folklore speaks of a before. They speak only in the present tense. They have no future tense, because the future is understood to be identical to the now.

You might ask what keeps it so. The answer is probably in the dampness—in the way water, the great agent of change, refuses here to rush or to freeze or to evaporate, but simply stays, participating without forcing the issue. Or in the youth of the mountains, which were thrust up into a pocket of existence so new they have not yet been taught by time how to fall apart. Or in the weirdness that seeps from the bedrock itself, a physical law that says: *Here, this is enough. Here, let this be the moment that preserves itself.*

If you visit, walk softly. Speak, if you must, in whispers. Do not drop a pebble into the cistern to see the ripples; the water will receive it, but the ripples will not spread as you expect. They will hesitate, hold their rings, and subside into the same flat dark surface that existed before your intrusion. The equilibrium tolerates you, but it does not need you. The city was here when you arrived, and it will be here when you are forgotten, the moss at the same length, the moths at the same pulse, the mist at the same depth, the young sharp peaks unweathered, unchanged, and finally, perfectly, themselves.
