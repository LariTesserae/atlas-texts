There is no floor here that was not once alive. No stone, no basalt, no silt of ground-down continents. The entire substrate is biogenic architecture—towering ribs of cellulose turned to travertine, vaulted canopies of petrified cambium, arches that were once xylem vessels wide enough to pass a whale. Elevated to extremes, the Litho-Garden rises in mountain ranges from the abyssal plain, its peaks worn by erosion so ancient that the concept of weather has forgotten it. What was once jagged and striving has been smoothed into a final geometry: bulbous spires, seamless basins, corridors that curl like the inside of an ear. The erosion did not destroy; it settled. It polished everything into the shapes a dreaming mind might mistake for halls, for staircases, for amphitheaters. But no hand ever held a tool here. No mind drew a plan. The construction is total, and utterly technological absent.

You are submerged in heavy water—nearly three-tenths from absolute saturation. Above, if there is a surface, it is irrelevant. The water column is so vast, so still, that pressure and depth have become scenery rather than threat. The temperature holds at a cool, stable notch just above the freezing point of ordinary seas, but there are no gradients. Move from the valley of a petrified root-system to the summit of a calcified crown, and the cold remains identical, as if the heat were painted onto the world in a single, even coat.

It is dark. Not the restless dark of night cycles, but a permanent, institutional darkness—light exists at a rating of three-tenths only through the rare, faint bioluminescence of the abundant fauna. They do not flash or pulse. They glow with a steady, centuries-old wattage: hanging jellies like blue lanterns fixed to specific coordinates, serpentine things with photophores that burn in unchanging constellations. The fauna are everywhere in number, yet the density of the space is so low, the scale so open, that they appear sparse. You drift through neighborhood-sized volumes of water between each presence. A colony of bone-white eels, thousands strong, occupies a single grotto and has done so, motionless, since before names. They do not hunt; they simply exist, mouths open, gill-fans waving in a rhythm that has never varied. A leviathan, if it passes, follows a groove in the water so old that the water has grown around it, a rut of permanent displacement.

And it is loud. The sound rating is moderate only by mechanical measure; to the senses, it is overwhelming because it is eternal. There is no source. It is a standing drone, a bass note struck once, perhaps by the collapse of the world’s first trench or the final exhalation of some titanic organism, and trapped forever in the frozen equilibrium of this place. With no thermal gradients, no currents, no particulate motion, the water conducts the noise without scattering it. It rings against the bio-architecture, which resonates in turn—each hollow stem, each vault, each porous wall acting as an organ pipe tuned to the same primordial frequency. The sound does not build or fade. It is a structural property of the environment, like the salinity. Living things here have evolved not to hear it, but to inhabit it, their bodies taut as drum-skins against the pressure of the eternal note.

Nothing changes. The dynamic signature is flat, a line drawn across eons. The great plant-mountains do not grow; their metabolism slowed to geological inertness long ago, yet they remain whole, neither rotting nor renewing. The water does not circulate. Particles hang in place, suspended in their own slow acceptance of zero momentum. If you were to return in ten million years, the same jellyfish would occupy the same cubit of darkness, the same hum would press against the same petrified petal. It is not death. It is a stasis so resolved that it resembles a completed thought.

The weirdness lies in the intimacy of the architecture. You pass through a doorway formed by the splitting of a trunk, and it is a doorway—it frames, it invites, it proportions. You enter a chamber where the ceiling is held aloft by pillars of lignin-stone, and the chamber has the acoustics of intention. But there is no intention. The world is a city built by the mathematics of growth, by competition for light that no longer reaches here, by the calcifying insistence of flora that once, perhaps, needed structure, and kept building until structure became landscape. The fauna treat it as habitat, but sometimes, in the loud dark, you see a pale, crab-like thing pause before an arch as if recognizing it, and the recognition is enough to hollow out your chest.

This is a submerged world at the end of its capacity to become. It has arrived. The plants are the buildings, the buildings are the mountains, the mountains are the seabed. The water holds them, cool and dense and absolute, in a darkness that is not empty but simply full of something that finished happening long ago, and decided never to leave.
