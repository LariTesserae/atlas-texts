The water is four degrees above its own freezing point, and it has filled the lower works for less than a century. There is no ground that was not poured, no wall that was not braced by calculation, no surface that does not bear the memory of a moldline or a weld bead. You are swimming through the Compensation Sea, the largest continuous liquid reservoir in the understructure, and despite its breadth, it is only a utility: a coolant bath and pressure mass for the foundry floors stacked seven kilometers above your head.

The ceiling is the sky. It is a continuous deck of ferroceramic trusses and blast-proof plating, curving away in all directions until it meets the far perimeter walls, and it is so vast that the lamps set into its grid do not read as individual fixtures but as weather. During the working shift, they burn at a strength that equals an overcast afternoon, a flat grey-green noon that penetrates forty meters before dissolving into haze. At shift change, they gutter in sequence, sending a false twilight racing across the underside of the world, triggering the vertical migration of the fauna. There is no mystery to the light. It is maintained by crews on the dry side, who replace ballasts and scrub algae from the housings.

Because the water is young, the architecture still looks angry. The seabed—if it can be called that—is not sanded smooth by epochs of current. It is a relief of sharp angles: drainage channels cut with jackhammer precision into poured slag-aggregate; pylons rising like raw incisors from the basal plate; the knife-edge rims of settling basins that have not yet accumulated enough accretion to soften their rims. Erosion here means a few decades of particulate abrasion, a dulling of corners so slight you can still cut your palm on a flange. The water has not had time to make things beautiful. It has only begun to oxidize the lowest tiers of reinforcement, blooming rust in tidy, almost respectful sheets.

And the space is empty. The Compensation Sea is continental, large enough to generate its own Coriolis currents and thermal gyres, yet its structures are scattered like buttons on a floor. You can fin through open water for six hours and see nothing but the thrum of your own exhalation bubbles. Then, abruptly, a heat-exchange pylon looms: a vertical volume the size of a municipal district, its sides ribbed with intake grilles that gulp the cold deep water and exhale it, warmed by a fraction of a degree, into the mid-level return. These pylons stand fifty kilometers apart. Between them, the water column belongs to the animals and the current.

The fauna are abundant. They were introduced deliberately during the flooding phase, a bioremediation package that escaped its containment protocols and self-propagated. Silver-bodied filter-feeders school in pulsing, countable millions, their gill-rakers stripping metalloids from the effluent. They move like sheets of loose chainmail between the pylons, twisting in unison when the false twilight passes overhead. Near the warmer outflows, where the temperature rises to a verdant ten degrees, bottom-crawlers mass on the ceramic substrate: armored things with hydraulic-feeder mouths, the size of terriers, grazing on the sparse vegetation that can survive here. That vegetation—hairline algae filaments and a few stunted, engineered kelps anchored to outflow grates—clings only where machinery bleeds waste heat. Everywhere else, the surfaces are bare, scoured by cold and chemistry, too hostile for colonization.

The sound is constant and loud. Water transmits the industrial voice of the upper works with terrible fidelity. There is the basso drone of the main circulation turbines, felt in the sternum before it is heard. There is the percussive, arrhythmic slam of drop-forges translating through the ceiling plates as a rolling, distant thunder. Closer by, the snap of thermal contraction in a newly cooled pipe, the hiss of a pressure-equalization vent disgorging a rush of bubbles toward the ceiling, the clicking of thousands of crustacean analogues scraping their way along a girder. It is not chaotic. It is the sound of a machine at steady state, too large to ever truly stop.

This is the rule of the place: it is active. Water is not permitted to be still here. Cooling cycles demand vertical turnover; the intakes draw from the frigid bottom and discharge laterally, creating scheduled currents that the animals have learned to ride like highways. The filter-feeders pulse through their migrations on factory time. The algae bloom and die according to outgassing schedules. Even the particulate load—the fine, metallic snow that drifts down from the perforations in the ceiling—falls in patterns tied to the smelting cadence above. Nothing is random. Everything is process.

And yet, for all its scale, for all the annihilating size of the trusswork vanishing into green murk, it feels normal. It is simply a workplace that happens to be underwater. Workers in heated suits descend in lock-step shifts to inspect the pylons, carrying waterproofed clipboards and replacement anodes. They do not marvel at the ocean they inhabit. It is a utility, a necessary buffer between the machinery of production and the absolute dark of the deeper substrate. The cold is normal. The filtered light is normal. The endless, engineered water, sharp-edged and ringing with the industry of a world built from nothing but intention, is exactly as designed.
