You open the pressure hatch, and the sound does not enter your ears so much as occupy your chest. It is a single sustained note of industrial white noise—air screaming through vents, a centrifugal rotor spinning at twenty thousand RPM somewhere inside the wall, water hammer slamming through a reciprocating manifold in cycles that make the floor jerk beneath your boots. You step inside, and the hatch seals behind you with a pneumatic hiss that you feel in your molars rather than hear.

You are inside a condensate recovery cell embedded in the superstructure of Peak Refinery 9, forty meters below the summit and six kilometers above the last breathable valley. The chamber is intimate: perhaps four meters square, barely tall enough to stand in, though the ceiling loses itself in a low-hanging cloud of atomized coolant. Every surface is made. There is no substrate here but manufacture. The walls are poured polymercrete still the pale grey of fresh ash, their pour-lines and die-stamps legible in the gloom—this room has existed for maybe eighteen months. The erosion is young. Corners are knife-sharp. A flange of bright steel juts from the east wall with machining marks still proud on its face, unsoftened by oxidation or time.

And yet it weeps. Water is the dominant element. The air is saturated, cool at eleven degrees Celsius, heavy with vapor that condenses instantly on your skin and clothing. The ceiling drools. A thousand beaded runnels gather at welded seams and fall in irregular rhythms, each drop tearing a tiny crater in the pool that sheets across the floor—only three centimeters deep, but enough to ripple and dance under the vibration. The far wall is freckled with mineral stains where leakage has already begun its patient work, pale galaxies of calcium and copper sulfate blooming in the corners, the nearest thing to vegetation this place tolerates. There is no fauna. Nothing has ever bred here. No insect has threaded the hatch seal, no rodient has gnawed the aluminum conduit. It is sterile by amplitude and chemistry alike.

Light is scarce. A single vapor-tight fixture in the ceiling throws a sulphurous amber disc that barely penetrures the mist. What illumination exists multiplies in the standing water, in the curved mirrors of standing droplets on every rail and rocker, in the glass face of a pressure gauge bolted to the south wall. The needle ticks steadily: two-point-four bar, two-point-six, then a shudder and a release as an automatic bleeder somewhere overhead opens a vent to the outside atmosphere. The pressure equalizes with a groan that you feel in your fillings. Outside, the altitude is lethal to the unaided lung; inside, the cell maintains its own weather.

The room’s machinery is sparse. Density here is a matter of absence. Against the far bulkhead stands the sole occupant: a vertical condensate pump in dull cast iron, pistons stroking inside a transparent acrylic shroud slick with condensation. It inhales water from the floor trough through flex-hoses ribbed with wire, then forces it upward into the distribution main. Every four seconds the entire assembly flexes on its mounting bolts, and the sound—*that* sound—swells in a crest that makes the piss-pale bacterial mats shiver on the lower ledge. The pump is the ecosystem. It is the predator and the prey, the weather cycle, the biological pace. Its tempo governs the fog, which churns and settles with each stroke, breathing in and out like a lung.

You stand in the deafening wet, and the room teaches you its rules. Nothing here is background. The cool is a pressure on your cheeks. The dim is an obstruction you must push through. The water is not scenery but process—harvested, cycled, bled. You are inside a pocket, a single-feature containment, a bedroom-sized omission in the mountain that exists only to wring the clouds out of the air and push them toward the turbines below.
