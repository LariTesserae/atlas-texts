You reach it only after the air of the lower world has grown thin and cold, yet the thing itself defies that altitude. It rises—a plateau, a massif, a submerged continent of wood—whose summit sits at the extreme ceiling of the sky, high enough that clouds break against its outer flanks like slow tides. From the outside it resembles a range of whaleback hills, smoothed not by recent ice but by time without measure: an erosion so ancient it has ceased to be violence and become instead a final, settled form. The wind has scoured every edge into a curve. The rain, over millions of years, has polished the surface to a dark, varnished sheen. This is the oldest stable state, the end of erosion, a place that has worn itself into peace.

But the surface is only a skin. The true country exists within.

Everything is plant. Not a covering, not a colonization, but *substance*. The geology here is lignin and root-stone, centuries of fused periderm compacted into load-bearing strata, aerial roots braided into arches thick as aqueducts, leaf-matter pressed into a kind of living soil that is itself alive, drinking from the condensation that ghosts its surface. Vegetation is total. There is no stone, no raw earth, no orphan patch of mineral that does not bleed sap when cut. And yet the space is empty—profoundly, dimensionally empty. The growth has turned inward for eons, hollowing itself out with the patience of a lung expanding into a ribcage. Between the fused outer shell and the labyrinthine core lies a network of voids so vast that whole valleys of air are trapped inside: cylindrical wells a mile across, vaults where the ceiling is lost in leaf-haze, spiraling shafts where a thrown stone falls for long seconds before striking a root-girder. The density is that of a held breath. You wander through a warm, woody desert of interior space.

It is warm. The outer bark faces the thin cold of extreme elevation, but inside, the mass acts as a greenhouse, a composting heart. Decay and respiration generate a steady heat. Warm, moist air rises through central chimneys and pools in the lower galleries, carrying the taste of iron and chlorophyll and something sweeter, like overripe fruit. Water does not run here in rivers; it arrives as atmosphere. Moisture from the cloud-break against the outer shell seeps inward, condensing on the cooler inner walls in a perpetual, mineral drizzle. It gathers in axils the size of bathtubs, beads on hair-fine rootlets, and falls in slow, heavy drops that never reach any floor, only other leaves, other roots, other reservoirs. It is damp the way the inside of a mouth is damp: expectant, intimate, breath-close.

Light is normal. High overhead, patches of new growth—translucent, parchment-thin, broad as lakes—diffuse the sun into a green-gold haze. Day passes as a brightening of emerald; twilight becomes jade. Shadows are soft, patient, and slow. There is no harshness to the illumination, only a steady, photosynthetic glow that seems to emanate from the architecture itself.

And then there is the sound. It is deafening. Not the noise of a storm, which builds and releases, but a constant, structural pressure. The fauna is overwhelming not because it fills every inch of space—indeed, the chambers are largely bare, the floors empty, the walls uncluttered—but because what life exists moves in inconceivable numbers and volume, and the hollow plant-body amplifies it all into a single, unending chord. Billions of condensation-bees form rivers of copper thread through the air. Bladder-bats boom in the deep shafts. Moth-winged pollinators rise in blizzards at each thermal shift, their bodies so numerous they change the temperature of the draft itself. The bark domes are resonance chambers. A single chirp becomes a reverberation; a wing-beat joins a drone that presses against the inner ear like deep water. Conversation is impossible. The scattered inhabitants do not speak. They communicate by tapping rhythms into the root walls, by gesture, by the reading of breath on skin.

Those inhabitants are few and far between. Their presence is scattered, almost hesitant: a platform of lashed bast-fiber rope creaking on a root-shelf; a half-carved ladder ascending into mist; a scatter of flaked chert tools left where someone cracked open a dried seed-pod; soot stains from fires that burn their own compacted leaf-pellets. Their technology is of the hand and the tooth. They do not build architecture—the plant has already provided cathedrals—but they leave faint marks, single structures, woven hammocks slung across narrow throats of air. They live within the organism, not upon it. They are gatherers of the insect tides, fishers of condensation, climbers who move through the warm emptiness like motes in a lung.

This is an active world, but its dynamism is ecological, meteorological, metabolic. The hollows breathe. Warm air rises during the condensing hours; cool, spore-heavy downdrafts trace the buttresses in the evening. Pollen drifts in grain-storms that turn the green air yellow. Predatory azures—hawk-moths with bodies as long as a forearm—hunt the twilight zones between lit and unlit chambers. The root-stone itself shifts, creaking as tissues swell with moisture and contract in dryness, a geological-scale metabolism. Seasonless, the plateau persists in a near-eternal spring, its cycles measured not in months but in the opening and closing of million-year-old stomata.

You can walk for days inside it, crossing from chamber to chamber by bridges of braided root, climbing staircases where handholds are the fossil whorls of ancient growth rings. The scale is open, regional, a town-sized void giving way to a valley of air giving way to a narrow throat that opens again into an abyss floored with distant leaves. And everywhere the sound: the deafening, nurturing, overwhelming engine of a world that has grown old enough to become hollow, warm enough to turn extreme altitude into greenhouse, empty enough to feel like a cathedral, and alive enough to never let you forget it.

Here, at the end of erosion, life has not conquered emptiness. It has sculpted the emptiness, warmed it, watered it with breath, and made of hollowness a final, enduring home.
