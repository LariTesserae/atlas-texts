The first thing that greets you is not optical. The light here is fierce, yes—almost violent in its saturation—but the body registers the place as sound long before the eyes can open against the glare. It is a pressure, a constant membrane against the eardrums that makes swallowing useless. The deafening quality of the tract is not composed of events: no shattering crashes, no sudden animal cries, no cyclical stamping or roaring. It is a sustained, stratified drone, a geological white noise generated by the marriage of raw stone and forced air. At this elevation—high enough that the atmosphere has begun to thin and lose its courtesy—the wind does not gust; it moves in vast, laminar sheets across unweathered peaks, moaning through fissures in igneous rock that has never been smoothed by time. Beneath this aeolian scream lies a deeper note, infrasonic and industrial: the slow, relentless turning of machinery inside the scattered megaplants. If you press your palm flat against the spongy ground, you feel it there too—a subterranean hum transmitted through capillary roots into the wet substrate, as though the plateau itself were a resonating chamber still tuning itself to the frequency of its own birth.

And it is newborn. That is the crucial thing. The geology here has not been *eroded*; it has been *exposed*. Erosion is still an embryonic concept, a future rumor. The mountains around you were lifted so recently—on a timescale that would make a geologist’s heart stutter—that the stone retains the shine of its underworld manufacture. Faces of basalt and anorthosite jut in pre-Cambrian geometries, unsoftened by rain or river, still off-gassing faint mineral warmth into the cool air. Where water touches them, it does not carve; it condenses. The entire region is saturated. Water exists not as lakes or torrents but as a hovering presence, a marsh without the marsh’s permission. The atmosphere weeps. Every surface is lacquered in a film of liquid so constant it might be the fourth phase of matter here: gas, solid, stone, and everlasting condensate. The cool temperatures—perpetually crisp, the breath of autumn preserved in amber—mean the vapor never fully lifts. It gloams against the raw rock and drips, seeps, and pools in shallow fracture-pans where it cannot soak away because there is no soil yet, only the raw anatomy of the world.

Except that there is no true *soil*, only *substrate*, and the substrate is plant. Vegetation here scored so near to absolute that it has transcended the category of inhabitant and become the medium itself. Between the scattered architectural presences, the ground is a vast, saturated felt of compressed and mineralizing organic matter—generations of collapsed pioneer growth compressed into a living rug, tens of meters thick in some hollows, threadbare over the fresh stone ridges. It breathes. It is wet through, steaming faintly in the brilliant light. Walking upon it is an uncertain act; the foot sinks slightly, and clear fluid wells upward to fill the print, as though the earth were a sponge that could not be wrung. This is the matrix from which the megaplants ascend.

They stand far apart. Density here is a forgotten language. You can walk for hours across the vegetal felt and see nothing taller than your hip until, on a raw horizon interrupted only by heat-shimmer and condensate halos, a single structure detaches itself from the brightness. Then another, leagues away, and perhaps a third at the limit of sight. The scale is open, regional, a township of towers spaced like pins in a map of their own making. Each one is a plant that has become architecture: trees only in the distant way that cathedrals are shelters. Their trunks are cylindrical, fluted, the color of oxidized copper beneath a chitinous bark; they rise hundreds of meters without branching, their crowns lost in the perpetual bright haze of the upper atmosphere. They are hollow, or partly so, their hearts excavated by their own biology—pith collapsed into vertical chambers, xylem vessels widened into corridors by centuries of capacious sap pressure. And the sap, here, is industry.

The scattered marks of the built environment are not separate from these organisms but married to them. Small structures cling to the root-flares and mid-boles like limpets: valve-houses of blackened ceramic, pressure gauges the size of dinner tables, intake lattices that breathe steam. The technology is industrial, scaled, electrified, but it is *botanical* first. The factories are inside the trunks. Osmotic pressure drives turbines. The ceaseless transpiration of water from the saturated substrate up through those fluted columns powers generation; the minerals leached from the raw geology and concentrated in the sap are refined in internal crucibles of lignin and cellulose, drawn off through spigots that drip into waiting catchment vats. The hum you felt is their metabolism made audible. Each megaplant is a vertical refinery, patient as a glacier, producing at the speed of growth. The industrial process here operates on biological time—no faster than cambium can divide. It is the only pace the world allows.

Weirdness is held at bay because everything follows an internal logic strict as engineering. What might seem alien elsewhere is merely practical here. There are no strange angles, no violations. The light is bright because the air is thin; the air is thin because the elevation is great; the elevation is great because the land is new. The plants are large because the substrate is fresh and the competition nonexistent. The fauna is almost absent—not through catastrophe, but through redundancy. What animal would need to exist here? The plants process, hum, drip, and transpire. The water cycles without assistance. A rare arthropod might be found in the catchment pools, a translucent worm threading through the peat-felt, but nothing with feathers or fur has reason to endure the sound. The auditory threshold is too high, the biological niche too completely occupied by vegetable patience.

Time, here, is a slow inhalation. The dynamic of the place is glacial in its patience. The rock still remembers being magma; the megaplants thicken their fluted walls at the pace of a whispered secret. Condensate gathers on a stone face, fattens, drops, and joins the substrate. Somewhere in the hollow trunk of a distant tower, a turbine adjusts its pitch by half a degree. The deafening hum continues unchanged. If you stay long enough—days, weeks—you begin to perceive that the sound is not static but an aggregate of innumerable minute shifts, a waterfall of near-motion held in absolute equilibrium. You are standing in a place that has not yet decided what it will become, only that it will be loud, bright, wet, and wholly alive.
