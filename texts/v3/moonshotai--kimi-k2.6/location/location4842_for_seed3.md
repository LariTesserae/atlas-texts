The first thing you notice is not a skyline but a scattering, as though someone had taken a city and cast its pieces across a slope too steep to hold them. You stand at the edge of a railhead that terminates in nothing but mist and a warning sign bolted into raw stone, and you look up. Strung across what should be an impossible distance are the lights. Not many. Sparse, as stars are sparse through cloud. Each one is a different color of industry—forge-red, arc-welder white, a queasy violet that makes your eyes water if you stare too long—but between them are leagues of empty air and the hulking shapes of mountains that have no business being this young.

This is the *Infant Spine*, and it is still being born.

The peaks here are not weathered. They have not been tenderized by epochs of rain and grinding ice. They are fresh extrusions, shoved up through the mantle by forces that have not yet been named by geologists because the stone itself has not decided what it is. Basalt flows cool beside seams of something like quartz but wrong, too glossy, too eager. Talus lies in knife-edged shards. There are no smooth valleys, only fracture planes where the crust has cracked like cooling glass. The erosion is embryonic—chasms that are less carved than torn, slopes of raw scree that haven't felt the patience of water. Everything smells of ozone and hot iron, of lithic amniotic fluid.

And yet it is damp. At this extreme elevation, the air is cool, thin enough to make your lungs work for every breath, and it cannot hold its moisture. Condensation beads on every surface. Water does not flow here so much as weep. It collects in the porous, pre-formation stone—rock so young it is still half-capillary, still learning how to be solid—and seeps out wherever gravity permits, and sometimes where it does not. Rain comes in intervals that seem disconnected from weather systems. You will see silver ropes of water falling from a cloudless smear of sky, stopping mid-air as if reconsidering, then continuing sideways to feed a hanging garden that clings to the underside of a catwalk. The damp is intermittent, accidental, moisture that has arrived from elsewhere and forgotten how to leave.

The city—if it is a city—has adapted to this. The built environment is urban by intent and scale, but sparse by necessity. There are no cramped streets, no dense quarters. Instead, there are *complexes*: foundry-cathedrals of riveted iron and soot-blackened brick, stabilization towers braced against the sky, habitation blocks stacked like ingots, each structure the size of a town square, each standing alone, separated by miles of vertical emptiness. They are connected by aerial tramways that sag between nodes, by pipe-bridges that carry steam and slag and signal, by staircases cut into the mountain flanks that ascend to nowhere and then, impossibly, continue. It is layered construction, infrastructure-dense at every node, but the nodes themselves are islands in a sea of raw creation. Between the fourth foundry and the seventh extraction pillar, there is only wind, fog, and the groaning of the crust.

This is an industrial place. The pistons in the stabilization plants beat a rhythm measured in geological patience, hammering certainty into stone that still thrums with potential. Steam escapes from vents in rhythmic, birthing clouds. Electrified fences hum around the exterior platforms, not to keep people in or out, but to contain the reality breakdowns. Generators the size of chapels turn day and night, powered by ore that is not yet fully committed to being ore. The tech here is mechanized, scaled for production: conveyor systems disappearing into mist, ore-crushers that grind with a sound like continents kissing, arc-lightning cages where raw material is *convinced* into stable states. Yet the pace of it all is slow. Change happens across decades. A new spur of mountain emerges over the course of a generation. A tramway is extended not in weeks but in years. The dynamic is glacial, patient, a weak gradient of human intent pushing against the patient rise of the earth.

Because the light is dim. The elevation is extreme enough to sit in a permanent bruise of cloud—not weather, exactly, but outgassing from the newborn rock, mineral exhalations that never clear. Daylight filters through as a tarnished pewter, never bright enough to cast a sharp shadow. The primary illumination comes from the foundries’ bellies: a arterial, forge-red glow that bleeds through smokestacks and cracked doorways, and from the aurora-like flickers of the reality-dampeners overhead, which stain the fog a queasy, intermittent violet. It is always twilight. Always the hour before a storm that never arrives.

And in this dimness, life is abundant.

The fauna here has never heard of ecological limits. With reality so thin, with causality running like watercolors in rain, creatures thrive that should collapse under their own impossibility. Flocks of flat-winged *something-like-bats* migrate in spirals between the complexes, their calls echoing from yesterday. Herds of glass-eyed grazers walk up vertical cliff faces, browsing on lichens that grow in dimensions the lichens have not yet agreed upon. In the damp hollows where condensation gathers, insects swarm in geometries—hexagonal clouds, cubic droning masses—that tangle in the fence-humming and die in crackling showers. There are the null-bears: large, many-jointed things that occupy two points at once and step between shadows cast by lights that have not yet been lit. They are drawn to the warmth of the forges, to the exhaust vents where the air is warmest and the rules are weakest. They are not dangerous so much as they are *adjacent* to where you are, slipping in and out of your peripheral vision with the sound of wet canvas tearing.

The vegetation, too, takes advantage of the world’s hesitation. Where the porous stone weeps, gardens erupt with tropical insistence despite the cool air. Mosses drape the stabilization engines in velvet cataracts. Ferns spill from expansion joints in the foundries, their fronds too large, too iridescent. In the chasms, fed by springs that run upward—water confused about the orientation of down—there are hanging jungles, dense with off-green growth that has learned to photosynthesize the violet flicker of the dampeners instead of the sun. The vegetation is lush, but compartmentalized: a riot contained in pockets, a verdant sentence scattered across a page of stone.

It is loud. The mountains themselves make noise as they accrete, a subsonic growl that lives in your sternum. The foundries answer with their own bass notes: the thud of compression hammers, the shriek of metal being taught what shape to hold, the unending exhalation of steam. Water drips in polyrhythms. The tramway cables sing in the wind. The fauna adds its layer—echo-locating clicks, mating calls that seem to originate inside your own skull. It is not a cacophony but a sustained chord, loud enough to make conversation a struggle, constant enough that silence would feel like a wound.

But the true texture of the place is the weirdness, the reality breakdown that undergirds everything. Here, at 2.6 out of three, the world has not finished compiling itself. The Infant Spine is not merely young geology; it is *pre-geology*, stone that contains all its possibilities at once, magma that refuses to fully solidify into a single history. The foundries do not mine iron. They mine the *certainty* of iron. They extract inevitability from the raw stuff of the world, refining the rock from a superposition of what-it-could-be into what-it-is. This process leaks. Reality frays at the edges.

You will walk a platform and find it longer on the inside than the outside. You will look down a chasm and see yourself looking up. The rain falls upward to feed the hanging gardens because the gardens were thirsty yesterday and gravity is still weighing its options. A null-bear will walk past you and leave pawprints that age backwards in the dust, newest at the beginning of its trail. The fauna is abundant because the carrying capacity is negotiable. The city is sparse because two complexes, correctly aligned, might be the same complex viewed from different angles of causality. The dim light is not just occluded—it is *unfinished*, photons that have not fully committed to their frequency.

You live here, if you live here, by learning the slow rules of a place that changes slowly but incompletely. You do not rush. The mountain will rise another inch by next year. The moss will claim a new pipe-joint by the decade’s end. A new reality-breach will open in the eastern quarry, and teams will cordon it off, and the containment generators will hum louder, and the word *loud* itself might fray at the edges where the soundwaves remember being silence.

It is a city of voids and sudden warmth, of industrial patience applied to a world still deciding what to be. You stand at the railhead. The mist moves off, revealing another peak, fresher and sharper than the last, pushing up through the cloud deck with a sound like a bell being struck in a dream. Somewhere, six miles away, a forge exhales red light. Somewhere closer, water drips upward into ferns that glow in the violet dark. A null-bear becomes visible, then doesn't, leaving only the wet sound of its passage and the faint, electric hum of a world holding itself together by choice, not by law.
