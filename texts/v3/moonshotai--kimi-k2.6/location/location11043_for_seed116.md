The water is warm, and it fills the room completely.

The room is not large. Thirty paces from wall to curved wall, perhaps fifteen from the floor to the apex of the dome—an intimate pocket, a single lung suspended so high in the constructed sky that the atmosphere outside has turned to bruise-purple twilight even at noon. This is the crown of a tower that was already ancient when the cities below were young, a needle of ceramic and pitted alloy thrust into the mesosphere. Outside, there is freezing death and silence. Inside, there is only the warm, amber-tinted water, and the sound of a world that has learned to persist without its architects.

Everything here is made. There is no stone, no native earth, no substrate that was not fired, poured, riveted, or drawn. The walls are hexagonal tiles of vitrified something—once white, now the color of old bone—seamed with copper that has long since dissolved into verdigris clouds. The ceiling is a geodesic blister of thick, flawed glass, each triangle scoured for centuries by high-altitude ice and radiation until they resemble cataracts. Through them, light enters not as a beam but as a diffuse, ordinary glow, the same mundane luminescence one might find in a kitchen window at midday, filtered down through leagues of thin air and murky water. It is enough to see by. Enough to grow by.

Erosion has finished its work. This is not a place of collapse but of final form. The sharp corners of the original industrial architecture have been worn away by warm currents and uncounted years until every surface has the yielding geometry of a river stone. A handrail, if you were to grip it, would fit the palm perfectly; the metal has been stroked by water into a shape that suggests it was always meant to be held. At the chamber’s center, a vertical column of machinery rises from floor to dome—once a pressure equalizer, perhaps, or a thermal regulator. It has shed its housings. What remains is a core of brass and stainless steel, pitted but stable, wrapped in a slow helix of crimson aquatic moss that blooms with tiny, star-shaped flowers the color of rust. The machine and the moss have reached an agreement.

The water is warm, consistently, unnaturally so, heated by some deep accumulator in the tower’s root that still cycles, still burns. Convection moves in lazy, predictable loops. You can trace them by the silver curtains of bubbles that rise from a fissure in the northwest wall—oxygen, or something like it, released by a valve that opens and closes on a geological rhythm, once every few hours, with a sound like a giant clearing its throat. The sound is loud. In this intimate, liquid room, there is no quiet. The tower itself groans as it flexes in the jet stream, a bass note so low it vibrates in the sternum, transmitted through the alloy bones and into the water. Against this, the higher registers: the ticking of thermal expansion, the hiss of steam from a pinhole leak, the constant, industrious clicking of the inhabitants.

Fauna is abundant, though the space is sparse. Between the central column and the walls, there is a gulf of open water where nothing lives but the current itself. The creatures here understand distance. Schools of minnow-things, their scales actual thin flakes of aluminum that have replaced true keratin over uncounted generations, drift in formation through the amber void, flashing like scattered tools. They feed on the bacterial mats that lace the tile seams, and they are hunted, in turn, by larger things: eel-shaped predators with skeletons of woven carbon fiber and teeth like broken needles, their eyes faintly luminous, not with bioluminescence but with the captured reflection of some tiny, still-active diode embedded in their foreheads. They dart from the shadows of pipes, strike, retreat. The food web is active, hungry, and entirely localized. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. This is a complete ecology in a teacup.

Vegetation is lush where it chooses to grow. It does not carpet. It accretes. Great, drooping fronds of kelp-like matter hang from a catwalk that spans the dome, their holdfasts drilled—not grown, but drilled—into the ancient iron, as if the plant itself had learned to use a tap and die. Below, in the warmest corner where a seep of mineral-rich fluid pools, a garden of anemones opens and closes in unison, but they are not anemones; they are colonies of filter-feeding tubeworms that have evolved crystalline casings, and they feed on the industrial effluent, their colors the colors of circuit boards: solder-green, capacitor-gold, copper-orange. They are beautiful. They are wrong.

The weirdness is subtle but pervasive. It is in the way the water refuses to stratify, maintaining an even warmth from dome to floor despite the laws of thermodynamics suggesting otherwise. It is in the scale of certain shadows, which seem to belong to objects not present in the room. It is in the certainty, swimming here, that the chamber is slightly larger than it was when you first entered, that the walls have receded by a handspan, that the water holds more volume than the architecture should allow. The erosion has not just worn the place down. It has settled it into a state that physics has agreed to overlook.

And it is loud. Always loud. The tower’s flex, the bubble curtains, the predatory strikes, the hum of a transformer that still somehow steps down voltage in the eastern wall, creating a faint electric tingle in the water that makes the mosses orient toward it like plants seeking the sun. The sound is the breath of the place. It is an active sound, a dynamic sound, the noise of a world that has not stopped moving even though its makers are dust.

This is the Warm Vault, or the Lily Sump, or simply Here—a pocket of warm, wet, industrial life suspended at the top of the world, complete unto itself, ancient, constructed, and waiting. It has reached its final form. It will outlast the tower, perhaps, when the tower finally shears off and falls. Until then, the minnows flash, the eels hunt, the moss blooms, and the water stays warm.
