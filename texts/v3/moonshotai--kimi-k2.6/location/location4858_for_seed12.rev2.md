You do not reach this place by crossing a border but by wading into thickness. Somewhere among the upper elevations, where the land has only recently shouldered its way through the roof of the world, the atmosphere yields. It ceases to behave like air and becomes something else entirely—a warm, stationary ocean suspended in defiance of falling. It is not rain, though it tastes of copper rain. It is not fog, though it obscures everything beyond an arm’s length. It is a dense, amber fluid that you push through with the labor of swimming, a wall of atmospheric water pinned in place by altitude and heat, pressing its weight against stone that has not yet decided to be land.

These mountains are infants. They breached in geologic seconds—thrust upward, bled out their magma, and froze into shapes too sharp, too sudden, for any wind to have softened. Erosion has not begun here. There is no history written upon them, no rounded river stone, no patient valley carved by water. Water does not cut in this place; it enfolds. The rock remains in the exact geometry of its catastrophe: basalt prisms standing like the pipes of an abandoned organ, obsidian blades still warm to the touch, radiating a dull infrared hymn into the syrupy medium. The stone is black, glassy, offended by the idea of time. Everywhere, the planet’s fresh skin creaks and settles, emitting a low subterranean bass note that you feel in the hollow of your chest more than you hear it.

Sound is a fugitive here. It travels a hand’s breadth through the fluid atmosphere and gives up, exhausted. The silence is absolute except for the slow hiss of thermal vents coughing steam into the coppery depths, the occasional bass groan of a cooling slab adjusting its weight. Mostly, there is the hush of a held breath, a quiet so complete that your own heartbeat becomes percussion.

Yet the place is lush.

Against every expectation, vegetation riots in the perpetual twilight. It does not need the sun, which has already been filtered by miles of suspended vapor into a dim, distant rumor. These are forests of chemophores—great thermal kelptrees anchored to the warm veins of raw basalt, their fronds broad and leathery, undulating in the slow, viscous currents of the thickened air. They drink the heat bleeding from the stone and transmute it into a wan, persistent glow: phosphor greens, fungal golds, the bruised violet of old contusions. Colonies of bioluminescent slime-mold drape the cliff faces in curtains the size of ballrooms, cementing the nascent geology even as they illuminate it, protecting the raw rock from a corrosion that has not yet arrived. The light they shed is dim—barely enough to silhouette a raised hand against the dark—but it is everywhere, an undersea gloaming occurring three miles above any ocean.

Life here is not the wheeling, shrieking kind. Fauna is rare, almost mythical. There are no birds, no insects, no herds, no vermin scurrying. Once in a season, perhaps, a shadow vast and mindless drifts through the lower fluid layers: something like a zooplanktonic leviathan, a weather pattern with a stomach, grazing the kelp forests in silence, its body translucent and simple as a storm cloud. No one hunts it. No one could. It passes like an eclipse, and the world returns to its vegetal stillness.

But there are presences. Many. Dense.

If you press through the copper medium, if you follow the warmth bleeding from the fractures, you find the settlement.

Not a metropolis of clangor and wheels. There is no metal here. No fired brick, no mortar ground by mills, no ax-split timber. Technology rests at the threshold of the hand—obsidian scrapers, bone levers, ropes braided from the silica-hair of cave molds, tools for persuasion rather than conquest. And yet the density of construction is undeniable: a vertical town, a regional saturation of habitation terraced and tunneled into the cooling slopes with a crowded intensity that would make any valley-sized city seem diffuse.

The inhabitants—patient, dim-lit, and slow-moving—do not so much build as negotiate with the geology.

They have learned the rhythm of newborn stone. When the mountain bleeds, they are there. With their primitive tools and a timescale measured in generations, they seed the lips of vents with rapid-crystallizing bacteria and mineralizing fungi they have cultivated since the stone first rose. They cannot dam a lava flow, but they can tempt it, shade it, encourage it to cool into an arch, a habitable tube, a terrace. Over centuries, the dwelling accretes like a tree ring, each generation adding a new layer of warm, silicate upholstery. The settlement is composed of these persuaded corridors: hundreds of chambers stacked into the cliff face, overhanging one another by scant inches, linked by catwalks of half-living basalt and rope. Every habitable pocket clusters around a thermal vent because heat is the currency of existence here. To live is to live in the warm fracture.

The density is such that to move through a central artery—if artery is the word for a fluid-filled crevice lined with glowing faces—is to be in constant contact. Shoulders brush. Shared warmth seeps through the porous walls. The population is layered, vertical, saturated. They do not shout; the medium swallows voices whole. Their communication is gesture, the touch of a palm, and the slow, deliberate bioluminescent pulse of cultivated molds painted in spirals across their skin. They move with the meditative calm of the stone around them, tending thermal gardens in chambers where the air—if it can still be called air—is thick enough to hold you up if you grow still.

The rules of this world are still being written. Nothing is finished. The mountains are still deciding their final shapes, their magma hearts still pulsing beneath the fresh crust. The atmospheric ocean has not yet chosen whether to remain suspended or to drain downward in some future cataclysm. The settlement, if it is a settlement, grows one persuaded cooling at a time, a primitive urban organism inserted into the slow conversation between heat and fluid.

It exists in the amber suspension above the world, patient beyond measure, dim, dense, strange, and still becoming.
