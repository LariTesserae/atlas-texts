The light arrives from everywhere and nowhere, as though the air itself were luminous. You stand—not on stone, but on a floor of compacted lignin, tan and cross-hatched with stiff vascular bundles that feel like frozen rope underfoot. Above, below, and to every side rise the walls of a cavity so large that weather happens inside it. Moist air masses collide over the course of days, spawning slow drifts of fog that cling to the vaulted cell-structure overhead. It is bright here, blindingly so, yet you are hundreds of meters below any conventional surface. Sunlight, or something indistinguishable from it, has been piped downward through the translucent meat of the plant: kilometers of fiber-optic xylem and siliceous phloem, diffusing into a white-gold haze that eliminates shadow.

This is the hollow of the Thuwoor, or so the scattered peoples call it—the living substrate that is world and architecture both.

The temperature hovers just above freezing. Your breath turns to vapor and joins the ambient haze, which never quite disperses. Dampness is the rule, though there are almost no standing pools. Instead, condensation beads on the vertical planes of rigid cellulose, swelling into clear hemispheres that tremble for hours before releasing a single cold drop. When the hydraulic pressure shifts—which it does, often—a new fissure will weep for a week, painting a dark streak down fresh, pale wood, before the wound is sealed by rising pith. The moisture is intermittent, capricious, born from the respiration of the structure itself.

And it is young. Not in the small way of saplings, but in the large way of freshly cleaved continents. The chamber walls, visible as faint horizons through the glowing mist, display none of the softening of age. Ridges of cambium jut in knife-edge escarpments. A fallen septum—a partition between two colossal cells—might block a valley for decades, its splintered end still ocher and fragrant, uncolonized by rot. The Thuwoor is in its growth spasm. It is an adolescent organism at planetary scale, still pushing outward, still dividing, still forgetting to make room for its own contents.

The turbulence of this youth is what makes the silence so unsettling. The soundscape is mostly quiet: a hush so complete you can hear the frost forming on your sleeves. But beneath it runs a subsonic restlessness. Sudden cracks, sharp as musket shots, announce the birth of new voids. The walls groan as turgor pressure mounts in adjacent tissues. Sometimes a whole district of the hollow will flex, the floor buckling upward by meters over a single afternoon, tilting the primitive way-marks and seed-pod shelters that someone left decades ago. Then, just as abruptly, the pressure equalizes and the floor settles, not quite where it was. Storms here are tectonic and botanical. Because it is vast. Vast enough that travelers speak of climates. Near the radial heart-roots, the walls glow amber and the air is heavy with volatile resins; in the outer rind-hollows, the light is bluish and thin, and frost rimes every surface in fractal geometries that repeat at the scale of landscapes. You could walk for months toward a glowing wall and never reach it, watching it merely shift from pearlescent abstraction to the visible weave of macro-fibers, each strand broad as a highway.

And you would walk mostly alone. Fauna is rare—a blessing and a loneliness. Now and then a pale creature, eyeless and many-jointed, scurries along the channels where condensation runs hottest, feeding on dissolved pectins. Once, perhaps, you see something vast drift through the mist far overhead, buoyed by hydrogen sacs, grazing on the microbial snow that sifts from the ceiling. But these are exceptions. The ecology is almost entirely vegetable, occurring in the slow spectrum: the Thuwoor’s own immune responses, the wandering mycelial fans that digest the rare deadfalls, the anaerobic glimmers that phosphoresce in the pressure shadows behind new growths.

Human presence, or something analogous to it, is scattered like afterthoughts. On a ledge of newly extruded heartwood, someone has piled stones—actual mineral stones, imported from some distant surface or regurgitated from the plant’s own siphoning roots—into a cairn topped with a flint knife. In a stable pocket between two titanic vessels, there is a shelter: ribs of wood stripped from a fallen septum, covered with hides cured in tannic acid, a hearth ringed with the pale clay that bleeds from fresh fractures. The tools are primitive. No metal. Bone awls, fiber cordage, hand-axes of lignin-stone, a whistle carved from a dried pore. The builders have learned to read the plant’s hydraulic moods, to predict which chambers will crack open and which will be crushed in the next cycle of expansion. Their artifacts feel less like conquest and more like polite requests to a body they inhabit.

There is an unusual property to the place, the subtle weirdness of scale and biology colliding. Sound does not attenuate as it should; the tubular geometry of the vascular structure carries whispers across tens of kilometers, so that a person chipping at a wall on the far side of a gulf might seem to be working just behind your shoulder. And light, though bright enough to sting the eyes, does not warm. The Thuwoor draws the heat upward, or consumes it, or transmutes it into the chemical work of growth. You stand in radiance and shiver.

Most strange is the sense of being inside a process rather than a place. The hollow is not a static cavern but a lull between two growth surges. You sense that the emptiness is temporary, an interstitial accident of biology. The plant has not yet decided to fill this volume. Eventually—decades hence, or centuries—it may extrude enough pith to bridge the gap, or a root node may swell to occlude the sky-bright vault. Until then, the emptiness remains, magnificent and cold and terribly bright, ringing now and then with the sound of its own becoming.
