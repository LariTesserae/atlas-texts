The sound arrives before anything else. It is not a single sound but a chord, a sustained collective drone that seems to come not from any one direction but from the architecture of the air itself—low, pressurized hums threading through colossal vertical channels, rising to gale-screams where apertures align, falling to wet, percussive ticking in the lower hollows where condensation beads and drops into abyssal depths. There is so much space for sound to move in. The place is mostly void.

You stand, if stand you can, on the inner curve of a stalk-wall. Here, *stalk* is the only word, though the scale profanes it. The wall curves away in both directions, an arc of pale, ligneous tissue rising so gradually that its true nature reveals itself only when you strike it: a resonant, ceramic thrum, the voice of a tree that has become a cathedral. This is not bark as you know it. It is dense, blanched, faintly translucent where frost has not coated it—more like the mineralized wood of a petrifaction still in progress, yet when you press an ear to it you hear the sluggish, distant pulse of sap migrating through living cambium deep inside. The plant is the substrate. The world here is made of it, not merely clothed.

You are high. Nine kilometers above the basal lithosphere, some say, or perhaps it is better to say nine kilometers above anything that is *not* this organism. The elevation is gained not by climbing but by existence; the entire continent is a shelf, a recently-emergent plateau of interlocked rhizome and compressed xylem thrust upward by tectonic ambition or by the slow hydraulic push of the growth itself—you cannot tell which, and perhaps there is no difference here. Erosion is young. The features are fresh, ungentled. Where the great stalks have cracked apart or where the plateau’s edge has sheared away, the breaks are knife-sharp, shards and splinters the size of cities jutting at vicious angles. No river has rounded them. No patient grit has softened them. Water here is too scarce for that. It exists only as intermittent moisture: a breath of fog that rolls through the tubular emptiness, a sheen of sublimation-ice on the inward-facing slopes, a thin and freezing rivulet that appears on a wall for an hour and is gone, drunk by porous tissue.

And it is dark. Light is barely a memory. If you look toward the sky—a distant circle of lesser darkness where one vast pipe opens to the outer atmosphere—you might catch a suggestion of star or cloud, but inside the matrix of hollows, darkness is total. The plants themselves offer no luminescence, save for a faint, disquieting phosphorescence at stress-fractures, a anaerobic glow where cellulose strains, but this is less light than rumor. You navigate by echolocation now, or by touch-tracing the wall’s ridged growth rings, or by listening to the wind’s change in pitch as the tube ahead widens or chokes. The darkness has bred a fauna of listeners. They are abundant. In the emptiness, that word becomes relative—*abundant* means that somewhere in the next cubic kilometer of void, thousands of creatures cling, glide, and shriek. You hear their wings brush the air: leathered, membranous, too light to register as mass. There are things like tadpoles that never touched water, swimming through fog-banks on undulating fins of skin. There are colonists of mite-ish grazers that rasp the pale wood for its locked nutrients, and the predators that bomb through the dark after them, navigating by the snap of a wing or the wet click of a mandible. The animals are loud, adding chatter and hunting-cries to the tectonic groans of the structure. The place is never silent. Sound is how it breathes.

There are scattered signs that others came before you, or before your kind. Primitive things. A platform of lashed fiber and splintered stalk wedged in a vertical crack, bearing the polish of repeated use—a roost, perhaps, or a shrine. A stone adze, simple metal absent, its binding of sinew or fiber long desiccated, left embedded in the wall as if the wood had tried to swallow it and been interrupted. Cairns of pale chipped flake mark passages through the larger tubes, small monuments to the few routes where air moves reliably and the dampness pools enough to support a temporary camp. The builders never attempted to reshape the plant. They knew better. They inserted themselves into the emptiness it already provided.

For the place is active. It is not dead architecture but dynamic, adolescent in its formation. You can feel it in the tremors when a new split opens in a stalk-wall, sharp and crying out in a subsonic crack that sets the drone-chord shifting to a new key. Weather forms inside the tubes as surely as it does across oceans. Warm, damp exhalations from deeper fissures meet cold downdrafts from the plateau’s rim, spinning fog-vortices that wander the vertical channels like blind spirits. Rain, when it comes, comes as a sudden vertical river down the central shaft of a stalk, a brief cataract that has nowhere to rest, sheeting off internal shelves and vanishing into lower blackness, leaving walls glazed and singing with new moisture. Ice veins expansive force, wedging cracks wider, exfoliating flakes of pale matter that drift down like the scales of enormous fish. The ecology pulses. The grazers migrate from tube to tube following the condensation cycles; the fog-swimmers rise when pressure drops, fall when it climbs. Nothing is still.

What is unusual about it—what nags at the edge of comprehension—is the regularity. The stalks did not grow like forest trees, anarchic and competing. They packed. Hexagonal in cross-section where they abut, each massive tube pressing against its neighbors like cells in a honeycomb, as if the plant had obeyed an architectural imperative rather than an evolutionary scramble. The hollows are too regular, the curves too engineered by chance. There is mathematics in the emptiness. Walking a ledge inside one tube, you find the wall bears the fossilized impression of what might have been a leaf, but the size of a sailing vessel, and the geometry of its venation is fractal, unsettling, suggesting a blueprint rather than a history.

And the scale. You could travel for months inside one stalk, descending or ascending, and never see its end. The network is continental. There are stalks that serve as arteries, their cores wide enough to hold weather systems—cumulus fog-banks clogging mid-levels, upper reaches gouting with spindrift, lower levels still as death. There are stalks so narrow that a man must wedge himself sideways, feeling the cold, damp walls on chest and back, listening to his own heartbeat compete with the external drone. Between them, where walls have collapsed or where growth failed to seal a gap, lie vast irregular chambers—cathedrals of pale wood and absolute night—where the sound collapses into booming, swimming reverberations and where solitary things wait, motionless, for the vibration of prey.

You are cold. The temperature hovers just below the threshold of mercy, and the dampness eats into you. But the cold preserves the freshness of the structure. There is no rot. Nothing decays fully here; the pure plant mass resists its own dissolution, too dense for fungus, too sterile in its heartwood for bacterial hunger. What dies becomes part of the wall, mummified and compressed, adding another layer to the substrate.

If you wait long enough in the dark, you begin to understand the logic of the place. It is an organism that has solved the problem of scale by choosing emptiness over fill, by becoming architecture rather than biomass. It is a forest inverted: not a solid packed with life, but a lattice defining a near-void, and life—resonant, sonorous, chittering, roaring—rushes in to populate the space it chose not to occupy. The world here is not what you walk upon. It is what stands aside to let the wind, the water, and the dark pass through. And it sings.
