You stand at a height where the lungs ache and the horizon curves too steeply, on a plain that should not be possible. The air is thin, almost a memory of atmosphere, yet the heat presses down with the weight of a foundry ceiling. There is no water here. None. Not in cisterns, not in veins beneath the crust, not as vapor in the breathless sky. What once was sea is now a vast bed of fused salt and vitrified silt, a ceramic scab left behind when the world above and below agreed to boil the oceans away. And there are no plants—nothing green, nothing rooted, nothing that photosynthesizes. Only the mineral, only the blown, only the hot.

The emptiness is architectural. The plain extends for thousands of kilometers in every direction, a continent lifted into the upper atmosphere by some long-ago convulsion that the bedrock still has not forgotten. Yet for all its openness, the place feels *hollow*. The crust is riddled with blowholes and resonant vaults, empty shafts that descend into blackness without bottom, as though the earth beneath has been quietly removed by a patient thief. You can feel it when you walk: the ground booms slightly underfoot, a drum-skin stretched over absence. Density, here, is a joke. The world is mostly gap.

But it is *loud*.

At first, the emptiness tricks you into expecting silence. Then the low end arrives—not through your ears, but through the soles of your feet, up through the femurs, a subsonic hum that lodges in the teeth. It is the sound of industry without architecture. There are no smokestacks, no assembly lines bolted down by hands or minds, no built thing at all—yet the sound is unmistakably that of mass production. Grinding. Venting. The rhythmic compression of bellows. The shriek of steam escaping valves. Somewhere, out in the hollow openness, *refining* is happening.

You see them first as heat-shimmer on the salt-flat, then as a dark, migrating line against the bruised sky. The herds. Fauna overwhelming. They are called, in the old gloss, many things—**Clangswarm**, **Forge-Beasts**, **The Treading Refineries**—but they have no proper name because no one built them and no taxonomy fully holds them. They simply are. Each one is the size of a railway car, low-slung and many-legged, armored in bands of slate-gray keratin and blue-oxidized copper that they have *excreted* and plated onto themselves like ceremonial mail. Their joints are true pistons, lubricated by a silvery non-aqueous resin that drips from their seams and hardens into wire. Inside their barrel torsos, stomach-chambers reach temperatures that can smelt ore. They eat the salt-crust, the feldspar, the veins of native metal sweating from the weathered domes, and they shit pure aluminum, extruded glass fiber, ceramic blanks. They are the industry. The technology is *them*, biological and geological at once, permitted by a physics that has grown permissive.

There are billions of them. Across the million-square-kilometer plateau, their numbers constitute a biological—or biomechanical—mass that should collapse under its own improbability. Yet the emptiness is so total, the scale so merciless, that you can walk for an afternoon and count only twenty dark shapes on the rim of the world, each one trailing a thin banner of white steam. Then, cresting a salt ridge, you might look down into a basin and see fifty thousand of them moving in a slow mill, a single organism of ingestion and production, and you understand that *overwhelming* is not a matter of crowding but of totality. They are the sole owners of this altitude. The only life. They fill the world not by proximity, but by *presence*.

And they are slow. So slow. Their many legs cycle with the patience of tectonic drift. A Clangswarm might take an hour to pass a single salt pillar. Herds migrate not across days but across centuries, grazing spiral paths that erosion has worn into the crust—established grooves, knee-deep polished channels where ten million years of treading have settled the form of the land into a kind of balance. The landscape and the beasts have negotiated with each other for geological epochs. The erosion is settled. The beasts wear the plain down; the plain, in turn, has worn them into their shape—long, low, heat-tolerant, thrifty with motion. Everything moves at the speed of cooling magma.

The heat itself is old. It radiates upward from the hollow depths, leaks from the beasts’ smelter-guts, pools in the salt-basins until the surface shimmers and ghosts appear. But the light—strangely, unnervingly—the light is *normal*. The sun, when it is not flickering, looks like a sun should. It casts shadows. The shadows, however, sometimes lag behind their objects by several seconds, as if the concept of light had to think about geometry before committing to it. Sometimes the shadows fall in two directions at once, then snap back. The sky above the herds occasionally mirrors them perfectly, an impossible flock inverted in the troposphere, not a cloud but a *repetition*, the atmosphere forgetting which side was supposed to be up.

This is the nature of the weirdness here. Reality is not merely strange; it is *broken in a settled way*, like a clock that keeps perfect time but occasionally counts thirteen. Sound travels faster than sight in certain hollows; you hear the steam-whistle of a distant beast before you see its vent open. Caverns that measure fifty meters across on the outside hold, in their black throats, distances that feel like hundreds when you shout into them. The Clangswarm navigate by magnetism and by singing to the blowholes—deep, organ-pipe drones that the hollow earth amplifies into a constant industrial threnody. The sound never stops. It is the aggregated exhale of a billion slow furnaces, a blanket of mechanical breathing stretched over an empty world.

There are no seasons, only thermal tides. There are no rivers, only the drip of slag cooling in the dawn. There are no builders, only the built-who-built-themselves, grazing, refining, extruding, venting, patient beyond measure, hot as ovens, loud as falling mountains, and slow—so terribly slow—as the long turn of a world that has forgotten it was supposed to be anything else.
