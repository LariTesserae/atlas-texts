The heat is the first thing, and it is the last. It does not fall from the sky; it rises from the ground itself, a low-grade geological fever baked into stone that has only just finished being born. You are in the Sump, a depression in the earth’s newest skin, so low that heavier gases settle here and never again mingle with the thinner air above. Thirty miles across, valley-sized, the basin holds a single continuous breath of sulfured, oxygen-thick atmosphere that presses against the eardrums like a warm palm. The elevation is nearly at the floor of the world. There is nowhere lower to go without digging, and even there the rock is hot and soft and newly extruded.

The stone is embryonic. No river has cut it, no glacier has plucked it, no wind has scoured its corners round. The basalt is raw pillow-lava, still carrying the thumbprint wrinkles of its own inflation when it bellied up through the melt. Hydrothermal seeps have built terraces of sinter and travertine in cascading rims, each pool a perfect cup of opalescent water, but the rims are sharp as broken crockery because time has not rounded them. You can press a fingernail into some of the softer precipitate and it will give slightly, like cheese rind. Everything is pre-erosion. Everything is proposal, not revision. The geology is under construction, yet construction has paused at the exact moment of finishing.

Light enters as if through deep water. A stationary deck of cloud—brown, green-gray, the color of old bruises—sits upon the basin like a poured lid. It does not migrate, does not rain, does not thin. Diffuse photons ooze through it and reach the floor in a permanent bruised twilight, enough to distinguish a hand from a shadow but not to read the lines of the palm. Against this dimness, the vegetation glows with a faint phosphorescence: not the dramatic blaze of deep-sea monsters, but a subdued, living suffusion emanating from heat-tolerant bacterial pagodas and the waxy fronds of thermotropic ferns. The light is dim. It is always dim. There is no day, no night, only the steady-state dusk of a world that has not yet decided to rotate toward or away from anything.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not run in channels—there are no riverbeds because erosion has not had time to bite. Instead, it bleeds through the porous young stone, pools in the raw dimples of the pillow basalt, and rises again as steam that does not drift. The humidity is absolute. The air carries micro-droplets suspended in stationary strata, warm mist layered upon warmer mist, so that breathing feels like drinking from a cup that never empties. Condensation beads on every surface, but the droplets do not fall. Some have been elongating toward the earth for centuries, held in the amber of frozen equilibrium, trembling but never landing.

And the life. The density is the first assault. It is impossible to point to a single unpunctuated surface. The vegetation is lush in a mode that predates competition for space; everything grows into everything else. Tiered canopies of black-green ferns interlock at shoulder height, at waist height, at ankle height, creating vertical floors of wet matter. Fungal shelves erupt in flanged fans, each bearing twelve smaller fungi, each of those bearing gardens of algae, each strand of algae threaded through with the filaments of symbiotic moss. Epiphytes have epiphytes. The word “ground” is approximate; the ground is merely the lowest layer of a living mattress that extends downward into the warm mud and upward into the mist.

But the flora is only the architecture. The fauna is overwhelming in the mathematical sense: overwhelming as a tide overwhelms a sandcastle. The air itself is a medium of bodies. Translucent, eyeless, heat-snouting creatures no larger than grains of rice fill every cubic meter, their populations phase-locked into a steady-state suspension. They are not occasional visitors; they are the weather. A stone thrown would find its arc blunted by the sheer viscous resistance of wings and carapaces. They have never evolved eyes because sight is a luxury in this perpetual bruised dimness; they navigate by the infrared topography of geothermal vents and the moist heat of neighboring bodies. Their wings beat at a frequency that has locked into the resonant cavity of the basin, combining with the hiss of un-drifting steam into a single, continuous chord. The sound is loud. It is not a buzz, not a roar, but an organ note held without breath, vibrating in the sternum, unwavering in pitch and volume. It has been exactly this loud since the stone cooled. It will not rise. It will not fade.

Here is the strangeness, the subtle wrongness that permeates the place: nothing leaves, and nothing arrives. The dynamic is arrested. A predator—a soft-jawed, many-legged thing the color of fresh clay—has its mandibles sunk into the flank of a burrower, and both have held that posture long enough for lichen to consider, then abandon, then reconsider growing across their joint flanks, because the lichen too is snared in the balance. Birth and death occur; spawn writhe in the warm seeps, and cadavers soften in the mats. But the rates are in perfect, frozen parity, so the exact number of wings, the precise arrangement of fronds and mist-wisps and suspended droplets, remains invariant. A swarm of midges hangs in a parabolic spray over a pool, each individual beating its wings in place, neither advancing nor dispersing. The pool’s ripples ripple identically, having rippled thus for an age. Time has not stopped, exactly; rather, all gradients have been flattened. There is no uphill for energy to flow, no direction for change to take. The heat leaks from the raw ground and is trapped perfectly by the stationary clouds and the breathing biomass. The oxygen consumed by ten trillion organisms is exactly replaced by the undying photosynthesis of the dim-loving canopy. The scream of the devoured is matched by the birth-cry of the devoured’s replacement.

You could walk for days across the open span of the Sump and the terrain would not alter its character. The same un-eroded ridges. The same mineral pagodas. The same three-layered canopy. The same chord in the chest. No structure has ever been built here; no tool has ever shaped anything; the concept of making is as foreign as the concept of weather. It is pure nature, but nature caught in the act of its own becoming, flash-frozen in raw, wet, biological warmth. It is a portrait of the moment after creation, hung in a dim room, breathing loudly, and forever awaiting the next moment that never comes.
