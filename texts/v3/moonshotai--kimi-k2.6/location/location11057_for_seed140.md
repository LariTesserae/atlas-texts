You do not see it. That is the first rule. Darkness here is not an absence but a presence—viscous, warm, and absolute. It does not merely hide; it consumes. Any photon that dares breach this high redoubt is swallowed before it can strike a surface, devoured by the scattered artifacts that stud the terminal rock like the broken needles of some abandoned god. You are high, impossibly high—near the roof of the world—yet the air does not thin. Instead, it thickens. It is warm, a held breath of geothermal generosity, and so saturated with moisture that it seems more liquid than gas.

The space itself is small. You could map it in an afternoon. It is the size of a nave, a grove, a single chamber hollowed from the oldest peak by erosion so profound that it has passed beyond violence into meditation. The mountain has reached its final form. Stone here is not jagged or even merely smooth; it is *resolved*, worn by epochs into shapes that feel inevitable, almost fetal. Ridges flow into basins with the logic of a water droplet finding rest. There are no sharp edges because the concept of sharpness has been forgotten by time.

And yet this finite stage is mostly empty. Emptiness is the dominant material. You could walk for long minutes, hands outstretched, and meet nothing but the sweating rock. The density of presence is that of a whisper in a vault. It is a near-void punctuated by intense, clustered presences—five, perhaps six, oases of being that huddle against the scattered dark-tech spines.

Those spines. They are the built things. Small. Partial. Occasional. Half-sunk in the flowstone like black thorns. They are not made of matter as your chemistry understands it, or perhaps they are matter compressed into a syntax that physics has yet to parse. Nano or cosmic in scale, though their physical envelope is no larger than your forearm. They drink light. They exude warmth. They are the suns of this blind heaven, though they give no dawn.

Around each spine, life has pooled. The vegetation is lush—obscenely, aggressively lush given the circumstance. Without light, the flora has dispensed with chlorophyll entirely. The plants are black, but a black so total it seems to possess texture rather than hue. Waxy, pneumatic leaves the size of hands cluster in spirals. Tubers swell with viscous, warm sap. Fringes of filament drip with condensed moisture. These gardens do not photosynthesize; they thermosynthesize, chemosynthesize, perhaps draw energy directly from the subtle warping of spacetime that the old tech perpetuates. They grow in directions that almost ignore gravity, spiraling toward the heat-bleed of the artifacts like moths frozen mid-flight. They are slow-growing. A leaf might uncurl across a decade. But in their stasis, they are profuse.

And where the plants clump, the fauna erupts. For a landscape so hollow, so gapped by emptiness, the biological noise is shocking. Each oasis teems. Armored crickets the color of wet slate scrape their legs in unison, producing a dry, rattling thunder that echoes off the polished walls. Amphibian-things, blind and translucent, plop into the mirror pools that gather in the stone’s micro-basins. Their calls are deep, subwoofer booms that you feel in your sternum before your ears name them. Insect analogs whir through the saturated air, their wings loud as playing cards in a bicycle spoke. The abundance is not distributed; it is hoarded. Each island of growth is a crowded metropolis separated from its neighbors by leagues of silent stone. Between the oases, you are alone with the wet percussion of condensation.

Water rules this place gently. It does not rush. It accumulates. The warm stone weeps continuously. Atmospheric moisture—drawn up from below, or perhaps transmuted from the rock itself by the ancient machines—beads on every surface. Droplets the size of pearls form on the ceiling of stone, fatten, and fall in slow, patient rhythms. *Plink. Plink.* The sound is loud, magnified by the emptiness, by the acoustic perfection of the eroded chamber. Water does not carve here anymore; the erosion has ended. Water only *tends*. It polishes. It nurses. It sits in black pools so still they feel like holes punched through the floor into deeper dark.

The dynamics are those of a held note. Cycles turn, but they turn across centuries. The thermal output of the buried needles fluctuates on geological schedules, and the gardens expand or contract by millimeters in response. A single breeding season for the loudest of the fauna might span a human generation. When the water table below shifts—a tectonic yawn taking centuries to complete—the weeping of the walls may increase by a single drop per hour. This is a landscape that has settled into its final form and now merely *maintains*, breathing once every age.

There is something slightly wrong, of course. The weirdness is subtle, almost polite. Water occasionally clings where it should fall, defying surface tension to hover for a moment in trembling, unauthorized spheres. Sounds arrive from the wrong vector; you will hear the *plop* of a translucent frog before you sense the displacement of air that should have preceded it. The darkness has a faint pressure, as if the absence of light had accumulated mass. And sometimes, if you stand very still between the oases, you will feel the smooth stone beneath you thrum—not with machinery, but with the slow, patient satisfaction of a place that has become exactly what it intended to be, and now waits, warm and wet and sightless, for nothing at all.
