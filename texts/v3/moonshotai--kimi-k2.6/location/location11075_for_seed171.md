It is not a forest. A forest presumes trees standing upon ground, and here the ground has forgotten how to be merely mineral. You are perched on the skin of a continent that has only recently finished being born—a high plateau thrust into the cool middle altitudes of the atmosphere, its raw geology still tender, still venting the heat of assembly. The mountains are infants, soft-faced, unweathered. Erosion has not yet arrived; there has been no time. Instead, another force enveloped the newborn stone before the wind could even taste it, and that force was green, or something very like green.

The vegetation is total. It is the floor and the wall and the ceiling. It is the architecture and the substrate, the upholstery and the body beneath. A density so extreme that emptiness has become theoretical. Every cubic handspan is claimed by living mass: compressed peat bales that groan when you step on them; aerial roots laced into thirty-meter trusses that could hold a cathedral’s weight; tubules of cellulose laid down with the precision of nanoscale engineering but never touched by intent, never built. There are no structures here. No ruin, no road, no stake driven by hand. The sophistication exceeds modern technology—photosynthetic arrays that harvest photons at thermodynamic limits, chelating roots that secrete acids stronger than industrial etchants to dissolve fresh basalt, hydraulic networks that span hundreds of miles and compute through pressure gradients—but all of it is grown, not assembled. Biology arrived at engineering before ever inventing the tool.

The elevation is high, yet the air is drunk with water. The cool temperature holds moisture the way a held breath holds smoke. Condensation is the permanent weather. Every surface weeps: the obsidian seams still sweating juvenile gases, the lithified pillows of newly extruded stone, the nano-structured leaf surfaces that draw water directly from the atmosphere and channel it inward with such violence that you can hear the capillary suction as a high, continuous whine. There are no rivers across this land because there is no surface left exposed; the water moves inside things, rushing down translucent vascular highways that parallel the grain of the continent like highways of pale glass. Marshes exist in mid-air, suspended in baskets of root-mass, water pooling in the cupped hands of fungal tangles before it is swallowed whole.

Light arrives diminished and bruised. Beneath the upper mantles, the world is the color of deep lake water at dusk. The canopy—if it can be called a single canopy, and not a solid atmospheric floor—is woven from near-black leaves engineered by evolution to catch every stray photon. Yet the darkness is not absolute. Certain species have grown crystalline silica hairs, fiber-optic raphides bundled like cable, that siphon the captured light downward into the body of the land. Stand motionless in the lower galleries and you will see faint honey-colored threads weaving through the gloom, not glowing themselves but conducting illumination like telephone wires carry voice, lighting internal cavities where spores ripen in ambered isolation. It is dim, but it is navigable. The darkness has a texture, wet and fibrous.

And the sound. With no fauna—no insect drone, no birdcall, no scratch of claw—the silence of animals is absolute, which makes the vegetal roar all the more annihilating. It is deafening. The sound is not an event but a medium, a pressure you move through. Billions of stomata snap open and closed with the percussive rattle of automatic gunfire, exhaling vapor that forms its own weather systems above the plateau. Water screams through microscopic channels under titanic vascular pressure, a white-noise wall that fills the skull. Thermogenic seed pods split along engineered fault-lines, detonating like popcorn in heated cavities. The youngest mountains, still flexing beneath their shroud of biomass, groan in infrasound that travels up through root cables and resonates in the hollow cylinders of fallen pillar-tubes, producing chords that roll for days across the dense acoustic field. The wind does not howl; it strikes millions of resonant culms and booming bark-flutes, and the continent becomes an organ pipe the width of a country. You feel the sound in your teeth, your sternum, the fluid of your inner ear. Earplugs would be ornaments. There is no quiet. The land is shouting its own name, continuously.

It is active, restless. This is not a still life painted in moss. The ecosystem is in violent, deliberate motion. Roots etch fresh geology with audible scratching, digesting the raw stone before erosion can claim it. Clouds assemble directly from the transpired breath of the canopy, rise, cool, and collapse back as drizzle caught by the same surfaces that spawned them. Hydraulic pulses surge through the great highway roots in slow peristaltic waves, shifting nutrient loads across distances that would span seas. Spores do not drift; they are pressurized and fired in golden volleys, impacting hostile tissue with wet thunks. The mat beneath your feet, were you to stand still long enough, would shift: inflating with fermentation gases, cracking, settling, groaning as new layers of organic masonry are laid down atop geology too young to have its own character.

That is the strangeness of the place, the moderate but persistent weirdness that unsettles the horizon. There is a sense that you are inside something that has not decided whether it is a landscape or a single organism. The hexagonal packing of the mainstem columns suggests design, yet it is only efficient growth. Water occasionally moves upward through certain tubules with such ferocious capillary draw that it seems to defy gravity for whole kilometers, until you realize it is only physics pushed to a biological extreme. Time feels thick here, perhaps because the sound pressure is so constant, or because the absence of animals has removed the familiar metronome of day and night, leaving only the slower pulse of geology and sap.

At the edges of the plateau, where the newborn rock is freshest and hottest, the plants have not yet fully closed their grip. You can see the raw material there: serpentine slickensides still bleeding mineral brine, tuff beds unsoftened by rain because no rain has ever directly kissed them, pillows of glassy basalt exhaling steam into the chilled wet air. But the roots are already reaching, already secreting, already enclosing. Erosion will never get a chance. The stone will be consumed, encrypted, translated into lignin and pith before the slow processes of weathering can even begin.

And so the world stands: a cool, high, dripping vastness packed to suffocation with life that has outpaced the inorganic, a continent-sized voice thundering in a register of water and cellulose, dimly glowing with piped sunlight, growing its own future over raw ground that has barely cooled from the forge. Nothing built it. Nothing runs across its floors. It simply lives, loudly, densely, technically, and without end.
