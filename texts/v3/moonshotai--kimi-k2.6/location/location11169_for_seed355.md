It is not night here. Night implies a day that has ended, a sun elsewhere, the possibility of dawn. This place has simply never admitted light. The darkness is old, settled, a mineral fact like density or mass. You do not see it so much as press against it with your other senses—the skin of your forearms registering the shape of nearby masses by their radiant heat, the wet air carrying scent and pressure instead of color. And the heat: it is a low, constant fever, the temperature of blood that never cools. The air is too thick to inhale quickly; you must drink it in sips, warm and supersaturated, so heavy with moisture that it condenses on every surface in a continuous, silent weeping. Water is not a feature of this landscape; it is the medium. It rises from below, drips from above, gathers in every depression, sheets across terraces in films too shallow for ripples, too hot for stillness. The water table breathes just beneath the crust of the world.

You stand in a basin. The elevation is low enough that you feel the curvature of the earth as a hand pressing down on the top of your head, a vast geological bowl cupped under a lid of absolute dark. The scale opens around you not as sky but as breadth, a regional sprawl that extends far past the reach of thermal sense. There are presences out there—masses, geometries, volumes—but they are scattered, isolated, separated by gulfs of steaming emptiness. The density of things is low. You are alone in a space built for multitudes.

And it is built. That is the first impossible fact.

At first you mistake them for formations: the pale ramparts, the repeating colonnades, the domed halls with their oculus holes open to the dark, the grid of avenues that run straight for kilometers before doglegging at angles that feel wrong in the gut. But formations do not have cul-de-sacs. Hot springs do not align themselves in boulevards. The longer you stand here, the more undeniable the urban plan becomes. This is a city. A metropolis of terraces and aqueducts, plazas wide as lakes, arcades that shelter invisible marketplaces, towers that rise in orderly tiers. There is infrastructure here—channels, catchment basins, intersections, districts—but no technology. Zero. No tool marks scar the pale stone. No ash speaks of fire. No wheel, no lever, no hammer. The city was never made. It was grown, deposited, accreted from silica-laden water over epochs so long that the broken physics of this place had time to organize chemistry into civic architecture.

The material is sinter, travertine, opaline mud: the pale, cauliflorous stone of thermal springs, here hardened into geometries that mimic intention. The erosion is established. These forms have existed in their wrongness long enough to weather smooth, to find equilibrium. Corners that point in unnamed directions have been worn blunt by winds that seem to arrive from last week. Balconies that overlook courtyards mathematically behind them have shed their sharp edges in patient, grain-by-grain surrender. The reality breakdown is not chaos; it is the settled form of this world. Gravity tilts differently from block to block. A street descends gently into the earth and emerges, with no transition, from the ceiling of a vaulted gallery, the perspective shift so gradual that the mind accepts it as a civic feature rather than a fracture in space. Water flows up certain staircases, compelled by local gradients that have been stable for ten thousand years. Reflections in the hot pools show angles the eye does not witness. It is weathered. It is in balance. It is simply wrong in a way that has become natural.

Vegetation is nearly absent. A few ghost-white films of extremophile lichen cling to the cooler flanks of the silica spires, so sparse they register as stains rather than life. And there is no fauna. None. No echo of wings, no insect tread, no fish in the boiling channels, not even the microbial froth of a healthy spring. The ecology is purely chemical. The dynamic processes are geological, thermal, hydraulic. The springs breathe in a slow rhythm, pressure building and releasing over hours, days. Mineral-laden water continually deposits new microns of stone, lengthening an avenue here, raising a tower there, sealing an old channel and opening a new one. The city is still growing at the pace of rock, a metropolis expanding its suburbs in geological real-time. Steam rises in columns that fold and merge with the regularity of deep breath. Condensation cycles paint the surfaces fresh every hour. Hot mud bubbles in distant pools, thick and silent. It is an ecosystem of heat and mineral, predator and prey replaced by saturation and precipitation, the hunt replaced by the flow.

Sound is scarce. The springs do not gurgle; they exhale in subsonic frequencies felt in the molars and the sternum. Water sheets over the terrace steps with a hiss so faint it is indistinguishable from the blood’s own rush. The mostly-quiet is absolute enough that you can hear the moisture condensing on your own skin, the soft *tap* of a hot droplet falling from a cornice into a deeper pool. But there is no voice to answer. The city’s activity is a silent metabolism.

What are the rules? Here, light is not the carrier of information. Heat is. Navigation is done by the gradient of warmth, by the pressure of humid air against the face, by the infrasonic maps throbbing up through the soles of the feet. Here, space is civic: the layout of streets and plazas influences probability itself, the avenues offering paths of least resistance not only to walkers but to causality, to the drift of moments. The past does not pass; it accumulates, deposited in layers like the stone itself. And here, the boundary between water and architecture is permeable. The aqueducts are not built to carry water; they are the scars of water’s passage, channels left behind by currents that have long since shifted, now serving as roads for the condensate that replaces them. The city is a fossil of its own hydrology, an urban plan drawn by fluid dynamics in a reality where the laws have agreed, reluctantly, to mimic a civilization that never existed.

You could walk for days. The scale is open, a valley-city that sprawls to every horizon, each district separated by steaming plazas of cracked, pale mud. There is always another terrace, another silent arcade, another hot pool reflecting a darkness that goes on forever. It is empty. It is waiting. It is growing, millimeter by millimeter, in the dark and the wet and the heat, a technological city without technology, a reality that has broken so thoroughly it learned to build.
