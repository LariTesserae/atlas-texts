You come upon it in a narrowing—a thermal pocket no larger than a drowned ballroom, embedded high in a ridgeline that the maps refuse to name correctly. The altitude is severe. The air is thin and papery, each breath scraping like a dried leaf across the tongue, yet the heat wraps the place in a dense, mineral-weighted blanket. It is the kind of hot that feels structural rather than climatic: not the blazing of a sun, but the patient exhalation of something vast and buried, pressed upward through strata that have not yet decided whether they are geology or machinery.

There is no water. None. Not in vapor, not in memory. The dryness is absolute, a zero-aqueous chemistry that should render the space dead. And yet the vegetation is lush, almost offensive in its vigor. Five—perhaps six—individual organisms dominate the chamber, spaced wide apart in a deliberate, scattered congregation that leaves the floor mostly bare. Each one is a *slag-bloom*, and they are magnificent. Their trunks rise like the fused ceramic walls of blast furnaces, chalk-white and threaded with dendritic silver. From these, fronds unfold in layers of verdigris and rust, leaves of hammered copper-thin tissue that flake and regenerate in slow, tidal cycles. They do not photosynthesize, or if they do, they metabolize something stranger than light. Their roots plunge into hairline fissures in the floor, drinking condensed metal vapors—cadmium, perhaps, or some salt that has never existed in the periodic tables you know—drawn up by capillary action in a medium that is dry as algebra. At their crowns, they fruit with clusters of berries like cooled obsidian or ruby glass, fat and glossy with internal heat.

Everything here is emerging. The world is embryonic, unweathered. The floor is a tessellation of hexagonal basaltic plates, each the size of a sleeping figure, and their edges are knife-sharp—no erosion has licked them smooth. They look freshly extruded, as though a die pressed them from the mantle moments before you arrived. Indeed, the geology behaves like industry in the absence of industry. This is not a ruin. There are no built structures, no artifacts, no hand of intention. And yet the place is industrial to its core. The hexagonal plates are pistons in the most ancient sense: every few hours, a subterranean surge of pressure forces one upward by a hair’s breadth, a tectonic breath in miniature, settling back with a subsonic *thrum* that you feel in your molars rather than hear. Along the walls, mineral-laden vapors condense into spiral forms that resemble drill shanks, lathe-cut screws, or the rifled barrels of cannons never fired. They are natural, of course—merely chemistry obeying corrupted parameters—but they carry the unmistakable silhouette of scaled production.

Reality here is broken, badly so, though the damage is quiet. The light—clear, normal, the color of late afternoon—enters from no discernible aperture. It simply occupies the upper volume, casting shadows that fall at conflicting angles. You have two shadows, sometimes three, each one lagging behind the others by half a second, as if time itself is stuttering through a stack of translucent frames. Distance is negotiable. A slag-bloom fifty paces away can take an hour to reach, while another, seemingly farther, arrives beneath your hand in five steps. The far wall is visible in perfect detail—veined with brassy intrusions that bleed upward in defiance of gravity, dripping toward the ceiling in honey-slow globules of molten alloy—yet when you walk toward it, the wall retreats at precisely your pace, preserving the pocket’s intimate scale against your intrusion. The space is contained. It will not be traversed, only attended.

It is mostly silent. The soundscape is a 1.2 hush: the faint expansion-sigh of cooling hexagons, the occasional *ping* of a copper frond contracting in the dry heat, the almost inaudible hum of the deep piston-plates maintaining their gradient. There are no animals. No insects stitch the air. No footfalls but your own disturb the mineral quiet, and even your footsteps seem absorbed by the hot, dry atmosphere, arriving at your ears muted and delayed.

The rules of the world are simple and inhuman. This is a heat-economy. The slag-blooms are not producers but *regulators*, capturing thermal differentials and storing them in their glass-fruit batteries. The brass dripping up the walls is not decoration but wiring—conductive plumbing for a magnetic field that herds the heat from place to place in slow, unthinkable drifts. Nothing is consumed; nothing is wasted. It is a closed loop of energy and mineral, patient beyond reckoning. The dynamic is glacial. A new frond might uncoil over a month. A fresh spiral gear of condensed zinc might complete its first turn in a year. The place is under construction, still wet from the mold of its creation, and it is in no hurry to finish.

You stand at the center, turning slowly, and the chamber seems to turn around you. The heat presses against your face like a hand. Six slag-blooms pulse with stored warmth, their verdigris canopies casting multiple overlapping shadows that slide across the raw hexagonal floor. Above, the brass droplets pause in their upward fall, quivering, waiting. Nothing built this. Nothing tends it. It is simply what happens when a fragment of reality gestates in too much heat, too high up, with too little water to soften the dream: a factory without founders, a garden without water, a mechanism teaching itself to be born.
