At first you mistake the sky for stone. Then the stone thins, and you realize you are underneath something—not buried, but held in suspension beneath a shell of translucent earth. The ceiling is a stretched membrane of mica, alabaster, and fired clay, hundreds of meters above you in places, thinner in others, so that the outside daylight filters through in broad, diffuse panes. The light is ordinary. It has the color of afternoon. It falls across the floor in pale geometric shapes that shift only when the sun moves overhead, giving the space the impossible feeling of an open valley trapped inside a geode. There is sky here, or something close enough to it: a bright vault that lets you see for miles. The scale is regional. You could lose a town in the middle distance and find only its echo.

The warmth does not come from that filtered sun. It rises from below, from the floor, from the fissures that pulse along the raw ridges. The ground is embryonic. It has not decided what it wants to be. Basalt pillows extrude from cooling vents, obsidian flows lace between them like frozen black water, and silica precipitates into fragile balconies that will collapse by evening and begin again by dawn. The erosion here is pre-formation; nothing is worn down because nothing has existed long enough to be worn. The geology is a child still learning its limbs. Ridges thrust up overnight. Steam hisses through cracks that open and weld shut with the rhythm of breath. You are standing inside a landscape that is dreaming itself into existence.

It is loud. Not the catastrophic roar of a collapse, but a continuous, layered percussion. The stone groans as it stretches. Water drips, not from a consistent source but from the condensation that beads on the warm ceiling and falls in scattered, fat drops—a kind of mineral rain. Ephemeral streams carve gullies into the newborn rock, flash-flooding for an hour, then drying to rust-colored stains. You hear the snap of cooling lava-skin, the hiss of steam striking cooler air, and underneath it all, the endless, mechanical chatter of chitin on stone.

Because the fauna here is overwhelming.

There is no plant life. None. Not a lichen, not a moss-thread, not the faintest smear of green. The dampness has nothing rooted to nourish. The warm light falls on bare rock uninterrupted by leaf or blade. And yet the space is saturated with living presence. The density is such that the air itself feels occupied. Near the thermal vents, the ground is not visible beneath the armor-plated swarms. They are a single organism in the aggregate, though individually they vary: low, crab-like lithovores the size of dogs that scrape minerals from the basalt with mandibles of tempered cermet; winged nymph-stages that cloud the light in pulsing, iridescent sheets; serpentine heat-worms that thread through cracks, their bodies segmented with silica plates. They crawl over each other. They fall from ledges in clattering cascades. They breed in the warm pools, they die in the flash-floods, they fossilize almost before they cease moving, their bodies contributing to the very strata of this emerging place. The fauna is the weather here. They are the wind, the rain, the soil.

In the center of the vast chamber, the swarm achieves a kind of city-state. A million million presences per cubic breath of air. The sound of their movement is a grinding, clicking, buzzing storm, a bazaar of biological noise so constant it becomes a pressure against the ears. You could walk for ten minutes and never set foot on bare stone, only on the interlocked backs of scuttling, indifferent things. They are eating the world as it forms, and excreting it anew, their digestive chemistry precipitating minerals that accrete into the odd columns and filigree towers rising from the swarm’s center. They do not have a king. They have momentum.

And scattered among this turbulence, the built things—small, faint, almost apologetic. A cairn of chipped obsidian on a rise that has only existed since yesterday, the stones lashed with dried gut. A shelter woven from the shed carapaces of larger creatures, dome-shaped, primitive, the work of hands that understood leverage and tension but not iron. A line of stone axes left beside a thermal pool, their edges still sharp. These are not ruins. They are waypoints, left by something intelligent enough to pause here but not enough to stay. The technology is early, pre-metal, a matter of knapped flint and bone levers and cords twisted from the sinew of whatever here passes for game. Whoever built these marks was passing through an instability, trying to rest inside a landscape that will not hold still.

The weirdness is subtle but insistent. It is in the way the swarm ignores you unless you stand still for too long, at which point they begin to treat you as a mineral deposit, testing your boots with their mandibles. It is in the instant-fossilization of the dead, their bodies turning to stone so quickly that the geology is partly composed of yesterday’s generation. It is in the light—ordinary daylight, yes, but filtered through rock that should be opaque, creating a quality of illumination that feels remembered rather than seen.

Everything is in motion. The dynamic is turbulent to the edge of chaos. A ridge you walk across may be a fumarle by evening. The swarm-front shifts like a flash flood, pouring into a newly cooled basin, consuming the mineral bloom there, then retreating as the heat builds again. Condensation rains down in one district while a steam explosion deafens another. There is no center that holds, only a thousand local frenzies that average out into a kind of screaming stability.

You begin to understand that this place is a nursery—perhaps for the stone, perhaps for the creatures, perhaps for both simultaneously. It is damp enough to prevent the dust of a desert, warm enough to keep the chemistry frantic, light enough to sustain the visual madness of the swarms, and closed enough beneath its shell to trap the noise so that the roar reflects off the translucent ceiling and redoubles, amplifying the sense that you stand inside a drum being beaten by the earth’s own unsteady heart.

The primitive structures are not abandoned forever. In the flicker of a shadow between passing swarms, you see a figure—if it is a figure—kneeling by the thermal pool, using a stone hand-axe to crack one of the smaller armored creatures, working with a patient, ancient motion while around it the world churns and builds itself anew. It does not look up. There is no time to look up. The stone beneath its knees is still warm from its birth, and the swarm is already returning, a dark wave cresting the ridge, loud enough to drown thought.
