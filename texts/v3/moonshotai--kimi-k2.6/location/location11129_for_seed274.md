You find it by descending through a seam in the lowland flats, a crack barely wide enough to turn sideways, which gives out not onto another surface but into a chamber that seems to have been held in place by the pressure of the earth above it. The air meets you first: a wall of wet heat, dense as steeping tea, carrying the taste of pennies and burnt quartz. It is a cathedral in scale but not in grandeur—more the size of a modest nave, though here the nave has been stuffed to bursting with mineral flesh.

Every sculpted inch is occupied. There is no floor, only a continuous field of rimstone dams packed so tightly against one another that the pools they contain are no larger than cupped palms, saucers, shallow soup bowls. Water stands in all of them, each surface so untouched by breath or current that it serves as a flawless lens, doubling the space in silent reflection. The walls weep. Not dramatically, not with the music of cascades, but with a patient, dermal seepage that beads and fattens and slides down sinters that have built up over millennia into melted-looking pillars, organ pipes, and fused draperies. The ceiling is low, enough to make a tall visitor bow, and it too is encrusted with pendant growths that seem to have been arrested mid-drip, silica-rich water thickening their tips without ever releasing them. It is a landscape that has finished growing but refuses to collapse.

There is no green. No fern frond, no lichen bloom, no algal slick. Life as the surface understands it has never taken hold here, or was long ago outcompeted by chemistry. The stone carries its own muted palette: opalscent creams, the amber of old fat, greys that shift to rose where the heat concentrates. If you look closely at the waterline of certain pools, you may see a film like gasoline rainbowing the surface, but this is mineral sheen, not bacterial mat—no living signature disturbs the purity of the place.

The light is dim and warm, arriving from a narrow rift somewhere overhead that admits a single slanted column of surface-day, tawny and dust-thick, which the steam catches and holds in suspension like breath on a window. Beyond this beam, the rest of the chamber glows with a low phosphorescence born not of life but of heat-retaining silica, a dull ember-radiance that makes the wet stone seem to perspire its own faint luminosity. Shadows are soft-edged and do not move. There is no wind to flinch them, no flicker. The temperature holds at the low edge of fever-warm, constant from floor to crown, a bathhouse without doors.

And it is quiet. Not the electric, ringing silence of deep winter, but the muffled, woolen hush of air so saturated that sound drowns in its own weight. Speech falls flat. Footsteps are swallowed by the humidity. Occasionally a drop detaches from a sinter tip and enters its pool with a *plit*, yet even this is absorbed so instantly, so without repercussion, that the ear deletes it, editing the sound back into the equilibrium. The pools never overflow. The seepage never slackens. Evaporation matches input; deposition matches dissolution. The chamber inhales and exhales on a geological metronome so precise that no human sense can detect the motion. It has been exactly like this for longer than memory permits.

Scattered through the density are signs of someone else having once understood this stasis. On a broad, flat shelf of flowstone sits a single stone pestle, its working end polished to a soft gleam by patient hands. Nearby, a cairn of three obsidian flakes has been balanced with deliberate care atop a rimstone dam, not marking a grave but perhaps a boundary, or an offering to the water itself. A line of river stones forms a narrow causeway threading between two pools, primitive engineering to cross a space too packed to step through blindly. There are no metals. No fired clay, only sun-dried mud that the ambient warmth has baked to the hardness of brick, preserving a single handprint, fingers splayed, pressed into what was once a wet bank near a larger basin. These artifacts do not decay. The steam prevents rot; the minerals encase; the changeless air does not weather them. They look as though their maker set them down this morning, though the obsidian’s edge has achieved a desiccated bluntness that suggests centuries of idleness.

Fauna here is a question rather than a presence. Once, perhaps, a pale thing came through—the shed husk of a cave beetle, no larger than a rice grain, rests on a dry ledge, its coat of chitin slowly being replaced by silica film, becoming itself a kind of sculpture. A thread-thin worm, translucent as saliva, might be seen threading through the water of the deepest pool, but it is alone, and its movements are so minimal, so attuned to the currentless equilibrium, that it appears to be drifting in its own held breath. There are no webs, no spoor, no song.

The rules here are those of a sealed jar. Water carries silica in solution, kept liquid by the earth’s low fever; as it reaches the surface it cools by a fraction, depositing its burden in imperceptible layers, only to warm again at the source and redissolve what lies nearest the vent. It is a treadmill. Nothing gains. Nothing loses. The pools are saturated to the precise molecular threshold where chemistry has agreed to a truce. Even the steam stands in stratified layers, warmer below, cooler above, locked in place. Time passes through the rift overhead, marked by the slow wheeling of the light-beam from afternoon gold to evening rust, but inside, the chamber is time-invariant. It does not age. It does not renew. It simply *remains*, packed to its absolute limit with water and stone and the memory of primitive hands, a warm, wet, lifeless hollow breathing its own unchanging atmosphere in the darkening earth.
