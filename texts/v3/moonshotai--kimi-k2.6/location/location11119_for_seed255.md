The first thing that strikes you is not the dark, but the noise. Even with your eyes open, you see nothing, yet the blackness around you is *populated*—it rings, ticks, splashes, and drones. It takes several minutes of stillness for your ears to map the space. You are standing on something level and slightly yielding. Not earth. Clay. Hexagonal tiles, hand-pressed, each one bearing the whorled impressions of whoever shaped them. Water lies in the slight concavities, lensing the darkness, so that when you shift your weight, silver ripples speak across the floor. The air is cool, a cellar-cool that seeps into your collar and never leaves, and every surface is beaded with condensation, as though the world here is sweating.

This is a made place. There is no natural substrate, no stone that has not been lifted, no clay that has not been wedged and stamped. The floor extends outward in every direction, a basin sunk low beneath some unseen ceiling, vast enough to hold a town yet packed so densely with construction that you must move through it as through a forest of artifacts. Pillars rise in irregular copses—stacked drums of soft limestone, each one turned by hand on some ancient pivot, their surfaces cross-hatched by stone adzes. They support nothing you can see; they simply stand, shoulder to shoulder, narrowing the paths between them. Between the pillars are walls that stop at chest-height, great jars the size of huts with mouths open to the dark, screens of mineralized reed through which you could poke a finger, and heaps of raw chalky rubble that are not debris but *material*, embryonic, waiting. The erosion here is backward: nothing is worn away. Instead, the geometry is *emerging*. New ledges of lime are just beginning to lip over the edges of channels; fresh accretions bulge from corners like tumors of architecture. It is a geology in gestation, built rather than born.

The sound has a source. Above, somewhere in the absolute dark of the vault, water condenses. It does not rain—there are no storms, no sheets—but the ceiling weeps. Millions of drops find their way down through fissures in the invisible roof and fall onto a landscape of ceramic. Every pillar capital is cupped to catch water. Every wall is crowned with a trough. The floor is ribbed with runnels, and where two runnels meet, a resonant bowl has been placed to amplify the impact. The resulting percussion is deafening: a randomized, relentless drumming that shifts in texture as vessels fill and overflow. Some bowls are large and produce hollow *gongs*; others are small and *tick* like insect mandibles. Water spills from one level to the next in thin, urgent streams, carving nascent grooves into the unfired clay, rewriting the terrain even as you stand there. It is loud enough to feel in your teeth.

You walk, and the rules of the place assert themselves. Light does not belong here. What little exists is the wet gleam of a slug’s trail, the pale gleam of gypsum, the momentary flash of a droplet catching some bioluminescence too faint to illuminate anything but itself. Navigation is done by echo and humidity. Cool air wells up from lower passages; damp air sinks from the weeping ceiling. The place breathes, but it is the breath of hydrology, of thermal gradients in stone.

There is no plant life. Nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes. The only biology is rare, rudimentary, and strange. You hear them before you see them: a sound like wet felt being dragged across grit, slower than a heartbeat. The Tenders—vast, bone-white gastropods, their shells broad and low, shaped unsettlingly like the adzes that scarred the pillars—move through the lower channels. They are scarce; hours might pass between sightings. They feed on the mineral film that lines the watercourses, and where they pass, they excrete a cementitious slime that hardens in the cool air, becoming mortar, becoming wall, becoming pillar. They are the only builders left, and their technology is the technology of the gut, of patience, of accretion. They are the reason the place is still *active*, still unfinished, a dynamic system of water and mineral and slow, mucous labor.

The weirdness nests in the scale. Everything here was made by hand, by primitive craft—bone spatulas, stone pounders, wooden paddles, simple metal perhaps in a few sharpened edges—but the cumulative result is a subterranean region, a hand-wrought valley. The tool marks are everywhere, readable as fingerprints. Yet the proportions are wrong. Steps are too deep for human legs; archways are too broad for human shoulders; the great jars, if they stored grain, would have fed armies of the giants. It is a place built for something, or by something, that measured time and space differently.

You stand at the edge of a new formation, a column just beginning to rise from the floor where a slug has circled the same spot for a decade, its trail building up like a tree ring of concrete. Water drips into the hollow at its center. The sound is a clear, high *ping*, still solitary, not yet joined by the chorus of overflow. It will be joined. The channel to your left is already deepening. The tile beneath your foot is newer than the one beside it, pressed with a slightly different thumbprint. The darkness hums with construction. Nothing here has finished becoming itself.
