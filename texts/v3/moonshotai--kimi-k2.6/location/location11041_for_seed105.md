You do not surface. There is no surface. Only the upward dark, pressing and endless, and beneath you the true deep.

You hang in a mountain range that happens to be drowned. These are not gentle hills but proud, weathered old peaks—the high backbone of a plateau that once, perhaps, shouldered against sky, and now shoulders instead against leagues of black water. The elevation is palpable. You feel it in the layering of thermoclines, cold upon colder, and in the way the silt falls like slow snow from greater heights above, settling on ridgelines that stand thousands of meters above the abyssal plain. The stone here is established, settled into its form like bone into socket. Erosion has not worn these mountains away; it has negotiated with them. Gorges run with smooth, geological patience. Scree holds its angles with the dignity of long habit. Basalt and schist predominate, shot through with veins of something older, denser, almost fused. It is a terrain that has existed in its own state for long enough to forget alternatives.

The water is absolute. It is old water, tired of the memory of waves, heavy with dissolved salts and minerals that keep it liquid even as the cold tries to make it glass. The temperature hovers at the lethal edge—near enough to freezing that the chill does not sting but saturates, entering slowly, a cold with tenure. It is dim. No sun. No bioluminescence to break the seal. The darkness is photographic, starless, a nothing that presses against the eyes.

And yet the place is lush.

Wherever the stone fractures and bleeds warmth—where hydrothermal vents puncture the frigid dark—the vegetation arrives with the inevitability of water finding lowest ground. It is not green. Without light, color is a chemical luxury. The plants are pale, rubbery, enormous: ghost-cylinders of cartilage-white trunk, veined with hollow channels, from which drape curtains of filamentous matter like the Spanish moss of a drowned forest. They are not scattered evenly. Density here is low, sparse. For miles there may be only bare stone, mineral snow, and the slow procession of currents. Then, sudden as a fever, a grove. A cathedral of sessile life. The trunks cluster around a vent’s exhale, their root-masses knotting into tangled superstructures that rise five, ten, twenty meters from the seafloor, drinking heat and chemistry. They sway not in wind but in the convection of cooling water, their hollow cores singing as the current passes through.

There are no animals. None. No fin flicks at the edge of vision. No worm threads the silt. No brittle star, no drifting jelly, no crab to scuttle behind a rock. The silence of fauna is total, an absence so complete it becomes a presence. You listen for the click, the snap, the biological murmur, and receive only geology and botany. The kingdom of the animal is annulled.

Which makes the volume all the more unbearable.

The Shroud is loud. The sound has no living voice, but it has endless instruments. The tectonic ridge beneath the plateau groans in its lowest register, a bass note so sustained it seems less heard than suffered, vibrating in the teeth and ribs. Against this, the hollow stalks of the pale groves act as pipe organs—thermal currents shunted through root-masses and volcanic glass split the drone into chords, shrieks, cavernous hoots. Vents open with the thunderclap of collapsing gas bubbles. Others throttle closed and wheeze. But the strangest noise belongs to the brine.

Here, the cold and pressure conspire to produce a phenomenon that the world above would call impossible: rivers of hyper-saline water, so dense with mineral load that they do not easily mix with the surrounding sea. They flow as viscous, glassy sheets down the mountainsides—not ice, but a gelid, shearing phase, darker than the dark around them. Where these brine-rivers grind against stone, they produce a constant granular roar, like millstones turning in a cathedral cellar. Where they touch the pale vegetation, they freeze it into eternal posture, etching the filaments into stillness. The sound is everywhere. It fills the open, regional scale of the place. It bounces off weathered escarpments and travels for miles through the dense medium, louder than storm, louder than swarm.

Scattered across the high ridgelines and passes are the structures.

They are small, isolated, never forming villages—only single walls, cairns, rough arches of stacked and cemented matter. Primitive. Pre-metal. They are built of root-stone: the calcified, silicate-laden residue of dead pour-trees, compacted by centuries of pressure and the unconscious hydraulics of living root-masses seeking purchase. No hand placed them. No animal mind conceived them. They are the slow architecture of botanical instinct, hydro-engineering performed without intent. A wall channels a warm current into a grove. A cairn marks the site of a vent that died ten thousand years ago. A ring of stones cups a brine-pool, altering its slide. They are weathered, worn, half-dissolved at their edges, yet they persist—tools without a tool-user, scattered marks in the emptiness.

But the true rule of this place, the law that binds its active processes into coherence, is stranger than mute construction. It is this: *the landscape remembers in sound.*

The calcified root-structures are porous, layered, acoustic fossils. When the tectonic bass strikes the proper frequency, an empty cairn will suddenly emit, with perfect fidelity, the ghost-hymn of the grove that built it. The weathered walls replay the collapse of ancient vents. The water itself, so old, so dense, so saturated with mineral resonance, transmits these pressure-ghosts across vast distances without loss. Time is not erased here; it is archived in the stone and replayed by the next seismic shift, the next thermal surge. The plateau is an instrument and a library. It recites its own history at deafening volume.

So the Shroud remains, dynamic and active despite the cold. Vents birth and die. Brine-rivers creep an inch a year, etching new scars into established stone. Groves migrate toward new heat, leaving behind skeletal root-dolmens that will, in another age, sing their absence. Mineral snow falls forever. The dark is absolute. The noise is total. And in the sparse, frigid openness of this high drowned place, nothing swims, nothing hunts, nothing cries. There is only the growth, the grinding, the groaning, and the endless, drowning roar of a world that has replaced life with loud, vegetable memory.
