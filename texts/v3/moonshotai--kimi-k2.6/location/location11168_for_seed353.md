The heat comes first. It rises from the ground itself, a sustained exhalation from deep within the earth, so that the high plateau feels less like a surface and more like a lid held over a slow-boiling pot. You stand at elevation, yes—high enough that the thickest layers of atmospheric dust lie below you—but there is no relief in the altitude, only a stale, pressing warmth that seems to radiate from the stone, or from whatever has replaced the stone. There is no water here. Not in any form you could drink or name. Humidity is a dead concept; the air is desiccated, carrying instead the mineral tang of hot iron and a sweet, resinous bitterness that clings to the back of the throat like smoked sugar.

It is dark. Not merely night, but a darkness with depth, a blackness that feels architectural. The sky, if there is one, is drowned in soot or dust or the simple absence of any stellar body. What little light exists—0.2 on any conceivable scale—comes from the landscape itself: thin fissures of magma-orange glowing between seams of compressed growth; the dull cherry-red of overheated pistons thrusting silently in the black; occasional sparks, blue-white and brief, coughing from somewhere deep in the morass. These are not stars. They are the symptoms of industry.

And yet this is a garden. Or rather, it is *all* garden.

The plateau is filled—packed to saturation—with vegetation, but vegetation that has forgotten the ancient contract with water and sunlight. These are thermosynthetic macro-organisms, silicon-lignin hybrids, sulfur-drinking colossi that have replaced every substrate, every floor and wall and foundational stone, with their own bodies. There is no natural ground left. The built environment and the living environment have achieved total synthesis. You are not standing on soil; you are standing on a mat of fused root-chassis, a pavement of calcified heartwood and conductive pith, compacted by millions of years into a near-flattened plain. Erosion here is not the work of wind or rain—there is neither—but the patient, geological grinding of growth against growth, of ancient trunks worn smooth by the friction of their own expansion, polished like river stones by the ceaseless, millennial rubbing of branches and pipes.

Because this is also a factory. An industrial ecosystem. The tech is unmistakable: smokestacks rise above the canopy, but they are hollow xylem towers, reinforced with excreted basalt-fiber and capped with vents of porphyritic bark. Conveyor systems run between the boles of trees that are themselves smelters, their cambium layers generating electrical potentials that arc silently from leaf to leaf. Gears of compressed amber and rusted tannin turn in thoracic hollows; boilers made of suberin and ceramic sap belch steam that instantly vanishes into the thirsty dark. The mechanisms are all grown, all alive, all furiously metabolizing the heat and minerals seeping up from the plateau’s underlying wound.

The density is absolute. There is no path, no clearing, no interval between presences. Structures press against trunks; trunks have absorbed machinery; machinery has been overgrown by new structures in layers so thick that the original ground plan is lost to a archeology of living tissue. The scale is open in the sense that you can perceive the regional sprawl—the valley-wide, town-sized expanse of the plateau stretches before you—but you cannot walk freely through it. You would have to climb, squeeze, or cut. And cutting would be loud, if anything here were loud.

But it is not. The sound parameter of this world is a hush so profound it becomes a physical weight. Despite the turbulence, despite the frantic dynamism, the silence is nearly total. The dense, packed biomass absorbs vibration. Pistons thrust without thud. Geysers of vented gas open their mouths and make no cry. Collapse, when it comes—and it comes often—is soft, a muffled, wet surrender of cellulose, like a body falling into deep moss. What noise remains is infrasonic, a sub-bass drone felt in the molars and the marrow, the collective heartbeat of a million tons of growing, straining, manufacturing flora.

And it is turbulent. Chaotic. Hyperactive. The landscape is in a state of constant, violent becoming. A smokestack may extrude twenty meters of new growth in a single day, its walls veined with fresh copper-analogue, only to calcify, fracture, and be reabsorbed by its neighbors by nightfall. Tumors of architectural ambition swell and burst. Pipeline-roots migrate across the flattened floor, seeking hotter veins, leaving behind necrotic corridors that scar over within hours. This is not erosion as decay; this is erosion as frenzy, a primordial, oldest-stable-state that has settled into a final form only in the broadest sense—the plateau remains, the heat remains, the system remains—but locally, nothing is still. It is an anthill energy without the ants. A bazaar with no merchants, only the stalls growing over one another.

There are no animals. Zero fauna. No insects stitch the air. No birds. No scavengers pick the fallen. When a factory-tree dies, it is consumed by acid-secreting lichenoids that are themselves part of the same superorganism. When a gear wears out, it is digested and reforged in the belly of an adjacent bloom. Death and manufacturing are the same process. The ecology is a closed circuit of plant-as-substrate, plant-as-worker, plant-as-product.

The weirdness seeps in at the edges of comprehension. Spaces do not keep their dimensions reliably. A root-chamber you pass through may, on second glance, be larger inside than its exterior suggested, the geometry warped by centuries of uneven growth. Time moves strangely in the heat; the turbulent dynamism seems to stutter, so that a wall may appear to grow before your eyes in a sudden lurch, then freeze for an hour, then convulse again. The darkness hides nothing because there is nothing to hide—only the endless, silent, boiling churn of the living foundry, burning hot, bone-dry, ancient beyond reckoning, and utterly, verdantly, industriously alive.
