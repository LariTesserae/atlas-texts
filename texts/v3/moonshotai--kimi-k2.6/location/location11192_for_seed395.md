The heat arrives in layers, each one closer to the skin than the last. You stand at the rim of a basin so broad that its far side has sunk below the curvature of the world, and the air you breathe has already crossed three weather systems to get here. This is a low place—not merely below the mountains, but pressed down against the warm membrane of the planet itself, where the crust is thin and young and still flexing in its sleep. There is no sea, no river, no stagnant pan to catch the light. Water, in any form you could cup in your hands, is a rumor from another kind of world. What exists instead is a vast biological enclosure, a city without masons, continent-wide and old only in the waythat swarms are old.

Call it the Great Thicketworks, or call it nothing at all. From the rim it resembles a metropolis drowned in sage and bone: avenues, spires, stacked terraces, and vaulted shadow-canals, all grown rather than assembled. The builders left no blueprints because they are still building. The erosion parameter here is embryonic; the geology is pre-form, still announcing itself in fresh scree and unweathered mineral flutes that push up through the floors of the avenues like elbows through cloth. Before the stone can smooth itself under wind or water, the colony seizes it. Saliva and root-gel and calcified exhalation cement the raw grit into walls, into thermal chimneys, into habitable density. Nothing is permitted to finish eroding. Nothing is permitted to remain empty.

The scale deceives the senses. Because the density is absolute—packed to the threshold of saturation—you might mistake a given district for the whole world. But the districts repeat. They repeat for thousands of miles. A single boulevard of packed clay and masticated cellulose might run straight for six hundred miles, flanked by spires that carry their own microclimates on their backs. The packed nature of the terrain means there is no unclaimed ground. Between the primary structures, secondary organisms have filled the interstices with hanging gardens of succulent flesh: aloes swollen with stored chemistry, lithops clustering like tiled roofs, air-roots draping across the gaps in braided cables. The vegetation is lush not in the manner of rain-fed jungles, but in the manner of a body entirely covered by skin. Nothing is bare. Every surface perspires with chlorophyll or wax.

It is warm. The warmth is structural. The basin traps its own exhalations, and the living masonry breathes—inhaling cool air through base-vents, exhaling it heated through chimney-stacks a thousand feet above the floor. This respiration generates a weather of its own: soft, dry thunderheads that form at noon and never drop rain, only static and the occasional desiccating downdraft. The light is normal, achingly so, cutting hard shadows between the spires at dawn and burning the corridors white at midday. But the corridors themselves remain cool, because the urban fabric is designed by biology to modulate. Capillary action, evaporation from waxy leaves, the thermal mass of a billion small bodies—these are the technologies at play. There are no tools here. No metal, no fire, no wheel. Chitin serves where steel should be. Mucilage serves where mortar should be. What looks like engineering is only appetite organized across time.

And it is quiet. The density ought to produce cacophony, but the acoustic rules are different in a city made of flesh and clay. The thick, rammed-earth walls swallow sound. The narrow streets deflect it upward into the thermal wash. What remains is a granular hush, the collective susurrus of a billion small mouths working: the scrape of mandibles grooming mineral from stone, the soft pop of succulents sealing their pores against the noon, the barely audible click of a gecko’s toe-pads navigating a ventilation shaft. Fauna is abundant. It is everywhere and invisible. Lizards the color of cured brick lie motionless on ledges. Serpents thread the hollow walls, sensing vibration through the masonry. Predators the size of a forelimb drift across the rooftops on membraned wings, but they do not cry out; hunting here is done by patience and thermal shadow. The only percussion is the rare collapse—an aging spire, too heavy with fresh accretions, slumping into its own substructure in a muffled roar of dust that the neighboring walls muffle before it can travel a mile.

The ecosystem is in constant motion. This is the rule of the place: nothing may rest, nothing may stockpile, nothing may finish. Raw geology emerges from the warm below, the colonies seize it, the vegetation colonizes the colonies, the fauna trim the vegetation, and the weakened substrates dissolve back into the scree that will be seized again tomorrow. Predator-prey cycles here are measured in vertical inches: the ascension up a new wall, the pursuit across a bridge of woven root-fiber, the ambush from a gallery still damp with the saliva of its construction. Water, the absent king, is hoarded inside the living bodies themselves. Every succulent is a cistern. Every insect is a sealed bag of chemistry. The city drinks its inhabitants, and the inhabitants drink the city, and the loop is tight enough that no rain need ever fall.

You could walk for years and never find an edge, only the gradation from one district to another—from the tall, windy chimney-stacks where the light is amber and the epiphytes hang like banners, down into the deep warrens where the warmth pools and the stone is newborn, still shedding the smell of its formation. You would never find a ruin. Ruins require a finished state, and this place has never achieved one. It is permanently under construction, permanently embryonic, raw geology midwifed into architecture by hunger and warmth. It is utterly normal. There is nothing here that could not be explained by enough time, enough heat, and enough life pressed shoulder-to-shoulder in a basin too wide for maps.
