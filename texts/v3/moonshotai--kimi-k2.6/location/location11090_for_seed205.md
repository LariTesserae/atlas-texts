There is a pressure here before there is a shape. Stand still on the Lignin Steppe and your ribs will know the place before your eyes do—a steady, granular thrum against the sternum, as though the air itself has mass and friction. The sound is not an event but an atmosphere. It is the accumulated drone of thirty billion wings, the creak of continental fiber flexing in the heat, the ultrasonic shrieks of glass-boned hunters diving through strata of humid air, all compounded into a deafening ordinary roar that never dips below a shout. To speak, one presses a palm to another’s throat and feels the vibrations. To listen for danger, one puts an ear to the warm ground and reads the tremors through the substrate.

There is no stone here. That is the first deception of the landscape. The elevation is high—three vertical kilometers above the theoretical datum of a long-vanished sea—but you are not standing on mineral earth. The Steppe is the weathered husk of a single, archaic organism, or perhaps a clone-forest that reached unanimity eons ago and fused into a continental carpet. Its body plan has long since collapsed into substrate. What looks like a bone-white plain is compacted frass and molt deposited over geological time, cured by heat and pressure into a kind of chitinous leather. What looks like basalt mesas and wind-scoured arches is phytolith—silica extracted from groundwater and excreted into structural scaffolding, harder than flint, veined with dormant cambium that still pulses faintly with sugary sap. The erosion is established, settled. These forms have existed in their current posture for longer than recorded memory. Their curves are soft. Their hollows are acoustic temples. They are in balance with the wind that made them.

And yet the vegetation is total. There is nothing that is not plant-derived. Even the air carries the vinegar tang of lignin breaking down in the ultraviolet. Even the dust is epidermal. The sparse density of the landscape is a matter of scale, not absence. Between the great Resonators—those fluted towers of silica-wood that rise every hundred kilometers or so—there stretches an emptiness so complete you could navigate by distant horizons for weeks. The flatness is interrupted by nothing but the slow rise of a root-buttress or the slender harp-string of a vine bridge spun across a minor rift. But look up, and the sky is weathered out by life. The fauna is overwhelming in the truest sense: it fills the vertical volume above the plains in such densities that noon can be dimmed to twilight by the passing of the Motes, those clouds of thumbnail-sized filter-feeders whose trillionfold wingbeats generate their own thermals and static charge. Beneath the crust of the Steppe, tunnel-symphonies of pale detritivores thread the compacted substrate like capillaries, their gnawing a constant subsonic rumble. At certain hours, the surface itself appears to liquify as harvest-swarms breach, devour airborne fallout, and submerge again, leaving only polished channels behind.

It is hot. The dark lignin and sun-cured chitin of the plains drink thermal energy and hold it with geological patience. Even at this elevation, where the atmosphere should thin and cool, the Steppe retains heat like a lung. Thermal devils dance between the Resonators, carrying spores and shed sparkling scales in spiraling columns. The air shimmers. The silica faces of the towers gleam with an almost molten luster, and to touch them at midday is to risk a sear. Yet it is a damp heat, curiously. The water score is low—there are no rivers threading toward an absent sea—but moisture is stolen from the air with obsessive efficiency. The Resonators themselves generate weather. Their hollow cores, heated by day, contract at dusk and draw vapor upward from the subterranean aquifers of the root-mass. This vapor nucleates around airborne particulates—skin flakes, spores, the very dust of old leaves—and forms thunderheads that belong to no larger system. Rain falls in discrete, spectacular cells, hammering the leather plains with drops the size of eyes, pooling in ephemeral lakes that vanish into the porous substrate within hours. Between storms, condensation beads on the glassy bark of the towers each dawn, running in silver filaments into cup-shaped growths that hoard the damp. Travelers drink by pressing their lips to these living vessels, or by harvesting the translucent, water-storing bladders that swell at the joints of certain parasitic vines.

The light is normal. The sun traverses a sky that shifts from thin gold at the horizons to a bruised cobalt at zenith. When the Motes are not eclipsing it, the illumination catches the phytolith outcrops and fractures into spectrums that paint the undersides of the passing flocks in transient rainbows. It is beautiful in the way a cauldron is beautiful—vivid, active, intent on its own processes.

What the builders have left is scattered and swallowed. At the base of a Resonator, you might find a cairn of notched silica flakes, each groove tuned to modify the local acoustic field—a primitive technology of resonance, pre-metal, carved by hand with tools of resin-hardened glass. Further on, a windbreak woven from shed wing-membranes and sap-stiffened cord may flicker in the thermal wind, its occupants long since merged with the harvest-swarm. These are not cities. These are scabs on a body too large to notice them. The builders—whatever lineage of opportunistic symbionts they belong to—do not conquer the landscape. They scarify it lightly, listening to the Song that moves through the silica veins.

Because the strangeness is this: the Steppe is not dead biomass. It is slowed-down life. The Resonators are organs. The thunderheads are exhalations. The deafening roar is its metabolism made audible. Sound propagates through the phytolith nervous system at the speed of chain-reaction, carrying chemical signals from one continental edge to the other over the course of a season. The overwhelming fauna are not invaders; they are microbiome, pollinators, antibodies, digesters. When the Stridulators in the deep hollows synchronize their chirping to a certain frequency, the walls of the spires exude a corrective sap. When a browsing shadow—one of the great, many-legged silhouettes that migrate between towers—scrapes away the weathered outer layer, new growth flashes emerald in the wound, racing to seal the breach before desiccation claims the moisture within.

You can walk for days and see nothing but the sameness of the amber plain, the distant promise of a Resonator’s fluted peak. You can believe you are alone. Then a shadow will pass over you, cast by a flock so vast its leading edge is lost in atmospheric haze. The ground will begin to vibrate with the tunneling chorus. The air will taste of vinegar and ozone. The heat will press down like a hand, and the moisture in your own lungs will condense on the inside of your throat, borrowed back by the air. The Steppe does not need you. It is a continent that has become a body, vast enough to possess its own physics, old enough to have settled into itself, generating its own damp breath and its own deafening song, sparse in silhouette but absolute in its green dominion, weathering onward in the high, hot, humming absence of stone.
