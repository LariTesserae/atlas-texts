You stand at a height where the horizon is a geological lie. The plateau extends farther than weather itself; storm systems are born here, mature, and die without ever reaching the edge. It is a tableland lifted so high that the sky above it is a deeper blue than elsewhere, and the cold is not an absence but a presence—a thin, crystalline gas that fills the lungs with the sensation of inhaling broken glass. Your breath does not cloud; the air is too desiccated for that, yet the stone underfoot is slick with moisture, and the cold seeps upward through your boots with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be.

But the cold is not the strangest thing. The strangest thing is the age of the stone.

This is an infant continent. The bedrock here has only recently finished cooling from its magma state, and in many places it is still cooling. There has been no time for erosion. There are no rounded hills, no smoothed river stones, no valleys carved by patience. The terrain is all knife-edge ridges, glassy extrusions, and basaltic columns that have not yet been toppled by frost or root. The rock is black, iridescent, and so fresh that in crevices you can still find the sulfuric breath of its birth condensing into yellow crystals. The plateau breathes. It is still settling, still contracting as it cools, and the ground hums with a subsonic tension—a constant, active dynamic of thermal adjustment. Fissures open and seal overnight. The elevation is not a finished fact but an ongoing argument between the planet’s mantle and its crust, and the crust here is winning, still rising by millimeters per year, still pushing the sky higher.

And it weeps.

Water is everywhere, but it is not organized into rivers. With nowhere to sink and no established channels to follow, it simply emerges. Every cliff face is a weeping wall. Every flat expanse is underlain by a water table so shallow that it seems the land is sweating. Water condenses on the cold stone, beads into droplets, and runs in sheets across the un-eroded surfaces. There are no deep lakes, only millions of mirror-bright films pooling in the first depressions ever formed on this surface. No mighty rivers, only first-generation rills cutting their inaugural grooves into virgin rock, carrying away glassy silt that has never been sediment before. The wetness is atmospheric, constant, a post-rain world where the rain never needed to fall because the air cannot hold its moisture against the cold stone. Touch any surface and your fingers come away wet. The water is young, too—oxygen-poor, mineral-saturated, tasting of iron and sulfur and possibility.

Against this rawness, the built environment.

It is urban, yes, but urban at the scale of a continent with the density of a desert. The metropolitan region—if it is one city—covers millions of square kilometers, yet you can walk for days without seeing a structure. When you do, it arrests the eye: a tower, slender as a needle, rising half a kilometer from a ridge, its material not steel or concrete but something that resembles fused ceramic grown directly from the bedrock at a molecular level. The technology here is beyond modern, but it wears its transcendence quietly. There is no glowing neon, no hovering chrome, no violation of physics for spectacle. The towers are spaced thirty, fifty, a hundred kilometers apart, connected by causeways that span the raw terrain without visible support, their surfaces absorbing the sound of any transit. The architecture does not conquer the landscape; it negotiates with it. Some structures are hollow spires that vent the plateau’s residual geothermal heat, accelerating the cooling of the crust in controlled patterns. Others are vast, low domes that capture the weeping water and filter it into reservoirs, their surfaces the color of old bone against the black stone. The city is infrastructure at a planetary scale, designed not to crowd but to administer a wilderness that is still being born.

The vegetation is sparse because the soil has not yet been invented. There is only rock, water, and cold. What grows here are the most ancient and patient pioneers: crustose lichens in maps of orange and sulphur-yellow, spreading millimeters per century across the glassy stone, breaking down nothing because there is nothing to break down—only mineral to stain. In the lee of the towers, where the beyond-modern tech has warmed the ground by a few critical degrees, stunted groves of something like willow cling to the seepage, their roots finding purchase in cracks that opened last Tuesday. They are sparse, yes, but they are the first colonizers of a world still deciding what it wants to become.

Yet the fauna is abundant.

Life here does not follow the temperate rules of grass-eaters and predators. The base of the food chain is chemosynthetic and lithotrophic: bacteria that thrive in the fresh mineral water weeping from the cooling rock, drawing energy from the planet’s own residual heat rather than a distant sun. These feed vast, slow herds of grazers—creatures the size of bison but with the metabolism of reptiles, their hides thick and folded like geological strata, their mouths adapted to scrape lichen and filter microbes from the water films. They move in extended lines across the plateau, following the seepage patterns, their dark shapes visible for miles against the pale stone and the black ridges. Predators move among them, lean and white-furred, but they hunt rarely; the cold demands conservation of energy, and they are built for waiting. The animals are quiet. They do not bellow or howl. They communicate through subsonic vibrations transmitted through the cooling crust, or through scent markers left in the water films. The abundance is not noisy. It is a silent profusion, bodies moving at the edge of vision across the vastness, filling the space between towers with a living density that belies the emptiness.

The light is normal. The sun crosses a sky that is, by turns, the clear violent blue of high altitude or the flat pearl of condensing mist. When clouds come, they are low and fast, dragged by the plateau’s own weather systems, which obey no coastal logic. Rain is rare; the moisture prefers to cling and seep. Snow falls, but it does not accumulate for long—the ground is too warm in places from below, too actively cooling, and the snow melts into the ever-present films of water, adding to the weeping.

Sound: The world is mostly quiet. The thin air carries little. The weeping walls make no noise. The herds move like shadows. The towers emit no hum; their engineering has solved friction and waste heat at scales that leave them silent. Occasionally, there is the sharp crack of thermal contraction—a rifle-shot echo as a new fissure opens, or a column shears free for the first time in its short life. Occasionally, the sky speaks with thunder from a storm that will never leave the plateau’s bounds. But these are punctuation marks in a long silence, the quiet of a place that is too large for echoes to return.

The rules of this place are simple and severe:

First, nothing here is finished. The land is still becoming itself, and all habitation is provisional, even if the provisions are made by engineering that manipulates matter at the nanoscale or the cosmic. The city is not built upon the plateau so much as it is built *with* it, a partner in a formation process that will outlast any structure.

Second, water is the true topography. It does not follow gravity so much as it follows temperature gradients, condensing on cold faces, evaporating from warm seeps, constantly negotiating the surface. To navigate here is to read the wetness.

Third, space is the dominant species. Distance is not an obstacle but an ecological niche. The city exists in the gaps. The animals exist in the gaps. The mind expands to fill the silence between presences, and the vastness teaches a different sense of scale, where a neighbor is someone within a day’s walk and a district is a territory the size of a small nation.

Fourth, the technology is old enough to be geology. It does not float or glow with impossible light. It simply does what geology cannot yet do—span, shelter, channel—and in doing so, it waits for the stone to catch up.

This is a place where a continent is being raised, and civilization has arrived early to help it learn what it wants to be. The vastness is not empty. It is expectant.
