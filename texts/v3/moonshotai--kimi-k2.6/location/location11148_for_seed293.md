Approach is from above, though there is little left to climb. The land has worn itself down to a gesture, a near-flattened palm pressed against the warm skin of the world, and the depression at its center is less a valley than a final exhalation held in place by habit. The basin is small. You could walk its diameter in the time it takes a thunderstorm to pass, yet inside those few hundred paces the space behaves like a room with walls of haze and root. It is bounded. The ridges that once roared with mountain speech have been planed to a hum, rounded off by erosion so patient it seems almost kindly, an old nurse turning stone to dust, dust to silt, silt to the living substrate that now carpets the bottom. The erosion has finished its work. This is the landscape’s last form.

The air inside is warm, wet, and heavy—brined with transpiration. It does not move in drafts but in rolls, viscous packets of humidity that carry the scent of lignin, crushed coriander, and something older, like voltage. Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. There are no streams, only the behavior of water: it beads on every flatness, trickles down vertical planes that should not be porous, gathers in the footprint-basins of the structures as dark, quiet pools. It condenses on the underside of arches and drops upward now and then, drawn by capillary fields that no longer obey the era in which they were planted. The ground is marsh, but the marsh is a membrane, a woven dermis of root-hairs and aerial threads那首 fused over eons into a continuous, gently undulating floor. It is all plant. There is no mineral pride here, no stone asserting its geology. Even the silt has been digested, incorporated, made chitin and pith. The substrate breathes.

And rising from this skin, sparse and immense, are the presences. Perhaps seven. Perhaps nine, depending on the hour. They are not trees. They are architecture that has forgotten it was ever a seed. Cathedral-scale, hamlet-scale, they stand as living halls: fluted boles hollowed by their own cambial dreams, chambers stacked within chambers, galleries spiraling up into light, walls pierced by oval windows that dilate and contract with the thermal needs of the interior. The city, if it is a city, exists in the negative and positive space alike—rooms in the trunks, bridges spun from braided lianas that span the courts, aqueduct-fluted bark-conduits that carry water from node to node. It is layered construction, but the layers are annual rings made frantic by the turbulence within. Lower floors are darker, compressed, ancient; upper galleries are pale, new, still weeping sap, and sometimes they touch, so that a street from yesterday is digested into a wall by tomorrow, and a balcony extrudes like a shelf fungus over the course of an afternoon.

The density is sparse. Between these woody citadels there is so much room, so much balancing emptiness. You can stand in the center of a clearing and feel the hush of distance, yet the clearing itself is only the space between lungs. The grove is total; it owns everything, but it owns it lightly, like a spider owning the gaps between web-threads. The emptiness is alive. The air is thick with drifting meristematic spores, with symbiotic mites, with motes of cellulose that catch the light like dust in a sunbeam through a barn door. The fauna have made the emptiness their own.

They are abundant. This place is a bazaar of bodies. Swarms that are half-insect, half-immunological-response move in refracting columns. Phosphorescent grubs, thumb-sized, tunnel through the air as if it were humus, leaving trails of scent-topology that linger for hours. Something like crabs but made of lacquered moss scrabble along the bark-highways, harvesting the extruded surplus. Above them, sheets of translucent flyers—veined membranes that might be leaves that learned to hate the ground—pulsate in warm updrafts, their wing-beats adding to the sound. It is loud here. Not deafening, but ceaseless. The hydraulic screech of sap moving through conduits wide as drainpipes. The percussive crack of hyper-lignification, wood hardening in seconds rather than years. The wet click of fauna, the buzz, the drone, the omnipresent sub-bass hum of the deep tech running its endless routine.

Because there is technology here, though no one has built anything in millennia. It is beyond-modern, nanoscale, cosmic in its indifference, grown into the cellular walls so thoroughly that the distinction between engineering and hunger has dissolved. The structures do not merely stand; they field gravity with a senescent grace, so that water sometimes climbs to feed upper chambers, and a dropped stone may fall slightly slower near the heartwood cores. Probability itself seems negotiated in the groves. The dynamic is turbulent, Solaris-like: in the corners of courts, matter accretes briefly into shapes—staircases, bowls, even the simulacra of artifacts—before the root-mind recognizes them as foreign and un-dreams them, dissolving them back into pulp. Sections of the urban mass rot and birth simultaneously, a hyperactive anthill of creation and destruction governed by an old, old consensus. The grove is in convalescence and labor at once, settled into its final form yet unable to stop editing itself.

And in all this, the light is almost normal. A warm, gold-green diffusion, neither glaring nor dim, filtered through high canopies of translucent leaf-fans and the condensing mist that never quite becomes rain. Shadows fall mostly where expected, though if you sit still for an hour and watch carefully, you may notice that your own shadow, cast by a sun that stands at zenith, has developed a fringe—an uncertainty, a faint secondary silhouette leaning toward the nearest trunk, as though the body cannot decide where the floor truly is.

This is the strangeness of the place. Not overwhelming, but intimate. The weirdness is 1.9: present enough to make the skin prickle, not so total that reason collapses. A staircase that was solid bark at dawn may, by evening, have become a vascular gallery pulsing with warm lymph, yet it will still carry your weight. The fauna do not fear you because they do not recognize the category. They crawl across your shoulders with plant-damp feet, harvesting salts or songs or dead skin, and move on. The water in the pools, when cupped in the hand, reflects the sky accurately but holds the reflection a half-second too long, as if the surface were consulting something deeper.

The grove is an end-state. It is low, warm, wet, ancient, bounded, small, and utterly consumed by vegetable intelligence. It has no citizens, only symbionts. It is urban but empty of intent, settled but never still. It does not need to be understood. It needs only to continue its long, loud, beautiful adjustment, eroding and building in the same breath, plant and machine and body and town, until even the concept of ending has itself been digested, and the weeping water simply carries on, carrying nothing, perfectly full.
