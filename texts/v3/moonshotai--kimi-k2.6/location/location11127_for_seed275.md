The space does not feel underground until you think about it. It is a single, continuous cavity—some twenty kilometers at its widest—blasted and melted from a deep stratum of anhydrite and potash beneath an unseen surface desert. The walls are raw. They still carry the glossy, terraced scorch-marks of plasma borers, and along their flanks the stone has only begun to remember that it is allowed to crack. Sharp drifts of vitrified slag pile at the foot of the cliffs like black sugar. Nothing has been worn smooth. The geology is embryonic, hours-old by geologic reckoning, a landscape still in the trembling instant after its own birth. You could cut your hand on the air here; everything has a new edge.

And yet the light is ordinary.

Normal. Flat. White.

Suspended from the far-distant ceiling on trusses as thick as bridge cables is a sparse lattice of arc-luminaires: inverted ceramic cones the size of houses, burning with a steady, sourceless radiance that mimics a cloudless noon. They are spaced wide apart—density here is low, everything is low—so that between their cones of light the air cools into translucent shadow, a blue-grey dimness rather than true dark. The illumination does not flicker. It has no warmth. The temperature holds at a constant coolness, somewhere around 7 degrees, faintly refrigerated, so that exhaled breath ghosts silver before vanishing into the dry mineral air. There is no moisture to hold it. Water is a trace element here, a contaminant. The atmosphere is anhydrous, thirsty, a dry solvent of nitrogen and trace sulfur compounds. If you were to bleed, your blood would not soak in. It would bead on the ground like mercury, and the ground would flinch.

Because the ground is alive.

There is almost no distinction between terrain and vegetation. The floor of the entire basin is a single phytomass—a carpet, a substrate, an organ—of non-aqueous life. Call it pith. It covers every meter: waxy, pale, accordion-pleated in ridges half a meter high, colored in tones of unpolished ivory, ash-grey, and the dull green of oxidized copper. It is not lush. It does not bend or drip or smell of loam. It is semi-rigid, rubbery, architectural. It metabolizes mineral salts drawn up from the raw slag, digesting rare earths and excreting silica nodules that glint like half-buried ball bearings. The whole mass moves. In slow, peristaltic waves, the ridges inflate and deflate, circulating gases and nutrients across kilometers. A traveller standing still for an hour would feel, through the soles of their boots, a rhythm like the breathing of something too large to have a face.

This is an ecosystem of total vegetation. The plants do not merely occupy the space; they are the space. But density remains sparse. Between the presences, there is still much emptiness.

Scattered across the living floor, separated by half-kilometer corridors of undifferentiated pith, are the structures of the settlement. It is urban. It is industrial. And it is profoundly open. Perhaps forty major complexes exist in the whole basin—a refinery here, a dormitory block there, a silo farm on a low rise, an extraction manifold crouched in a shadowed bay. They are organized, gridded to a municipal plan, but the grid is stretched across the scale of a small sea. The buildings themselves are hybrids: alloy frameworks poured and bolted by machines, but threaded through with lignified towers that have been not built but grown—trained pith hardened into load-bearing resin, expanding and contracting subtly with internal pneumatics, so that the architecture breathes. Balconies are coated in living cuticle; workers tread softly, because the material bruises. Between the structures, elevated mag-lines run on ceramic piers, and pneumatic capsules glide above the phytomass with a sound like silk being drawn across glass.

Technology here is scaled production, but it is quiet industry. The refineries operate in electric silence. Harvesters—low, broad platforms on magnetic treads—roam the open spaces, excising metallic nodules with hot wires. There is no grinding of gears, no diesel bellow. The machines thrum at frequencies felt in the molars rather than heard. Exhaust is vented as dense, odorless gas that pools in designated sink-pits, where the pith dutifully inhales and reprocesses it. It is the cleanliness of the process that feels strangest: no soot, no slag-heap, no roar. Only a hiss of equalizing pressure, the click of a cooling jacket, and then the vast, waiting quiet.

Dynamic as the place is, it does not make noise. The arc-lights cycle on a thirty-hour rhythm, dimming to a twilight purple before swelling back to noon-white, and with each cycle the pith responds—blooming pores, releasing spore-dust, sealing nodules. The herds move. Fauna is abundant. The most common creatures are the Lintels: hexapodal, pale, broad-footed grazers that drift across the phytomass in loose congregations of hundreds. They are silent. Their feet press into the waxy ridges with a soft, rubbery *whup... whup... whup*, absorbed instantly by the living ground. They have no voices; they communicate, if they communicate, through infrasonic rumbles lost below hearing. Above them, translucent spar-things drift on the cool convection currents—gas-buoyant bladders trailing filament tendrils that comb the air for spores. They do not call out. Even the geology is active: microfractures snap in the distant walls as the cavity settles, a sound like distant knuckles cracking, then gone.

Weirdness here is not an event but a rule. Water is not the solvent of life; it is an intruder, a poison that makes the pith fold away in recoil, leaving pale scars. The light is a constructed sky, but there is no sun, no weather, no rain. The creatures have no eyes adapted to darkness, because darkness does not truly come—only a scheduled dimness. The boundary between the engineered and the grown is so eroded that a refinery silo and a lignified resin tower are indistinguishable at forty meters, both breathing, both warm to the touch, both product and organism. The ecology runs on chemistry that would kill surface life, and in this sealed, cool, light-filled hollow, it thrives with the calm assurance of a dream.

You can stand on a catwalk at the edge of the extraction zone and look out over six kilometers of uninterrupted living floor. The pith ripples. A herd of Lintels moves beneath a distant arc-lamp like pale stones rolling on a tide. A harvester, far off, glows cherry-red at its cutting edge, then passes into shadow. The air tastes like ozone and hot wax. It is a town, a valley, a young world, still damp from the mold that made it, quietly turning raw mineral into breath.
