The water is not merely present; it is the containing fact, a warm, dark broth that has replaced the sky. You are submerged in a basin so low and broad that its edges are lost not to distance but to the upward curving dark. This is an inland sag, a warm sea trapped in the geological hollow of an ancient landmass, fed by sluggish rivers that carry no silt of light—only tannins, rot, and dissolved stone. The temperature hovers at the threshold of fever, a geothermal exhalation from the basin’s deep seams, warming the column from below while the blank lid of the surface traps it in. There is almost no light. A score of photons, perhaps, in an acre; not enough to betray a hand before a face. The black is structural, a quality of the fluid itself, thickened by centuries of leached humus until it drinks illumination like a sponge.

And yet, the place is built.

Rise from the featureless silt of the lowest floor and your rising body will find—again and again, repeating with the uncanny rhythm of urban planning—the architecture of the *Sedimenters*. They are, or were, colonial organisms: tube-dwelling, calcifying, inexorably communal. Over spans that make stone seem impatient, they precipitated calcium from the warm water and constructed a metropolis of hexagonal spires, arched viaducts, and interlocking courts. None of this is technology. There are no tools here, no wheels, no fired clay, no logic external to biology. The city is a secretion, a reef, an exoskeleton of the commons, weathered now into settled forms so established that age cannot be told from permanence. Erosion has been at work long enough to soften every acute angle, to round the balcony-edges of the tower-apartments, to fill the lower avenues with drifts of pearl-grey sand. The erosion is in balance. What crumbles from the crowns supplies the foundations. The place has reached its shape and now maintains it, breathing in and out through slow chemical tides.

The scale is regional. You could swim for hours through the boroughs of the dead colony and never cross the same plaza twice. The towers stand three to four stories high in the measure of air-breathers, though here “stories” are measured in strata of occupation. Between them run corridors of shadow so narrow that a drifting body must turn sideways, so dense with layered presence that the water itself feels crowded. Density is not merely a condition of the stone; it is aquatic. The black soup teams. There are the *Clickers*, shrimp-like, armored in manganese-dark carapaces, colonizing every internal nook in clattering aggregations thousands strong. There are ribbon-eels that wind through the hexagonal windows, never hunting in haste, their bodies phosphorescing only in the infrared, invisible to dark-adapted eyes. Filter-worms extend fans from portholes. Isopods the size of thumbs graze the facing walls in sheets, a rustling carpet of mandibles. Above, in the water-column streets, drift globular predators like living lungs, pulsing through the avenues on silent jets of displaced water. Fauna is abundant, lavish, every cubic arm-span owned. Yet the overwhelming impression is not frenzy but compression—all these lives layered, folded, stacked ten-deep in time-share, a vertical favela of warm flesh and carbonate stone.

Vegetation, by contrast, is nearly absent. A few pale, thread-like filaments of chemosynthetic algae cling to the uppermost spires where warm currents buffet most aggressively, but they are sparse as whiskers on a drowned cat. No green here. No leaves. The base of the food web is microbial, a snow of organic particulate that falls in slow, eternal blizzard from the sunless surface, eaten and re-eaten before it touches the lower wards.

The quiet is immense. Sound is 0.8—mostly absent—because the blackwater swallows vibration. You hear your own pulse, and beyond that, the low tectonic groan of a tower settling into its own weight, the hiss of a million microscopic mouths filtering, the *pop* of a pressure bubble releasing from a thermal vent. Predation happens as a soft concussion, a sudden absence of clicking where a Clicker once was. No cries. No songs. The darkness and the warmth and the density together enforce a monastery of near-silence, a hush broken only by the wet crunch of calcium giving way under the patient abrasion of time.

The place is active, dynamic in its stillness. Warmth drives convection cells that wander the avenues like slow storms, carrying scents of mineral and spawn and decay in loops and eddies. The Sedimenter city breathes with them. Upper towers warm, outgas, and shed flakes of nacre; lower chambers silt up and are evacuated by burrowers, who open new routes that redirect tomorrow’s current. Predator-shadows drift on schedule. The population pulses with spawning tides that turn the water milky with gametes, a cloud-burst of genetics that coats the ancient walls before settling into the crevices. Growth and erosion hold equal reign. Nothing is static; everything is certain.

There is an unusualness to it, a weirdness held at low frequency but impossible to dismiss. It is the eeriness of geometry without mind. The towers align too well. The cul-de-sacs echo municipal planning. You find what can only be called a plaza, a deliberate clearing between six equal spires, and the urge to name it Civic Center is biological in the observer. The fauna navigate by it. The eels know the arterial channels; the isopods evacuate certain “streets” in synchronized migration as if obeying traffic laws emergent from nothing but current and appetite. The city behaves like infrastructure because it *is* infrastructure—just not built by hands. It was grown, vomited, crystallized by the patient hunger of organisms that needed neighborliness the way coral needs calcium. The result is urban in every respect except intent. A settlement-scale fact. A nature that has, through the long pressure of warmth, darkness, and drowned quiet, coincidentally reinvented the neighborhood.

You could rest here, floating neutrally buoyant in a boulevard of warm black water, and feel the presences around you—clinging, filtering, waiting, digesting—layered like tenants in a tenement that has stood since continents were differently arranged. You would not see them. You would only know, by the subtle displacement of heavier water, that the borough is occupied, that the walls are alive with ten thousand quieted hearts, and that the city, having never known light, has no need of it to continue its ancient, thriving dark.
