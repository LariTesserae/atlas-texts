You come upon it suddenly, a pocket in the cliff-face no larger than a chapel nave: perhaps forty feet in its longest dimension, twenty in width, and open to the sky only through a crooked cleft that angles toward the pole. The elevation is extreme enough that the air carries a permanent silver thinness, a cool that does not merely touch the skin but seems to originate inside the bone. You are above the tree line in the broader sense, yet this crevice sustains a world of green so dense that moving through it requires the effort of pushing through stacked, wet textiles. It is a single room hewn not by hands but by a recent violence of the earth, a space so freshly emerged that the stone has not yet learned the habit of roundness.

The rock is basalt, extruded and cooled in a geologically recent paroxysm, and it fractured into hexagonal columns that now lean against one another like pipes dropped in a warehouse. Their surfaces are not weathered but *broken*—conchoidal fractures glint with a vitreous luster, edges keener than a flaked blade. There has been no time for erosion to soften them. Dust does not accumulate; there is barely any soil. What little substrate exists has been manufactured on-site by the vegetation itself, a thin paste of acid-leached stone and organic fiber clinging to fissures. The columns stand or lie tumbled, shoulder-to-shoulder in places, separated by gaps no wider than a forearm. The density is architectural, oppressive, layered: crustose lichen rivets itself directly to the glassy stone; above that, a mattress of sphagnum and haircap moss swells two handspans deep in the troughs; from the vertical seams, ferns and clubmosses unfurl in such profusion that their fronds interlace across the voids, creating a secondary ceiling of green. Trailing lichens hang in gray-green skeins from the higher columns, swaying when the wind moves through, soft as stripped insulation in a derelict mill.

Light here is a rumor. The cleft above admits nothing direct; what illumination exists is a diffuse, submarine gray filtered through perpetual altitude-cloud, barely sufficient to distinguish the black of wet stone from the black of shadow. It is a darkness you feel on your retinas, a weight that makes the air seem viscous.

Water does not fall here as rain. Instead, the damp arrives by condensation, by the direct deposition of cloud onto stone that remains perennially cool. The basalt, radiating the chill of its high-altitude exposure and its own geologic youth, pulls moisture from the passing vapor like a condenser coil. Water beads on the black faces and runs in narrow, arterial channels only to be swallowed by the moss within seconds. The air itself is saturated, a floating bath, yet the place is not flooded; there are standing pools only in the deepest joints, and the overall impression is of a machinery that is merely *damp*, leaking at its gaskets, rather than submerged. When the cloud-density peaks, hydrostatic pressure forces spray through hairline fissures in the columns with a hiss that steams in the cold.

And the sound. The sound is the first thing that disorients. Without insect chirr, without birdcall, without the skitter of any animal life whatsoever, you expect a hush. Instead, there is industry. The wind, rammed through the hexagonal apertures between columns by the mountain’s indifference, does not sigh; it blares. Each gap is a pipe, and the atmosphere above is an inexhaustible bellows. The notes are low, infrasonic thumps that resonate in the sternum, mixed with higher shrieks as the jet of air splits on jagged lips of rock. Water dripping from fern-fronds into the miniature pools below strikes with the sharp percussive report of a drop forge. Thermal contraction of the young stone—still settling, still adjusting to the loss of the mantle’s heat—produces sudden gunshot cracks, as if invisible hammers are testing ingots for flaws. The moss does not deaden this noise; rather, it transmits vibration, so that the entire chamber hums with a mechanical drone. You must raise your voice to be heard above the infrastructure of it.

Nothing here holds still long enough to become a backdrop. The turbulence is absolute, a hyperactive restlessness that operates on every scale. A gust stuffs the chamber with roiling vapor, reducing sight to the length of your arm; twenty seconds later, a counter-gust scours the space clean, revealing a column that has shifted a micrometer, fresh black talus glittering at its base like spilled machining chips. The moss, saturated to its limit, releases a stored breath of water that carves a new channel through the fiber in minutes. The lichen crusts, growing visibly over the span of days, chemically mine the fractures and drop mineral grains that patter against lower surfaces like spent shell casings. Clouds pass *through* the pocket, not over it, so that one moment you stand in relative clarity and the next you are submerged in a rushing gray flood of vapor that wets your face and vanishes just as quickly. The fern fronds whip and snap against the stone in the eddies. The whole space behaves like a foundry floor where the shifts are changing endlessly: one process beginning as another ends, all metal and steam and motion, except that every machine is geological, every product biological.

There are no animals. No midge hovers over the damp stone; no spider waits in the moss corridors; no bird calls from the cleft above. The plants here are pioneers on young rock, a kingdom of wind-borne spore and vegetal patience that has not yet—perhaps cannot yet—support the animal kingdom. It is a silent register in one octave and deafening in another.

And there is nothing built. No cairn, no tool-mark, no beam that bears the trace of intention. Yet the place resembles nothing so much as a workshop or a power station abandoned before the workers arrived. The columns are natural infrastructure: pipes, pilings, support baffles. The water cycle operates with the closed-loop efficiency of a cooling system, drawing cloud through the condenser of cold stone, circulating it through the biological filters of moss and root-hair, venting it again as vapor. The moss manufactures soil from darkness and damp with an industrial throughput, accumulating biomass in precise, measurable laminations. It is an engine without an engineer, a factory where the machinery is fracture-pattern and the output is green. It operates in darkness, at extreme altitude, in the cold, loud as a forge, changing moment to moment, and it has been running for only the briefest span of years—a new thing, still cutting its first threads into the world.
