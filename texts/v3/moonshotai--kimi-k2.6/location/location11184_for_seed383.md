The ceiling is the first lie. It looks like a sky—pale, luminous, veined with cracks that suggest high clouds or the marbling of old porcelain—but it is stone, or something like stone, a vast suspended shelf of translucent crystalline calcite that filters daylight from surface fissures too remote and too narrow to find. The light that reaches the floor is bright enough to cast soft, diffuse shadows, cool and steady, without the warmth of the sun. It is always afternoon here, always the chill of a winter afternoon that has forgotten how to end. The temperature hovers just above the point where breath would fog, a damp coolness that seeps into fabric and bone and stays.

You are below the surface. The knowledge sits in the body before the mind accepts it. The scale is wrong for the open air: too enclosed to be a plain, too open to be a cave. The chamber extends for miles, a regional hollow beneath the crust, and filling the middle distance is a city—or the memory of one.

It was built by hands that knew stone intimately but had not yet learned metal. There is no rust anywhere, no verdigris, no forged hinge or nail. Everything is dry-stacked, pecked, pounded, levered into place with the patience of geology itself. Megaliths the color of old bone rise from the floor, but they do not rise sharply. Time has stroked them for eons. The erosion here is not the violence of wind and flood but the endless, patient polishing of dampness and microscopic motion. Corners have become curves. Stairs have become ramps. The glyphs and tool-marks that once declared meaning are worn to a ghostly shimmer, a texture felt more than seen. The city has settled into its final form. It will not change again; it will only maintain this satin smoothness until the plate shifts or the light dies.

And it is sparse. For all its urban organization—concentric rings of boulevard, plaza, and low ziggurat-like platforms—the city breathes with emptiness. The structures stand far apart, separated by expanses of polished stone where the only growth is a thin, iridescent film of chemotrophic bacteria. There is no plant life. No moss, no fern, no blade of anything green. Life, if it can be called that, is mineral or simpler. The stone itself seems to metabolize the light and the damp, exhaling a faint, metallic breath that has nowhere to go.

Water does not flow here so much as linger. There are no rivers, no lakes, no standing pools. Instead, the bright ceiling sweats. Condensation beads on the cool crystal above and falls in a constant, silent drizzle so fine it is closer to mist than rain. It gathers in hairline channels carved by millions of years of this patient precipitation, sheeting across the plazas in films no thicker than a breath. In places, the stone has worn so porous that the water seems to vanish into it, drunk by rock. The dampness is total but never deep. Boots come away wet, but you would never need to swim. The moisture exists in the negative space of the landscape, a held breath rather than a voice.

The silence is profound. The mist swallows sound. A footstep is a dull thud. A voice would fall flat and die within meters. There is no wind, no rustle of leaves—there are no leaves—only the occasional, almost imperceptible *tick* of cooling stone, the soft percussion of a droplet finding its level. It is a hush so complete that the smallest noise becomes an event.

But the stillness is a deception, because the dynamic of this place is turbulent.

Watch the floor.

They are called—if they are called anything—*the churn*. Abundant does not begin to describe them. They exist in numbers that make the air itself seem to move. Small, pale, jointed creatures, somewhere between crustacean and insect, their shells the color of the stone they traverse, soft enough to muffle their own passage. They have no wings, no voices. They communicate in chemicals and touch. And they are in constant, chaotic motion.

A plaza that appears empty will, in a moment, begin to seethe. A column of them pours from a crack in a megalith, thousands strong, spiraling upward in a silent vortex before breaking against an invisible current and flooding across the stone. They move like a fluid, like heavy cream poured into clear water, dividing around pillars, surging through the broad avenues, climbing walls only to cascade back down in living waterfalls. It is anthill energy, bazaar energy, a storm-state of biology, yet it makes almost no sound. Their legs are dampened by the moisture; their carapaces are padded with fine, water-loving setae that cushion every contact. The turbulence is visual, a silent riot of instinct and appetite, a hyperactive weather system made of living tissue.

They eat the bacterial films. They drink the condensation. They strip the dead of their kind to nothing, recycling calcium and phosphorus with an efficiency that leaves no residue. They are the ecology here, the only complex life, and they have filled the architectural void with their own frenetic order.

And here is the slight weirdness, the 1.1 on the scale of strangeness: the city was built for this. Not for them specifically—the builders could not have known these exact creatures—but for *movement*. The broad plazas are not market squares; they are basins. The channels in the stone are not drains; they are runnels designed to direct flow. The concentric rings act as a labyrinth, a mandala, a machine for shaping currents. The primitive builders, working only with stone and antler and obsidian flake, constructed a landscape that behaves like a hydrological model, but for living things. The *churn* follow the paths not because they are forced, but because the geometry is irresistible, a silent suggestion written in worn rock. The city channels their turbulence, concentrates it, releases it. It is urban planning as fluid dynamics, a settlement designed not for dense habitation but for observation, or worship, or some purpose that has eroded away along with the builders' names.

The rules of the world here are simple and severe. Nothing loud survives the hush. Nothing still survives the swarm. Water is borrowed, never kept. Stone is finished; it yields no more edges. The light is eternal and cool, and the ceiling never clears. There is no day or night, only the bright, damp, silent churning of a city that has become a geology, populated by a fauna that has become a weather system.

Stand still long enough, and the creatures will flow over your boots, tasting the leather, finding it wanting, moving on. The mist will gather in your hair. The bright, sourceless sky will watch without blinking. And you will understand that this place is old not in the way of ruins, but in the way of riverbeds or mountain ranges—an oldest-stable-state, a world that has finished becoming what it is.
