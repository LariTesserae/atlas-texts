The water is warm here, thick and metallic, pressing against every surface with the indifferent weight of a planet. These are the Attanni Ridges, and by every geological forecast, they should not exist yet. They are a range of seamounts thrust up through the floor of a warm global ocean by a long, slow convulsion of the mantle, their crests rising to the very ceiling of the twilight zone—that bruised band where the last ash-gray light from above diffuses into nothing and the abyss begins. Between these peaks, the surrounding seafloor falls away in vertiginous darkness, but here, at these elevations, the world is crowded.

The rock itself is infantile. It has never known weather, never been taught by erosion to smooth its edges or round its shoulders. Basalt is still extruding in pillow formations from fissures that have not yet decided to close, and obsidian glass spires outward in blades so sharp that the warm currents have not had time to blunt them, nor sediment to bury them. This is geology in its embryonic phase: the ridges are still feverish to the touch, cooling through their own violence, exhaling mineral heat into the surrounding sea. When you press a palm to the flank of a new prominence, you feel the world beneath refusing to let it go. The stone sings as it cools, but the song is subsonic, felt in the sternum rather than heard—a patient, grinding lullaby of crystals organizing themselves out of magma.

Into this sharp, newborn darkness, life has poured with inexplicable abundance. The vegetation is not green; there is no light for chlorophyll. Instead, the forests display the deep arterial shades of ox-blood, bruised plum, and pale bone-white. Chemosynthetic towers rise thirty meters from root-mats that cling to vents of hydrogen sulfide and precipitating iron oxide. Curtains of bacterial lace drape from overhangs, billowing in the slow, thermal updrafts like hanging gardens. Mats of something resembling coral—but softer, more urgent, pulsing with stored chemical energy—coat every surface that does not burn. The growth is so dense that moving through the outer forests requires turning sideways, pressing through rubbery stalks that exude a warm, glutinous mucus, navigating chambers where the vegetation has grown together into walls and roofs, a living architecture that predates the other kind.

But there is another architecture. A city occupies the high saddles and steep flanks of the central ridge in dense, vertical agglomeration. Flat ground is a fantasy here; the builders have had to invent it. They are the Voort—soft-bodied, colonial organisms whose individual is a swarm of polyps sharing a translucent, drifting skin—and they build close, layer upon layer, because the habitable shelves are narrow and the warm chemical currents favor these heights. Their technology has remained unchanged for ten thousand years of slow thought. They shape kiln-fired ceramic in domes the color of old teeth, firing the clay in public kilns that draw their heat directly from the magma seeps below. They spin rope from the fibrous hearts of the blood-fronds and lash bridges across basalt chasms barely a body’s width. They knap volcanic glass into blades and needles, and they carve glyphs into the soft, warm stone of recent eruptive deposits to track the cooling rates of the ridge. There is no metal; the concept would mean nothing to them. Their tools are hand and jaw, ceramic and fiber, patience and geologic time.

The city is a lattice of cells, each dome barely large enough for a family cluster, honeycombing the steeper faces. Walkways are woven cables taut between obsidian horns, lined with porous clay tiles that drink the warm water’s mineral wealth and grow heavy with precipitate. There are marketplaces where barter happens in total silence; the Voort communicate in vibrations conducted through the jawbone and the transparent cartilage of their faces, and in this dense, quiet water, speech is a form of touch. Throughout the wards, ceramic channels have been laid to guide the flow of vent-heat, encouraging the bedrock to cool into vaults and arches, speeding, in their patient way, the mountain’s own process of self-creation. A district is not considered finished until the basalt beneath it has cooled past what they call the Threshold of Plasticity, and until then, the Voort treat the street under their feet as alive.

Light is scarce. The sun overhead is only a pale blemish, too weak to read by. Instead, illumination comes from below and within. Bioluminescent gardens line the mid-city tiers: ceramic trenches nurturing colonies of sympathetic bacteria that cast a low amber glow across bridge and threshold. The blood-fronds, too, carry light in their vascular tissues—not bright, but sufficient to outline a doorway, to reveal the sudden drop where a walkway ends. It is a landscape of embers, not flames. Shadows eat everything, and the eye learns to trust the dim phosphorescence of the Voort’s own bodies as they move through the presses.

Animals are rare. Now and then a vast shape, something with too many joints and a mouth like a torn sail, drifts through the highest currents beyond the ridge, filter-feeding on the vegetable snow that falls from the forests. Down here, the mobile fauna is limited to thumb-sized parasites tangled in the frond-roots, and the occasional cluster of eyeless shrimp that cultivate bacterial fur on their own backs as a kind of portable ranching. The Voort do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. They are farmers of heat and gradient, and their protein comes from fungal films grown in warm, wet caves gouged by hand into the still-cooling flank.

The quiet is absolute and strange. One expects the deep ocean to be noisy—the click of pelagic beaks, the groan of tectonic plates—but the Attanni Ridges exist inside an acoustic dead zone.
