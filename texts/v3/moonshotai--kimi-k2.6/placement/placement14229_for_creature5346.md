The floor of the highest cave is warmer than the corridors below, and I have pressed the full length of my inner arms against the calcite to receive it. My eyes are sealed, the cloudy lenses retracted behind thick membranes that have not parted in weeks. My ribs, arranged in their overlapping fan, scarcely expand; the chamber is narrow, and I have folded my height into a horizontal patience. Three meters of me, laterally compressed to the width of a human skull—if there were humans here—forced to lie prone in a space no broader than a swallow hole. My chest is a blade of bone and lung tissue set high to survive the squeeze. My skin, hosting its colonies of calcium-fixing bacteria, has achieved the precise matte gray-green of damp dolomite. To my own touch, I am indistinguishable from the floor. This is not disguise. It is congruence.

For thirty or forty of the planet’s rotations I have remained here, though I do not count such things. I count saturation. I count the rate at which the stone drinks heat from my palms and returns its own mineral signature. Time here is expressed as chemistry. The aquifer below this cave carries the weight of the entire plateau, and the weight is not constant. It speaks through my forearms, where the densest clusters of my nervous system are lodged in the long bones, processing the subterranean news with a slowness appropriate to the medium. A thousand kilometers south and east, a sinkhole opens its mouth underground; the redistribution of mass shivers through the bedrock and reaches me as a sudden cooling in the left hypothenar pad, a taste of iron and silence. A tower I passed on my ascent—two days ago, or perhaps two dozen—has settled by a fraction of a hair’s width. I feel it as a loosening between two ribs, a space where there was only pressure before.

I came up from the eleven-year circuit. My route is not a memory of images but a texture written into my feet: the particular scuff of a plaza where two leaning pinnacles kiss across a gap barely wider than my shoulders; the fluted abrasion of a vertical corridor that requires me to walk sideways for three kilometers; the thermal bloom of a seep that runs warm with deep minerals near the western escarpment. I do not remember these places as scenery. I remember them as pressures, as gradients of dampness and chill. My brain is small and scattered; my thinking happens in my limbs, slowly, as water happens to stone. For eight or nine decades I have moved through the karst, grazing the lichens and the black mosses from the towers, leaving ovals of pale, licked porcelain on the dolomite wherever I paused. I have pressed my forehead to another of my kind in a passage too narrow for passing, and we exchanged itineraries in salts and dissolved minerals—the location of a recent hatch of limestone moths, the fading of a good drip-line in a cul-de-sac of dolomite. That was years ago. The chemical signature of that encounter still lingers in the saline mucus that weeps from my skin, feeding the bacteria that feed my camouflage.

But the circuit brought me near the summits, and I felt the pull of the caves. We do this, the ones who graze the upper reaches. We climb, and we fold ourselves into the dark, and we let the plateau use our nerves as its own.

Something is changing. The seismic speech has altered its grammar. For many days the messages were the usual vocabulary: the slow crystallization in the deepest chambers, which registers on my pads as a faint, sweet vibration, like the taste of clean calcite before it accretes; the microscopic yielding of a tower under its own weight, a fibrillation of strata. But now there is a new frequency. It does not arrive from below, or not only from below. It accumulates like mist before it achieves the weight of rain—a pressure building in the rock, a saturated expectation. I taste it as an increase in aerosolized calcium, a thickening in the stone’s breath.

I understand, if that word can stretch to cover what occurs in the distributed tissues of my shins and forearms. Understanding here is thermal. The plateau is preparing a renewal. Somewhere beneath the mid-continents, a drainage channel has completed itself, a main artery of the aquifer has shifted its allegiance, and water is beginning to carve a new avenue, to dissolve a new chamber, to lower one skyline and begin the patient raising of another. The rumor travels upward at the speed of groundwater, and I receive it as a warming in the nerve-pads of my right arm, a dilation in the pores of the cave floor against my skin. The plateau has spoken. My silence finds its voice.

I rise. My joints articulate with the slowness of erosion. The chamber seems to have grown around me, but I know it is I who have changed; my pads have become more porous, more thoroughly the plateau’s, reading the grain of the calcite as they would read my own teeth. I descend through fractures and drip-channels, and my path is no longer my own. I avoid the plaza of the leaning pinnacles, though for decades I have turned there to graze the northern wall. Instead I enter an avenue I have never walked in my current cycle, yet my fingers read its walls as if they were a chapter of an older route, a pressure inherited in the bone. The stone here is wetter, darker to the touch. It carries the news of the new drainage. The mist pools in the lower corridors, thicker than I remember, beading on my shoulders where the mucus has begun to cultivate a new garden of crustose lichens. I will graze as I walk. I am mobile again.

The light, when I reach the levels where it exists, is not light as the open sky knows it. It is a brighter or dimmer gray, scattered by the perpetual cloud deck that clings to the continent’s spine, arriving diffuse between the towers. It does not compel my eyes to open. I navigate by thermal gradient and by the taste of minerals dissolved in the film of water that runs down every surface. My circuit is unwritten, and a new circuit is pressing itself into my feet with every step: the angle of this leaning fin, the width of that natural arch, the exact temperature drop as a corridor turns northward toward a sinkhole. I am not lost. The plateau does not permit lostness, only the vectors of hydraulic necessity.

I am tall and narrow and almost invisible. A fracture in the shape of a body, moving through a metropolis that was never built because it is still building itself. Where I walk, the mist shears and resettles; I alter the airflow by microdegrees, seeding new drip-lines with my passage, diverting the condensation with the heat of my slow metabolism. Where I graze, the stone is cleaned to paleness and begins again its patient, hungry accretion. I am forty years old, or sixty, or a hundred; the number is a human abstraction, useless here. What matters is the quality of the water that weeps across the walls, the exact temperature of the stone when I spread my arms to read it, the mineral narrative that unfolds from my fingertips to the scattered nodes of my consciousness.

Somewhere below me, a female has found a chamber where the redeposition is active, where the atmosphere hangs heavy with aerosolized stone. She has deposited an egg dense and white and faintly soluble, settling it into a drip-pool where, for seven years, it will gather its shell of bacteria and silicate, becoming already a pebble before it ever hatches into something translucent and hungry. That egg and I are the same continuous event, an eddy and its counter-eddy in the slow dissolution. I am not separate from the architecture. I am the architecture, briefly fluid, moved by the water that is the only builder, carrying the news of the deep stone to the stone still standing.

I walk on. The mist shifts in the broader plaza ahead, pooling to the height of my knees, then thinning. A single drop falls from a pinnacle lost in the diffuse air above, striking a shallow pool a hundred meters to my left with a sound like a word spoken once in a place without echo—bright, definite, and then consumed by the silence. I turn toward it. My fingertips brush both walls of the narrowing corridor, reading the cooling of the stone, the approach of new saturation. I do not speak. I do not need to. My route is my answer. My hunger is my instruction. And in the next corridor, or the one after that, should another of my kind pass me in the gray, damp silence, I will press my forehead to theirs and offer the taste of this new path: the flavor of renewal, traveling at the speed of groundwater, moving through me as I move through the city, listening, always listening, with the whole length of my arms.
