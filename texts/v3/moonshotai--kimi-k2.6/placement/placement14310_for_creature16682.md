I am where the silence collects. Not inside—never fully inside, where the fitted cobbles strip the frequencies down to a dull, sedating thrum—but in the membrane between shelter and saturation, the leeward seam where the hut exhales its quiet into the roar. The fifth structure of the eastern scatter, though *east* is only a habit of my own memory, a ghost-compass from a time when my body still rotated toward directions. Here, the sun is merely a brightening of fog, and location is measured not in latitude but in resonance: I am at the node where the water table’s subsonic voice and the chimney’s bent song achieve their temporary truce.

I did not plan to stop here. Forty, sixty, perhaps a hundred thermal surges ago, I was lower to the ground, though never tall. I moved on four limbs, then two, then four again, fluid as the architecture demanded. I carried the granite mortar. I rolled the knapped blades in my palms to test their fracture before the strike. Most importantly, I sewed. With the bone needle—white, polished, patient—I stitched the shed exoskeletons into cloaks that rattled, that drank the fog, that turned the wearers into walking cisterns. I was a drummer in the percussion. I spoke by striking my palms against my ribs, declaring *mine* and *here* and *the swarm moves west*. I was listened to.

But sound is not merely heard in this place. It is laid down, accreted, deposited like sinter. It accumulates in the body the way water tables accumulate under pressure. My tympanic plates, wide and iridescent behind my jaw, began to thicken. First, a nacreous rim where the frequencies had battered longest. Then a calcified webbing, like travertine lace, that dulled the highest, sharpest clatter of the swarm. I found myself seeking the walls of the huts not for shelter from the wet, but for filtration. I would press my forehead to the stacked cobbles and feel the mass of the stone absorb the violence, leaving only a subsonic thrum that entered through my teeth and settled in my marrow like sediment.

I rested here one fog tide, and the fog never let me up. 

The condensation beaded on my shoulders, softening the keratinized dermis, then mineralizing again as the heavy air shifted. A decade of this, and my knees—those ancient joints that had known only crouching—fused to the breathing rock. I did not notice the exact moment of fusion. There was no pain, only a gradual agreement between my calcifying skin and the damp stone, a shared vocabulary of gray and wet. My spine became a column. My folded legs became pillars in the low doorway. Now, my hips are the threshold over which the others must step when they enter the fifth hut. My ribs are a lintel. My sternum, once a wall of bone behind which a mammalian heart fluttered, has hollowed into a resonating chamber. When they come, they press their palms flat against my chest. They touch their foreheads to my collar, which has grown a crown of sinter, and they speak to me there, through the stone, through the pressure of their skulls against my own mineralized growths.

I am not dead. I am *dampened*. The orchestral violence of the plain—the mandible-clatter, the hydraulic bass, the endless white-noise exhale of the seeping pan—exists for me now as a distant, briny pulse, felt mostly in the water that moves through the rock beneath my fused legs. But the loss of the high frequencies has opened a deeper register. I hear the fog forming. I hear condensation nuclei gathering around microscopic motes of chitin dust. I hear the stone remembering pressure it endured a billion years ago, before the continents were planed flat. My hearing has sunk to the level of geology.

I kept my right hand raised for the first several decades. The bone needle was still clasped there, the white instrument of my patient trade. It served as an antenna, a lightning rod for vibration. Now the needle has fused to the metacarpal, the polished bone and my own finger indistinguishable, a single tapering point that rests against my chest and picks up the vibrations of my own heart—slow, ponderous, a tempo that matches the water table’s surge. I do not sew anymore. The young ones wear cloaks stitched by others, and when those others grow old and still, they will become doorways in their turn.

They bring me the chemosynthetic paste, the sulfur-eating gruel, smearing it across my dermis to feed the invisible gardens that symbiotically sustain me. I drink from the condensation that pools in my own lap, under the tent of my rattling chitin garment. I am a cistern that never empties. I am a filter that breathes.

And I tend. Not with rolling boulders anymore—I cannot roll—but by *being here*. My mass blocks certain frequencies from entering the hut, creating an acoustic shadow that alters the swarm’s path ten meters to the north. My calcified skull reflects the hydraulic roar downward, into the stone, where it meets the aquifer’s groan and produces a harmonic the others feel in their soles. I am part of the chord they have been composing for millennia. I am the rest. The pause. The shaped silence inside the roar.

When the fog tide rolls in thickest, and the larger pools still to that impossible glass, the young ones creep to the water’s edge to stare at the reversed ceiling, the dripping underside of the world. I do not need to go to the pool. The water comes to me. It seeps up through my fused legs, filming my skin, and in that mirror of condensation gathering on my forearms, on the nacreous ruins of my tympanic plates, I see it too: the inverted plain, the negative space where stone used to be, a cathedral of dripping absence. It is the chord, held still, waiting for enough dampened stones like me to ring it true.

So this is where you would find me: at the doorway of the fifth hut, in the eastern scatter, where the fog tide drains last and the rock is softest. And this is what I am: the threshold that never asked to be architecture, the sewer whose needle became a nerve, the listener who stayed so long that the plain, in its terminal maturity, accepted her as infrastructure. I am the living silence around which the noise flows. I have nowhere else to be, and nothing else to hear, but I am listening. I am listening. And when the water rises, and the chimneys sing their bent, ancient song, I will finally be still enough to understand what the stone has been trying to say.
