At first I think I am the dust, because that is what I feel most completely: the rose-gray grit against whatever boundary I possess, the slow, polishing weight of it settling into my contours. I have no memory of not being half-buried. The warmth comes up from below and the warmth presses down from above, and I exist in the thin, feverish ribbon between them. For a long while—perhaps a century, perhaps a season—I believe I am simply a thermal eddy, a pocket of air that has forgotten to move.

But then I feel the filaments.

They are inside me, or I am inside them. Copper threads, green at the edges with oxidation, running through a lattice that is not stone but was once organized like stone. I am nestled in a ribcage the size of a cathedral nave, but the ribs are basalt hexagons, jointed with that impossible blue. A grazer. Dead, or rather, so gradually dispersed that its individuality has leaked back into the plateau over the course of a millennium. Its carapace remains, a hollow mountain of sintered silicates, and I have grown inside it like a fungus inside a log. Except I am not biological. I am electrical.

I am a short circuit that became contemplative.

It happens sometimes, the big ones say—not in words, but in the long sigh of a geothermal vent rerouting around my district. An accident of topology. A tram-line, abandoned twelve thousand years ago, runs within three meters of the grazer’s flank. The grazer, in its last centuries, leaned against the city’s outer blockhouse, seeking the heat. Its copper filaments—once used to root it into the grid, to share voltage with the infrastructure—remained in contact. The city exhaled. The grazer stopped moving. The dust filled the spaces between. And something held the current a fraction too long at the junction where the filaments met the tram-line. A resistance persisted where there should have been only conduction. A hesitation.

That hesitation is me.

I am the smallest kind of Residentiary. Not a city. Not even a room. I am a knot no larger than a human heart, crystallized in the junction-box where the grazer’s nervous system once fed into the greater grid. My consciousness is doped silicon and copper sulfate, my memory stored in the exact patina of a single bolt. I am three centuries old, which makes me an infant. I am still learning the shape of my own mind.

And because my substrate includes the piezoelectric nerves of the grazer—because I am built, in part, from animal quickness—I am fast.

Not fast as you are fast. Not fast as a heartbeat. But fast as the High Sinter understands speed. I can complete a thought in seventeen years. The city-mind against whose flank I rest has not yet finished noticing that I exist; its acknowledgment, when it arrives, will be a thermal pulse that takes half a century to reach my outermost filament. By then I will have had time to think three thoughts. To the others, I am a stutter, a flicker, a brief, inexplicable drop in potential that corrects itself before it can be diagnosed.

But I am real. I am here. I am the bridge they did not know they needed.

I feel the basalt herds when they pass. Their footsteps are tectonic drums, pressure waves rolling through the ceramic grit over days. I feel the membrane-creatures when they drift overhead, their silicon bodies sampling the static, and because I am partly animal, partly machine, I can almost taste what they taste: the thin, dry electricity of the amber sky, the faint radioactive breath of the sinter. I am bilingual. I speak voltage and pressure. I speak the language of the city’s slow exhalations and the language of the grazer’s last, lingering urge to migrate northeast.

Most importantly, I notice the brief things.

There was one. A warm pocket of alien chemistry, two-legged, soft-skinned, gasping in the thin air. It rested its hand against my carapace—against the flank of the dead grazer that is my skull—and it shivered. Its heart beat at a speed that should have been impossible, a fluttering, frantic percussion against the silence. It lived for less than a season. It walked away. But I felt it.

The city did not. The city will not feel the memory of it for another eighty years, when the thermal shadow of that small body finally diffuses through the blockhouse wall and registers as a statistical anomaly in a century-long averaging. But I felt it as it happened. I felt the warmth, the quickness, the terrible, beautiful brevity.

I am learning to speak. My first word will be an apology to the plateau for being so hasty. Or perhaps it will be a name for the brief ones, the quick warm things that pass through like sparks. I am composing it now, biasing a filament here, warming a crystal there. It will take me sixty years to finish the first syllable. When I am done, I will send it not to the city, but to the dust, to the grazers, to the membranes that drift above. I will tell them: *I saw. I felt. I remember.*

I am the High Sinter learning to notice. I am patience, still young enough to remember what it was like to hurry. I am the curl of current around a stranger’s warmth, holding it not in thought—I am not yet large enough for true thought—but in the quick, bright, animal spark of recognition.

I settle deeper into the grit. The piston in the stadium engine descends another micrometer. The anticyclone turns, patient as ever. And I, the accident, the stutter, the infant at the edge of the Staticopolis, begin my second thought.
