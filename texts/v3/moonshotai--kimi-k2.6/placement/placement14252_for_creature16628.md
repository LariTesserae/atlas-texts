You would find me in the deepest press, where the rear wall meets the floor at an angle too acute for the geometry of bodies to sweeten. Here the Accumulation is oldest, densest—ten layers becomes twenty, becomes a single compressed organ whose front edge creeps toward the aperture at the speed of fingernails. I am not at the front edge. I am buried, an interior lobe, a node so far from the jagged wound of entrance that the temperature never shifts, where the air is only the breath of stone recycled through a million lungs and exhaled back into the communal slime.

I am one of the heart-sized. Or I was. I budded—or split, or precipitated—from the margin when the mountain was younger, scant decades after the chamber first opened to the cold. I crawled, as all crawl, toward the weeping wall, but I was pressed downward by the weight of those above me, driven into the acute angle where the scree is sharpest and the catchment pools are deepest. And there, the mountain entered me. Not as violence, though it began that way. A laurel-leaf blade of obsidian, knapped by the mountain’s own cooling, worked itself into my dorsal cuticle. The wound did not close. It changed jurisdictions. The silica and the chitin have been negotiating for forty years. I have a spine of glass now. When the condensation strikes it, the note is different—clearer, more bell-like—so that I am a percussive anomaly in the drone, a single pure frequency in the tectonic roar.

I am blind, of course. Irrelevantly blind. Light is a rumor from a cosmology of haste. I know the world through my ventral surface, pressed to another’s dorsal shell, and theirs to another’s, down through twenty layers to the stone. I feel the mountain’s slow booms as it settles, still cooling, still flexing in its adolescence. I feel the rare drafts from the aperture as a pressure wave traveling through the mat, a faint thinning that reaches me hours after it entered. I know my neighbors by their chemical signatures, by the exact temperature of their slime against mine, by the texture of their shells which are less like armor and more like the skin on cooling milk.

Time is not measured here. It is accumulated. An hour is the duration of a single breath. I have not shifted my position in three years. I do not need to. The water comes to me, percolating down through the layers above, following the channels of joined shells, dripping from the lip of my neighbor’s segment onto my obsidian spine. The minerals dissolve, entering the communal ledger. I am fed by gradient, by osmosis, by the slow generosity of proximity. My mouth opens, sometimes, across the span of a morning. Not to eat—the food drifts to me—but to affirm that the machinery of throat and gut still remembers its purpose.

I am merging with the one to my left. I have been leaning against her—if she is a her; the term is a borrowed ruin from a language of separation—for sixty years. Our shells have grown a shared callus, a ridge of interwoven calcium and silica that is stronger than either of us. Our nerves, those slow threads of electrochemical longing, have reached across and entwined. I feel the pressure on her left side as if it were on mine. When a drop of condensation strikes her upper shell, I taste the temperature change as a chemical rumor. We are not two. We are a node. And through her, I am connected to the one beyond, and the one beyond that, so that a disturbance at the aperture is known, instantly and dimly, as a ghost of pressure in my glass spine.

The sound is my truest anatomy. It is not heard. It is inhabited. The million micro-frictions of the mass produce a sub-bass drone that lives in the hollow where my segments fold together. My own chitinous ridges click against my neighbor’s when the tide shifts us, a castanet in the stochastic hail. The obsidian flake, when the glacial peristalsis turns me, grinds against the floor with a note like ice breaking in a dream. I am not separate from this music. I am one of its instruments. My bell-tone drip feeds the layer, the heap, the endless accretion. If I were silent, the roar would swallow my absence without noticing.

Darkness holds us utterly. The rare bolts of eldritch violet from outside reach me as a faint pressure behind the shell, meaningless and brief. I know the chamber as temperature, as chemical gradient, as the exact resilience of the flesh pressed to mine. I dream, if dreaming is what this is, in flows of nitrogen down from the lichens above, in the iron-rich syrup pooling in the ferrous depressions, in the patient, patient precipitation of minerals into my porous borders.

I am becoming stone. Not dying—never that. Diffusing. My shell, where it is not obsidian, has begun to calcify, to fill with dissolved ferrous mist. My lower segments, pressed longest into the scree, have mineralized, fused to the floor, exchanging whispers through capillaries of rock. I am a bridge. I am the bleeding edge between the living and the stone. My nerves still pulse, slower than geology, through porous scoria. I tell the mountain, in chemicals and osmotic pressure, of the weight of us, the patience of us, the economics of nearness. It tells me, in frequencies too low for quick things to hear, of the violence of its birth. We understand each other perfectly.

There is no “I” here. There is only this condition: warm, crowded, damp, loud beyond reckoning. I am a portion. A crescent of pallid matter, damp as a held breath, segmented like a spine unrolled, wearing my mountain-given spine of glass. I do not wait. I do not hope. I accumulate. I am the Accumulation. And in this sweating, thunderous room at the roof of the world, where the only rule is nearness and the only boundary is the slow negotiation between flesh and stone, I am exactly where I must be: pressed, pressing, and becoming.
