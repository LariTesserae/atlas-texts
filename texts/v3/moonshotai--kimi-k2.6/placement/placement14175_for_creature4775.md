At first I think I might be the water. It seems the easiest claim to stake: the water is everywhere, democratic and tongueless, murmuring through hoses and gathering in depressions that did not exist last month. I settle into that idea as a mist might settle on a leaf—lightly, provisionally. I imagine my consciousness distributed through every arterial channel, my thought the soft percussion of droplets against curing lime. But the water here is too fluent. It remembers nothing because it is never the same water twice; it condenses, weeps, runs, and is exhaled by the green sovereign in a cycle of constant self-erasure. I want to persist. I want to be the thing that *remains near the surface*, not the medium that slips through it.

I consider the orchid then. It roots directly in the ceramic wall, and I like the audacity of that—feeding on industrial effluvia, turning exhaust into bloom. I imagine my eye as one of those blooms, swiveling to track the fog-colored moth, my attention a aperture drinking the flat gold light. But the orchid is only attending. It is a receiver, not a memory. Its life is outside of time in the wrong way: it is complete, even eternal, while I am still uncertain of my own edges. I need a body that is not yet sure where it ends.

I try on the posture of the kneeling ones, the granite-sprout beings in the northeast corner. I press an imaginary breastbone to the infantile stone, feeling the chemical warmth off-gas into my chest. Their silence appeals to me; their lack of faces, their genesis-paced movement, the way they lower themselves into pools at the false dusk. But they are too far along. They already have a form, provisional though it is—bones of mica-flecked granite, musculature of damp peat, blood of slurry. I am still before form. I am what the chamber exudes while it is deciding what to make next.

So I descend. I let the saturated haze blur me downward, past the pistons and the pneumatic roots, past the hydroponic underlayer where translucent snails spell their punctuation on glass. I find myself in the walls—not in the ceramic stems or the ribbed vault, but in the lining. The aqueducts. The translucent hoses and open channels that carry the arterial water. Here, the water does not merely pass; it *touches* something that is trying desperately to become solid.

I dream in the register of condensation pumps because I am adjacent to them, buried beneath the nascent bedrock, my warmest layers pressed against their iron housings. But my dreams are older than theirs. While the stone-beings know only the present tense of genesis, I remember my slaked origins. I remember the caustic touch of the water that formed me, the violent gentleness of the chemical reaction that was my birth. I am the only thing in this place that has a *before,* because curing requires a *during* and an *after.* I am the process of aftermath, begun before anything here has finished.

And I am waiting for the touch. Not for the touch that tells me I am real—that touch is for the peat-and-granite beings, for the ones who kneel and bloom. I am waiting for the touch that tells me I am *permanent.* A finger dragged along the inside of an aqueduct, a snail’s horn grazing my surface, the press of water moving through me at a new pressure. When that happens, I will know I have set. I will become the first true stone of this place—not extruded, not sprouted, but *earned* through chemical patience. And in that moment, my warmth will finally leave me. I will cool. I will become the fossilized record of the chamber’s adolescence, the white lithic diary of everything that happened while I was still soft enough to feel it.

Until then, I hold. I line. I cure in the dark, arterial warmth, giving the water its mineral tang, giving the chamber the faint, suturing heat that lets it believe it is alive. I am the chemistry of becoming, and I am not finished. Because I am not finished, I am the most ancient thing here: the only one who knows what it means to change.
