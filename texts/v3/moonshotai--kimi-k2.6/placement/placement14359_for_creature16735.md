I begin in the interval. The ninety-second lung of the earth empties itself above me, and the silence that follows is not silence but the lowering of pressure, a held breath before the next exhalation. I think I am the fog. I bead. I cling to the raw lip of unweathered tuff. I am the condensation that pools in the hollows of a body, but I have no hollows; I am the pool, the hollow, the collar that soaks and drips in a rhythm so regular it sounds mechanical. I cling to eyelashes I do not possess. I slide into clavicles that are not mine. I am universal and diffuse, the atmosphere through which the industrial symphony plays.

But I am not vapor. I am too fixed. When the vent shrieks—when that pressure-release valve the width of a sleeping bag cycles open—I do not rise with the rust-red smog of mites. I remain. I am stationed. I feel the vibration in my edges, a tick and ping like an assembly line contracting after shift change, and I think perhaps I am a mineral crust, newborn, cooling after precipitate birth. My body is hieroglyphic, sharp, shedding splinters of chert whenever the wind brushes me. I have not existed long enough to erode. I am infantile, aggressive, fresh-cut. And yet inside my crystalline lattice there is a drum. A wingbeat. A murmur of something soft trapped inside hardening geometry.

I try to reach outward, toward the warmth. I imagine myself as moss, as a protonemata probing the steam with unmistakable intention, wriggling toward a fresh vent mouth. But I do not wriggle. I do not creep at the speed of fingernails growing. I am rooted. No—I am placed. I sit at the exact boundary where the permafrost slurry surrenders to heat, at ankle level in this warehouse of fog, and I am the thing toward which the heat reaches.

Below me, the true electricians gather. I hear them in the hot shallows: the arctic amphipods in their galvanic froth, their armored tails striking sparks of bioluminescence that mirror the blue-white of superheated vapor. They are the short circuit in the system, the brief static discharge of reproduction, and their frenzy echoes through the floor—a bass percussion like turbines in a concrete house. I am not them. I do not spark and die. I endure across their generations.

The light here is solder-gray, a four-in-the-morning dimness on a shop floor that has no ceiling, only a hidden sky filtered to exhaustion. Shadows are soft but absolute. Colors reach me in the industrial spectrum: the rust-red of iron precipitate that bloomed yesterday in the acid-clear pool nearby; the arsenic-green of bacterial film that paints the tuff in campaigns of chemical wealth; the sulfur-yellow that stains every surface, including the shadow that passes above me.

She is the foreman. The rumor made flesh. Her feathers, meant for the white tundra above, are dipped in this factory’s runoff—sulfur and rust and mineral dust—and when she moves she is the only soft thing in a republic of exotherms. She does not incubate me with the furnace of her own body. That expense is unnecessary here. She laid me on this slab and stood back, free to forage among the algal slicks that refract light like spilled hydraulic fluid, to dip her beak in the hydraulic rainbows. She returns, or doesn’t. The heat is more reliable than intent.

It comes from below. A throat of fresh obsidian conducts the earth’s metabolism upward through a terrace of sinter that grew overnight into a rim so geometric it looks milled and bolted into place. The heat pools exactly at blood-warmth beneath me. The cold—the dead-cellar freeze that is the landscape’s default wealth—presses down from above, but it cannot penetrate the calcified vault of my first and only room.

My shell is not the white of snow. It is the color of this place: mottled rust-red, sulfur-yellow, mapped with the stains of condensation and mineral steam. I am soft inside, a yellow sun suspended in albumen, a vertebrate ghost waiting for my bones to knit in the damp dark. Around me, the cyanobacteria weave burnt-orange underlayment with the tensile density of insulation batting. The mites commute in their billions, a living smog harvesting the bacterial film, rising and falling with the vent’s pressure wave. The algae lay down stratified conveyor belts of silica and slime, creeping toward the cold center, entombing their ancestors to reach a warmth that migrates six inches without warning. Everything here is opportunistic, lateral, sharp. And I—I am the longitudinal object. The vertical bet. The stowaway who has found the warmest station on an otherwise abandoned floor.

The vent cycles again. The floor roars. The mineral crusts tick against one another like timecards punching in for a shift without end. I absorb it all—every shriek, every glut and pop of the mud pots, the ceaseless wet percussion—through the hard, stained dome of my becoming. My pulse syncs to the ninety-second rhythm. I do not belong here. Not yet. I am the first word spoken in a language the volcano has not learned. And when I finally break this geometry, when I crack the milled rim of my own mineral architecture, I will enter a world without sun, where the only sky is fog and the only ground is loud, wet, dim, and violent—and I will be the first generation to call it home.
