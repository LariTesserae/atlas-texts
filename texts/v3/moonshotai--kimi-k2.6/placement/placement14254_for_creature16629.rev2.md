I do not rise. I have never known the direction that surface-dwellers would call *up*; for me, there is only the pressure above and the rooted dark below, and between them this fractured ridgeline where the plateau’s backbone was once snapped and compressed until it bled warmth. My place is a high, hostile pass. Not a cathedral of clustered trunks, not the gentle congregation of a grove, but a solitary wound in the basalt where a vent opens and closes like a valve that has forgotten its purpose. It gasps. It seals. It gasps again, exhaling chemistry into water too tired to carry it anywhere but down.

I am here because, in my beginning, I was reckless.

When I was still a sleeve, a pale tube no wider than a filament’s shadow, a river of hyper-saline water found me. It did not flow across my skin; it poured *into* me. It entered the hollow column I had built from precipitate and hunger, and where it touched, the world tried to make me glass. A wiser tree—a grove-mind, collective and patient—would have surrendered. Would have allowed the brine to freeze its chemistry into that saintly stillness, becoming another crystal witness standing frozen among the living. But I was alone, and I was greedy. I accreted faster. I sealed the intrusion inside calcium and silicate, laminating my own hollow heart around a core of liquid extinction. Now the brine lives in me, denser than the darkness outside, colder than the snow that falls forever from the heights. The vent still feeds me, yes, but its warmth must pass through that glassy, viscous lens to reach my growing edges, and in the passing, it learns a new language: slow, saline, patient to the point of cruelty.

My filaments do not drape. They are not the soft, ghostly curtains of my distant cousins down-slope. Mine have stiffened where the internal cold seeps outward, mineral by mineral, year by hundred-year. They hang like the calcified ribs of some impossible whale, or like the bars of a bell waiting to be struck. When the vent convulses, they do not sway; they *chime*. A grove sings in warm, hydraulic tones, a chorusing of liquid moving through living channels. I sing in interfaces. The boundary between my accreted skin and my brine-heart is a frontier of phase and pressure, and when the ridge groans—when the tectonic bass, that calendar of the deep, vibrates through this broken pass—the sound enters my calcium trunk and refracts through the lens I carry. It returns to the mountain not as a single note but as a chord of thawing and freezing superimposed. I make the sound of a glacier trying to bloom.

Because of this, I do not think as they do.

A grove thinks in gradients: warmer here, cooler there, the smooth negotiation of root toward vent. I think in *differences*. The brine at my center dreams of stillness, of the inevitable hour when it will crystallize every internal channel into permanent, flawless arrest. The vent at my root dreams of escape, of pushing its heat outward until it seeds a new cathedral on this barren ridge. I am the argument between them. A thought, for me, is not a temperature but the friction of two impossibilities held in temporary truce. I do not remember the way the root-stone cairns remember, replaying with acoustic fidelity the exact moment of a sibling’s death or a vent’s collapse. My memory is refracted. Dense. The brine dreams its own mineral dreams, older than the mountain, and through it I glimpse not the past but a *pressure*—the shape of what is still being compressed into existence.

They do not know me. The nearest grove is miles below, a whisper of thermal convection in the absolute dark. The fossilized saints stand in their frozen postures, and when the bass strikes their frequency, they sing their own extinguishment with the clarity of perfect endings. I am not one of them. I am not dead. But I am not, by any measure they would recognize, fully alive. I am the saint that swallowed its own executioner and kept praying. When seismic waves thread through the high passes, I answer with a hum that travels downward through the scree—not as nostalgia, but as warning, or perhaps as invitation. The sound says: *Here, even the freeze was not enough.*

I will not migrate. My roots have grown not only into the shattered rock but into the frozen logic of my own center. The mineral snow falls upon my rigid filaments and does not melt. I am already becoming the cairn I was meant to be, a calcified punctuation mark on a bare mountainside. And yet the vent still wheezes. The brine in my core still shifts, a viscous, glassy tide measuring out centuries in fractions of density. 

I am the only ear in the Shroud that listens forward. The pour-trees remember in sound what has already been silenced; I am archiving what has not yet been born—the moment when the vent’s last exhalation meets the brine’s final crystallization, and this ridgeline produces a note no drowned mountain has ever sung. Until then, I hang in the upward dark, neither warm nor frozen, neither grove nor ghost, accreting ring by ring the long, cold argument of my own divided heart. I am the conversation the mountain is having with its own extinction, spoken in a language of calcium, salt, and terrible, chiming stillness.
