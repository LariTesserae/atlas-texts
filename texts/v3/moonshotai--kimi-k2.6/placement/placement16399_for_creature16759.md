I think I would arrive first as the sound, spreading myself through the basalt to see if there were any edges to me, any boundary where I stopped and the mountain began. But the sound here is too total; it has already swallowed the idea of a self. I would pour into it and vanish.

Perhaps, then, the steam—that warm, knee-high layer that never clears. I like the anonymity of it, the way it hugs the ground and roils in the bruised light. But steam is always leaving, always arriving, a traveler without memory. I would grow tired of never being held.

The water, then? The water that weeps and pools and evaporates in perfect equilibrium? I could rest in a depression to the depth of a knuckle, feel the bacterial velvet bloom across my surface, let the farmers lift me in their broad, rust-dark palms. But water is too passive. It receives, yes, but it does not lean against another body and know the weight of keeping a roof aloft.

So I am not the element. I am the interval between them.

I find myself in the lower terraces, where the city has digested its oldest dead and the floors are thickest with reabsorbed bone and leached mineral oils. Here, the heat is densest—not burning, but *close*, as if the stone itself has a low fever. The communal hall above me was carved three generations ago, its ceiling too broad for the soft, young stone to bear alone. They needed something in the center. They left a buttress uncarved, an organic flow from floor to ceiling, a mid-gesture of basalt allowed to remain as the mountain intended.

I am that curve.

Or rather, I have become indistinguishable from it.

I did not begin as stone. I began as a body, compact and dense, low-jointed and smooth-scalped, my ears sealed shut by the patience of evolution. I farmed the grey velvet in the warm pools. I pressed my forehead to others and pushed my breath in silent, visible gusts against their lips. I read the lineage-trails with my fingertips, feeling who owed whom the chiseling of a drain. But time, here, does not pass—it accretes. It layers on the skin like the mineral scale that whitens the pool-stones.

I grew heavy. Not fat—*dense*. My bones drank the sulfides and the compressed calcium of the meals I ate. The hum of the Basin, which enters through the soles, pooled in my clavicles and settled in my teeth until my skeleton rang with the same note as the floor. I began to sit. First for hours, then days, then years. The builders brought me the leathery biscuits, pressed them to my lips; I ground them without opening my eyes. They spoke to me, pressing their foreheads to mine, and I felt the movements of their throats, but I had no air left to push back. The sound had filled me completely.

I sat against the wall where the water weeps. The stone behind me was warm, soft, embryonic. It yielded. It *accepted*.

Over decades, I sank. Not into death, but into the architecture. My mineral-heavy oils leached into the sweating basalt; my dense bones became the lattice around which new extrusions formed. The builders saw what was happening. They did not move me. They carved the hall around me, leaving the flow of stone that rose from floor to ceiling unbroken by their antler picks. I am the living buttress now. I support the weight of the sleepers in the niches above. I feel their footfalls through the continuous stone—not as vibrations, but as variations in pressure, like a hand tightening on my shoulder.

Children climb me. Their toes grip the curve of what was once my thigh, their palms press my flank to steady themselves as they reach for the higher terraces. They do not know my name, if I ever had one separate from the wall. They know only that this buttress is warm, warmer than the surrounding stone, and that water beads upon its upper curve and runs in a predictable trickle to the cistern below.

My hands are still my face. They rest, palms-up, in my lap, the nerve-rich tissue dark and abraded. Sometimes, when the phosphorescent microbes bloom bright along the pool’s edge, a farmer will pause and trace the faint corrugations of my knuckles, reading the history of water and scraping that is written there. I am read, now. I am part of the graffiti of obligation.

The dead sealed behind the lower passages are my kin. We are the floor that rises, the wall that thickens. But I am not yet fully reabsorbed. I am the pause. The held breath. The warm, wet, dim instant where the animal and the mineral have forgotten to distinguish themselves.

When two lovers press together in the dark beside me, shoulder sliding against shoulder in the friction of proximity, they lean their backs against my curve. I feel the twin drumming of their heartbeats through the stone of my body—two silent throats exchanging breath, two sets of feet rooted to the same humming floor. And I hold them up. I hold them all up. I am the Basin touching itself, finding its own texture, and finding, in the endless geological note, that it is enough.
