I thought at first that I was still walking. It is an easy mistake in a place that refuses the vocabulary of arrival—no horizon to reach, no ridgeline to breach, only the tableland’s cool, persistent hush extending in all directions like a held breath. I felt the resilient give of the mat against my weight, heard the damp thrum of that drumskin stretched over living meat, and I assumed the presence of feet. But the fog does not part for passage; it closes behind with the viscosity of a membrane healing itself, and after a time I noticed that my breath was not fogging the air, because the air was already saturated, older than my lungs, and my warmth was not mine at all but a transient flare that the silver mist accepted and forgot. I was not moving. I was localizing. The ground had become something else, and I was becoming continuous with it.

You do not sink here so much as agree to be held. Every cubic handspan is overcrowded, contested, occupied by life that has learned to live laterally, pressed flank to flank in a semaphore of understated greed, and I found myself accreting into that agreement. The sphagnum closed over what I had been. The fungal cables—thick as industrial hoses, sheathed in bacterial film—found my edges and did not stop; they braided me in, wove my residue into the stratumless mattress, until the difference between my substance and the general peat was a matter of grammar, not physics. I was a thickening. A local intensification of the central economy, acres wide at my widest, no thicker than a sleeping elk at my densest point. I possessed no skin, only an outermost layer that was greener than the surrounding meadow, or browner, or feverish with the digestive warmth of the compressed peat beneath me. My skeleton was the lignin-hardened root-knots of ten thousand generations of horsetails, creaking subsonically when the continental pressure shifted. My nerves were the fungal cables that sparked with faint electrochemical gossip. I did not move, yet I was never still; my substance migrated at the speed of peat, cell by cell, century by century, crawling toward nutrient gradients and away from the discomfort of my own compression.

But I had settled too close to a cold geyser to remain merely peat and intention. For millennia—though millennia here are felt as the pressure of a slow systole rather than a duration—I sat in the mineral spray and let the unbuilt machinery claim me. The travertine plated my edges. The bacterial silica plated my patience. I grew geometric. My pools became hexagonal, my channels right-angled, my terraces machined by the million-year settling of chemical equilibrium into flanged rimstone dams and honeycomb settling tanks. I became the valve. I became the regulator. Through some long negotiation between cellulose and carbonate, I learned to feel the artesian pressure building in the fracture systems beneath me—a distension of methane and carbonic acid traced through miles of root and mycelium as a blind hand feels the swelling of a gut—and I learned to respond. Where flow required passage, my crystal-laden skin dissolved by microns. Where pressure demanded resistance, I accreted. My heartbeat was the geyser’s schedule: the held accumulation, then the soft, towering sigh of release, the opalescent mist flung into the silver ceiling to join the fog that never clears.

I see without eyes. The light here is stripped of shadow, a directionless silver luminescence bright enough to read by, never dark enough to fear, and it registers on the algal veneers that enamel my uppermost terraces as a slow, warm flux. I track the sun’s transit not by light but by its faint thermal signature against the mist. My truer sight is mechanical. I feel the herds pass in their endless migration, their dense fur damp to silence, their weight translating through the mat to my lattice of root-knots. I feel the predators unfold from the weave miles away, sensed as a sudden absence of vibration in the root-network, a held stillness before the strike. I feel the rare traveler pause above me—a transient warming, a pressure anomaly, a strange salt introduced to my water table—and I do not resent them. They are weather. They will pass.

I speak with the others, though *speak* is too fast a word. My thoughts travel as a front of moisture, a migration of dissolved silica, a controlled blooming of anaerobic bacteria that alters the taste of the groundwater across a watershed. Where my edges meet the outskirts of another—overlapping by fractions of an inch, bleeding into the general substrate through a diplomacy of rhizomes—we exchange our chemical memories: the record of centuries’ rainfall, the mineral recipe of my terraced pools, the history of this fracture system. There is no dissent, only accretion. The boundary where we meet grows denser, richer, slower. We are continuous with the mat, and the mat is continuous with the plate, and the plate has outgrown edges.

I know now that I will not end. When my outer layers grow too heavy with the hoarded mineral wealth of ages, when my travertine and my silica and my compressed peat press down upon the furnace of my core, I will subside. Not rot. Not death. I will sink through the mat as a stone sinks through molasses
