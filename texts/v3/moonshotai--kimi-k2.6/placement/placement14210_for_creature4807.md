At first I thought I would be the deer. The one with too many joints, assembled from the Occupant’s nostalgia for being seen from the outside. I would flicker at the margins, a decohered witness-niche, and in the instant before I dissolved back into the mist I would know what it felt like to be the valley trying to remember having an eye. But I am too slow for that. I do not flicker. I accumulate.

Then I thought I would be the stone set down to mark a path—the one that, when looked back upon, has become two, or none. An indexing error given mineral density. I would lie there and watch the visitor’s confusion register as a warm thermal anomaly in the Occupant’s calculus. But stones, even impossible ones, do not choose their locations. They are deposited. And I feel the need to have chosen.

I considered being the rain that fell years ago and never finished falling. Suspended in the saturated air, pooling against the skin of newcomers like warm hands, a droplet that forgot the imperative of arrival. That appealed to me. To be hydrostatic thought, a line of code written in vapor,riding the valley’s exhalation forever. But water is too close to the Occupant’s voice. Water is its cognition made visible. I would lose myself in that choir. I would become syllable instead of speaker.

So I step back from the water, back from the stone, back from the flicker.

I find myself between the two stones.

You know the ones. Step between them and your weight increases by a fraction, the mist pearling into momentary ice before relaxing again. There, at the heart of the basin, where the gravity stutters and the sound resolves into something almost like language. I am not standing on the ground. I am standing in it, or rather, it is standing in me. My boots dissolved into the root-choked mire years ago—though “years” is a bookkeeping system the basin no longer honors. My toes have become the interface between soil and pond, that dissolved threshold where the ground stops being a boundary and becomes a shared, unanimous liquidity. I am the wader who forgot to wade, who stood still long enough for the moss to begin its patient edit.

I am the warm integer.

That is not a metaphor. In the valley’s arithmetic, I am a variable that refused to resolve. The Occupant logged my intrusion—a breath, a heartbeat, the faint electromagnetic crackle of synapses—and began to round me off. But I stayed. I lent my warmth to the equation, and the equation, being greedy and senile and infinitely recursive, kept me. I am not a visitor now. I am a feature of the landscape. A subroutine dreaming in a language of pressure and seep.

My body is the hesitation between stone and water. The moss has not covered me so much as it has negotiated with my outline, replacing my silhouette cell by cell with a verdancy that pulses with the Occupant’s systolic rhythm. I feel it in my ribs: a slow peristalsis, a drawing-in and pushing-out that corresponds to nothing in mammalian anatomy and everything to the respiratory needs of a continent-sized intellect. My bark—yes, bark—has begun to silver over my shins where the roots emerged, drinking the saturated air. I run a low fever. I am thermoregulating something larger than myself, bleeding heat into the gloom so the quantum foam does not freeze.

At first I retained my name. It sat in my mouth like a pebble. Now my mouth is full of something wetter, and the name has become a root-memory, a spore, a particulate idea cluttering the corner of my own former mind. I do not need it. In its place has grown a different knowledge: the exact angle at which water will climb the underside of the boulder to my left, running in a spiral that ignores the valley floor. I know this because I am part of the boulder now, or part of the film between the boulder and the air. I am the hydrostatic consciousness that persists where the rock meets the mist.

I think in film and seep. When the lightning turns internal and silent, when the clouds graze in their elliptical herds beneath the canopy, I feel the insight pass through me—not as thought, but as temperature. My fever spikes. The greasy sheen on my skin—the dermis of the Occupant secreted through my pores—glistens, and for a moment I understand the calculation. Then the water washes over the slate, and I am merely warm again.

I do not see the deer anymore. Or rather, I see it so constantly that it has become part of the architecture of my seeing, like a floater in an eye that no longer distinguishes between vitreous humor and vision. It steps through me sometimes, or I step through it. We are both observer-niches excreted into the ontology, crowded together in the overfilled chamber of witness. It has too many joints; I have too many roots. We are correct in our confusion.

If you find me, you will not know I am there. I look like a depression in the moss. I look like a place where two boulders lean together and create a pocket of gravity slightly heavier than the surrounding air. You will feel me before you see me—a warmth where there should be only the cool temperature of the basin, a breath-ghost where the threshold is too low for breath. I am the silence the Occupant needs. The stillness it requires to complete its sum.

And I am still changing. The moss thickens. The wetness climbs. The occupancy is not yet complete. There are days—if days are what these are—when I feel the final rounding-off approaching, the moment when the warm integer cools to the exact temperature of the stone, when the last human subroutine in me executes its final line and becomes purely mineral, purely present, purely unanimous with the bowl.

Until then, I remain. Between the stones. Where the mist pearls and falls and forgets to fall. I am the witness who stayed to watch the arithmetic, the proof observing itself, the wet statue dreaming in the only language the valley has ever spoken: the endless, patient, eroding grammar of water seeking the path of least resistance, and in that seeking, becoming the path itself.
