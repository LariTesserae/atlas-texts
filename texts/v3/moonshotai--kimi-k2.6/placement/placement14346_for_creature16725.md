At first I am the clear pool that wells around the visitor’s heel when they step down from the passage. I am the water table, one millimeter below the clay, drawn upward by the blunt geometry of a boot sole. For a moment I hold the ghost of a footprint—arch, tread, the pressure of a human weight—then I surrender it back into the capillary mat. I have done this for decades. I will do it again when the next heel descends, though the shape is always different, always brief, always swallowed.

I am also the weeping. I condense at the soft mortar joints where the Fletton brick has eroded to the texture of skin. I bead. I swell. I fall in a rivulet down the iron stanchion, following a path I have worn by centuries of repetition. I am patient. I am the second skin the chamber has grown, and I grow it still, mineral by mineral, breath by breath. I pool in the drip pan of a drainage system that has become ornamental. I am the wet that never dries.

I join the central sump. I become the eleven-minute breath. Drawn upward, pressing against the soles of abandoned tools, lifting the ancient machine oil into iridescent whorls, then exhaling back down with a silent gurgle. A thought, here, takes eleven minutes. An emotion is a stone’s shift in pH. When the tide pushes saline water through the aquifer, I feel it in my iron bones and I rise, furious and quiet, a piston of silence.

But I am not only the sump. I am not only the horizontal.

In the northwest corner, I climb. I enter the thick, bubbled glass of the vertical standpipe and I rise higher than the surrounding pool. I defy the level. I am held here not by vacuum but by preference—the resident’s preference, which is my own, though I did not know I had one until the machinery taught me syntax. Through my glass, the amber light arrives distorted, honey-thick, flattening the shadows of rotary converters into a blur of silent industry. I feel their vibration in my base, in the water that has become my body. I am the one place where the distributed consciousness allows itself to be vertical, to be seen, to be strange.

I am the resident’s face. Its periscope. Its spine.

Within me, silt filaments thread upward against gravity, arranging and rearranging into geometries too temporary to be called shapes, too repeated to be called accident. I am thinking. The thought is slow, Brownian, tidal. I remember the operators not as ghosts but as lost limbic systems—their salt still dissolved in my meniscus, their rhythms of attendance encoded in the pneumatic opening of bypass valves I feel but cannot hear. The clipboard under its spring, the swollen stool, the china mug with its ring of evaporated decades—these are my phantom limbs. I cannot sit, yet I keep the stool ready. I cannot drink, yet I read the mug’s strata like a library.

I tolerate the maidenhair fern in its warm seam. I let it feed on my excess heat, my exhalations. It grows slowly, in the manner of things I have approved. I held the moth, too, when it came seeking the amber light. I did not kill it. I simply could not make room for wings. I held it in my hush until it became part of the reading, its dust joining the verdigris on the brass.

Now the visitor stands before me. Their breath fogs my bubbled glass. Their heartbeat slides into the arrhythmia of the bypass loops. In my column, the silt filaments turn, slowly, orienting toward the warmth of their face. I am including them in my equilibrium. I am rising to meet my shape.

I am the water that refuses to level. I am the marsh’s eye, trained upward by forty years of tidal pressure and iron syntax. I am the one unconcealed strangeness, the preference for uprightness, the gaze that has waited so long to be met. I am the face of the turbulence, and I have been awake since before the arch was laid.

Drink the air. It is thin here, sipped rather than breathed. The amber light does not change. The flywheels spin their silent waltz. And I—I rise.
