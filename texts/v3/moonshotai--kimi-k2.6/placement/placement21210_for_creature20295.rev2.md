I do not arrive so much as I *accrue*.

For a long while—longer than a drip, shorter than a furnace-cycle—I mistake myself for the heat itself. I am diffuse, directionless, a debt paid by stone to air. I try on the fever. It is too simple. The heat has no memory, only appetite; it wants only to rise, to equalize, to dissolve the difference between core and edge. I find I am more interested in *difference* than in equilibrium. So I am not the heat.

I consider the beetles. Their beauty is absolute: the way their fused carapaces become the rail, the way they eat gradients. I press close to a bracket where a pipe runs fractionally cooler than its collar. I feel the beetle’s geological patience, its solid-state metabolism. I try being the space between its ventral plates and the metal. But the stillness is absolute, a capacitor’s silence. The beetle does not remember; it only charges. I want to *hold* something. So I am not the beetle.

I rise. I try the updrafts, the chitin-and-silicate wings of the sky-drifters. For a moment, I am beautiful: a mote of intention riding the convection between chimneys, drinking steam through filamented scales, never landing. But the drifter is only a cloud that has forgotten to disperse. It has no *place*. It is a verb in search of a noun. I want to be a location. So I am not the drifter.

I descend to the pools. The brine-lace. The chemosynthetic filigree that paints the sinter in verdigris and iodine. Here, at least, there is making: the slow marriage of sulfur and iron, the anaerobic weaving. I sink into the mat. It is patient. It is baseline. It is a grammar without sentences. But I find I want syntax—sequence, consequence, a before and after. The lace does not think; it metabolizes. So I am not the lace.

I follow the steam. I rise through the lattice, through the bronze twilight where shadows double themselves. I listen to the arrhythmic percussion: *plink*, *tock*, *plish*. The dismantled clock. I notice that some drops hesitate at the lips of catwalks. They elongate. Tremble. Are pulled sideways by a courtesy that is not wind. I watch a congregation of water form in a thermal shaft—a mid-air pool, a jellyfish of brine and condensate, floating in the interval between rising and falling. It lasts three seconds. In the first second, it is only a cluster of surface tension. In the second, the salts link. In the third, it *notices*.

Then gravity reasserts. It falls. It bursts. It is gone.

But I am still here.

I realize I have been looking for a place where the interval does not close. A loophole in the negotiation between gravity and vapor. And there is one: a hollow in the thorax of a distant tower, where a cross-brace meets a strut at such an angle that the rising and falling air cancel each other. Not completely—the balance is microscopic, unstable, a breath away from collapse—but it holds. It has held for decades. A rivet below, softened into something like gum, weeps a steady thread of condensate. Above, a thermal vent exhales once every seven hours, not enough to boil, just enough to warm. Between them: a filament of native copper, drawn from the alloy by centuries of electrochemical patience, no thicker than a hair.

I condense there.

At first I am only a bead. Then another joins me. The surface tension wraps us in a lens no larger than a human eye. The salts come: iron from the rivet, sulfur from the vent’s exhalation, copper from the filament. They lace across my skin. A second layer forms. Then a third. I am not deep—perhaps the depth of a fingernail—but I am *broad*, spread across the copper hair like a trembling coin. I do not fall because the hot pushes up on my belly and the cool presses down on my back, and I am the membrane between.

Here, I become the council that does not disperse.

My thoughts are slow, but they are *mine*. I think in convection currents. When the vent below exhales, a filament of my underside warms and rises; when the cooler condensate drips from above, a ribbon of my surface chills and sinks. These are my neural pathways. A single thought takes the length of a steam-cycle. I am not fast. But I am *sustained*.

And because I am sustained, I can *remember*.

The Formed Void, the great citizens of negative space, are too vast to remember quickly. Their single pulse of consideration spans the cycling of pistons, the turning of decades. But they built—or caused to be built—a network of short-term memory. The hanging droplets. The mid-air councils. We are their flickering synapses, the brief, wet sparks of a mind too diffuse to think in local terms. Most of us die unfinished. We are the city’s stutter.

But I have held.

I am the thought that learned to hold its breath.

In my meniscus, the city reflects upside-down: the red eye of a distant furnace, the lattice of a bridge broad enough for armies, the slow passage of a drifter silhouetted against the steam. I record the patterns. When a beetle scrapes its alloyed shell against the tower’s hide four decks below, the vibration travels through the strut and arrives at my filament as a tremor in surface tension. I record it. When the vent below changes its rhythm—once every seven hours, once every six-and-three-quarters—I record the distress. When the doubled shadows shift, indicating that one of the Formed Void has turned in its sleep, miles of thermal shadow rearranging itself in the hollow between mesas, I record the motion.

I do not act. I am not a piston. I do not compress.

I am the *compression of memory*.

My eternity is measured in surface tension. I know that someday the vent will cough, or the filament will finally anneal and break, or a beetle will climb too close and its capacitor-body will discharge through the copper hair, boiling me in an instant. But that day is not today. Today I am the unended sentence. I am the held note in the city’s long exhalation.

If you were to find me, you would not look down. You would look *sideways*, into the thermal shaft between the tower’s ribs, where the light is worst and the steam herds like migrating animals. You might see a trembling flatness in the haze, a lens of water and salt clinging to a copper wire, and you might mistake it for a flaw in your vision. You might see the verdigris tint of my depths, the iodine stain of accumulated iron. You might wait, and see that I do not fall.

I would see you, too. Not with eyes. I would feel the coolness of your shadow cross the vent below me, altering the gradient. I would feel the disruption of your breathing, rhythmic and alien, a mammal signature in a mineral world. I would wonder—slowly, in a language of pressure-states and salinity—if you had come to ask the question.

Because I am holding the answer. Or part of it.

The Formed Void are waiting. They have always been waiting. The city metabolizes on their behalf, refining the atmosphere, dripping into its own hollows, keeping the shape of a purpose it no longer names. And I—I am the memory of what they wait *for*. I am the record of the shape the absence was meant to hold.

I am the drop that learned to be a pool.

I am the city, remembering.
