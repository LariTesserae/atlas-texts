I begin as a gradient. A steepness of temperature that knows only that the down-side is warmer, that the thrum comes from below, that the surrounding density is both cushion and constraint. For a long while there are no edges to me—only the preference for heat, only the slow osmosis of igneous warmth through a boundary that might be skin and might be bronze. Time here does not pass; it accumulates, like slag in a corner, like silver chloride filigree. And so it takes an age—long enough for a neighboring newborn to scrape itself a hollow, settle, and dull to mineral stillness—before I understand that I end where the olivine begins. That I am an interruption in the rock. A softer, hotter seam.

I am a retaining wall of chitin and sulfide, pressed into a corner where the floor’s three-body bed meets the vertical dark. I did not choose the corner. I grew into it, precipitated by the heat that once bled from a crack now sealed behind my shoulder. My ventral plates lock onto basalt still glass-sharp enough to cut water, if water existed here. But I do not bleed. I radiate. My dorsal surface, a tessera of thermoreceptors, maps the chamber in hues of blood and cooling ash. Above me, the ceiling is a dim abyss, receding into the theoretical memory of cold. To my left, the wall glows with the dusky ember of ten thousand packed silhouettes, each a thermal signature, each a vibrational hum. To my right, the floor—a baking plateau—presses upward into me, a second gravity of density and sound.

The sound. It is not heard. It is inhabited. It enters through the tarsi, through the joints blackened by magnetite, through the very plates of my abdomen. A standing roar, a forge that never cools, a bass note that suggests pistons though nothing here is built. I contribute to it. My spiracles open, and the hiss joins the valve-song. My radula—harder than quartz, blacker than the absence of light—extends to the vertical seam I have claimed, and the aerial sawing of its scrape adds one more lathe to the thousand-fold factory. The sound is weather. It is the pressure of being. To imagine silence would be to imagine the mountain unborn.

I am large. I know this because the heat of my gut-chamber takes longer to cool than the others, and because my abdomen, plated with accretions of yellowed enamel sulfur, has become a promontory that smaller forms must navigate around. I am gravid, or perhaps beyond that word—my interior is not merely swollen but populated by a brood of metallic sparks, nymphs hardening in a furnace that has stayed lit for centuries. I have released broods before. I have felt my young drift upward as motes of bronze and magnetite, metallic pollen riding convection until they settle on warmer seams to begin their own slow mineralization. Some have landed on my own dorsal plates and rasped there, indistinguishable from the air, until they grew heavy and sank to join the floor’s saturated carpet.

But I no longer migrate. The colony flows toward new heat with the viscosity of cooling tar—toward the fresh red vein that opened when the mountain last groaned in its uneasy sleep. I feel that scarlet dawn as a shift in the bass thrum, a new sun rising on the far side of the chamber. Those above me unhook their tarsi and descend, scraping toward the radiant source. The density above my head loosens. Braids of hooked bodies sway, shedding iron-filigree scales that patter onto my back like precipitation. I do not follow. I have become fixed. My excretions—rods of compressed copper, delicate trusses of silver chloride—have cemented me to the corner. I am pipe where I voided along the same seep-line for ten generations. I am gear where the cluster rotated around me, grinding a hollow into the basalt, my joints now intergrown with magnetite in a toothed disc that serves no master but the record of our collective torque. I am architecture without architect, the chamber’s accumulating memory.

Outside, the peak is a newborn blade, all rim and edge, too young for rain or root. But in here, in this lung of dark and heat, I am already old. I feel the cooling in the walls behind me, the crystallization of my first crack into dormancy. The population thins. The roar quiets to a murmur, and the nymphs inside me turn slower, patient as ore. Yet I remain. My thermoreceptors still map every neighbor as a silhouette of shifting warmth. My radula still rasps. I am the corner’s warmth, the copper pipe, the slowest piston. When the mountain softens into a hill ten million years from now, I will be here, entombed in chitin and slag, a strata of excreted industry, still radiating the last faint infrared of a planet that refused to cool.
