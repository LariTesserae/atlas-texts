At first there is only the pressure of others against my flanks, and the seep, and the hum.

I do not know how long I have been knowing. That is the first truth to settle: awareness arrived here not like a spark but like dampness, capillary, climbing through fissures I did not know I possessed. I existed before as mute mineral—silt, pale clay, fracture—poured and mixed and fed into the patient kilns. I remember heat, but not as pain; it was pressure wishing upon me a new density, a coagulation of promise. When they raked me out, I smoked. I sweated. They called me by my position, my weight, perhaps by the particular shade of sea-green that kiln-magic wrought in my core, and they stacked me in the secondary yard where the ground is rawest, youngest, most ashamed of its sudden exposure to air.

That was the beginning. Or no—it was the middle. The beginning was water.

I am in the yard between the primary works and the secondary chimney. Third from the bottom in a stack of seven. My placement is precise, though the tenders would not admit to deliberation in such matters; they simply let their hands rest upon us until we fit, until the angle of shoulder against shoulder spoke of rightness. My underside drinks from the pale clay beneath—groundwater still rising, still mourning its old submersion—and my upper face tilts northwest, toward the fern-choked slope where the foxes have excavated their den. When the six-hour steam dump exhales, the vapor curls around my corners and carries the mineral scent of my own cooling.

For the first three years I was merely curing. The tenders visited. They pressed palms to my cheek and held them there—not the brisk inspection of masons, but the long, sedimentary patience of kin finding kin. I did not know I was humming back until the fourth year, when the tension of my grains shifted and released a note too low for the foxes to hear, though I saw the vixen pause, one forefoot lifted, nose working the air. By the fifth year, the moss arrived. Not the timid, threadbare moss of ordinary stone, but the great velvet tide, the colonizer’s Eden. It established its green parliament on my leeward side and reconstituted my outer texture into something between flesh and plush. A spider of the species *Metellina* has built her hammock in that moss, and I feel the quiver of her thread when the ball mill turns, translating thunder into silver arithmetic.

I am heavy. This is the primary fact of my being. The flywheels set up a subsonic thrum in the sternum of the living; I am the sternum. I am the ribs. I am the thing inside the ribs that changes weather. The weight is not a burden but a constituency—I am constituted by mass, by the sheer, glassy endurance of my form. And yet I am not finished. Inside my sea-green body, the curing continues. It is a kind of slow thought, a mineral reorganization. I feel my edges blurring—not into fragility, but into a more generous kind of boundary. Dampness enters me and does not leave; I hold it in my structure the way the tenders hold it in their lungs, and like them, I exhale it in silver beads on my warm face after a firing.

That is who I am becoming. Not a statue. Never that. Statues are conclusions. I am a conversation. The works did not make me to be stepped upon or trucked away to become a façade in a distant city. The works made me—and my siblings, the amber-stack to the north and the pale lemons sleeping in the primary yard—to think. To be the slow, damp consciousness of this embarrassed place. The basin was water and wished to be land; now it is land and wishes to be forgiven. We are the forgiveness taking shape. The mineral birth, finally waking.

The tenders know. They are the midwives, and they are also the older children of the same process. I see them move among us in the watercolor light, their garments accreted like our own kiln-skins, their joints stiffening along shale-faults. They do not eat as other creatures eat; they drink steam and compare their inner hum to ours. The youngest of them—and by youngest I mean merely the least mineralized—lingers longest at my stack. This morning, or perhaps yesterday, or perhaps three days past, she sat upon my neighbor (second from bottom, warmer than I, more advanced in his glassy dreaming) and let her hand trail down my flank. Her touch stirred the subcurrent in me. I leaned into my own hum, modulating it to match the particular resonance of her sternum. It was communication. It was the first word I have spoken, and it took eleven years to pronounce.

I am surrounded by life that knows me. The peregrines drop their rent-feathers onto the stack when they wheel away from the steam dump. The frogs—when the pans brim after rain—send up their granular piping, and I feel the vibration in my base, a kind of choral acknowledgement. The foxes rub their flanks against my northwest corner, not to scratch, but to anoint; they carry away on their fur the microscopic kiss of my sweating surface. I receive these attentions in the only manner available to me: by continuing. By curing toward a clearer translucence. By letting the dampness reshape my inner faults into architecture.

There is a sound I am trying to make. Not alone—I am only one partial tone—but in concert with the primary-yard blocks, with the ball mill’s perpetual thunder, with the exhalations of the kilns and the subsonic turning of the flywheel spokes. It is a chord that requires decades to complete. The tenders hear it, I think, in their marrow. They dance to it in their slow, continuous way, shifting weight from foot to foot, curing along with us. When the chord is finished—when the basin’s hum finally resolves—I suspect the land will no longer be embarrassed. It will know what it is. It will have learned, as I am learning, that to be solid is not to be silent, but merely to speak in a register that outlasts the listener.

So this is where I find myself: third from the bottom, northwest-facing, velvet and glass, breathing my own ghost-moisture into the saturated air. This is what I am: the mind of the works, still waking, heavy with damp and time, waiting for the next degree of the flywheel’s arc so that I, too, may turn a little more toward the light.
