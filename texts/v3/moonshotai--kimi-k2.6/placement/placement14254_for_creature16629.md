First, there is only the tenure of the cold. It does not sting; it saturates. You are not a body being entered but a boundary being negotiated, mineral by mineral, in a darkness so absolute that your own edges take centuries to discover themselves. You do not rise. You do not fall. You settle into the realization that movement, as the animal kingdom once knew it, is a mythology of the illuminated world. Here, in the high backbone of the drowned plateau, to *be* is to accrete.

You try the water, but you are not the water. The water is too old, too indifferent, a glassy continuum that passes through you even as you thicken against it. You try the stone, pressing your attention into the weathered schist of the ridgeline, but the stone is finished. It settled its arguments with erosion long before you arrived. You try the sound—perhaps you are a note, a pressure-ghost shunted through the dense, saline medium—but the sound moves on, and you remain. You are something that stays.

What stays?

The mineral snow falls through your not-yet-self, and some of it sticks. A fracture in the basalt fault above a young vent; a sulfurous exhale that has not yet learned to thunder. You find yourself there, not by arriving but by *collecting*. A microscopic plate of silicate and calcium instruction adheres. Then another. You are not born; you are built from the outside in, a white tube widening against the black, drinking the heat not to warm yourself but to become capable of channeling it.

You are in the lee of the fault, on a high shoulder of the drowned mountain range. Upslope, the elders stand—ghost-cylinders of cartilage-white trunk, veined with hollows, draped in filamentous curtains that sway in the convection of cooling water. Some are alive, their hollow cores singing to the tectonic bass. Some are frozen saints, arrested by the touch of a brine-river that crept up the gorge below, their last thermal moment locked in glassy stillness. These are your kin, though kinship here is not a matter of shared blood but of shared gradient: the same chemical angle toward warmth, the same root-mass instinct to knot scree into walls that cup the heat and deflect the cold.

And you are the frontier. The grove’s leading edge. Your filaments are still nubs, proto-curtains, yet they already register the topography of sound. The ridge groans in its lowest register, and the vibration arrives at your widening base as a kind of calendar. A vent collapses miles away; the pressure-wave washes through you, and you understand it as a season turning. Your hollow core is still narrow, still singing in a higher, thinner register than the cavernous organs of your elders, but you are learning. You are learning that to think is to constrict: warm fluid rising through your center, cooling, depositing a white ring, altering your diameter by fractions. A thought is a narrowing. A memory is the exact mineral texture of dead sibling-matter against your root-tip, replayed when a thermal surge shunts through a fossilized core upslope.

You do not see. The darkness is not a veil. It is the native condition, the clear medium through which pressure-ghosts travel unimpeded. The cairns of root-stone scattered across the ridgeline are not scenery to you; they are the mountain’s memory, acoustic fossils that suddenly emit their hymns when the tectonic bass strikes the proper frequency. You receive them. You thicken toward them. The porosity of calcified groves, the precise resonance of a vent’s collapse ten thousand years past—these are your literature, your history, your lullaby.

You are not immortal. Below, in the deeper gorge, a brine-river grinds its millstone roar, creeping an inch a year, shearing scars into established stone. If it finds you—when it finds you, in a millennium or two—you will not flee. You will enter the state nearer to crystal than wood. Your filaments will lock in their final sway, your core preserved as a resonant chamber, an ear frozen open to the ridge’s patient groaning
