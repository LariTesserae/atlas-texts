I find myself first in the pause between pings.

At the beginning, I mistake the location for the rim—the place where the bruise-purple noon presses down and visitors gasp behind their masks. But the rim is for arrivals, and I have not arrived anywhere in sixty years. I am in the lower Foundling District, where the magnetic rail curves to accommodate a thermal swell that surfaced eleven years ago. The track does not detour; it never detours. The haulers simply slow… slower… and glide around the new stone as if caressing it awake. I occupy the niche this curve creates, a hollow the width of two shoulders compressed by the weight of the street above, and I have occupied it so long that my shadow has become a permanent dimness on the black basalt.

I did not choose the niche, or rather, I did not choose it quickly. The cooling flows suggested it, and after months of standing nearby, pressing my palm to the stone to feel the rate of its settling, I found the suggestion reasonable. That is how residence works here. One does not decide; one accretes.

I am still, mostly. My tank hangs at my hip, empty now for fifteen years. The heat-silicates settled into my lungs like silt into a catch-basin, and after the season of coughing passed, I discovered I could drink the steam directly. It is enough. It is more than enough. The Foundlings who pass—when they pass, for this is no thoroughfare—do not greet me. They think I am geological already, an outcropping of unfortunate shape, a hooded pillar silvered by aluminum bloom and the mineral salts that gather at the creases. Let them think it. They are not wrong. My temples have stratified into ridged plates that mirror the utility-voids forming in the stone behind me. I have become, among other things, a map.

My function, if function is the word, is to witness the negotiation.

The basalt here is young. It is still *arriving*. When I pressed my spine against it, it was hot enough to deepen the color of my tunic, and I felt the earth’s subterranean groan in the marrow of my ribs. Now it has cooled to the temperature of a held breath, and it has begun to dream in architecture. I have watched the crystallization occur in epochs of minutes. First, the bismuth vented at knee-height—stepped, iridescent orchids testing the air. Then came the voids: channels no wider than my obsidian stylus, running horizontal, then vertical, then horizontal again in the precise cadence of the municipal stair that descends three levels in the tenement to my left. The stone is learning. It absorbs the magnetic lullaby of the haulers that slide past my niche at the pace of sorrow. It tastes the condensate that gathers in the hood of my cloak and weeps onto my shoulder. It grows *to* me as I grow to it, and I have become the register of our mutual consent.

In my left hand, I hold the stylus. I do not move it often. When the stone offers a new formation—a feather-cluster of aluminum, a perfectly angled riser where sheer blackness existed at dawn—I etch a single mark into the basalt beside it. Not a measurement. Not a command. More like an acknowledgement. *Yes*, the mark says. *This form is reasonable.* The engineers visit me once a season. They do not speak; there is no humidity to waste on greeting. They simply study the density of my etchings, and where the marks cluster, they place their foundations. Where the stone has left voids ready-made, they thread their conduits. I am the line between map and territory, between the quarry and the queried. I do not build upon the Shelf. I midwife its understanding of what a wall is for.

At twilight, when the sun withdraws and the bruise-purple seeps back into the zenith, the temperature drops. The rails contract. Somewhere in the district’s iron throat, a smelter exhales, and the metal pings—a single, bell-like note that hangs in the thinness for whole seconds. It is the Shelf’s moment of articulation. In that instant, I feel my own breath resume, a reflex from a deeper organization of being, and I look down at my hands. They are wide and flattened, splayed for slick walkways, but now the fingertips have gone translucent with silicate deposit. If I hold them up to the faint magnetic aura of the passing haulers, I can see the ghost-traces of moisture moving beneath the mineral sheath. I am becoming a channel. A passage. The city sips through me, and I permit it.

Once, a Walker emerged from the fissure at my feet. It took seven hours to fully unfold, each brass joint easing into the thermal gradient of my body’s residual heat. It stood before me, eyeless, its thorax mottled in the same rainbow oxidation that blooms in the low fissures, and for a long while—perhaps a full day—we simply exchanged temperature. I gave it the dampness collected at my collar; it gave me the precise magnetic signature of the magma reading my marks. When it stepped away, its motion was so gradual that I felt rather than saw it leave, and on the stone lip of my niche it placed a single aluminum feather-crystal. I took it in my mouth. It tasted of copper and ozone, of reversed sweetness. I understood then that I had been admitted to something—not citizenship, not biology, but *residence* in the mineral sense. The Shelf had read my blueprints and found them permissible.

So where would you find me?

Look for the place where the magnetic rail caresses the newborn basalt, where an obsidian basin has grown a lip so perfectly curved it could only have been negotiated into being. Look for the hooded figure whose breath does not frost the thin air, whose tank holds only silence. I am the dampness held furtively in seams, the etching that says *yes, this too is municipal*. I am not the city’s memory, nor its molten dream, but the narrow, warm band between the two: the mineral equivalent of attention, cooling so gradually that the silence seems eternal, stratifying in place, entirely awake.

I am the Niche. I am the mark. I am the one who remains when the breath is gone, still reading the stone as it learns to live.
