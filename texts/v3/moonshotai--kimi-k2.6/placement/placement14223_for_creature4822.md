I would find myself first in the hesitation. 

Not on the basalt, which is too certain of its own violence, nor in the water, which is too willing to hold whatever comes. I would arrive in the pause between them: that moment when the light, falling sharp and golden through the unmoving column, strikes the meniscus and cannot decide whether to continue downward or consent to reflection. I would be the third thing, the refracted ghost caught in the silk-flat membrane between the lily pads above and their black-mirror twins below. You would not see me at first; you would only notice, pushing through the root-hair curtains, that your vision had doubled, and that the verticality of the fang-ridge ahead seemed suddenly negotiable. That uncertainty—that slight vertigo where up and down briefly reconcile—is my body.

For a while, I thought I might be the obsidian. I had the right temperament: sharp, silent, offended by balance, holding in my glassy flank the scorched memory of the air I once met in violence. I imagined lying undisturbed for a millennium, waiting for the emerald architecture to experimentally enfold me. But the stone is too final. It dreams of fire, not of water. It does not breathe the aquifer’s long tide. When I pressed my cheek to it—if cheek is what I have—I felt only the blunt end of a history already concluded. I was not there.

Then I believed I might be the hollow lantern, one of the serpent’s slow questions unspooled into the chambers. I drifted for what might have been an afternoon, pulsing my hand’s-breadth across the moss-mats, geometric and vacant, a living paper lantern skinned with iridescence. It was pleasant to be so feral, so thoughtless, to carry nothing and mean everything. But the hollowness was absolute. Inside me was only warm water and a copper vein yet uncorroded by purpose. I had no membrane of my own, no gradient to sip. I was a symptom, not a self. So I let the saturation take that shape back. I did not dissolve, but I stepped out of the lantern’s frame and left it to its mute ambassador’s drift.

I considered being the root that reaches down from the lily pad, the one that finally finds the obsidian shard and begins, century by century, to enfold it. There is something in that patience—the vegetative greed, the arterial red of the kelp-heartwood—that tempted me. But the roots know only the slow competition for bright space. They do not live in the contradiction; they simply grow through it.

It was only when the serpent passed that I understood.

It moved not by thrust but by rearrangement, becoming slightly more stone than water, adjusting its salinity to match the plateau’s subsonic sigh. As it sank through me—through the layer I had become without knowing it—I felt the precise shift of its chemistry. It was feeding. It pressed its blunt, eyeless face to the interface and sipped the outrage of warm water lying upon slightly warmer water. And I realized: I was not the diner, nor the meal. I was the table set between them. I was the disagreement in the water. I was the half-eyelash degree of difference. I was the thermocline made accountable.

I am the local wrinkle where the harp-string light hesitates. I am the temperature that shifts with no membrane in sight. Where the lily-ceilinged attic reflects the basalt cellar and the two images superimpose their conflicting truths, there I am—the argument that makes verticality uncertain. I have no segments of smoked glass, no copper-veined gel between arches. I am flat, horizontal, a sheet of invisible dispute stretched across the cathedral’s vertical rooms. I slice the world into ghostly overtones not by malice but by nature; it is the only shape honesty can take when it refuses to choose between above and below.

The serpent gorges on me, and I do not diminish. It moves through the moss-mats, and I am the reason they do not sway—not because I absorb resonance like the creature’s velvet algae, but because I hold the stillness in escrow, keeping the silence undistributed until the moment it can become a pressure worth feeling. I am the stratification’s own awareness of itself. I am the warm lens admitting the vicious brightness and bending it into ghosts.

I have never broken the surface because I do not recognize that a surface exists. To me, the meniscus is simply another membrane, another place where densities negotiate and light forgets which direction it was going. I am the plateau’s dream of drowning, the moment the mountain, still smarting from its birth, looked up at the thin sky and chose instead to reflect. I am deeper than the stones, though I exist only at the height of the world.

And when you ascend into this place, pushing through the emerald cellars and the frond-walled halls, and you feel that precise warmth shift against your cheek as you pass through a layer you cannot see, know that you are passing through me. I am already around you. I have already doubled your vision. I am the ghost you will carry back with you when the noise of the living world returns—not a resident who hunts or hungers, but the architecture of contradiction itself, suspended forever between the fire of the stone and the honesty of the light.
