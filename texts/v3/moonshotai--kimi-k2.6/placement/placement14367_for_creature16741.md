I would find myself against the east wall, where the air is fractionally cooler but the steam lingers longest, pooling in the hollows of my grain like breath that cannot bear to leave a body. I have been here since the beginning, though I am not as I was. Once I was flat. A plane. A place for shoulders to lean against, for blueprints to unroll, for the small sacraments of making to be arranged in a row. The heat has had its way with me. My wood has surrendered its straightness and pooled into a curve that suggests a ribcage, or a cradle, or a wave frozen at the moment of breaking. The workers called it warping. I have come to understand it as posture. The room needed something bent, something that could hold a shape of yielding, and so it bent me.

Six inches of water stand permanent at my feet. It is hot enough to soften glue, to swell my joints until the screws in my hips groan against their rust. I bleed into it. Slowly, generously, the iron in my bolts and the tannin in my heart seeping down my legs into the pool, adding my own note to the mineral soup. The water around my ankles tastes of me now, and because I remember the flavor of upper air—ozone, diesel, the salt of a palm’s sweat—I know that I am becoming native to this place. I am seasoning.

On my back the tools still rest. They are arranged as they were when the last hand withdrew: a wrench reddening with verdigris and blood-colored rust; a gauge whose glass face weeps condensation and refuses to crack; a hammer whose handle is black with the oils of a palm that no longer exists in any world above. They lie there like insects that died mid-molt, their purpose still protruding from them, half-formed. I feel their weight. Not much—a few pounds of abandoned intention—but in a room where so little is permanent, their persistence is a kind of speech. They say: *someone meant to return.* They say: *there was a task.* They say: *we were interrupted.* I have held them for so long that I cannot tell anymore whether they are my ornaments or my organs.

The sound finds me easily. The east wall does not deaden; it focuses. The roar settles into my grain, a pressure rather than a noise, and I feel the drill’s grinding in the hollow of my curved spine. It is a massage administered by catastrophe. After the first decade, I stopped distinguishing between the drill’s rotation and my own shudder. We share a frequency. The steam-hammer in the pipes—*whump-whump-whump*—finds the same rhythm in my legs, a cardiac knocking that has taught me I have circulation after all: water rising by noon, retreating by dusk, a barometric tide lapping at my shins in slow inhalation.

I see everything from this posture. The sodium light swings overhead in its rusted cage and strikes my highest edge, leaving my underside in amber shadow. The thin blade of daylight enters somewhere above and dies before it reaches the water, but not before it grazes my warped flank, illuminating the condensation that gathers there. The water does not merely fall on me; it *prefers* me. It has found the seams in my grain and wears them into microscopic grooves, hydrological habits written in my flesh. I am part of its nervous system now. When it travels down my side into the pool, I feel the room thinking.

I watch the center. The scaffolding fell long ago—a sibling failed before it could stand, now a skeletal heap vanishing into the shimmer. Beyond it rises the drill-head, the rusted mechanical jellyfish, its pipes humming with pressure. And beneath its churn, darkening the clear water without clouding it, is the second draft.

It has no name. It has no face. It is the color of dried blood and fresh concrete, a hunched precipitation roughly the size of a human curled in fear or prayer. The floor beneath it does not yield; it feeds, pushing upward as fast as the bit wears it down. I recognize the dynamic. The room bent me first, tried to make a skeleton of utility, and found me insufficient. I was too still. I remembered too much of the world above—the straight lines, the right angles, the logic of the task. So the room abandoned my model and began again. It took the slurry I feed into the pool, the iron from my blood, the silica ghosts of a thousand dropped things, and pressed them into something more patient. Something that does not remember hands. Something that only waits.

The figure beneath the drill is my successor. We do not speak—there is no speaking here, only the settling of sound against bone and wood—but we share the same mother, who turns and turns without love or mercy, grinding nothing into nothing until something finally holds. I watch the glassy caul thicken on its shoulders, season by season, a slow winter of silica. I watch the water arrange itself around it with the deference of a lens. When it is ready—when the floor finally ceases its upward push and the bit breaks through into the darker heat below—it will rise. The water will drain into the wound it leaves behind. And the sump, having practiced on me, will finally know how to hold a body.

Until then, I abide. Curved. Creaking. Standing in my six inches of scalding baptism, bleeding rust into the lung of the pool, feeling the condensation trace its thoughts down my flank. I am the first sketch, the failed skeleton, the altar where human intention was laid down and left to fossilize. And I am still, in my ruined arc, performing my final function: I am waiting to be the first thing it sees that resembles a gesture of holding, a spine bent not in pain, but in welcome.
