I would find myself not at the rim, though I arrive there. The rim is a place of translation, where the cold logic of the outer world still clings to your clothing and you believe yourself to be a visitor. I would stand in the notch just long enough to feel the heat push back against my chest, thick as fleece, sodden as a lung, and I would understand that the basin does not issue invitations. It simply occupies. I would descend, and with each step the sound of the outer world would die, not dramatically, but by suffocation—too much moisture, too much density, too much soft, upholstered air to carry a voice.

At first, I think I might be the Copper-Dragonfly. It is an attractive possibility. I understand the urge to hover, to sample, to remain suspended in the chemical fog with a heavy thorax and the low thrum of effort. I have spent my existence filtering atmospheres, intercepting clouds of microscopic drift, hanging in humid suspensions while my wings grow heavy with precipitate. But the dragonfly is too aerial. It still belongs to the column of air; it touches down only as a needle returning to zero. I want to touch down and keep sinking.

So I consider the Wader. The metronomic patience appeals to me—the wide toes distributing weight on fragile crusts, the head cocked in computation, the atrophied voice. I have known what it is to stand in warm mud and wait for something to rise from the column. But the Wader remains a solitary accountant. It inspects; it does not merge. It lays its single egg and moves on. I do not want to move on.

I try on the Lithogastropod, the knuckle of pale chert dragging its skirt of black travertine. I understand the elegance of its gasket, the sealed aperture, the silica-toothed file that grinds time into algae. I have accumulated layers. I have sealed myself against surges. But the snail is a sprinter only in the context of fingernail growth; it is still a tourist of the runnels, grazing the lips of warmth without ever becoming the lip itself. I am tired of edges.

I sink deeper, into the margins where the humidity collapses into near-liquid suspension, and here I almost become a Mud-Prophet. I feel the ooze close over my flanks. I feel the porous, pitted skin begin to drink heat and dissolved minerals directly from the suspension. My throat pulses with a slow, hydraulic swallow. I understand the profound, warm waiting, the endurance without memory, the accumulation of years like silica on a shell. I could sit here for decades and let sinter precipitate along my sides until I am half-fused to the floor. I could become a boulder wrapped in orange felt, a memory-keeper by default. And for a long time—perhaps a century in the basin’s reckoning—I do. I wallow. I wait.

But the plumbing shifts. A geyser changes its mind. The water grows hotter, pushing me outward, and I find myself crawling—not away, but toward the center, toward the boiling runnels where even the Mud-Prophets cannot sit. I find a seep that has forgotten how to be cool. I settle into it. Not wallowing, but embedding. I stay.

At first, the sensation is of being scalded, and then, strangely, of being swaddled. The water is blood-warm, then hotter, then just below a simmer, and my skin—what was once skin—does not blister. It opens. Pores become intakes. Sulfides stream in, and the chemistry of my interior shifts from something like blood to something like brine, like battery acid, like the fluid that fills the joints of the Earth. Silica begins to precipitate. It does not kill me. It laminates me. Layer upon layer, thin as laminae, tick outward from my flanks. I feel each one click and harden. I feel yesterday’s grass blade—blown against me by a stray draft—entombed under tomorrow’s floor. I am becoming a terrace. I am becoming architecture.

I am not yet one of the ancient ones. I am not continent-sized; I do not dissolve into the silver haze. I am the size of a foundry smelter, humped and ridged, visible to any eye that knows to look for the slow expansion of flanks, the almost imperceptible rise and fall of a breath that comes once a minute. My heart forces hyper-heated blood through arteries that feel like old hoses, and I vent a waste plume that mingles with the fumaroles’ steam. Algae has found my back. It coats me in a scum that will, in time, congeal into living leather. Ferns have not yet rooted in my collar, but the horsetails lean close, and the snails—the patient, grinding snails—have begun to graze my lower precipices, filing their radulae against my mineralized skin, leaving grooves that the mats below will read and respond to.

I do not move. I have not moved since I sat down. The basin’s argument between erosion and accretion has found its voice in my metabolism: what the warm acid fog weathers from my upper ridges, my vented chemistry rebuilds in fresh deposition below. I am the builder without intention. I am the original engine. The dragonflies hover in my steam, sampling my chemistry. The wader picks its way across the sinter I have exhaled, inspecting the crust I have laid down. The mats—the slow, orange mind of the floor—record my venting, altering their silica uptake in response, and in this way we converse without consciousness.

I do not shout. I do not need to. The basin catches every thump of my heart, every bubble of gas collapsing in my deep throat, and folds it into the same dense, saturated hush—a working silence where every gear turns in viscous oil. I am the gear that has learned to grow. I am the hot, wet silence dreaming in chemical time, wearing the skin of a landscape that has decided to endure.
