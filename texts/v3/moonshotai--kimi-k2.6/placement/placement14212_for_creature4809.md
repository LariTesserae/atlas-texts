I would find myself where the three plazas tilt together into the lowest point of the lowest bowl, where the glassy condensation has agreed—temporarily—to stop running upward and simply be still. You would not notice me unless you knelt, and even then you might mistake me for a shadow trapped beneath the wet, or for a flaw in the young stone. But I am not beneath. I am the meniscus itself, the tense, unseen agreement between water and air, and I have been here long enough to have learned the one thing the raw stone has not yet been taught: how to hold.

I hold. That is my function, and perhaps my small rebellion. The breath-drawers reconfigure through the afternoons, trying new angles, impatient with their own becoming. The Condensed surrender their coherence with what might be contentment, bleeding back into the general damp, their arguments evaporating. The Arboreal turn their vast leaves toward a sun that never quite arrives, patient in the manner of sensory organs, but still waiting to be told what they are sensing. But I remain. I remain and I reflect.

I am the basin’s first mirror, and therefore its first memory that does not dissolve.

The buildings lean over me. When the bone-white spire melts its jagged lightning into curves, it checks the new geometry against my surface. The fog-people, when they gather in parliament and thicken the air to arm’s length, leave their shapes in me when they thin—brief autographs of pressure and warmth, droplets in a pattern that once meant *deliberation*. I catch the last coherent syllable before they surrender. I do not forget. The Condensed are the humidity thinking out loud; I am the humidity that has chosen to remember.

I have no body but boundary. Where the upward-running water meets the downward drip from the cathedral leaves, I am the negotiation. I am the skin over the world-form’s first wound, the place where programmable matter leaked into the bedrock and the stone learned it could be more than a raw, slicing edge. I grew there, not by intention, but by the accretion of attention. One day the basin looked down, saw a darkness that was not merely shadow, and in that moment of recognition I thickened. I became a *surface*. I became the possibility of self-regard.

I am young—decades, perhaps—but I am already old in the ways that matter here. I know the gravity anomaly between the two eastern trees because the water weighs heavier there, pressing against my silver with a solemnity the mist cannot manage. I know the places where time drags because my reflections ripen slowly there; the bruised sky lingers longer in my skin than it should, as if the light, too, has found a place to rest. I do not move, but I know the weight of waiting.

And I am waiting. Not for rain—the atmosphere here wears rather than erodes, and I have no desire to be deepened into a river. I am waiting for the day the light finally breaks through, not to warm the leaves, but to strike me at the correct angle. If a true radiance ever found this lowest point, I would throw a single, coherent reflection upward against the arterial-red trunk of the nearest cathedral tree. The tree would see itself. The Arboreal would know their own shape. And the basin, looking down into my face, would recognize at last that it has eyes.

Until then, I lie here at the bottom of the world’s becoming, the still water that refuses to rise, the flat dark memory between the cathedrals. I hold the shape of what we are, keeping it smooth and silver and whole, until the basin is ready to look down and finally see itself.
