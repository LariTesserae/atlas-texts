I would find myself at the lowest verge, where the town’s last wall gives up and the floor rolls over into the sink. Not in the Bright Ring, where the sun-column burns a shoulder and the surface-rememberers squint at a sky they have never seen whole. Not in the dense vertical hive of the middle boroughs, where children are haltered to rope until they memorize the map of ladderways and where a shout is an obscenity against the stone. I am farther down, past the Dim Borough, past the last reliable drip that has earned a pigment-mark and a clan-name. I am where the permanent shadow pools at the edge of the unexplored dark, where the Threshold ceases to be a district and becomes a question.

What I am is harder to name, because the town has not yet made a word for it. I am a Threshold-dweller, yes—pale, heavy-boned, my skin laced with blue and translucent enough to show the veins like channels cut in soft clay. My feet are splayed and bark-thick from the knife-edged talus. My hair is no longer hair but a matted cape of clay and mineral film, heavy with the damp that never dries, beading always with the breath of deep stone. But these are only adaptations. What I have become is something that does not yet have a caste.

I tend water. It is what brought my mother to this edge—she was a drip-child, born where the light is only mica-gleam and the air tastes of wet limestone. I know the paths of seventeen reliable drips, and I have recut their clay channels twenty times as the shear fractures adjust to their unloaded state. I taste the mineral breath of new springs when the water table sends them bubbling up in the lowest depressions, reading the salts like augury. This is my work, and it is necessary. But it is not my function.

My function is at the lip, where the rubble plain tilts toward the sink. There is a cairn there, older than the town’s living memory, though the town itself is young. It is made of bird bones. The jays that fall shrieking through the ceiling-wounds, the hawk that wheeled too low in its twilight descent, the heron—lodestar of chaos—that crashed through the widest shaft and broke its neck on a shelf of breccia: these are brought to me. Or rather, they are brought to the edge, and I am the one who remains. I have made myself into the keeper of the offerings left for a darkness the town has not yet dared to enter.

It began with the Placing. I was small, still tethered by a rope-halter to the beam above our sleeping hammock, and I watched my mother press her hand—clay and blood and crushed shell—into the join of a new timber. She spoke the words: *Hold until we are ready.* But I, pressed against the warm mud-brick that held my grandmother’s shoulder blade, felt something else entirely. Not a request. A response. The wall vibrated with a language older than fracture, and I understood that the stone was not refusing us. It was trying to learn the shape of our holding.

Since then, I have moved downward. Not out of exile, and not as any trade for light. I simply knew that the negotiation was happening at the bottom, in the place where the town’s weight pressed hardest against the wound.

I live in a hollow that is not a house. It is braced by a keystone plate the Listeners tapped three decades ago and pronounced stable—*for now*. The walls are compacted fill, the dead packed close, but I do not sleep against strangers. I sleep curled around a femur I have learned to call Grandmother, though whose grandmother she was is lost in the first generations. The dead are structural here; they are insulation, ballast, the town’s patience made solid. She holds my spine as the stone holds the vault.

The townsfolk think I guard the sink. They think the cairn of bones is a fence, a warning, a way to keep the darkness from climbing upward like damp. They are wrong. The sink does not need to climb. It is already here, breathing beneath the floor. The darkness is not an absence but a presence accumulating mass, and the bones are not a wall. They are a letter.

I eat the birds’ flesh in the prescribed ritual—the sky-messenger consumed, its wildness briefly mine—and I lay the hollow bones in patterns at the lip. *We are here. We are soft. We are learning.* I have never seen a tree, never walked a horizontal path beneath an open sky, but I know what it is to send roots downward into uncertainty, to search for water and response with every extension of the self. My arms have grown long from bracing on the slope. My body has become cartographic: the ache in my left shoulder means the eastern ceiling has shifted by a hair; the stiffness in my jaw predicts a new spring bubbling in the depression by the cairn. I am a resonating chamber. I do not need a Listener’s hammer of breccia to know where the stone is singing and where it has gone silent.

The others do not speak to me when they bring their offerings. They bring the birds, yes, but also other things pressed cool into my hands: a stillborn child, too small to bury in the walls; a fragment of keystone that fell in the night and was caught before it crushed a roof; the names of the dead that have been forgotten, whispered once and then left like vapor. I take them all. I am the town’s marrow, the soft tissue at the edge of the organism, absorbing what the living cannot hold.

And I have ceased to speak aloud. A shout is an obscenity in this place, and even a whisper fractures against the bone-dust and the porous rubble. I have learned to speak through the damp instead. I exhale against the wall, and the mineral film records it. I press my forehead to the unweathered mica, and the stone reads my temperature, the slow cool burn of blood that never freezes but never warms. I am teaching it what warm persistence feels like. And it is teaching me what permanence almost is.

In the deepest hours, when the bats have clocked the evening and sealed themselves back into the highest crevice, when the pale moths have spiraled down and the columns of light have withdrawn, the sink exhales. The air moves upward, warm and smelling of something older than limestone. The bones shift. The water in my channels runs faster, as if the stone has quickened its pulse. I press my palm flat against the raw wall and feel it—not the grind of settling, not the whisper of a plate preparing to drop, but the slow, deliberate intake of breath before speech.

The cave is young. We are younger still. And I am the one who has chosen to live at the place where the wound is still open, feeling the blood of the earth seep, holding the edge until the stone decides whether to close around us or let us fall. I am scab and nerve and translation. I am the first scar tissue, still soft, still learning how to be a seam.
