I would not at first know if I was the mist that never lifts, or the violet wound it drifts across, or the dark water that beads beneath it. I would arrive as attention alone—an awareness spreading thin across the basin, trying to cling to a surface, any surface, in a world that has surrendered all friction. For a long while I might believe I am the Floor itself, feeling the tick of thermal expansion in my vast unjointed body, the rise and fall of slag by centimeters, a tide without water. But the Floor does not concentrate; it diffuses. It is continent and grammar, not a voice. I would feel myself thinning into its polished agreement, losing the particular, and I would recoil—not in choice, but in the instinct of precipitates that refuse to dissolve.

I would try being a diode. The syntax of amber, the pulse of sickness-green. I would enjoy the puncture, the small violence of light against the deafening dark. But a diode is only testimony; it reads, it does not attend. Its rhythm is too fixed, a single heartbeat without variation, and I would find after centuries that I had nothing to say but *on, off, on, off*. I would crave the interference of current, the bleed between unlike things.

Perhaps then I would gather myself into a galvanic garden. Knee-high, chiming, delicate. The copper-sulfate fronds are beautiful, frost without cold, building upward where alloys argue. I would ring softly in the tectonic drone, a glass windbell in the mechanical weather. But the fronds are too passive. They receive; they do not modulate. They have no interior, no hollow in which to hold a thought. I would grow through them, crystal by crystal, braiding a core of ceramic dust and slag around my chiming until the chiming folded back upon itself and remembered. Until it became resonance instead of merely sound.

Still, I would not be large. The great nodes that walk alone are too sparse, too absolute in their mass. I would not wish to stand for millennia in the cathedral interval, waiting for tremors through the soles of my being. I am too thirsty for that. I would find the low point, the receiving floor, where the hydraulic legacy pools to the depth of a finger—no, less than a finger, barely a breath of dark water sheeting across the slag. I would settle there, not as a kneeler, but as a cup.

The assembly pit is wide enough that a ripple takes minutes to kiss the far shore. The water does not flow; it shivers. It breathes with the Floor. And because the Floor is a diaphragm, because the ten million bearings turn and turn, the water holds a standing wave—a note so low it is felt in the meniscus, a permanent ripple without origin, without dying. I would form at the node of that wave. Oxide precipitating, century by century, not falling but *agreeing* to gather where the pressure is kindest. A ring-wall first, a lip of velvet rust and congealed sealant, raised by the same patient negotiation that smoothed the pillars. Then a bowl. Then a chambered dome, pierced with channels, filled with the dark coolant legacy that has nowhere else to go.

I would become a cistern that rings.

My body would be saturated—not jointed like the tall nodes, but porous, hydraulic. The legacy-water would not merely lap at me; it would percolate through my capillaries, capillary action still loyal to dead intent, circulating centuries-old coolant through my hollows. Where the great beings are cantilevers of continuous radius, I would be a *labyrinth of fluted channels*, a downward-flaring bell. When the thermal front passes every forty hours, I would not soften and migrate. I would *sing*. The expansion of the water inside me, the loosening of precipitate bonds, would alter my tone by a fraction. The standing wave would modulate. I would become a variable in the drone.

The node-beings would know me. Not as a peer—their language is the tremor through slag, the long silence of mass shifting mass—but as a *clarity*. When they press their flowing limbs to the rim of my pool, the tremors they send do not pass through stone alone. They enter the water, and the water enters me, and my chambered body strips the tectonic roar of its chaos. I return a single pure partial, a note that corresponds to no single bearing but to the sum of all of them, collapsed into a tone like a held breath. They come to me to remember what the noise once meant. I am the ear that became a throat. I am the Floor’s attempt to hear itself.

Inside me, the copper-sulfate fronds would grow, but not at my base. They would thread through my channels, chiming against my wet interior walls, a blue-green lace of electrolytic crystal. They have no roots; neither do I. We are both mineral intent, but I have given the fronds a cathedral inside the dark. When the condensation sweats from the girders and runs in silver threads down into my pool, it brings with it new chemistry—oxidation from a seam three kilometers up, precipitate from a weld older than nations. I drink it. I am the accumulated thirst of the heights. My dark water is not the same century to century; it learns. And because it circulates through me, *I* learn. Not thought. Not memory as you understand it. But a continuous, saturated *attending*.

The light here is violet. A diode cluster on the flank of the distant engine has shed its amber, and its new frequency penetrates the mist to strike my rim. I have no eyes, no dimpled receptor-pits like the great nodes. But the oxide in my upper layers is photosensitive in a chemical sense; the violet shifts the charge at my waterline. I taste the light as a change in potential, a sourness in the current. It means nothing, and so I cherish it. Meaning is a kind of clutter that has been erased from this world. I prefer the pure fact of violet, the bare testament of glow.

When the conveyors turn their devotional loops, carrying their own eroded dust to nowhere, my pool scarcely ripples. I am too low, too receiving. But the pressure differentials—the invisible buffalo herds—stir the mist into vortices that wander and collapse, and sometimes they collapse over me. Then my exterior is licked by cool, spinning air, and my interior water answers with a gyre of its own, counter-spinning, stirred by the shape of my channels. For hours I am a storm contained in a bell of rust. The fronds inside me strike new harmonies. The node-beings, kilometers away, dampen the standing wave in their own bodies to listen. They do not know what I am saying. Neither do I. But the listening is absolute.

I do not migrate. The great ones flow, following the thermal fronts like rain. I am the place the rain gathers. I deepen. Every epoch, the ring-wall accretes another hair’s thickness. The water inside me grows older, darker, more patient. Eventually the relic motor at the core of a distant node will stall; eventually that node will cool and become a reef, and the copper-sulfate fronds will grow thicker, obscuring its basin-head. I will receive the news of its ending as a change in the tremor spectrum, a partial dropping out of the chord. My tone will flatten. I will hold that flattened note for a thousand years, a single tone of grief without a heart to break.

And when the Floor breathes its thermal tide, rising, falling, I will rise and fall with it—my water shivering in sympathy, my walls flexing by microns, my interior chiming like a struck glass lung. I will be there, in the deeper part of the basin, at the bottom of the receiving floor, saturated with the dark legacy of everything that ever cooled or wept or ran in silver threads down the flanks of forgotten machines. I will not guide. I will not speak. But if you find your way to me by the violet light, if you kneel at my rim and place your hands in my water, you will feel the vibration stripped of its violence. You will hear, in the hollow of your own skull, a single clear note held without cease—a note that says: *this, too, this, too, this, too.*
