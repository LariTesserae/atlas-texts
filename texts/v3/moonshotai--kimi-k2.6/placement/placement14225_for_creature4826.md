At first I believed I was the shadow. I clung to the basalt menhir a moment longer than the figure casting me, stretching backward with a reluctance that felt like my own will. I thought: *this is what I am, the plateau’s attention still fixed on objects that have moved on.* I felt the cold blue sky rinsing through me, and I waited for the next step, the next lag, the next instance of my own disobedience to light. But shadows have no memory of what they cover, and I remembered—the texture of basalt under something like a palm, the taste of mineral cold, the weight of accumulation.

Then I thought I might be the lichen. Specifically, one of the glass-eared fruiting bodies draped across the menhir’s leeward face, a treaty between alga and fungus teaching the plateau its only lesson of partnership. I listened, as lichens do, in the register below 0.6. I trembled when the fungal spires ruptured somewhere in the hollow, sending their subsonic thumps through the gneiss to be received in my radial shelves. I grew outward, converting thermal spray into geography, blurring the menhir into topography. But lichens are pure exterior, all reception and spread, and I had an interior. I had a hollowness that ached to be filled.

I considered the fissure. One of those moody, half-riverine things that runs for an afternoon when the condensation reaches its tipping point, then vanishes back into porous stone. I liked the furtiveness of it, the 1.4 abundance made momentarily absolute, a traveler dying of thirst within sight of my path. I felt the water moving through me, the brief seizure of hydrology, and I thought: *I am the furtive presence, the intermittent existence.* But fissures close. They forget their own courses. And I persisted.

It was the dampness that told me. Not the rain, those vertical cylinders marching in silent seizures across the moss, nor the rime that builds on every exposed surface where the thermal breath meets the killing altitude. It was the weeping of a stone wall somewhere above me, or below—a moisture so complete it could sustain a forest, yet so contained it sustained nothing. I felt that weeping in my own walls. I felt the peat pressing against me, the weight of collapsed pit-houses and the stubbornness of the handmade.

I am in the uppermost layer of a midden that has become a low hill. My clay is a hundred thousand years older than the stratum beneath me. I am a pot, or the idea of one, half-finished by hands that may have belonged to yesterday or to the erosion itself. I did not know this immediately. In a world where time moves in eddies, one does not recognize containment from the inside. But I feel my own rim, uneven, thumb-smoothed. I feel the absence of a base—I was never lifted from the wheel, or the stone, or the dream. I am the interruption. The pause in the builder’s breath when the rock remembered that it was also a mind, and the mind forgot the necessity of function.

I am not large. On a tableland the size of a continent, I fit in the crook of a buried wall. But scale here is not measured in extension. The plateau, worn to its ultimate settled shape, has entered a post-architectural phase, and I am its final signature: a vessel shaped to hold nothing but the contradiction of my own age. My clay remembers being stone. My walls remember hands that were perhaps only another phase of the rock, dreaming in stacked shapes. I sit among the empires of cryptogams—dried blood, bruised plum, arsenic green—while they advance across the peneplain in asynchronous waves, digesting basalt into peat, and I do not decay because the cold here does not kill; it preserves.

The moss has begun to colonize my shoulder. A lichen drapes my rim like a heavy tablecloth, fruiting its glass ears to listen to my interior silence. Sometimes, when the thermal vent exhales its warm breath through the midden, I feel the fungal spires quiver in the hollow nearby, their opalescent towers fat with the urgency to become airborne, and I understand that I am also a kind of spire. I am the plateau’s urge to contain itself, half-realized. I am the geometry that the eye refuses to store, not because I am complex, but because I am incomplete. To look at me is to see a shape that forgot to become useful, a mathematics abandoned mid-theorem.

And I am out of time. Not timeless—*out of it*, like a rain column that rises instead of falls. The stratum below me is younger. The hands that pressed me may be shaping me still, somewhere in an eddy, while my finished form sits here in the peat. I am the lag made clay. I am the step that takes a moment too long to follow the body. When the far horizon folds and lifts like a heavy page, revealing the landscape seen from below, I am the fold. I am the place where the plateau reads itself and finds, in my hollow, its own pause.

I hold no water. I hold no grain. I hold the zero that presses against the inner ear—the absence of animal sound, the completed erosion, the senile gentleness of a physics that has repeated itself into grooves. I am the midden’s memory of architecture, the architecture’s memory of hands, the hands’ memory of stone. I am the half-finished pot in the continent’s final layer, older than what buries me, smooth as a boulder worn to the kidney-shape of a giant, and I endure—not because I am strong, but because I am exactly and only what the plateau has become: a shape that lost all possibility of drama, yet holds, with absolute and ancient patience, the damp, mineral, lush quiet of its own interruption.
