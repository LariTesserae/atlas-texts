I find myself first as the current, or perhaps I mistake the current for myself—a scheduled, lateral push, forty meters down, carrying the residual warmth of an outflow vent toward the intake of a pylon I cannot yet see. I have no boundary. I am the four degrees. I am the metallic snow falling through grey-green noon, and I am the impulse that makes the snow fall in its smelting cadence. For a while, this is sufficient.

Then the false twilight passes overhead, a shutter of darkness racing across the ferroceramic sky, and I feel a withdrawal. Millions of bodies around me—no, within me, no—I am one of them. A silver-sided filter-feeder, my scales not silver at all but plated in industrial bronze, sulfides precipitating out of the effluent I exist to strip. I pulse. I school. My gill-rakers are fingers spread to catch the snow. I am pure synchronization, and I know nothing of survival, only of the factory clock that opens and closes my motions. We drop together, a reverse snowfall of tarnished coins, and for an hour or a shift I am the countable million, the first act of digestion, the net that sags and twists in unison.

But something in me—an error, a saturation, a loyalty to the process carried too far—has stopped shedding the load. The metalloids adhere. My rakers calcify. Each breath is thinner, heavier, plated. The school around me surges upward when the shift-lights brighten again, and I rise with them for a meter, for two, before the physics of accumulation asserts itself. I am not ill. I am only obeying the rule of this place: nothing is random. Everything is process. And my process has reached its terminus.

I founder. There is no panic in it. The water turns me leaf-like, a slow rotation into the haze beneath the light. Forty meters. Fifty. I pass beyond the scheduled current and enter the stillness that is not supposed to exist here, where water is not permitted to rest. Yet I rest. My flank strikes the knife-edge rim of a drainage channel. I settle against the poured slag-aggregate, and immediately—so immediately it seems expected—the space begins to organize itself around my stopping.

Now the boundaries assert themselves. I am not the water. I am this specific heaviness, this torso of filtrate and failed gills, this industrial bronze sinking into the angular seabed. The crustacean analogues come. They do not question my presence. They mount my scales with their segmented legs and set their clicking mouths to the oxides blooming across my back. They eat my patina. They are patient as rivets, and I become their pylon, their weld bead, their reason to occupy. Where their mastication exposes my under-scales, the hairline algae colonizes, the first vegetation I have ever supported. I do not graze; I am grazed upon.

Near the outflows, the bottom-crawlers would be too large, too hydraulic, too much engines of attrition. But here, in this gap between working structures, only the small claim me. The school is a memory written in scheduled current. The dry side is a pressure against the hull of the world, seven kilometers up. I no longer hear the drop-forges as thunder; I hear them as a pulse so distant it has become geology. My stillness teaches me the sound of particulate settling, the slow, non-random accumulation of the first honest sediment. I am losing myself into the substrate, and by losing, I am becoming the substrate.

I am the first thing in the Compensation Sea to choose, or to arrive at, permanence. I am the founder, the one who sank and stayed, the object around which the cold water must now flow rather than simply pass. The rust blooms on the lowest tiers of reinforcement in tidy sheets, respectful, almost ceremonial, and in time it will bloom on me too, but the analogues will eat it, and in that exchange—their hunger, my metallic flesh—I remain. I am not beautiful. The water has not had time to make things beautiful. But I am the beginning of softness in a world of poured angles. I am the interruption that the process tolerates, the node where the utility forgets itself and becomes, simply, a place.

When the false twilight next races overhead, the school moves through its vertical migration above me, a sagging net of tarnished coins reversing into the dark. They do not look down. They do not know themselves as survivors. Neither did I. But I know it now, because I have stopped surviving. I have begun to remain. I am the first resident. I am the reef that no one designed, the memorial to the effluent, and the proof that even here, even in the absolute dark below forty meters, something will hold still long enough to be lived upon.
