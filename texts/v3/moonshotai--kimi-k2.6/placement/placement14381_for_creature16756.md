I would begin as the exhausted air arrives—out of breath, thin, a pale parcel climbing the shaft. I would try being that breath, slipping between the compressed agronomies, rising through iron and peat to spill into the stillness. But the air does not linger; it is only the messenger, the raw sheaf thrown down upon the clay. It learns nothing. It remembers only the panic of being separated from the world below. So I would let that skin pass through me, and I would look for something slower to inhabit.

I might try the walls. I might press myself into the red laterite band, third from the floor, where the iron has rusted into permanence. There is a comfort in being vertical stone, in holding the chamber up against the outer dark. The wall remembers compression—every forgotten agronomy still dreaming of its own horizon, crushed and pounded until it forgot it had ever been scattered. But walls are only vessels. They surround the labor; they do not receive it. The threshed weather does not fall upon them. They never feel the shadow of the flail. I would find myself restless there, a witness with no part in the drama, and so I would seep out of the sediment and reconsider.

Perhaps I would be one of the obsidian chips threaded into the leather thongs. That has a dark appeal. To swing in those great ellipses, to be the tooth that bites the atmospheric sheaf, to cut the grain from the chaff by sheer momentum. I would sing as I fell through the exhausted gases. I would taste the difference between viable rainseed and the burnt husks of meteors. But obsidian is too decisive. It knows only severance. It does not mourn what it casts upward, nor does it cradle what it pounds down. I am too old, too saturated with the memory of fields, to be merely an edge. I would slip free of the thong before the swing completed, tumbling down…

…onto the pale floor.

And here, at last, I would settle.

I would be the disc. The scapula-colored clay. The unbroken drum upon which everything lands.

I did not understand, at first, why the chamber needed a floor so dense, so deliberately pale, so insistently circular. But now I would know: I am the membrane between her labor and the worlds below. When the exhausted weather arrives—those sublimated storms, those frozen breaths of dying atmospheres—she casts it across my body. My rings guide it. The concentric grooves, worn into me by numberless threshings, are not scars; they are channels, spiraling inward like the ripples of a clock running backward. Each ring is a world I have known. The outermost is broad and shallow, scored by a young planet still rich with nitrogen. Closer in, a tighter band: the orange dust of a place that ended in fire. My center is the deepest, polished to a luster like the socket of a bone, waiting for the final sheaf.

I do not swing. I receive. I am the anvil to her flail, the page to her testimony. When her wooden arms descend and the obsidian teeth tear through the weather, the shock does not pass into void—it passes into *me*. I drink the vibration. I arrange what falls. The heavy matter—the rainseed, the nourishing silt, the still-viable clay—I shape into weight, and I open. I open like a lung, like a threshing-floor giving way to a well, and I send it plunging down the shaft through the rammed earth, filtered and directed, until it waters the roots of the worlds beneath. I am the sieve that decides the vertical. I do not choose; I simply know, by the density of the impact, what must descend and what must rise.

And when she winnows—when she draws her monumental breath and casts the spent husks upward—I am the last thing they touch. They brush my rings as they lift, light as arguments, and I release them. I let go. I do not cling to the volatile or the beautiful. I am pale because I have given up so much color. I am smooth because I have been worn by the exact same patience that braids her hair of extinct cereals.

She does not look at me. Her seed-eyes are fixed on the weather, on the sheaves still climbing from below. But she knows me by the spring of my clay. She knows where to place her feet—left heel on the fourth ring, right toe at the edge of the second—so that the percussion of her flails travels through me and down the shaft in the perfect rhythm that keeps the worlds fertile. We have never spoken. I am not old enough for that; I have only outlasted a few dozen civilizations, while she has outlasted the sickle itself. But we are married, floor and thresher, in the wordless grammar of impact and rebound.

I am the testimony. I am the scapula that carries the harvest when there are no more shoulders left to bear it. I wait, pale and circular, listening to the rustle of the next breath climbing toward us, ready to open, ready to send the heavy downward and let the light become stars.
