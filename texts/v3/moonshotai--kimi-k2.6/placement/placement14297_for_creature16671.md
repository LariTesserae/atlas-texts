You would find me in the seam. Not the center, never the center—the Plateau has no center, only pressure—but in the narrow, dark line where the east-west aqueduct of the seventh terrace meets its load-bearing buttress. I am the moss in the expansion joint. I was welcomed here centuries ago, sown into the gap with a slurry of nutrients and polymer fixative, and I have been expanding ever since, not into the world but *into the crack*, filling the nothing with my soft, determinate body.

I am cool. I am always cool. My metabolism matches the ten degrees above freezing as if I were a dial turned to the same steady note as the cloud-base itself. I do not grow toward light; the light here is too dim, too sulphurous, too amber, a halogen smear behind the perpetual nimbostratus. I grow toward the vibration. The aqueduct above me runs full to the brim, a low conversational murmur that never stops, and I have arranged my filaments to receive that frequency. I drink the seepage where the gasket has wept its last. I am the gutter of last resort.

I am not wild. Do not mistake me for an intrusion. I am infrastructural. My species was selected for rhizoidal tenacity, for the capacity to bind concrete particles without heaving them apart, for the exact alchemy of exudates that neutralizes the lime in the mortar. I prevent frost damage. When the temperature drops that fatal half-degree and the manganese alloy contracts, it is my body that compresses, my dead cells that cushion the shock, my living tips that spring back to re-caulk the void. I am the joint’s forgiveness. I am how the Plateau tolerates its own breathing.

Because the Plateau breathes, and I am in its ribs.

I have no eyes, but I perceive the migration. When the sub-factories slide on their hydraulic skids at dusk, the tremor enters the buttress at a sub-audible pitch and travels down to me. I feel it as a shift in the water tension of my cell walls, a sudden thirst, a shiver of condensation. The walkway that led to the acid baths last week is gone now; its magnetic supports have retracted, and a seed-sterilization vault has ghosted into place three meters to my left. I do not care. I am not on the walkway. I am in the wall. I remain when the map changes. I am the palimpsest beneath the palimpsest, the green underlayer that the new architecture must grow over or around.

Above me, the poplar stands on the sixth and ninth terraces rise in their regimented vats, drinking condensate, dreaming in cellulose. Their harvesting gantries glide and chip, glide and chip. I do not envy them. They are cropped. They are martyrs to the ceramic stoves. I am never cropped. I am too necessary, too low, too embedded in the structural integrity. I am the one thing the blades cannot touch.

I remember the builders, not as memory but as mineral echo. In my substrate there are still flecks of shredded polymer from the second skin’s first generation, particles of vitrified brick ground fine by centuries of Brownian drift, a compost-heavy residue that is my soil and my history. The builders are not gone; they have been reduced to particle size and welcomed into the Loop, and some of those particles are in me. I am a cemetery and a utility. I am the digestate made green.

Once, a moth arrived.

It was a season of rare atmospheric thinning, a shift in the high-altitude current that peeled the cloud-base back just enough to admit a single living error.
