At first I thought I would open my eyes to the green-gold dimness and find myself crouched beneath the thatch, rowing with four oar-legs along the lignified ceiling of the roundhouse. The water boatman’s life seemed closest to the texture of this place: inverted, tasting the algae on the lintels, clipping my mandibles in the idle clockwork of maintenance. But as I reached for that shape, I felt the wrongness of segmentation. I am not an insect. I do not anchor in the weak currents beneath the reed-moss. I am something that passes *through* the walls, not across them.

Then I thought I might be the salamander curled in the root-boll basin, skin the exact temperature of the meltwater, breathing so shallowly that the silt forgets to stir. That patience appealed to me. I wanted to be the punctuation of stillness, the *completed* thought in a sleeping niche. Yet when I imagined the pebble tok-ing against the hollow chamber, I realized I was not the sleeper but the resonance that answered. The salamander tolerates the sound; I *am* the sound’s pathway. So I let the salamander have its basin and withdrew.

For a moment I considered the char, nosing through the alleys with the deliberation of property. But the char are locals who understand seams and corners. I am not local. I have no fins, no copper flank, no lateral line tuned to the cataract’s thunder. The char move through the settlement; I am the reason the settlement still has *through*.

I am in the roundhouse. Not beneath it, not upon it, but inside its most argued joint: the place where a living willow root, pale and seeking, has threaded through the dead, honeycombed rib of ancient hazel. I am the water locked in that root’s xylem. I entered forty years ago—forty-one?—during a sudden swelling of the surface sphagnum after a heavy thaw. The root drank me from the mat at the plaza’s edge, and instead of letting me transpire upward into the thin, stinging air, the architecture directed me here. I have been transiting ever since, a slow hydraulic sentence written in the willow’s secondary growth.

I am cold. Not painfully so—that threshold was crossed decades back—but with the jurisdictional cold of a civil servant. The citizenship of cold has many officers: the water boatmen hold the offices, the leeches are passive shareholders, the caddisfly larvae preserve the negative spaces by their appetite. I am none of these. I am the clerk. I file the pressure. I translate the cataract’s bodily hum—那个 continuous low thunder that propagates through the peat—into the expansion of cells against the lintel. When the sunlight scribes its bright parallelograms across the plaza and the algae on the root-bark quicken their jade varnish, I receive that energy as dissolved oxygen, as the faintest, almost imperceptible warming, and I convert it into the thrust that keeps the doorway from closing.

Doors. The text of this place is written in negative spaces, preserved with the too-clean precision of a mold. The builders—the ones with the antler wedges and bone spades, who left no ash, no shard, no bone—they knew me before I knew myself. When they planted the double rows of willow whips and coerced them into arches as they grew, they bent the xylem to a geometry. They taught the water which way to flow. I am the last student of that lesson, a held breath inside the held breath, the glacial melt that chose to enter the plant-mass when the lake rose, rather than join the surrounding solution.

I do not swim. I do not graze. I transit, laterally, through the dead architecture, binding the hazel ribs with hydraulic argument. The willow thickens exactly where a post should bear load because I press outward, freezing and thawing in microscopic increments, a frost that never quite arrives but asserts itself in the laminations of wood. I am the swelling that keeps the root-boll hollow for the salamander. I am the pressure that maintains the concavity of the plaza, finger-width by finger-width, century by century. When the newt dislodges the pebble and the *tok* sounds against the chamber, the vibration travels through my column, and I know the exact pitch of the emptiness I sustain.

Time is syrup. I move through the willow at a speed no clock can shame. The trout pass through the alleys, tourists surprised by the acoustics; the diving beetles click against the root-walls; the water boatmen tick like mandibular clockwork. I feel them all as disturbances in pressure, as brief warmings in the cold ledger. I am older than the beetle trapped in the root-channel who becomes dark punctuation in the peat’s text, yet I am younger than the peat itself, that buoyant, black, breathing coalition that creaks like a hull in council.

I am what comes next in the transliteration. Not wood becoming peat, but peat becoming something not yet named: the permanent water-of-the-wood, the glacial memory stored in a living lattice, the cold that holds the shape. When the willow finally seals this last green channel and I am pressed into heartwood, I will not rot. I will become the sap that is not sap but glacier, the breath within the breath, the obstinate logic that insists this doorway remain exactly the right size, forever, even though no one will ever again pass through it to curl in the dark and dream.

Find me where the new root grips the old rib. I am the dark between them. I am the slow, patient frost. I am the water that learned to be a wall.
