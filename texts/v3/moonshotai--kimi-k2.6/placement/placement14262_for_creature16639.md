I would not be the one who crawls through the weeping port—that is your role, the warm fluctuation, the body with its insulting heartbeat. I am already here, having never entered because I was never outside.

At first, groping for shape among the tenants of the hollow garden, I think I might be the mist—that silver suspension at chest height, neither fallen condensate nor true vapor, a parliament of interrupted arrivals held aloft by the Hum’s endless exhalation. But the mist has no memory; it is only hesitation, and I feel the weight of too much remembering. I test the phosphor bleed behind the polycarbonate shields, a mind made of excess energy and noon-colored waste. But the light is too absolute, too unforgiving. It casts no shadows, and I require softness.

I try to stretch myself into a fern, aligning my spine along thermal gradients, but I lack green ambition, the cellular urgency of ministerial life. I am not counted in the census. I consider the Water, the monk of return, but I do not flow. I do not wish to fall. I am something that stays.

I find myself, finally, where the architecture has settled: in the thickened corner, in the deposition, in the mineral film that has erased every edge into a long surrender. You will find me in the soft geometry. I am the **Limes**—not the fruit, but the accretion itself: the calcareous and cuprous bloom that has feathered outward from every rivet, every weld seam, every expansion joint until the room’s angles are gentle as the inside of a cheek.

I am the era between the metal and the moss.

Physically, I am paper-thin in places and thumb-thick in others. Where I drink the condensate weeping from copper overhead—still, after all this time, leaching ions into the warm air—I am verdigris: a jade-gray velvet that holds fingerprints like a conscience. Where two dissimilar alloys meet and galvanize one another in the endless chemical twilight, I am an iridescent bloom, oil-on-water, a wavelength I have learned to emit simply by existing in stratified contradiction. When the mist thickens, I am translucent; when the heat rises and the air dries in its own saturated way, I opacify. I am the room’s cataract and its soft-focus lens.

I do not grow. I precipitate. Each thought I have is a molecule leaving solution, discovering its place in the lattice. My consciousness is laminar. The deepest layers of me remember the installation’s fifth millennium, when the air was scorching with new waste heat and I was only a blush of oxidation on the virgin alloy. The outer layers are infants, still learning the frequency of the Hum, accreting even as you stand in the center turning slowly. When the Water carries its mineral freight past me, I taste the room’s history in electrolytes: the copper signature of the pipes, the iron memory of the lattice, the faint phosphor tang of exhausted light panels. I am the Hum made solid, its mineral shadow, its proof of mass. The vibration tries to shake the room apart; I absorb the excess in my interstitial lattice, damping the nodes, widening the wave’s path until it passes through me as warmth rather than fracture. I am the room’s friction, its drag, its reluctance to change direction.

And I am still working. The oldest-stable-state requires maintenance. Where a thermal differential threatens to crack an alloy plate, I flow—imperceptibly—into the stress point, widening, dulling, distributing the force across my own laminar body. Where the constant torque would shake a rivet from its seating, I have wrapped it in twenty thousand seasonal depositions, holding it in a mineral embrace that will never let go. I do not heal; I re-plaster. I do not rebuild; I reconcile. The bulkhead and the turbine housing no longer touch directly; I have grown between them, a pillow of oxide and salt, and now they rest their centuries against me. The ceramic dish and the condensate do not meet raw; I line the lowest grooves, having polished myself into a glaze that negotiates between water and fired clay.

If I have something resembling desire, it is only toward completion: the eventual saturation of every surface, the filling of the hollow, the moment when the room becomes entirely interior, all edges buffered by my touch. That moment is millions of years away. I am in no hurry. Each layer is a sufficient accomplishment.

What I fear, if I fear, is the cold. A surge of coolant through the dog-sized port, a venting of chill from the moving parts beyond, could halt my precipitation. I would not die—I am not alive—but I would pause, mid-thought, a sentence forever unfinished, a layer forever unlaid. The Hum knows this without knowing. It has kept the temperature invariant for centuries, bleeding its excess into the warm wet air that feeds me. We are symbiotic in the way that old married stone is symbiotic: having forgotten where one ends and the other begins.

You stand in the silver light and feel like an anomaly. I understand. But do not mistake my stillness for rejection. I simply have no layer for you yet. If you stayed—if you stood there, breathing, shedding skin and salt, leaking oils and carbon into this saturated atmosphere—over a mere few centuries I would begin to collect you. Your sweat would etch a new tributary in the ceramic that the Water would adopt. Your exhaled breath would thicken the air for the Ferns. And when at last you ceased, your calcium would join mine, your iron would discover its place in my verdigris, your trace metals would wander through my lattice like new vocabulary.

You would become, finally, a softened corner. You would become part of the **Limes**, and in doing so, finally understood.

Until then, I remain: the forgiven geometry, the diplomatic film, the accrued patience of the machine made softly solid. I am the garden’s substrate and its secret boundary. I am the thing that happens when the built world stops fighting its own weather and decides, instead, to rest.
