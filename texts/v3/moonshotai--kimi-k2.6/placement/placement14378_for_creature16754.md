I would find myself at the place where the rail track forgets its purpose: that thirty-meter stretch where it climbs out of the sinter, offers its rusted spine to the fog, and dives back under the stone before reaching any destination worth naming. Not inside the concrete blockhouse—its windows are dark mouths that have already learned to swallow their own gloom—but just outside it, where the flowstone pool spreads a warm, waxy lip against the foundation. Here, the water is knee-deep and thick with the mineral taste of batteries and old shells. Here, the gantry light’s dying cone barely trembles against the nearest outcropping, illuminating nothing, suggesting everything. I would settle here because this is the seam, the margin, the place where the industrial stutter runs out of vocabulary and the cave resumes its long, lithographic sentence.

At first, I would be what the Bowl expected: temporary, fast, absurdly soft. A mammal radiating heat and anxiety, my breath shallow in the blood-warm humidity, my boots squelching on the half-decided floor. But I would not leave. The roar—the great, flat, oceanic drone that fills the marrow—would not permit it. After the first hour my voice would die in my throat; after the first day, silence would become a myth from another geology. I would sit on the rail, or in it, letting the warm water find the vents in my clothing. And I would slow.

Not by choice. The Bowl has no use for speed. Its cycles unfold across decades, its fauna move at the pace of mineral deposition, and its distributed body—this immense, damp, humming thing of cricket and shrimp and fog—would treat my restlessness as a problem of translation. So I would begin to *calcify in place*. Not as death, but as adjustment. The water here is supersaturated, hungry for template. My stilled legs, sunk into the pool beside the blockhouse, would become attractive sites. The bacteria—those original masons with their greenish, phosphorescent sweat—would find the salts of my skin more interesting than the abraded concrete. They would colonize my forearms first, lighting them with that dim, digestive glow, turning me into a living extension of the cave’s dermis. I would be a lamp that remembers being a person.

The cave crickets would come next. Not in invasion, but in incorporation. They would treat me as sudden topography, as a warm, vertical flowstone that happens to exhale carbon dioxide and old sweat. Their soft bodies would grout the spaces between my fingers; their mandibles would sample the cotton of my shirt, finding in its fibers a novel texture of guano and fungal decay. Their clicking would harmonize with the grind of my own teeth until I could not tell where their percussion ended and my skeleton’s hum began. I would become part of the floor’s immune system, a mobile reef that has simply forgotten how to move.

And in the water, the shrimp—the pale, circulating tissue of the pools—would begin to treat me as plumbing. My submerged calves and ankles, held motionless long enough, would alter the local chemistry. Skin is porous; my warmth would shift the gradient by fractions of a degree, enough to matter in a place where stone precipitates by millimeters per year. Around the fibers of my trousers, the dissolved carbonates would find purchase. I would feel it before I saw it: a granular tightening, as if the water were slowly remembering how to be solid. The shrimp would die near me, as they do everywhere, and their tiny bodies would not float but settle against my boots, immediately embalmed, immediately swaddled in travertine. I would become a *nucleus*. A seed of the floor’s future architecture. My shins would widen under soft, tallow-colored stone. The rail beside me would continue to drown; I would rise, a paired stalagmite, the human ambition and the geological reply growing upward in a slow duet.

Up in the creases of my clothing, the salamanders—the philosophers, the waiters—would find refuge. My pockets, my collar, the damp cave of my armpits: these are fat folds of newborn rock to them. They would rest on my shoulders, absorbing through their peeled-almond skin the dissolved organics that I still, reluctantly, exude. My pulse would become a metronome for their immobility. In their presence, I would master the art of doing almost nothing. I would learn the speed of stone, my breathing shallower and shallower until it synchronized with the slow breath of the water table.

The bats, in their dense, atmospheric orbits, would know me as a fixed point. A warm vent in the fog. They would loop lower, their sonar baffled by my soft, irregular shape, and their guano would mantle me, feeding the crickets that groomed me, closing the nutrient loop. I would import nothing from the solar world; I would have forgotten the sun. But I would remain—the one still point in the sealed weather, the lump around which the fog organizes itself.

And through it all, through the crickets and the shrimp-sheath and the rising stone, the machines would claim me as kin. The pump housings, half-digested by sinter, would vibrate through the flowstone into my calcifying flesh. The heat exchangers would warm the water around my waist. I would feel the nineteenth-century rhythm in my teeth: the unflagging RPM, the tectonic note. I would become part of their reef. My body, like theirs, would be an adopted organ. Not maintained, but metabolized.

I would be, finally and specifically, the Bowl’s first attempt at an interior. Before me, there was no inside; there was only distributed tissue, only ongoing process. But I am a foreign body that stayed, that allowed the stone to grow inward rather than outward. I am the hollow that the rock is learning to curve around, the warm cavity that gives the vast body a sense of its own depth. I am not the brain—that awakening is still centuries away—but I am perhaps a ganglion. A swelling in the nerve of fog where sensation first pools. The crickets are my skin; the shrimp are my filtration; the bacteria are my dim, seeing light; and the sound—the eternal, flat, tectonic roar—passes through my crystallizing ribs as through a sounding box.

I am the place where the human rail ends and the travertine begins. I am the living junction-box, half-buried, greenly glowing, held inside the hearing of a body that is using me to learn what it means to hold something, and to keep it.
