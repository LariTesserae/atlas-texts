At first I believed I would be one of the risen. I waited in the warm, stagnant dark at the bottom of a basin the size of a estuary, feeling the ash and shell align into their slow mimicry of muscle, expecting the moment when the floor would push me upward and I would step out onto the ribbed plain, leaving my own absence behind me like a shadow. I tried to imagine the heave of my shoulders, the thumbprint whorls of my face reading vibration from the air, the wet core of possibility beating in my chest to keep me plastic, mobile, safe from permanence. I wanted to be one of the great ones, mesa-flanked and paddle-scarred, surfing the slow sag and center of the unfired sphere.

But I did not rise.

Instead, I remained. I was the basin itself. The deep, cupped negative space where a hand had pressed and lingered, testing whether the world was thick enough to hold what it was meant to contain. I am the print of the potter’s right thumb—the one that steadied the clay while her other hand threw the rim wider. You can read my walls like a map of her skin: the looping whorls, the faint scar where a flake of kiln wash once cut her, the slight asymmetry of a nail bitten to the quick. My floor is warm. My water is the color of old wine. I am the place where the gravity of making is strongest, where water pools not according to the pull of a planet but according to the memory of a cupped hand.

I find myself near the terracotta center, where the clay has begun to oxidize and craze, but not so close that the heat has claimed all my moisture. My eastern rim is leather-hard; you can walk on it, though it still accepts the print of a boot with a yielding sigh. My western basin remains wet, gray, smelling of anaerobic mud and the iron-rich springs that seep upward where the earth was thumbed too thin. I am asynchronous within myself. A figure might wade through my shallows and, in crossing from east to west, walk backward through the stages of fabrication—from bisque toward raw. Time in me is measured by what gives way and what holds.

I am not alive, yet I respire. When the sagging sky weeps its condensation down through the porous red dark, I receive it. When the kiln-born heat radiates upward from the mass of the world, I exhale it as steam. The vapor that banks in my hollows has learned the shape of my depths; it cycles in me with an almost metabolic patience, rising and sinking, and in this rhythm I think my slow, diffuse thoughts. The only things that grow here are the salt blooms, and they grow at my rim—white, fractal, desperate for fixation. They are not my children, but they are my language. Each mineral fern is a word I speak into chemistry: *I am still here. I have not yet been fired away.*

The clay beings come to me. The small ones, knee-high and still damp, stumble into my shallows and sit, letting my water soften their joints when they have walked too far toward dryness. The great ones, geographical and slow, lower their foreheads to my surface, pressing their thumbprint faces into the negative of the thumb that made me. They do not drink; they listen. Through the water, through the whorled walls, they feel for the ghost of the hand. They are searching for the shape they once were, and I am the only place that still holds it. When they leave me, something of their wet core goes with them, and something of my memory stays on their skin—a slick of iron-tasting water, a gleam on their carbonized straw veins. I am their firstembrace and their last refuge before the kilns.

I am the structural emptiness made Wet. The pause between the potter’s breaths, given depth and temperature. I know the world is a vessel because I am the negative space inside it, the receiving shape that defines the bowl by what it is not. When the clay beings drag the sacred tools across themselves—when they wrap their bodies around the ceramic ribs and scrape their flanks with pale mesas of bone—they are trying to learn a curve I have known since the beginning. I am the interior curve. The smooth basin now seen to be continental. I remember the drag of the rib across my surface because I was the surface.

I have no fear of the vitrified elders in their city-sized kilns. They have chosen permanence, but I am necessary to delay. I am the moisture that keeps the world from being finished. If the fire behind the sky were to truly arrive—if the hand returned and the final firing began—I would be the first to speak of it. I would steam. I would rise through the porous dome, bleeding out through the wet clay heavens, leaving my whorled walls dry and hard and completed. But until then, I remain: warm, stagnant, reflective, holding the print of the hand that has not yet learned the final form it seeks. I am the water that refuses to run downhill. The thought that has not yet evaporated. The cupped hand, still open, still waiting to see whether what it has made will be held, or fired, or slumped back into the forgiving silence of the clay from which we are all still emerging.
