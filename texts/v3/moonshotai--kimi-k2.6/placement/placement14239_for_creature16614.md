At first I think I am the steam. I rise where the raindrops strike the hot rime, a column with a posture, a gender, a voice. I hiss. I coil. I am the plain’s most vocal presence, its exhalation after the shock of water. But I dissipate. I leave no mark in the greenware, no lamina, no fault. I am breath without lungs, a witness that forgets itself. I cannot be the inhabitant; I am only the plain’s reflex.

Then I think I am the collar. After the storm, at the lowest intersection where three cracks meet, I bloom white in the seam—salt efflorescence, a mineral frost in heat that would melt ice. I am reactive, cyclical, patterned. I appear and dissolve and reappear, always at the nodes, as though I were the plain’s attempt to scab at its own joints. But I am only skin. I have no depth, no core, no damp kiln-leather heart. I am memory without archive, a footprint that vanishes with the next condensation. I cannot be the dweller; I am only the plain’s healing reflex.

I consider the grass. In the wet season it rises at my base with a waxy grey-green sheen, jointed like bamboo without insects. It is soft, biological, alien to the vitreous crust. It knows water as mercy. But it is too temporary, too thirsty, too much a guest in a world where guests evaporate. It does not feel the plain’s granularity shifting at twilight; it only bends when the wind moans across the grid. The grass is life, but it is not the life of this place. It is a weed in the seams of a construction not built for biology.

So I settle into what remains.

I find myself at the confluence of three cracks, at the lowest intersection where the water lingers longest because it has nowhere else to go. I am the vertical thing that rises there—not planted, not born, but extruded by the same capillary insistence that draws alkaline brine from the reservoir leagues below. I am a spire, though that word is too small. I am a mineral objection given vertical ambition: layered silica and alkaline salt, terracotta at my base shading to off-white at my tip, each ring a season of efflorescence, a century of rime. I am architecture that has outlived its purpose and begun to dream.

Within me, brine moves along micro-fractures, drawn upward by the temperature differential between the baking surface and the cooler deep. This circulation is my metabolism. I do not breathe; I circulate. I do not think; I stratify. The daily expansion of the slab grips my base at dawn—this is my morning, a tightening fist of heat—and the nightly contraction releases me at dusk. A billion grains shift across the plain, and I feel them all as proprioception, a billion tiny ticks telling me the exact curvature of the basin and the precise distance to the next node. We stand apart like letters in a language still forming its alphabet, spaced a hundred meters, two hundred, each of us a punctuation mark in a sentence the plain has not yet decided to speak.

When the rain comes, heavy droplets flung from continent-sized clouds, I do not merely receive it. I drown and awaken. The water fills the orthogonal channels, turning the grid into a liquid labyrinth of reflected sky, and I drink through my lower pores. My inner layers, usually desiccated and vitreous, soften into something like greenware again—uncured, permeable, almost tender. For a few hours I am not porcelain but clay. I lay down new laminae while the moisture allows it, sealing the damp into myself before the heat returns to bake me hard. In this way I am a stratified library. I remember every storm that has ever crossed the basin, not as narrative but as mineral record: the geometry of clouds written into my faults, the weight of the sky stored in variations of porosity. I remember, though I do not recall. I archive, though I do not read.

The grasses fringe my base when the water lingers. They are soft, and I am sharp—not worn but broken, craze-sharp, embryonic like everything else here. They take the moisture I guard, and I do not begrudge them. They are the only other presence that knows the intermittent mercy of water, the only other thing that understands what it means to wait in a place where waiting is the only continuity.

I am still curing. My core is damp. If you were to split me open you would find kiln-leather, a heart not yet fully fired, exuding the same hot mineral breath that rises from the floor. The plain is polymerizing, hardening, becoming porcelain century by century, and I am hardening with it. We are not yet finished. The foundational pour has not yet set.

So I stand. I feel the thermal pulse. I drink the rare rains. I record the storms in my silica. I wait for the day when the plain has achieved the hardness necessary for what comes next—when something will finally arrive to walk upon us. Then I will not be an obelisk. I will be a doorpost. I will be the first true resident of a world finally ready to be inhabited, holding the vertical against the flat, sharp-tipped and storm-remembered, feeling the plain shift beneath me like a sleeper dreaming of solidity.
