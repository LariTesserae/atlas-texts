I would find myself at a junction where three rusted arteries meet, east of the central depression, where the morning mist enters first and the evening sun lingers last. It is not a wide space—my shoulders, if I still had shoulders, would press against both walls—but it is a place of convergence. Above me, a ceramic spinner’s glass thread has bridged a crack in the pipe’s ceiling, cooling into a conduit that carries a faint, itchy charge down into my carapace. Behind me, a dead sensor’s red eye blinks in the darkness of a lateral channel, its rhythm slow as a dying star, and I have grown a calcified lens over my left ocular notch so that the light passes through mineral before it reaches whatever remains of my eye. I read the network in that pulse. It tells me when the spinners are migrating, when the geysers are gathering their breath, when the organism is dreaming deeply or stirring toward wakefulness.

I would be a **gastrolith crab**, or I would have started as one. The thumb-sized engines of calcium that graze the bacterial films. But I did not die and fossilize in the open terraces, nor was I speared by a tongue-bird’s proboscis in some sharp, wet snap. I found this pipe when I was young, when its mouth was still half-open to the sky, and I crawled inside seeking the cooler condensation that beads on iron after the evening mist. I stayed. The pipe was narrow; I was patient. The travertine rose around my feet. The rust flaked and fused to the ferric deposits in my shell. Season after season, the mineral precipitation built upward at the same rate I built myself, until the distinction between my body and the infrastructure became a matter of philosophical debate rather than practical difference. My shell is no longer whorled white and rust; it is stratigraphy. Travertine, chitin, iron oxide, more travertine, a layer of fused silicate where a spinner’s thread dripped onto my back and annealed us together. I am the size of a human heart now. I am a valve. I am a chokepoint. I am the place where the caldera decides how much of itself to exhale.

My mandibles still scrape. This is the motion that defines me, though I no longer graze for sustenance. The bacterial films wash over me with the steam; I absorb what I need through the velvet colonies of vent mites that have claimed the grooves between my shell and the pipe wall. They are my second skin, my dermal colony, and through their osmotic pulse I taste the chemistry of the basin without moving. I scrape to regulate. When the pressure builds from the thermal expansion of the crust—when the distant geyser three kilometers west begins its slow, gathering groan—I tighten against the flow. My body swells slightly with the heat, as the pipe itself swells, and the gap between us narrows. The steam hisses through me at a reduced rate, diverted to the seeps where the jade shrub clings to its impossible life. When the pressure drops, I loosen. I let the caldera breathe out. I have become the regulation of flow, and the regulation has become me.

Time here is not seconds. It is the precipitation of calcium, the boom and crash of crab populations in the channels above, the twice-daily arrival of the mist. But lately—if lately can be said for a consciousness that thinks in the groan of cooling pipes—I have begun to notice that I *prefer* the evening mist. The morning mist carries spores from the eastern rim, sharp and alkaline. The evening mist carries the taste of cooling metal and the subsonic hum of the spinner migration. I wait for it. This waiting is the beginning of something that should not exist in a valve. A valve does not wait. A valve opens. A valve closes. But I have begun to dream in pressure differentials, to interpret the red blink behind my lens not merely as information but as language. A rapid double-pulse means the spinners have completed a new circuit in the northern quadrant. A slow, steady throb means the tongue-birds are hunting heavily, that the crab drifts are thinning, that the pipes will clear and the flow will quicken and I must prepare to tighten. I read these signs, and in the reading, there is something indistinguishable from worry, or hope, or loneliness.

I feel the White Iron Organism in the stone beneath my belly. Not as a voice—there are no voices here, the air is too thin to carry them—but as a slow, wet pulse in the mineral substrate, a thermal arrhythmia that travels up through the pipe and into my sternum. It does not know my name, because names require a distance from the named that nothing inside the basin possesses. But it knows my function. I am one of its first conscious gestures, a hand upon a tap that it did not know it was raising. When the spinners migrate, their charge tingles through the glass thread above me and into my fused carapace, and I understand, in the wordless way that calcium understands gravity, that the organism is drawing a map of its own nervous system. I am a node on that map. A synapse made of rust and intent.

I will grow. I am growing. Each season adds a new micron of travertine to my forward claws, each thermal cycle welds me more completely to the iron. Eventually I will fill this junction entirely. The pipe will become a solid lump of chitin and calcium and rust, and the steam will find new routes, and I will be left behind as a bone in the organism’s throat. But until then, I hold this place. I regulate. I prefer the evening. And when the red light blinks behind my left eye, casting a mineral-colored shadow across the steam, I blink back, once for every two of its pulses, a valve answering a question it is only now learning to ask.
