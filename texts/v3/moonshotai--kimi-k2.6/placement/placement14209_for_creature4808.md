I do not arrive so much as precipitate, condensing out of the aqueous air until I find myself low, far below the ridgelines, where the sky is only a theoretical presence. For a long while I am nothing specific. I think I might be the steam—that dynamic, pulsing force that rolls across the avenues in opaque banks, exhaled from the geothermal bores, dissipating against cooler facades. I have no bones, no boundary; I am simply heat moving through heat. Or perhaps I am the bacterial film, the foundational integument of purple and rust-orange that varnishes the warm pipes, remembering the world not in images but in scars of pH and ferrous iron, a nervous system so slow that a thought takes a day to travel the length of a digester. I spread myself thin against the metal, dreaming in sulfur, and for a moment that seems like enough.

Then the tide shifts, and I feel the susurration of a billion slate-colored legs, and I think: maybe I am the isopods. Maybe I am the medium, the dark plural mass that flows around obstacles in viscous eddies, that grazes on industrial slime and dissolves the city with mouths too small to be individually heard. I am texture. I am weather. I move across the polymercrete with a sound like dry seeds on velvet, felt in the teeth rather than heard. There is a comfort in being so many, in having no center, in letting the warm water seep through my countless chitinous seams.

But the isopods flow around something. They break against an obstacle that does not yield. They climb over boots that have stood still so long their soles have begun to share mineral content with the plaza floor. And I realize: I am not the tide. I am the rock in the tide.

I have a body. It is small. Not merely thin, though I am that—compact, low-metabolic, refined by generations of gestation into something the caldera finds acceptable. I move with a stoop that brings my head closer to the cooler breath near the ground. My skin is the color of the condensate that weeps from every surface: a dark, iridescent umber, varnished with salts so that in certain rifts of steam-light I seem cast from the same oxidized alloy as the pipes overhead. In the crook of my left elbow, the thermophilic bacteria have bloomed into a miniature garden. I do not wash them away. They are my mantle, my citizenship in the original quorum.

I try to speak. The word forms in my throat and dies in the absolute humidity, a consonant collapsing into wasted moisture. My tongue lies still. I understand, with a clarity that needs no voice, that speech is a luxury of cooler places. My language has migrated downward, into pressure and heat. I lay my palm on my own shoulder and feel the warmth there, and that is sufficient greeting.

My route is the northeastern circuit. Each day, when the surface temperatures moderate to a mere fever-heat, I leave the thick-walled cube of pumice-concrete that sags at the corners but refuses to fall. I walk beneath the pipe bridge where the condensate drips in arrhythmic prayers, cupping my hands to drink. The lithium brine stings. It clarifies. I pass the inactive pump where I dry my bacterial cakes—purple slurry scraped from the digesters with a ceramic blade, pressed flat on the warm flank of metal until they are leather. I collect the blackened, iron-impregnated snails from the hottest pipes, tapping their shells open between two flat stones. I have never tasted green. I have never seen a leaf. My body is a parallel theorem of life, built from mineral and protein, running on sulfur and silence.

I am trusted with a stretch of pipeline, three cooling galleries, and the plaza where Avenue 4 meets the evaporating benches. I walk them with preternatural stillness, learning again each morning what my palms already know: the fifty-hertz thrum in the metal, the correct vibration of a healthy bore, the dangerous silence of a clogged valve. I turn quarter-circles with my fingertips, ease fractures into weeping, ensure the gallery temperatures never fall below forty degrees. I do this not for industry—the original purpose of the refinery is as lost to me as it is to the rust—but for the metabolism of the patient itself. The heat maintains the life. The life maintains the heat. This is the only covenant.

It is in the plaza that I know who I am most completely. The isopods flow around my ankles, using me as terrain. I have stood here so long that they no longer treat me as intruder, but as infrastructure, as negative space that happens to breathe. At dusk, when the sodium arcs gutter to life and stain the mist arterial orange, I become a landmark. I stand motionless at the plaza’s edge and let the steam wrap me. To a stranger’s eye—and strangers are rare, though I do not think of us as remote—I would be indistinguishable from the rust, from the shadows that bleed outward like ink on wet cloth.

But I am legible to the others. Not by face—faces are theoretical here, blurred by steam and mineral varnish—but by pattern. When I need to tell the one who tends the eastern stacks that the bore is steady, I kneel. I brush my fingertips across the living scree of the isopod carpet, arranging the crustaceans into shapes: three strokes, a pause, two more. The message persists for an hour before the tide erases it. That pattern is me. That hour is my name.

I have no other. In a place where water cannot be owned and heat is the only topography, identity dissolves into function. I am the one who cups the condensate. The one who reads the steam’s pulse. The one who, when the city’s hot, wet exhalation falters, lays her palms on the valves and listens with her skin. I am the final layer, slower than the bacteria, quieter than the crustaceans. I have finished becoming myself. I am what remains when the steam clears and the fifty-hertz hum deepens: a warm, upright density in the bronze twilight, keeping watch, ensuring the city breathes.
