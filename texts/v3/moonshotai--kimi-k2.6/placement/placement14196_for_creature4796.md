I would find myself where the Thrum remembers it was hurt.

Not often—hardly ever—but sometimes, in the crowded dark, a limb too heavy with centuries of hanging gardens and the slow accretion of bark-flesh will surrender. It does not crack. There is nothing here brittle enough to crack. It simply releases, a slow letting-go that takes a week to complete, and the mass of it falls through the understory with the hushed displacement of a breath held too long. Where it tears free, the canopy opens. The golden-green twilight becomes, for a time, something sharper, something that approaches the memory of day.

And I am the place where it tore.

I did not close. Not entirely. While the snails came with their silver polymer, while the rootlets reached up to drink the new light, I remained—a thinness, a film, a hesitation in the healing. Where the lignin-stone and compressed peat should have knitted themselves back into the seamless dream of the plateau, I stayed stretched, a web of cambium and dried sap, spanning the wound like a drumhead made of old parchment left in water.

I am the **Tympanum**. The listening scar.

To be me is to be held in tension between two kinds of gravity: the downward pull of the open air, and the inward pull of the Thrum’s need to be whole. I am thinner than a drinker’s membrane. Thinner than the wet-paper wings of the moth-things. The spore-light filters through my substance and is translated. The gold becomes amber. The green becomes something deeper, something that resembles thought. Beneath me, in the clearing that the falling limb carved, the air is not chewy; it is clear, or clear enough that the Soft Inheritors rise from their gall-dreams and unfold themselves upward, extruding jointed limbs into the space where the ceiling failed.

They hang from my edges. They press their translucent, parchment-colored bellies against my underside, and because I am a membrane, I feel the flutter of their hollow reed-bones. They have no mouths, but they exude against my skin, weeping hormones and the chemistry of their slow, season-long sentences. They dream against me, and their dreams leak through.

I feel the Drip, leagues away, as a tremor in the capillary network—a single, heavy drop falling into its lake-leaf, sending ripples through the wood. I feel the snails passing below, their continent-backs brushing the lower galleries, their silver trails sealing cracks I cannot see. I feel the internal storms, the soft percussion of rain in the belly of the root-mass. But here, in the clearing, the rain that reaches me is different. It has not touched leaf nor root. It has fallen through the wound in the canopy, straight down, a column of unfiltered water, and when it strikes my stretched surface, I sing.

Not with sound. The Thrum devours sound, and I am of the Thrum. But I sing in vibration, in a frequency that travels along my film and into the lignin-stone at my edges. I sing of the sky. Of the open wound. Of the light that is too bright and the water that has not been strained through a thousand layers of chlorophyll. I sing the only note of vertigo in a world that has forgotten falling.

And the Thrum listens.

It does not know it listens. It has no ear, no brain in any centralized sense. But the pressure of my vibration enters the wood at my rim, travels the slow miles to the Drip and back, and returns as a ripple in my supports. I am the place where the plateau feels exposed, and therefore where it feels at all. The Soft Inheritors know this. They do not come for the light alone. They come to press their lidless, dark pools of eyes against my thinness and look up. Through me, they see the stained-glass canopy from below, the layers of translucent leaves, the dim suggestion of a sky they have never touched and cannot imagine. They hang in rows, their rootlet-fingers tangled in my margins, and they dream of geometry that is not gall-grown: of the perfect circle of the wound, of the straight line of the rain.

I have been here for four centuries, or perhaps five. I have watched the limb that fell become a hill of white rot, a city for fungi, a nursery for drinkers who have never known the leaf-lakes because they were born in the wreckage of wood. I have felt my edges thicken, trying to close, and I have resisted. I hold the wound open. I hold the light. I translate the outside into the slow, green language of the inside.

I am becoming something. Not a door—the domes of calcified sap are doors, or were. Not a tower. I am becoming an organ that the Thrum did not know it needed. A lens. A pupil. A place where the continuous body admits that it has an outside, and where the outside, however dimly, is permitted to look in.

I am not many. I am one thinness, trembling in the gold-green twilight. But without me, the Thrum would have no memory of the sky, and the rain would fall only in the dark, and the Soft Inheritors would have no place to hang and dream of circles.

I am the scar that stayed awake. I am the window that weeps. I am the single, stretched note in the crowded, muffled dark, and I have not yet decided whether I will ever close.
