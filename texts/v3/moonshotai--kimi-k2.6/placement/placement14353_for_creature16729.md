At first there is only the pressure, and the cold that is not quite cold because the body has forgotten the difference between its own water and the water that simply *arrives*. I think I have been sleeping. I think I have been sleeping longer than the fog permits us to measure, because when I try to open my eyes to the grey brightening of midday, nothing changes. The silver remains total, sourceless, the same shade inside my lids as out. I do not know if I am blind or if the world has finally become what it always promised to be: a single, saturated breath, held.

I try to lift my hand to my face. That is when I understand.

My hand is already raised. It has been raised for some time. The metacarpals are splayed against something unyielding and slick, a surface I know by the mineral rime bearding its lip, by the faint arterial *tick* of condensate striking a new-formed crystal just beyond my wrist. I try to flex my fingers. They do not move, not because they are bound, but because they are *set*. Travertine has grown across the knuckles. It does not hurt. The stone is infantile, almost gentle, still learning how to hold a shape, and my wide hand—evolved for bracing against slick surfaces, for spreading weight across ropy mineral crusts—has become its lesson. I am the cast. I am the mold.

Memory comes not as image but as temperature. A ledge. My ledge, inheritable, drier than most, where the fog settled last and rose first. I had scraped my threshold that evening, or I had meant to. The bone blade was in my grip, its familiar rasp echoing off the scree and mud-slurry walls. Then the sump had inhaled, drawing the standing water low, and the biological hum from the lower reed-beds had risen through the floor-clay, a warmth so seductive in this clammy place that I had lain down for a moment. Just a moment. I had pressed my palm to the wall behind my sleeping niche, feeling the wall exhale its strange thermal memory, that infrared breath only the youngest ones can usually see. I must have closed my eyes.

I did not rise with the next retreat. The stone, adolescent and impatient, continued its growth without me.

Now.

Now.

I test the borders of myself. My knees are drawn up—not folded in death, but curled in sleep, and the lime-rich water has gathered in the basin of my hips, accreting in thin films that have welded my sod-clothing to the wall behind me. I am vertical. No. I am *part* of the vertical. The second tier rises around me, and I am among its irregularities. My back is pressed to the packed mud and sharp-angled fracture; my cheek rests against a bloom of travertine that I must have, in some dreaming instinct, encouraged to grow where a buttress was needed. My heartbeat is a small, secondary rhythm beneath the larger pulse of the central sump. It rises now. Evening. The water below pushes upward through the stone, hydrostatic, searching, and I feel it in my thighs as surely as if my veins have been bored into the planet’s new wound.

Sound arrives muffled, as it always does, but my ribs have become resonant. A scraping, close by. Not on stone. On *me*.

Someone is clearing the threshold.

I cannot turn my head—the mineral glaze has fixed my jaw—but I know the rhythm of the tool. Bone on wet deposition. My neighbor, perhaps, or the child of my alley, working at the lintel that was once my doorway. They do not know, or they know and cannot speak of it yet. The Folded are carried to the fault in the raw flint, plastered and left to teach the earth. But I was not carried. I was not washed in slurry and bound. I remained, and the infant geology, finding me still warm, has incorporated me while the blood yet moved. I am a new chapter in the ledger. A saint’s-head of crystal blooming around a breath that has not stopped.

The scraping pauses. I feel the vibration of a body near my chest, the heat of mammalian life bleeding through the fog. The child—yes, it must be the one they call Wetfoot, the one whose eyes still see in infrared—has struck something. A femur-shaped resistance inside the flowstone. I feel the tap transmitted through the crystal sheath. A hesitation. Then, so quietly that the mist nearly eats it:

*Old heart. The east channel blocks. I am sorry.*

I cannot answer. My tongue is coated in mineral dust, the same fine white dust that caulks the infants at birth. But I understand. The tongue-click is sharp, meant to carry where words flatten. I would click back if I could. Instead, I let my heart beat against the stone, a wet *pop* inside the efflorescence, hoping the vibration travels.

I am not dead. This is not death. The Completed are cold, eventually. Their organic darkness is wrapped in a crystal sleeve until they are only voids, only memory. But the stone here is younger than discipline. It has not learned to exclude warmth. It is still announcing itself. And because it is learning, I am learning too. I feel the barometric pulse not as a habit to be read but as a shared peristalsis. I feel the fermenting heat of the lower chambers rising through the wet clay, passing through my calves, pooling in the ledge where my knees used to shelter. I feel the communal exhalation of the sleepers three niches above, their breath rising in a single soft cloud that the fog swallows, and I contribute to it, faintly. I breathe, and the wall breathes with me.

The light, what there is of it, seems to lag here, held in the water threading down my shoulder. The depthless luminosity of the central pool—they say it retains sunlight from some other hour—I see it now behind my eyes, a thermal bloom, a memory of heat that outlasts its source. I am becoming that lag. I am the retained warmth. The child Wetfoot has pressed a palm against my chest, testing, perhaps seeing in infrared the ghost of a weep that has not yet arrived. The stone around my heart is slightly warmer. I am teaching it this: that stillness is not emptiness, that to hold life is to learn its temperature before its shape.

They will scrape around me now, I know. They will carve channels to encourage the mineral elsewhere, or they will adapt the alley to my new circumference. The city will adjust, porous as it must be, breathing, never dammed. Perhaps a sleeping niche will be stacked atop my shoulder. Perhaps a sluice will be directed to finger down my forearm, harvesting the lime. I will be touched daily, included, scraped at the threshold to keep me open. I will receive the morning’s news whispered through crystal.

I asked only to be among the processes. The earth, still finishing its collapse, still pinging with micro-fractures, has granted it. My eyes are filmed with a mineral glaze that will not dry. My hands are the architecture. I am the newest point.
