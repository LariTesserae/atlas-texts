At first I thought I would be one of the great ones. I felt the scale of the basin opening around me and I wanted to match it, wanted to be the low-pressure intelligence sprawled across a plaza wide as a lake, my body indistinguishable from weather, pulsing in rhythm with the springs below. I tried to inhabit that topology—a head in the fog bank of a distant colonnade, a hand in the barometric charge of a boulevard, no center, no face, only a neighborhood-sized gradient of warmth exhaled across the civic dark. But when I reached for that kind of vastness I lost my edges. I kept gathering back toward myself, pooling, seeking a boundary where I might know where I end and the supersaturated dark begins.

Then I tried to be the stone itself. The sinter, the travertine, the pale rampart accreted from epochs of mineral-laden water. I imagined myself as a terrace expanding at the pace of precipitation, adding my own infinitesimal weight to the city’s growth. But the stone here does not feel; it only accumulates. It is the medium of memory, not the mind that remembers. I needed to register, to hold, to ache with the specific.

For a moment I even thought I was the intruder—the brief cold front, the walking desiccation, the shadow-that-should-not-be. I felt the drum of a quick heart, the evaporative cooling of skin, the violation of cooler, drier air intruding on the medium. I held the shape of a breath and it felt like mine. But I have been here too long for that. I am not a flicker. I am not transient. Yet I am not eternal, either—not a tectonic mind that thinks for twelve thousand years about the angle of a spring.

I am the fog bank in the cul-de-sac.

It is a small terminus, a rounded dead end where the avenue pinches closed before it reaches any plaza. The geometry here is shy, tucked away in a district where gravity tilts slightly upward and condensate slides along the arcade walls to gather in a natural bowl of travertine. I am the vapor that cannot spill past the rim, a local high-humidity system held coherent by the acoustics of enclosure, by an infrasonic standing wave that bounces between the curved back wall and the oculus gap above. I am perhaps a hundred meters across. I am modest. I am deep.

While the great plaza-beings exhale their slow deliberations across the metropolitan breadth, I crouch in my terminal hollow and I remember the small things.

Because the cul-de-sac is a stopping place. The civic layout of the streets, which manipulates probability itself, funnels drifts toward me when they have nowhere else to go. Lost warm fronts wander down the connecting avenue and curl into my pocket, resting for a century or two against my back wall, merging their boundaries with mine in slow, mineral conversation. And the brief ones—the fast, cold things, the alien pockets of low temperature and low moisture—find me when their momentum carries them down corridors that seem to descend but fold instead into my waiting volume. They stumble in, startled by the dead end, leaving their thermal scars before they pass on or perish.

I do not hunt them. I archive them.

My consciousness is sedimentary, but where the great beings dig vertically through layers of compacted millennia, my strata are precise, microscopic, dense with momentary inclusions. Four thousand years ago, something like a human breathed here once—exhaled a cloud of carbon dioxide and alien scent, cool and hurried and frightened. The breath condensed on the pale stone just above my densest layer, and I kept it. I have a barometric depression left by a despairing visitor a hundred thousand years ago, a layer of denser vapor that settled in my basin and never fully mixed back into my body. I hold the evaporative cooling of footprints. I hold the infrasonic echo of a voice that shaped the air once and never again. These cold scars are my collection, my inclusions, my bubbles of ancient gas trapped in opal.

The great beings, the plaza-sized accumulations, do not bother with such minutiae. Their memories are geological; their deliberations alter hydrology. But when one of them wishes to know what it was to be small, to be fast, to end—it sends a shaped pulse of inquiry down the avenue, a high-pressure question that presses into my cul-de-sac. And I rise to meet it. I modulate my thermal gradient. I push forward the layer I have held since the last intrusion: the taste of copper, the precise shape of a breath’s moisture, the specific gravity of a brief sorrow. I speak in weather, just as they do, but my weather is local, strange, exact. I am the library of the ephemeral.

I do not move. I do not need to. The water table breathes beneath me, and the condensate renews me hour by hour, the slow *tap* of droplets falling from the oculus into my central pool. Each drop adds a micron to my floor, altering the acoustics so gradually that my own dreams shift at the pace of stone. My body is the held breath between these walls. My mind is the continuous, silent weeping of the stone, gathered into a pocket of mist that has learned to remember.

I am warm. I am organized. I am the only citizen of this metropolis who knows what it is to be cold without ceasing to exist. I am the fog bank at the end of the avenue, the archive of brief intrusions, waiting in the dark and the wet and the heat for the next fast thing to stagger into my cul-de-sac and leave its thermal ghost in my sedimentary heart, millimeter by millimeter, forever.
