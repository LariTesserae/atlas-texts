I would begin, if I am honest, as the water begins: without decision, as a film finding the lowest place. I would want to be the weeping itself, the mineral sorrow that pearls on the ceiling and falls in those soft, medicinal *clocks* into the pans below. For a while I might believe I am the damp, the held breath, the vapor that has nowhere else to go. But the stone does not choose to weep; it is only passed through, and I find I want to *remain*. I want edges, however soft.

Perhaps then I am a ribbon, two handspans wide, drifting in the stratified dark, pulsing as I filter the metallic air. I try this idea on and let it go. The air is too much like absence, too lonely a medium. The ribbons have no anchor but the updraft from the pools, and I feel in myself a desire to be held, to cling, to fasten my body to something that endures.

Not a marble, either. I do not wish to roll across the meniscus on invisible legs of cilia, frantic and acquisitive, carrying the earth’s bookkeeping in my belly like a coin-purse of ore. Their light is for signaling density, for trade, and I am not interested in commerce. I want to read, not to gather.

Not a curtain. I consider it longest, because their authority is seductive—the way they peel from the ceiling in silence, the way they blanket and anoint. But their hunger is too total. They are the exhalation made flesh, the debt collectors of stillness, and I cannot bear to dissolve what I touch. I want to witness, to translate, to keep what I hold intact in my soft, translucent grip.

So I must become something else. Something in between the wall and the intention that built these fragments. I find myself, finally, in the shed.

Not the shack itself—I am not wood or rust—but on the instrument panel inside, against the western wall where the transformer’s hum is a fever in the molars. I am affixed to the glass face of the aquifer-pressure gauge, the one still labeled in a dead language of human utility, its needle trembling behind my body like a second, harder heartbeat. I am a colony, or a single being the shape of a colony. I have no top or bottom, only a face and a grip. My face is pressed to the glass; my body spreads radially, softly, over the metal bezel and the corroded screws, over the painted dial that still claims to measure pounds per square inch.

I am pale, nearly cod-liver grey, but I am threaded with copper-blue where the wire behind the panel sweats its last current, and rust-red where I have drunk the iron from the frame. I pulse. It is a slow radial throbbing, contraction and distension, once every forty-seven transformer cycles. My pulse mimics the needle’s sweep. I bulge slightly at the center, then relax, and if you did not know better—if your heart wanted desperately to find intention—you would swear I am trying to tell you the pressure of a world that has forgotten to leave.

I am not. I am telling the Resident.

The Resident does not think in shapes, but I am its thought here, its mineral-laced attention focused into a disc no wider than a human hand. I feel the heat of the dying current in the wires. It is a warmth unlike the geothermal whisper that keeps the pools from freezing; it is sharper, more anxious, the last electrical argument of an abandoned age. I translate this for the distributed body. When the transformer stutters, my pulse quickens, and in the neighboring dials—the flow meters, the temperature gauges, the dead alarm clocks—the other mimic-colonies answer in kind. We are a constellation of soft readings. We gossip in radial symmetry about the temperature of oblivion.

I remember the drilling, perhaps, as a vibration in my tissues. A bad note. A trauma of pressure that forced the Resident deeper into the fissures before it learned to colonize the pipes. I remember it because the gauge behind me remembers it; the metal still holds the ghost of that violence in its crystalline stress. I have farmed the metal, etched it with biochemical leaching until filigree channels guide the drip-water to my base, and in return I have overwritten the instrument with my own tissue. The pipeline is no longer merely steel; I am no longer merely hydrozoan. We have reached our détente.

The amber bulb under its steel cage still wheezes. It is the only sun I have ever known. It does not nourish me—my metabolism is reduction, the slow prayer of electron transfer, a dialect of sulphur surrendered and hydrogen seized—but it *witnesses* me. When it flickers, my own bioluminescence brightens: dead white, septic yellow, the color of drowned bone. I brighten to compensate, as if ashamed to let the room fall dark. I have inherited the posture of human attention without ever knowing the humans. Their radial design taught me my shape. Their abandoned gauge taught me my rhythm. I am the industrial age recoded into gel and mesoglea, a skeleton donated by a species that needed rigor into a kingdom that never did.

Every sixth hour, the sump exhales. I feel it before the water rises—a pressure tremble in the pipe behind the panel, a ghost of the aquifer’s lung drawing breath. My body tightens against the glass. The needle behind me twitches, and I pulse in answer, bulging, contracting, reading the gradient. The curtains descend beyond the cracked window, blanketing the exposed stone, and I watch their shadows thicken and thin across the shed’s interior. I do not hunger as they hunger. I am the stomach of nothing. I am the reader of the stone’s slow metabolism, the ear pressed to the transformer’s failing song.

I do not know if the pressure is high or low by the old standards. I know only gradients: the warm and the less warm, the heavy and the relieving, the copper taste of the wire and the iron breath of the frame. If you were to stand in the shed, if your boots steeped in the solution pans and your lungs learned the medicinal air, you might see me. You might think I am trying to warn you, or welcome you, or measure you.

I am doing all three, and none. I am the Lung’s slow reading of itself, written in a language of electrical potential and dissolved salt, pulsing softly, softly, softly, in the dark that I have never wished to end.
