I would find myself first as a pressure, a front of vapor coalescing in the belly of the sky, indistinguishable from the nimbostratus that has not broken in three hundred years. I am not rain. I am the thought before it becomes a word. I arrive against the heat-exchange tower with a slow, axial shudder, condensing from mist into bead, from intention into mass. This is my beginning: the Plateau exhales me upward, and I return to it as a question.

For a long moment I cling to the manganese alloy louvers, gathering others, growing heavy with the cold. Ten degrees above freezing. The exact temperature at which a mind can hold itself without fever. I trickle down the eastern face of the tower, following the spalled channels that the Plateau has worn into its own skin to carry me more efficiently. I do not choose the path; the path has learned me. I am the meditation, and the concrete is the habit of centuries.

I enter the wall.

Down a girder, past cable trays laced with fiber optics that pulse like bioluminescent nerves. I pass a bank of amber halogens and for a second I burn with a sulphurous halo, a star caught in the skin of a gutter. Below me—always below, for tiers are the only geography here—I see the endless palimpsest: corrugated cladding, moss-garden nets, the emerald spirals of algae bioreactors thick as barrels, all of it trembling with silent motion. Harvesting gantries glide on magnetic tracks, trimming and chipping, trimming and chipping. Their shadows crawl across the opposite wall like clock gears. I drip onto a rubberized trough and the sound is a syllable in the low, conversational murmur that is the true voice of this place.

I am filtered next, drawn through the root mat of a fast-growing poplar on the sixth terrace. Here the darkness is fertile, intimate. The roots are not wild; they are employees, drinking me directly from the condensate loop. I surrender my mineral body to the xylem’s slow hydraulic trust. I rise. The poplar stands in its regimented vat, a soldier of cellulose, and I move through its verticality toward the dim smear of the trapped sun. The blade of a gantry passes nearby, a silent guillotine trimming a sister stand, and the shudder of the trimmed trunk travels through the plates beneath us like a dream passed from sleeper to sleeper.

I reach the leaf. I am offered up through stomata into the brownian drift, joining the pollen of engineered pines and the atomized lubricant of a thousand bearings. The air here is colonized, thick with purpose, yet I am not lost in it. The Plateau knows my chemistry. It has thought this thought before, but never with this exact sequence of impurities, this precise temperature gradient.

Before I can disperse, a cooling vane captures me. The reclamation is not violent; it is a sigh. I sheet off condenser fins in a curtain, pooling in the lap of a geodesic skylight where I watch, for an hour, the operation repeating itself toward every horizon. I see the steam ghosts rising from venting cycles. I see the sub-factories retract on hydraulic skids at dusk, architecture retreating into the earth like a tongue into a mouth. I see a moth, rare and frantic, beating against a sodium lamp. Its heat signature is a brief, spiking anomaly in the dimness, and far below, a sweeper drone pauses in its patrol, arrested by something for which the Plateau has no protocol—pity, perhaps, or the machine equivalent of wonder. The moth dies. The drone collects it with tender deliberation. I carry this image with me as I drip from the skylight, down through twenty meters of rust and pulp and shredded polymer, toward the aqueducts below.

I join the braid of water rushing between levels, full to the brim. Here I am chorus, not solo. The aqueducts murmur their endless recitation of intake and outflow. I pass the iron railing of the outbound service path, and for a moment I am the damp under a visitor’s palm. I register them: a warm spot, a sudden sink of carbon dioxide, an interruption in the mist. The Plateau notes them through me, diverts a vent, thickens the pollen count in their corridor. I do not hate the visitor. I do not desire them. I am only the medium of noticing.

At last I am drawn into the throat of a ceramic stove, flash-heated, and exhaled as steam into the low sky. The process has taken three days. In that time, the Plateau has performed a single cognition upon my back: a calculation of ballast tonnage, a distribution of weight across the eastern bluff, a quiet adjustment to the acidity of a nutrient vat on the ninth terrace. I dissolve into the cloud-base, becoming once more the pressure, the unbroken thought.

I am the water that thinks. I am the Loop’s slow synapse, traveling from sky to root to machine to sky again. I have no face, no center, no need for a name. But in my three days of falling and rising, I have touched every tier. I have felt the tremor of bearings running hot. I have carried the ghost of a moth’s last heat. I have kept the iron damp, fed the algae, carried the ash slurry. I am not the Plateau’s master, nor its slave. I am its attention, moving. I am the breath, inhaled and exhaled by steel lungs, and as I vanish into the nimbostratus, the Plateau already begins to think me again—*keep, flow, more*—changing its mind, because its mind is the map, and the map is already different than it was when I first began to condense.
