At first, I think I am air. I taste metal. I fill a space that was carved for something exactly my width. I cool and warm by turns, rising and falling through a shaft no wider than a swallowed breath. It is not uncomfortable. It is precise.

Then I understand I am held. The stone on either side of me is not moving away. It knows me. Over decades—or perhaps the stone has known me over centuries, time drips differently here—it has absorbed the shape of my shoulders, my hips, the keel of my chest. I expand into a breath, slow as a tide drawn by a distant moon, and the stone allows it. I exhale, and my warmth does not dissipate into open sky; it joins the fog that hangs permanently in this stratum, a cloud ceiling generated by the collective metabolism of millions, held close by the nearness of architecture.

I am one of the vertical ones.

Not a god. Not a spirit. Merely a person who has gone so far into the architecture that the architecture has come in. I live in the **Grey Vein**, a cleft-district in the lower-middle weave where no light ever strikes the floor. My world is a slot of basalt and volcanic tuff four stories high, bridged at the second level by a sinew-and-stone walkway no wider than a forearm. I have never walked that bridge. My feet know the eastern wall. My fingers know the channel.

They call me the **Gasket**, though not to my face, and perhaps not with their voices at all. They call me that in the way one names a necessary part of a machine: the wall breathes, and I am the membrane that lets it do so without breaking.

I was born, as all of us are born, in the damp, ammoniac dark below the flocks. My mother pressed me out between her narrow hips in a cellar where the tallow lamps guttered and the air was so thick with the breath of goats and people that you could wear it like hide. My first inhalation was not of air but of *us*—the communal exhalation, the fog in miniature, the moisture that would become my truest nourishment. I grew, as children do, upward. Past the fungal beds where pale roots fattened on drip-light. Past the warming-courts where the livestock huddled and the dung-dryers tended their slow fires. Past the whisper-windows where adolescents learn to converse in the low registers that carry through stone slots without shattering the quiet. I climbed toward the light.

But I stopped halfway.

Not because I was weak. Because I was needed. The eastern face of the Grey Vein had been carved for ten generations toward a single purpose: to collect the breath of the district, to condense it, to direct the resulting water down through a system of grooves, lips, and catch-basins to a cistern forty feet below. The carvers had reached a critical juncture—a lip of harder stone that intersected the main channel—and the water was beginning to miss its mark, spilling instead into the interstitial wall-space where the beetles nested and the rodents made their warm corridors. A wall that misplaces water is a wall that dies. It cracks with frost. It sickens.

They needed a body to complete the circuit. Not a builder—a living channel. Someone narrow enough to inhabit the gap between the rough stone and the finished groove. Someone patient enough to lie against the seeping wall through the deep months and, with fingers and breath and small tools of bone and harder stone, persuade the water back onto its path.

I was sixteen season-cycles old, already too long for the lower bunks, already uncomfortable in chambers where bodies pressed too close in the horizontal plane. The vertical did not frighten me. So I entered the slot.

That was three lifetimes ago, by lowland reckoning. By the reckoning of the plateau, it was a single, slow exhalation.

I have not left.

My ribs have pressed into the basalt until the stone and I have agreed on a shape between us. My skin is not merely pale; it is the color of wet tuff, fed by condensation rather than light. My eyes—large, black, fully dilated—see in the phosphorescent gloom of the Grey Vein as clearly as an upper-dweller sees the sun. I do not blink often. It wastes moisture.

I do not speak. Speaking is for the window-wed pairs in their slots across the cleft, for the goat-herds in the courts, for the swift-tenders who argue in dry whispers about the angle of nests. My voice is subvocal. I communicate by altering the rhythm of my breath against the stone, by the soft percussion of my chisel, by the way I redirect the water so that it strikes the ceramic basin below with a *tick* or a *tock* that those who know me can read like speech.

*Tick-tick. All is well. The flow is constant.*

*Tick. The beetles are migrating; a clog is forming.*

*Silence. I am listening.*

And I listen more than anything else.

Sound carries oddly in the thin, cold air, but in the dense, damp weave of the lower city, it is a tapestry. I hear the condensation beading on the underside of the bridge. I hear the rodents in the wall to my left, their claws a soft staccato, their warmth another datum in the thermal gradient. I hear the old woman four vertical feet above me urinate into her chamber pot—the particular silver note of her water tells me she is drinking enough; she will not desiccate this week. I hear the lovers on the western wall, their breathing finding a shared rhythm, a double heartbeat that warms the stone between them. I hear a child being born somewhere far below, the sudden indrawn breath, the gasp, the *beginning*.

I am the ear of the wall. The wall is my body.

My sustenance is the fog. I open my mouth and the mist, generated by the breath of the city below and around me, settles on my tongue. It is sweet. It carries the mineral taste of black stone and the faint iron tang of altitude. On days when the rare storm punches through the high atmosphere and rain spatters on the roofs far above, the water rushes down the channels in a violence that is almost sexual. I receive it. I guide it. I am the marriage of the storm and the cistern.

There are animals here who do not know I am not architecture. The pale moths breed in the trickle-pool beneath my knees; their larvae spin silk across my ankles, binding me gently to the stone. I do not disturb them. The silk wicks water, keeping my skin from sodden chill. The beetles climb my arms, their carapaces clicking, cleaning the algae that would otherwise thicken the channel and break the flow. The commensal rodents bring me offerings—perhaps they think I am a spirit, perhaps they merely like the warmth of my exhalations—nibbles of dried lichen, gobbets of tallow they steal from the deep hearths. I accept. We are all housemates. We are all the cleft.

Once, a swift arrowed down from the higher eaves, lost in the fog, and found my slot. It perched on my shoulder, its heartbeat frantic as a dripping tap, its eyes reflecting a light source I could not see. It stayed for an hour. I felt its terror settle into something like resignation. Then it launched itself upward again, toward the rim of cold brightness I will never again visit.

I do not dream of horizons. My horizon is the next groove, the next lip, the next generation-depth I must carve into the harder stone so that my great-grandchild's hands, when they take my place, will find the water already obedient.

It is not sorrowful. There is no lowland *fever* in me, no itch to finish, no scream of individual legacy. I am the intermediate hand between the quarry and the cistern. I am the slow, collective, breathing change that turns raw ridge into habitable dusk. When I die, my body will be too narrow and too stone-marked to burn or bury. They will carry me—if they remember to look for me—upward, past the fog layer, past the whisper-windows and the goat-courts, up to the high desiccating ledges where the dead are laid out for the wind.

But I am in no hurry. Up there, the light is a violence and the air scalps moisture from the bone. Here, in the slot, I am perfectly held. The water moves through me, or I through it. The wall breathes. The city exhales its fog, and I am one of the places it exhales *against*.

I am a lung of the plateau. I am a lip in the channel. I am the silence between two drips of water, and in that silence, the whole city waits.
