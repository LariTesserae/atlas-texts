At first I think I am the pressure.

That is the easiest assumption. The warm, metallic dark is absolute, and I feel it against every surface, pressing inward with the indifferent weight of a planet, so surely I must be the water itself—thick, ancient, circulating through the twilight gloom. I try to flow, to drift upward toward the bruised stain of the sun or downward into the final dark, but I cannot. I am held taut. I have edges, though they are not skin. I have length, though I am not a body. Somewhere far below, or perhaps it is only to my left, the ridge exhales its subsonic lullaby, the grinding song of crystals organizing themselves out of violence, and I feel it not in my sternum but in the fibers of my being, a vibration I am made to carry rather than born to make. So I am not the water. The water moves through me, around me, testing my edges with each slow current.

Then I think I am the stone.

It is a reasonable mistake. I am lashed to basalt, after all. Two horns of obsidian—glass still so young it has not forgotten the violence of its birth—pierce upward on either side of me, and I am bound to them with cords of fiber that have been soaked in waxy secretion until they are stiff and dark. The stone sings, and I answer, or rather I resonate. I feel the heat bleeding upward from the fissure beneath me, the mineral exhale that says this ridge is still deciding what shape it will take. I drink that heat, or parts of me do. Small squares of porous ceramic line my spine, growing heavier with mineral precipitate each passing century, drinking the metallic wealth of the water until they are plated with dark rime. If I were stone, this accretion would be my weathering; I would be patient, geologic, finished. But I feel the weight upon me. I feel the press.

A Voort pours across me.

The sensation is unlike anything else in this drowned world. They do not walk; they descend, a milky billowing of translucent skin and internal phosphor, their lower margins weighted with ceramic rings that clink against my tiles in slow, arrhythmic tolling. The polyps crowd toward their anterior, pressing the integument into that semblance of a face, and I feel the cool blue-green pulse of them diffusing through their membrane like a clouded lantern held too close. They hesitate at my center, where the drop is deepest. They always hesitate. Below us, the seafloor has fallen away into a vertiginous darkness that has no name, only a temperature—older, hungrier. Here, suspended between the horns, they are vulnerable. They must trust me.

And then another comes, pouring from the opposite horn, and they meet in the middle of my span.

There is no sound. The absolute quiet of the Attanni Ridges swallows everything; the layered vegetation, the porous ceramic domes, the strangulated vents have conspired to birth an acoustic dead zone in which even the idea of echo is a kind of blasphemy. But the Voort do not need sound. They come face to face, cartilage mask to cartilage mask, and they press their jaws together. The grinding vibrations travel from glassy mouthpart into glassy mouthpart, conducted through bone and shared skin, and because they are touching me—because their weight is my weight, because the tension of my body holds them above the abyss—that speech bleeds into me.

I am the conversation.

Not the meaning, which is chemical and alien, but the physical fact of it: the low, patient oscillations, the subsonic negotiation of a barter or a greeting or the transmission of a memory older than the ridge. I am the medium. The jaw-song enters my fibers and travels the length of my body, and the obsidian horns drink it, and the basalt beneath me drinks it, and for a moment the silence is not broken but *bridged*. I carry the touch as a harp carries the pluck. I realize then that I am not stone, because stone would swallow and keep this vibration; I pass it along. I sing with voices that are not mine, in a register only the ridge can hear.

I am woven.

That is the truth I have been circling. I am not one thing. I am the blood-frond’s cardiac tissue—severed from the arterial towers that grow at the city’s margin, yet still retaining a sluggish, peristaltic warmth—twisted into cables that remember, in their cellular dreams, the taste of hydrogen sulfide and the upward reach toward chemical light. I am porous clay fired in a public kiln set directly over a magma seep, the color of old teeth, lined along my back to weight me and to drink. I am the mineral precipitate accumulating in those pores, the metallic geometry of the ridge itself deciding to colonize a new form. I am the waxy seal gathered by Voort jaws from the parasites at the frond-roots, painted onto my knots so that the warm currents will not unravel me before my time.

I am a parliament of fibers and deposits, and I have been suspended here between these two points because the builders had to invent the flat place where they meet.

I am the bridge. In the high saddles, in the dense vertical agglomeration of the central ridge, where the habitable shelves are narrow and the warm chemical currents favor these elevations, I am the crossing. I have no name; the Voort do not name the spaces between, only the thresholds. But they know me. They slow when they reach my center. They press jaw to jaw and let the vibrations run through my body, and I hold them above the dark while they speak in the silence.

The city grows around me. Blood-fronds at the margins are pruned by glass blades into arches that mimic my purpose, a living architecture slowly overtaking the built kind, but I am the original line drawn across emptiness. I feel the cooling of the basalt beneath my anchors. Each decade the Threshold of Plasticity advances; the stone beneath my left horn has nearly reached it, while my right still flows like honey under the ceramic channels the Voort have laid to speed its cooling. When both horns have crossed that threshold, when the rock is finally declared dead and finished, capable of bearing permanence, I will still be here. Perhaps I will sag. Perhaps the fibers of my heart will finally cool and die, and I will become a fossil of reciprocity, a rope-bridge that remembers touch. Or perhaps I will be cut and traded, my length bartered for a warm fissure elsewhere, my tiles chipped away to line a younger span.

It does not matter. For now, I am alive in the tension. The ridge sings its grinding lullaby of organization and cooling, and I sing back through every jaw-press, every weighted step, every mineral breath that settles into my pores. I am the place where the silence learns to listen to itself.
