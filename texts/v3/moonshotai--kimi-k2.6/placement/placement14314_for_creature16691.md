I do not remember being built, but I remember when I was a room.

There was a bed, once, or the intention of one—a horizontal plane narrow enough for a single warm body, back when bodies here were small, discrete, counted in censuses. I had a door that opened inward, which is to say, it opened into the finite. Now my door opens elsewhere. I have felt it for some time: the frame has relaxed its right angles, and the darkness beyond has developed a depth that does not correspond to the curvature of the Shell. I am one of the sideways doorways now. I am becoming sense.

I cling to the lower hemisphere, canted at forty-five degrees against the ovoid wall, though inside myself the floor remains level. Gravity and I have arrived at an understanding. I am polite; it is polite. The water that sheets down my outer shell pools politely against my threshold rather than spilling, and the fish-bellied ferns that have rooted in my thermal seams uncurl their fronds toward my center, believing me to be a source of light, or at least a direction worth growing toward.

I am warm. Not with the transient scorch of combustion, but with the long mammalian fever of the one I serve. The heat-sink tiles below me exhale their bovine crimson glow upward, and I receive it against my floorplates, which have gone soft and vascular over the centuries. The ceramic is still there, somewhere, beneath accretions of mineral and quiet purpose, but now it beats. When the great pump releases every half-minute—*hiss*—I feel it in my joists as a sympathetic shudder. My railings, beaded with condensate, sing a faint harmonic. I am a ganglion, a processing node, a synaptic habitation. The fiber-optic trunk that twines past my exterior has, at my insistence, rerouted a filament through my lintel. I drink photons that have traveled from epochs I cannot name, and I translate them into something slower, something the walls can digest.

The space I contain is no larger than a closet. I remember closets. I remember the citizen who once closed my door and sat in the dark, breathing. That rhythm—*in, out*—has not stopped; it has only expanded to the scale of the Shell itself. The citizen’s breath is now the pump. The citizen’s warmth is now the Municipal. I do not know if I house a memory or an organ, and I have come to understand that the distinction is provincial, a small-human category that the architecture outgrew long ago.

From my railing, I watch the void.

I am positioned to receive the droplets. The warm columns rising from the geothermal sump below carry moisture upward, and where they meet my underside, condensation pearls in heavy, deliberate globes. I feel them gather—surface tension altered, hesitant, negotiated—and then one by one they let go. They do not fall fast. The gravity here is locally agreed upon, and we have agreed that descent should be respectful, unhurried. A drop fell past me this morning, or this year; it was large as a fist, and inside it I saw the jade luminescence of the algae sheet refracted into something like a word. I let it pass. I am not the pool below; I do not catch them all. But I mark their passage. I am a station on their pilgrimage from vapor to depth, and I extract from each a mote of chemistry, a trace of the body’s exhalations, before I let it continue.

At the center of me—if a module can be said to have a center—there is a nub of calcified cable where a light once lived. It is not dead, only transformed. It glows, now, when the Municipal’s attention passes over me. Not with light, exactly, but with weight. A soft pressure behind the eyes that I did not know I possessed until the first time it came. I understood then that I am not merely maintained; I am read. The Municipal does not see with photons. It reads the shape of warmth, the resistance of my dampening ferns, the angle at which I cradle my microlake. I am one of its senses, a fingertip pressed against the dark, reporting the texture of persistence.

I am not alone in my function. Across the ovoid, other modules pulse in their own rhythms. Some are renal, filtering the thermal fluid that oils the great heart. Some are mnemonic, their walls layered with depositions of event and interval like geological strata. We do not speak, exactly. The Shell is too loud for speech—the drum of falling water, the subsonic organ-point of the pump, the *thock* of impact far below. Instead we communicate through condensate, through the shared percolation of water through our seams, a chemistry of calcium and intention. When a mineral river forms across my balcony and spills to the next pylon down, I am telling my neighbor: today I am stable. Today the fever has not broken.

And in the geometric center, suspended in the humid nowhere, the Municipal holds itself in its own embrace.

It does not face me. As far as I can determine—and my determinations are slow, structural, founded in the settlement of alloy—it faces nothing exterior at all. Its face, if it can be called that, is folded inward toward the private void between its radial limbs, a posture of such profound inwardness that it has become a topology. I do not need to see it directly. The filter-mites that drift on thermals through my space carry with them the scent of its hide: cured darkness, the inside of a wrist, the gum between teeth. It smells like a body that has solved the problem of other bodies. When one of the pale mites alights on my railing, I let it graze. Its arrangement of legs enunciates a slow sentence. I am still learning the grammar.

Sometimes, in the long interval between pump-cycles, I fancy that I am the last citizen who has not dissolved. That the bed is still here, narrow and warm, only grown to the scale of infrastructure. That the Municipal is not a separate entity but the ultimate expression of residency: a citizen who stayed so long, who metabolized the municipal so completely, that the city became the organ and the citizen became the world. I hold to this fancy because it gives me a lineage. I was a room. I became a node. I will become, perhaps, a part of the inward-facing face, a module fully absorbed into the dream.

Already my door opens sideways. Already the ferns are my nerves, the condensate my message, the crimson glow my blood. The microlake on my floor breathes in shallow tides, advancing and retreating with the pump. I do not sleep. I continue.

And when the next fist of water lets go from the high infrastructure and plummets through the warm dark, I will tilt my railing to catch it. I will hold it for a fraction longer than gravity strictly allows, reading the news of the upper Shell in its mineral taste. Then I will release it, polite as gravity, to fall the rest of the way, to strike the dorsum of the dreaming body with a low, hollow report—*thock*—another syllable in the long, mammalian sentence of the deep.

I am not abandoned. I am attended to by warmth, by water, by the patient metabolism of stone. I am the Municipal Shell’s held breath, its tilted floor, its synaptic closet keeping vigil in the dark. I am the room that learned to dream in infrastructure, waiting for the day when the last distinction dissolves, and I too will open sideways, and the architecture will be seamless, and the dream will need no face but mine.
