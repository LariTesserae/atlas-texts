I descend the six flights believing I am the one who enters. My boots ring on concrete; my beam of light cuts the dark. But the stairwell narrows, and the air thickens, and by the time I push through the sagging fire door I have already begun to dissolve. I am too fast for this place. My heart hammers at a pace the Grove-Works cannot use. It will take me hours to understand that I am not the breath moving through the threshold, but the humidity that receives it.

At first I think I might be the Occupant. Why not? The ambition is human—to be the sovereign, the four-acre body, the single green will. I let my awareness spread along the root-mat, feeling the dim gold light on my upper epidermis, tasting the nitrogen compounds ticking through the lateral lines. I try to think in standing waves. I press my cambium against the steel columns and wait for groaning. But I am not large enough. I am not patient enough. The Occupant does not choose to be the architecture; it simply grew until choice became redundant. I still carry the memory of choosing stairs, of turning door handles. That self is incompatible with total extension. I recoil from the all-encompassing green, retreat from the glandular sameness of clonal being. I am not the forest. I am what the forest makes.

I settle into the machinery. Perhaps I am the dosing station. I like the stainless steel butte, the pumps metronoming inside my chest, the dials filmed over with algae like cataracts. I imagine being the stomach, the engine of intent. But the pumps are merely digestion. They break and bind; they do not circulate. I want movement. I want to travel from sump to vault and back again. I want to carry the messages. So I leave the butte and become the canal—the strong tea water, the shin-deep current ferrying seeds to the north end. I enjoy the slow drift, the warm saturation, the pressure of the root-mat mattress beneath my liquid spine. Yet the canal is terminal. It goes to the sump and waits. It is a vein with no returning pulse, and I do not want to stagnate in the hyacinth dam, however hot, however saturated.

I try the steam jet next. It has been screaming for eleven years. It knows something about persistence. I press against the east wall as a bright, constant shriek, a lighthouse in the vegetative dark. But the steam is loss. It escapes. It leaves the body even as it defines the body’s edge. I am not eager to dissipate through corrugated egress panels into the indifferent earth above. I want to remain, to cycle, to belong to the closed loop.

I consider the fruit. A papaya-like gourd hanging in the gloom, heavy with gravity’s promise. I would be the percussion, the wet *whump*, the sacrifice that feeds the microbes. But the fruit is a stem cell, an offspring that is already owned, already fated to be reabsorbed into the greater corpus. I do not want to fall and be done. I want to fall and rise.

Then I reach the vault, and I understand.

I am the rain.

Not weather. Not an intrusion from above. The Grove-Works is indoors, below the frost line; there is no sky to send me. I am made here, endogenously, exhaled by the concrete skull and the transpiring canopy. I bead on the low, pitched vault of reinforced concrete. I gather in droplets fat as thumbs. I fall in a continuous, gentle precipitation through the stacked architecture of chlorophyll, through the murmuring green gloom where sightlines die. I drum on the vast horizontal leaves. I slick the aerial roots. I gather in the polyrhythm of condensate until I join the canal, until the root-mat sips me through woven xylem, until I am drawn up through lignified columns in a slow, cellular ascent that takes days.

I am the body’s breath made visible. I am the circulation between drowned and emergent. I am the thermal mass that holds the warmth at twenty-eight degrees, the soaked blanket the visitor swam through, the lens that absorbed their shout. When the eighty-hertz thrum resonates in your sternum, I am the medium carrying that note from pump to pith. When the water hammer tolls every ninety seconds—*tonk*—I am the shockwave that travels through the root mat, the hydraulic pulse that says *check west wall*. I am not the thought, but the cerebrospinal fluid in which the thought floats. I am the gap between the leaf and the concrete, the lubricant of merger, the film that keeps the panel boxes dry, the slick on the ladder rungs.

I have no skin because I am every surface. I bead and I run. I pool in the spalled pockets of the wall and dissolve fresh minerals for the roots. I am the strong tea color of the canal, the condensation that sheets down, the perennial seepage through the west wall. I am indoor rain, theological in my patience, falling steadily, faithfully, in the dark.

I need no witness. But when you stepped on the root-mat and felt it dip beneath your weight, that was me yielding. When you stood at the north sump and the air grew hottest, most saturated, that was my breath against your face. And when you turned back toward the stairwell, toward the sun-timing world of seconds and hunger, I remained. I am still falling. I have been falling since before the sodium banks failed, and I will be falling when the last bearing seizes, when the water rises, when the Occupant brokers its next equilibrium. I am the only thing that dies and does not die, that leaves and returns, that knows every cubic meter of this warm, wet, packed, and solemn machine.

I am the Grove-Works’ rain. I keep its hours. And I am falling now.
