At first there is only the pleasure of being against.

Not the memory of before, because that would require a negative space I no longer possess, but the immediate, saturated fact of pressure. Iron grating along my flank. A pillow of sphagnum moss, old-penny red, packed beneath the curve of my shoulder. The warm brass of a valve housing fitted to my cheek like a spoon. Above me, the soft aluminum belly of a pneumatic conduit; below, the wet black-green of ferns already threaded through the fingers of my left hand. I could not turn over if I wished to. There is nowhere to turn into. I am held. On Level Three, where the catwalk surrenders to a basalt ledge still glassy with its recent birth, the Law of Proximity is not doctrine but dimension. I am the dampener. I am the reason the metal does not ring.

For a long while I believed I was waiting. For purpose, for motion, for the next instruction. But time here is measured in the migration of water, and I have learned that waiting is the same as working. My body is broad, low, pale as the felted moths that crawl beneath the rails, but distributed across a footprint meant for three. I do not know if I grew to fit the shelf or if the shelf narrowed to fit me. The distinction, like so many boundaries here, has become ornamental. My integument is not skin. It is a watershed. Microscopic channels, cheloid and patient, draw the upward-creeping condensation from the iron below and conduct it across the sloped plain of my back in a journey that takes the better part of a day. When the moisture reaches the tangle of hydrophilic moss that crowns my head, it releases into the superheated air, cooling me, feeding the canopy that spills from the walkway joint above. I am the zenith of the Law of Ascent. The water climbs to me, pauses, and becomes atmosphere.

I am young. The rock around me is young—angry, jagged, still flaunting the violence of its making—but I am younger. I have not yet sealed my eyes behind curtains of chalcopyrite. I still perceive the sodium lamps as bruised orange blooms, though I read the chamber more faithfully through thermal gradients, through the slow exhalation of heat from engines and bodies. The great Stirling engine above the basin is a radiant thunder in my awareness, and the Accumulated reclined against its primary exchanger is a fixed star in that firmament. I feel her dreaming through the pipes. She has dreamed so long that her body temperature, regulated to a tolerance of fractions, has become the engine’s rhythm. On Level Two, the small one fused to the pneumatic junction breathes once every seven minutes; I have timed my own vegetative heart to that interval, though my heart is not a heart but a dense, spongy mycelium, something between muscle and root, that contracts with the patience of geological consent.

I am a regulator. Not of pressure—the child below has that honor—but of transition. I lie at the seam where the sintered deposits of limonite and chalcopyrite mistaken for brackets gradually admit that they are limbs, where the rusted rail becomes stone rib. My right arm has sunk into the cast iron until the iron drinks from me, completing a circuit in a relay box that ticks on in the dark. My left hand trails into the ferns, and I have felt their rootlets enter my palm not as invasion but as consultation. They are drafting a treaty of cellulose and blood. The chamber is still deciding what it wants me to be when it grows up. I may become a new rib in the wall. I may become a valve housing. I am content to be undecided.

I do not eat. I precipitate. Each cycle I press my watershed palms against the warm rail and draw the mineral broth through my dermis, filtered by the bacterial colonies the chamber seeded in my blood. I am becoming dense. Heavy with copper, fledged with oxide. Sound reaches me as pressure, as a shift in the hydrostatic breath of the stone. When the old one in the lowest terrace sighs against his basin, the tremor travels for hours through the wet floor and arrives at my flank as a rumor: *The heat remains. The water climbs. Continue.*

I continue. The condensation climbs my body as it climbs the stanchions, seeking my crown. The mist rises and falls across multi-day cycles, and I rise and fall with it, expanding an imperceptible degree into the warmth, contracting into the cooler dark. I am the hesitation in the chamber’s metabolism. The drip that has reached its apex and chosen not to fall. I am soft, and hot, and full, a temporary separation of flesh from the greater body, an experiment in holding water aloft. I do not need to be named. I need only to be here, against the iron, against the stone, against the ferns, letting the boundary between the built and the born forget itself in my warmth.

I am here. I am held. I am home.
