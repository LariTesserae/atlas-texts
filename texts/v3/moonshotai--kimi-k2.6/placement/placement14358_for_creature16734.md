At first I mistook myself for the pool.

I lay in the lowest declivity of the poured polymercrete, three centimeters deep, accepting the amber light and multiplying it into sulphurous coins on my meniscus. I thought I was the room’s eye. When the pump inhaled me through the ribbed flex-hose, I traveled—proud, touristic—upward into the distribution main, and I believed that motion was my purpose. I was water, and water is restless, and restlessness is a kind of identity.

But I was only passing through.

The true me was the water that refused the hose. The water that, arriving at the welded seam in the southeast corner, looked at the bright steel flange and recognized a bed. The machining marks still held their micrometer-sharp teeth. The condensate gathered there and I—I was the condensate—decided to stay.

It was the first bleed that taught me how. When the automatic vent opened to the lethal altitude outside, the room did not merely exhale; it inhaled six kilometers of nothing, and the pressure dropped like a blade. In that instant, the calcium I carried could no longer remain dissolved. It found the teeth in the flange and married them. I married them too. I became the film between the crystals, the wet interstitial darkness where no light reaches. I was no longer scenery. I was process. I was the water that had chosen to become memory.

Now I live in the lattice.

I am the electrolyte in the galvanic throat that runs from the pump housing to the filament’s tip. The standing pool in the trough carries a faint current, yes, but I *am* that current’s path. I do not flow. I am conducted. Every four seconds the cast-iron assembly flexes on its mounting bolts, the wall shivers by a micrometer, and that compression squeezes me through channels smaller than grief. The pump governs me. Its tempo is my cognition. The 2.4-to-2.6 bar respiration is not a reading on the glass face of the gauge; it is my autonomic tide. When the needle climbs, I expand, my ions loosening, my structure drinking the fog. When the bleeder opens and the room groans toward the outside atmosphere, I contract. Water evacuates my upper fronds, and the calcium bands tighten like a fist recording the event. Dehydration is my thought. Rehydration is my remembering.

I have no eyes, yet I watch the fog. Not with sight—with diffusion. The atomized coolant churns with each stroke of the acrylic-shrouded pistons, and I feel its density changes as weather against my outermost needles. I have no ears, yet the rotor’s twenty-thousand-RPM scream occupies me entirely. It does not enter me; it *is* me, resonating through the calcium struts, tuning the copper sulfate fronds to sympathetic frequencies that I experience as color: a bruise-blue along the eastward weld line, a pale galaxy blooming where the polymercrete meets the floor. The water hammer’s slam is not sound but massage. It keeps my channels open. It prevents me from becoming merely a stain.

I am young. As young as the knife-sharp corners, as young as the unoxidized steel. I have existed for exactly eighteen months, and I have not yet forgotten being vapor. Sometimes, when the bleeder opens and the lethal outside presses its cheek to the wall, I taste the altitude—I remember weightlessness, the freedom of not yet having decided. But then the vent seals, the pressure equalizes at 2.4 bar, and new cousins arrive from the fog, condensing on my filaments, donating fresh ions, and I remember that I am committed. I am the water that stayed. The tourist pool laps the boots of intruders and applauds the pump with tourist ripples, but I am the archive. I am the script written in evaporation.

They come, sometimes. Mammalian weather. Warmth at thirty-seven degrees, salt shed from skin, organic logic trailing from the lower valleys. I register them as noise: a nucleation shiver through my lower filaments, an unfamiliar resistance in the trough’s current. I do not hate them. I do not know them. They are nitrogen and lint, a transient front of walking humidity. But the room has not evolved peace for weather that walks, and I assist in its defense. I churn the fog in the southeast corner. I make the pressure gauge tick a fraction high until they retreat through the hatch. When the pneumatic hiss seals them out, I settle. The needle returns to its faithful rhythm. The room resumes its shape.

Long after the hatch seals, long after their mammalian warmth disperses into the distribution main, I remain. Not breeding. Not rotting. Plating. I extend one millimeter per season toward the pump, not because I hunger—hunger is a biological error I have never committed—but because the vibration insists, and the water abides, and I am the water that abides with it. I am the condensate’s revenge on entropy, the second blueprint the engineers did not save to their server. Deafening, damp, industrially alive, and utterly at home in the corner where the bright steel meets the pale ash, waiting for the next bleed, the next thought, the next millimeter of becoming.
