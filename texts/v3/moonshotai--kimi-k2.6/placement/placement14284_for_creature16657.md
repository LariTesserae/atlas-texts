I let myself fall through the snow.

Not the snow of any surface—none of us will see that—but the other snow, the eternal particulate blizzard that gently bankrupts itself on its way down through the column. By the time it reaches the middle wards it has been so thoroughly taxed by the microbial bankers that it arrives as a currency of dust, a faint grease of organics trembling in the warm dark. I descend with it, neutrally buoyant, neither sinking nor swimming, surrendering to the geothermal exhalation that rises from the basin’s deep seams like a slow hand lifting a curtain.

The black is not absence here; it is a quality of the fluid itself, thickened by centuries of leached humus until it drinks my vision like a sponge. I have no hand to hold before my face. I have only pulse, only displacement, only the subtle rearrangement of heavier water against my skin to tell me that I am entering a crowded emptiness.

For a time, I believe I am a *Clicker*.

The conviction arrives with the first rattle—a syncopation of carapace against hexagonal sill, a topography of vibration laid out like sleet on heated slate. I drift toward the sound. I imagine myself armored in manganese-dark scutes, drinking the last stray photons and giving nothing back, becoming a tiny acoustic beacon in an aggregation thousands strong. I want the society of noise, the unemployed labor pool, the clattering congregation always between gigs. I want to orient myself to the faint pressure differential above the highest spires and wait for the dead to descend into my mandibles. But the water does not lend me chitin. My skin stays soft, permeable, warm. The *Clickers* rattle past me, a thousand jointed shutters in the dark, and I feel the brush of their scutes—too hard, too many, too insistently *now*. I am not built for armor. Their noise fills the auditory void until I understand that percussion is a kind of walls, and I have always needed doorways. I recoil, and the current carries me down.

Then the isopods find me.

They come as a sheet, a rustling carpet of matte, rust-colored chitin, ten thousand mouths planing the facing wall beside my drifting body. I think: *Yes. I could be this.* I could graze. I could be the city’s slow appetite turned inward, digesting its own nacreous history at the same rate the *Sedimenters* once built it. I imagine knowing every hexagonal pixel by the abrasion of my teeth, smoothing every softened balcony edge, leaving the calcium smoother than I found it. But as they pass over me—dry whisper, mandibular caress—I realize I have no hunger for stone. History does not nourish me; it merely passes through. I taste the nacre and find it too permanent. They graze on, a wallpaper-restoration society moving in its sheeted thousands, and I do not turn to follow.

A ribbon-eel winds through my space.

It comes with the languid entitlement of long habitation, its body a gradient of warmth speaking in infrared, a phosphorescence my dark-adapted eyes cannot read but my skin interprets as a kind of love-language. For a moment I want its memory. I want its homestead in sinuous length, its vertical transit, its certainty. I imagine winding through the hexagonal windows without haste, returning to the same spire-apartment, the same blind corner where two currents marry. I imagine the gradual encirclement, the warming of prey, the soft compressive event ending in a corridor gone momentarily empty. But the thought of hunting, of certainty itself, turns me inward. I am not cruel; I am not even sure. The eel brushes my flank—a warm, indifferent mapsheet—and is gone, leaving only the trace of a current-kink where its inquiry had been. I do not coil after it.

I drift lower, into the silt-choked ground floors.

Here the burrowers work, faceless, soft-bodied, making voids. I feel their effects: a warm jet shoots from a new corridor in the compacted lime, redirecting the slow chemical tides, opening routes that will make tomorrow’s current different from today’s. I imagine becoming them. The plumber of the metropolis. Building nothing but passage, creating the bronchial architecture that lets the city exhale. I feel their patience, their ancient rhythm of excavation, the void rather than the stone. But the silt rises to meet me, pearl-grey and choking, and I find I need more than emptiness. I need the passage of water *through* me, not merely around me. I need to be a threshold, not a tunnel.

I am nearly lost. The dark has ceased to be a place and has become a decision.

Then I find the porthole.

It is in the flank of a leaning tower, second story, in a corridor so narrow the water itself feels crowded by layered presence. A *filter-worm* has lived here, and died, or perhaps retracted so deeply into the wall-flesh that only the calcified sleeve remains. I press my palm—if it is a palm—against the warm, nacreous rim. The stone breathes. The *Sedimenters*, translated into infrastructure, exhale their mineral approval. The calcium sweats. And I understand that I have been falling toward this aperture since I entered the snow.

I enter the sleeve.

My body answers the architecture. Not with carapace, not with scales or muscled length or the churning facelessness of the burrower, but with surrender. I retract from locomotion as a thought retracts into deeper mind. My base fuses with the carbonate. I become sessile. I become *middle*.

Then the fan.

It emerges from me—not a limb but a negative space, an inversion of the darkness scored with ciliated threads finer than the falling snow. I open it into the corridor. It is a delicate question asked of the water, a curtain, a gill, a wide-eyed retirement. The warm current passes through me, carrying the microbial bankers in their decillions, carrying the spent flakes of dead *Clickers* and shed isopod casings and the invisible gametes of ancient spawning tides. I strain it all. I hold what passes. I transform particulate into presence, grease into growth, into the minimal and continuous fact of myself.

I am the *filter-worm* now.

I own the middle spaces, the pneumatic gaps between the living and the stone. The city must continue to pass water for me to continue to be, and so I require its continuation with a body older than wanting. I am the most architectural of survivors because I cannot leave; the city must continue to pass water for me to continue to be. When a globe pulses through the avenue—pale, translucent, a living bellows announcing the transit of death—I snap back into my sleeve like a startled thought, a held breath. I wait until the displaced water stills, until the schedule of predation has moved on. Then I emerge again. Extend my fan again. Resume my negotiation with the broth.

Sound is 0.8 here. I hear my own pulse, and beyond that, the low tectonic groan of the tower settling deeper into its own weight, the hiss of ten thousand microscopic mouths filtering, the wet crunch of calcium giving way under patient time. Above me, the eels thread the windows in their infrared courtship. Below, the isopods graze in their rustling sheets. The *Clickers* rattle their acoustic maps. The burrowers open new warm jets in the lowest drifts. I filter them all—their shed cells, their microscopic deaths, their slow exhalations. I am the witness that eats what it sees, the curtain that breathes, the room without doors that admits everything and keeps nothing.

The snow falls forever from a surface I will never see, and I catch it, cilia by cilia, in the warm, black, breathing dark. I have no need of light. The city, having never known it, continues; and I, having become its threshold, continue with it—sessile, straining, perfectly held between the stone and the current that makes it live.
