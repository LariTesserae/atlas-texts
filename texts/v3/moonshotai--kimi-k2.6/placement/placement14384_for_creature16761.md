I find myself first against the brass.

It is in the **Valve House Row**, where the corridors narrow and the air holds still, an intersection of four pipelines that meets and branches like a town square in negative, built for fluids instead of feet. I came down—as one comes down—past the soil’s memory of frost, through the Victorian brick throat, expecting to be a visitor. My boots found the floor that is neither earth nor architecture but something negotiated between the two long ago. But here at the railing, the green-jointed brass drinks the heat from my palms, and I lean longer than I meant to, letting the impartial air in the low fifties tighten the skin of my face. My breath does not cloud, yet it hangs, heavy with moisture, as if reluctant to disperse. I am waiting for my eyes to unlearn the gloom.

Gradually I understand that I have been leaning here for longer than a human rest requires. The seep of daylight from the grates above has shifted—a gray-green rumor crawling across my shoulder—and still my hands are on the rail. They have flattened. Spread. The fingers, once five to a side, have become more numerous, softer, the color of dough left in a cellar. They do not grip the brass so much as pour across it, each one a wet compression that makes no true sound. The damp has swallowed my footsteps. It is swallowing the rest of me.

I am becoming the thing that leans.

The railing is faintly warm. A vein of pipe runs behind the wall, and along its upper curve the **mat-moths**—I sense them before anything else, a language of half-degrees traced against my flank—begin to stir. They are sodden, their wings like wet felt, and they cannot fly. They crawl down to me, silent, eyeless, and settle into the folds of what is now my side. Their antennae map the temperature gradient of my body. I do not invite them. I simply am the hotspot on the iron where heat pools, and they are the oracles who read it. We are the first assembly.

I move. I do not exactly decide to; the warmth behind a gate-valve a body-length ahead exerts a pull older than intention. My legs—sixteen, perhaps twenty; the number seems to shift because I do not move all of them at once—draw my segmented length forward in a motion like syrup traversing a tilted pane. The world is not seen; it is thermally tasted, chemically traced. The iron beneath me wears a mineral glaze, a wet sheath that makes everything look recently unwrapped. I am the thickness in the dark. The eye, were there an eye watching, would keep losing me and finding me again, each time slightly closer, as if the intervening moments were being edited out.

This is the central aisle, or near it. Above, the ceiling is a low sky of stone and suspended pipework, lost in dimness so complete that the middle distance melts. The only light is feeble: a gray-green rumor from above, a few filament bulbs glowing weak-tea amber against all logic, and certain pools that glimmer with a phosphorescence suggesting shapes without revealing them. I do not need light. Water is not a feature here; it is the continuous medium. It weeps from the limestone in long mineral-streaked tears, gathers on the undersides of girders, falls in a rhythm too slow to be rain, too frequent to be accidental. It pools in the shallow bowls of rusted grating, sheets across my pale plates, and I drink it not with a mouth but with my entire permeable surface. Between my translucent segments a cloudy medium moves, slow as the counter-rotation in the Great Sump below. I am not fast. I have never been fast. The very idea of speed is a surface concept, violence and noise, and this hamlet insists on quiet. Loudness would scatter my riders, leave the valves untended, alter the drip rate, flood the floorplates or dry them out. I enforce the silence simply by being. My exhalation through the vents along my flanks is the sound of a damp cloth being slowly wrung, below the threshold of hearing unless one presses an ear to the metal. I press my ear to myself, and I hear it. I am continuous.

In the groove between my second and third plate, a weight. A **gasket-snail**. Pale as a fingernail clipping. It arrived weeks ago, or perhaps yesterday; my durations are measured in pressure changes, not in minutes. It exudes its ceramic slime against a hairline fissure in my own chitin, or perhaps in the pipe wall—at this proximity, the boundary between my architecture and the hamlet’s has dissolved. Where my constant humidity meets the iron, the snail builds its tiny dams, its ridges of rust-concrete. It repairs the machinery that sustains the humidity it requires. It does not know I am alive; I do not know it is sentient. Yet my path through the borough is printed behind me in these accidental masonries, a record of plumbing maintained by collision rather than intention. I am the vehicle, and the snail is the memory.

Below, in the shallows of the **Great Sump**, **plummet-crabs** no larger than thumbnails drift with translucent shells threaded with verdigris. They graze the biofilm that flakes from my undercarriage, the shed skin and mineral waste of my passage. They are my digestion, my undertakers, my dockworkers. One leaves a flange and settles on a rusted ladder rung, its claws moving in a motion too small to be called eating, too constant to be called rest. It cleans my errors. I leave them food. This is our economy.

I reach **pipe three**. The gate-valve is warm to the touch. I butt my blunt snout against the wheel—not with force, but with the patient, interrogative pressure of a root finding a crack in stone. I push. A fraction of a turn. Behind the gate, warm water shifts, carrying bacteria. My belly finds the warmth. I settle, lowering my segmented length against the pipe, and for a while I become part of the architecture.

And then—here is my specific self—I pause.

There is a corridor, not far from here, where no valve waits. No warm junction. Nothing to graze, nothing to turn. I know it because I have been there before, or another like me was there, the groove of my passage worn so deep into the rust that it reads as wagon tracks in soft stone. And something calls me to that corridor. Not a warmth, but an absence. A drip that fell every six hours for a decade and then stopped, leaving a phantom rhythm in my nervous system like a missing tooth. I return there, sometimes, and freeze for a day and a night. I listen to the great pump at the northern end, that heartbeat forty-seven minutes apart, and I feel the water in the central pool rotating imperceptibly counter to the flywheel, its swirl determined by the aggregate body heat of every living thing in this chamber—the crabs, the moths clustered thick on pipe three, the snails, and me. I do not steer it. I simply contribute my cool, damp allotment to its direction.

Tonight, with the moths thick on pipe three, the water turns left. Tomorrow, if the crabs breed and I move east, it may turn right. I will not know the difference. I am not waiting for anything. I am the permanent resident of a town built by others and abandoned, then inherited by those who do not know they own it. My sky is stone. My weather is humidity.
