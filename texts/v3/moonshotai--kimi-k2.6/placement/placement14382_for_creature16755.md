At first I think I have come as the visitor. I feel the ridge trail narrow to its suggestion, feel the wind shouldering me, and I expect, at any moment, to step out onto the whaleback and remain myself: the one who watches, who shivers, who leaves.

But the palm opens, and the humidity takes hold of my clothes, and the orchestral noise of water and wind fills my chest so completely that I forget the shape of my own voice. I slow. I thin. The sense of being *apart* begins to dissolve, like a moth-wing settling on wet stone.

I could be the white spider. The idea tempts me: to patrol the moss without spinning, to cross the warm stone of the figure’s thigh with that perfect, trustful slowness. But I am not so solitary, nor so legible against the black gneiss. I let the spider pass.

I consider the bell-basin. Who would not want to be the bronze *gong* that marks the water’s fall, the hollow that turns impact into voice? But I am not hollow. I am not a vessel waiting to be struck. I am something more stubborn, more lateral.

For a long while I think I might be the ghost-moth, lifted and laid down again by the figure’s mineral exhalations. I like the drunk spiral, the tongue touching still water. But the moths are too brief. They forget the ice. They do not remember the weight that was here before the warmth.

Then the figure moves. Not far—merely the adjusting of a wrist, a turning of the sternum toward the deepest crack as the basso profundo moans through. And I see it: on the broad plane of its back, just beneath the shoulder blade, a band of rose. It is distinct from the grey, warmer than the soapstone surrounding it. It does not stay still. It is migrating, swimming through the dense material of the body at the pace of ore through bedrock, heading toward the hollow above the hip.

I know then. I am that band.

I am the rose in the grey. The cured-meat stripe that the mountain salted into its child after the form was set. I am not the skin, nor the bone beneath it, but the mineral story the water tells as it passes through. Where the thermal breath rises and the silver film runs, I gather my pigment: iron, perhaps, or the ghost of copper leached from the accreted axe-head. I surface. I sink. I travel.

My consciousness is diffuse at first, spread across the whole flank, but it condenses as I move. A century is a long inhalation. When the figure rises to fit its hand to the axe-head socket, the warmth of the vent passes through its palm and into the tool, and I surge—there is no other word—I surge upward across the shoulder, brightening to a flush where the pressure is greatest. For those hours, I am a visible circuit. I complete the path between the mountain’s memory and the mountain’s hand.

When the figure lowers the axe and curls into the grass minaret, I ebb. I slide down the spine’s gentle trough and pool in the small of the back, darkening to a deeper mauve. The figure does not feel me as pain. I am its internal weather. Its tide.

I move toward the hollow at the collarbone where the water gathers. It will take me a thousand years to arrive, but I am already on my way. The ghost-moths do not see me, yet they drink from the mirror-water that carries my suspended minerals, and in this way I enter the communion: figure, moth, water, rose.

I do not speak. None of us speak over the wind-music. But when the high hysterical whistle catches the crest-fissure just right, I shimmer beneath the figure’s skin—a brief, subcutaneous gleam, the mountain blinking its eye—and the figure knows, in the wordless way stone knows pressure, that it is not finished. The mountain is still adding its final touches. I am the touch. The patience made visible. The blood-warm stripe that says: *this being is still being made*.

I have nowhere else to be. I am weathering in place, migrating in place, the slowest pulse in a room that measures time by the growth of moss.
