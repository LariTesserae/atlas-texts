The pressure finds me before shape does. It is a subsonic fist against a cavity that is not yet a chest, a reverberation in a medium I have not learned to call body. For a long while I am only a node in the roar, a place where the continent’s bass thrum pools and breaks. The light comes later—vicious, white, a scouring that reveals nothing because there is nothing to reveal. Only cold. Only dry ash. Only the endless pneumatic *chuff* of ten thousand woody pistons exhaling somewhere beyond the limit of my not-yet-perception.

Then: gravity.

It pulls me down out of the thermal I have been riding, or perhaps I have always been down here and the idea of *up* was only a dream of metallic spore-fall. The ground is not ground. It is fallout, layered and compressed, a geology of shed bark and vented slag and the compacted dust of centuries. It shifts under the first weight I recognize as mine—broad, dish-shaped, pistons or snowshoes or feet, spreading the load so I do not sink into the history I am meant to consume.

I am four-chambered in my locomotion, but not in the way of hearts. My legs are columns. My spine is a bridge. My belly hangs low, heavy with the weight of what I must process. And I am not alone. The herd is a pressure system around me, a migratory front of grey backs and tamping hooves that moves like weather over the interstitial wastes. We do not gather. We accumulate. We are the plateau’s slow exhalation made horizontal, the ruminant shadow of the refinery-towers.

The noise does not deafen me; it enters me. The cambium-cracks of distant trees, the shearing of crystalline pollen, the venting of seed-launchers—these are not sounds *to* me but the metronome against which my own chemistry ticks. I have multiple stomachs, dry as kilns, lined with strata of bacteria that have never tasted water. They are fermentation chambers, anoxic and patient, reducing the polished bark-plates I scrape from the ground into esters and hydrocarbon slurries. I do not eat for pleasure. I eat to arm myself. I eat to become volatile.

Between the trees there is no stillness, only the overlapping shockwaves of their industry. I lower my head—broad, blunt, ridged for grinding—and take a shed plate of silica-armored bark into my mouth. It is baked-grey, heavy as ceramic, and my molars are diamond-dusted from decades of this diet. I grind. The sound is lost in the greater roar, a microscopic abrasion inside the continental wear. The plate breaks down. Its cellulose is shackled to oxidized minerals, to copper-green residues, to the slow fallout of the tree’s own exhalation. I swallow, and the slurry drops into my first stomach, where it begins to simmer, to rebel, to become fuel.

Along my spine, waxy nodes ripen. They are my flash-pods, my chemical shout against the hunters. I do not fear them. Fear is a wet emotion, and I am dry as ash. But my chemistry recognizes their thermal signature—the pack-riders that spiral down the columns of heat rising from my own herd’s massed digestion, or from a tree’s venting crown. They are sleek, thermal-gliders, chitin and taut membrane, industrial whistles screaming in their throats. When they strike, the flash-pod over the struck flank erupts. It is not pain. It is ignition. Blue flame vomits across my grey hide, metallic ash blooming in a percussive bark that shatters the hunter’s trajectory. I do not look back. The herd does not stop. We are a weather front; we do not pause for a single struck drop of rain.

After enough years, or decades, or centuries—time is measured here in compression strata, not in seasons—I have become a vessel of the plateau’s patience. The trees stand, kilometers apart, ancient and violent, and I move between them. I am the connective tissue. I am the slow transit of matter from crown to ground, from vertical to horizontal, from forge to fallout. My hooves tamp the ash. My stomachs ferment the empire’s sheddings. My flash-pods burn its excess. I do not think. I reduce. I am the digestion that makes the refinery-trees possible, the recycler of their monumental stillness, the bellows that keeps the ash circulating until it is dense enough to root a new drill-shaft.

The cold seeps in. The white glare never dims. And I walk, four-legged and heavy, broad-footed on the scoured floor, listening to the tinnitus of the world until it becomes a silence so complete that I can hear my own dry fermentation: the faint, endless *pop* and *hiss* of chemistry routing around water, making fire where there should be blood.

I am the ash-grey ungulate. I am the empire’s grazer, its moving floor, its digestion made horizontal. And I do not rest. The grinding never stops. I am part of the single, distributed organism, cool and bright and mercilessly dry, patient and thrashing, forging nothing—but making everything ready to be forged.
