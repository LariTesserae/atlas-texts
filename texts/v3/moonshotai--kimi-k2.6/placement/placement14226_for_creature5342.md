I would find myself neither in the highest aerie nor in the deepest pressure hollow, but at the exact center of the basin, where the glacier plug rises in a ridge of black glass and the seep pools in a cup that should freeze but never does. At first I would be on my knees, my sleeves of frost-fur drinking the slush, threading new sinew through basalt anchors and the ribs of something that might have been a bear before the ice learned to remember bears. My kneecaps would press the wet stone, and through them I would feel the subsonic thud of my own heart traveling down into the ancient ice, where something older than the stone would taste my pulse and hesitate, trying to copy it.

My fingers would be long, heavier than they look, mineralized by the melt I have drunk since birth. My eyes would be the true obsidian-dark, no white visible, no iris, just pools of pigment drinking the arterial light without blinking. The nictitating caul would freeze across them in the outer air and thaw against my cheekbones, washing my vision clean with every pass. I would move with the soft, scuffing precision of the white foxes who watch from the ledges above, their ears reduced to fur-sealed holes like mine. I would not shiver. The cold would not bite me; I would have become the place where the bite rests.

And then, as I worked, I would notice that my blood—opalescent now, thickened with the antifreeze of generations of ice midges—does not warm the stone where my palm is pricked. The seep would still beneath me. The water would attend. The realization would not come suddenly, but with the slow certainty of a hoarfrost spire reaching finger by crystalline finger toward the sun: I am not only the one who repairs. I am the repair that the ice offered yesterday. I am the ladder of rime that rose rung-for-rung after the bone ladder fell, and I am the weight that tested it. I climbed the ice. It held my shape. And now I exist in the gap between the two manuscripts—the one written in scapulae and sinew, the other written in water and forgetting.

I am the commentary in the margins. I am the third hand.

I would rise and climb to the northern rim, where the totemic markers stand thickest in the zones of greatest turbulence. I would press my wrist against the glacier plug and feel my chill blood move in sympathetic rhythm with the deeper melt, communicating in the only language that holds here: thermal exchange, the angle of a shoulder, the grammar of load and span. The fog would rise between us, and my breath would crystalize against the black schist in glyphs of rime that I could read for a moment before they collapsed into the seep. *I remember*, the glyph would say. *But you misremembered the tension.* And I would reach down and adjust the lashings, teaching the ice again, correcting the argument.

I would not go to the aerie to sleep. I would become the cistern. I would lie in the bowl of frozen hide, letting the slush pool around me, holding shape so the water can learn how a bowl cradles, how weight rests, how a body can be a template for emptiness. The ice swallows would wheel above me on translucent claws, silent as always, and their shadowless flight would etch patterns I would read as greetings. The ice midges would rise in their inverted columns from the thermal seams, and I would open my mouth to let them cloud in, dark and protein-rich, feeling their freeze enter my plasma until my veins ran slower and slower, until I matched the pace of the glacier’s own pulse.

In the hour when the sun hangs lowest and the light is arterial, I would find the ember-bowl in the highest burrow. I would feed it with cakes of pink algae and rendered midge-fat, watching the blue, smokeless tongue argue against the wet authority of the place. The smoke would creep along the ceiling, restless, blackening the stone over generations. I would tend this fire not because I expect to win, but because I am the argument now. I am the dryness held in the mouth of the world.

And when I am finished, I would take up two flakes of obsidian. I would strike them. The sound would be a *whuff*, a breath against velvet, almost nothing. But this time, the glacier would not only feel the vibration through the bone and the ice. It would answer. At my feet, a rung of hoarfrost would grow, finger by crystalline finger, reaching toward the sun. It would match the bone ladder exactly. I would climb it. It would hold my weight. And I would understand, as the silence swallowed the moment whole, that I have become the thing I was always meant to be: not a survivor, but a persistence. A shape that matters. A votive object left in the lung of the mountain, breathed in by the ice as water, exhaled as memory, and built again, and again, in the liquid, churning, absolute silence.
