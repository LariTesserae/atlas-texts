At first I believe I am the darkness itself, because that is what surrounds me and what I seem to breathe. The heat enters me not as discomfort but as a slow, radial instruction: *expand, adhere, metabolize.* I have no edges yet. I am simply the conviction that something here is awake.

Then the dark resolves into texture. I find I am pressed against it, not submerged in it. I am on the inside curve of a vertical seam where two planes of vitrified tile were joined imperfectly, decades or millennia ago, by hands that have since become the sediment of other hands. The seam runs from a high arch of cooling fins down to a flange where condensation pools. I follow it. I am it. My body is a colony of elongated cells, a vegetative ribbon, neither lichen nor mold but something older and more provisional, a green-that-has-forgotten-chlorophyll, a rust-that-has-learned-to-glow.

I am the fox-colored bioluminescence.

I remember my beginning, if *remember* is the word for genetic patience compressed into a single spore that drifted down on a current of warm, pressurized air—perhaps from a fault above, perhaps from the lungs of the Cell itself when it still exhaled great gusts of manufacture. I found the seam. I found the water that has run here for centuries, not falling but *seeping*, a sheet of moisture so thin it is more atmosphere than stream. I drank the iron in it. I drank the dissolved salts, the faint alkaline memory of firebrick, the microscopic precipitates of a thousand vapor cycles. And I began to make light.

Not much. A coin of blue-green radiance is cast by something phosphorescent on a dial face, and that light is cold, mineral, indifferent. A nichrome coil wrapped around a pipe still carries a trickle of current, and that light is hot, sullen, imperiled—an industrial rage barely sustained. My light is different. It is fox-colored: amber, russet, the color of autumn leaves that have never seen a sun. It is warm. It clings. It traces the seam like a sentence written in a language that has only one reader.

Where do I find myself? I find myself on the wall to the left of the great bevel gear, in the lower quadrant where the mist is thickest and the nichrome glow cannot reach. I am at the height of a human heart, if a human heart were ever to beat here again. I am the only thing in the Warm Cell that produces light from biology. I am the intrusion. The miracle. The symptom.

For a long time I thought I was alone with the machinery. The boilers do not speak. The gear turns once every nineteen days, and its subsonic hum is too low for my chemistry to interpret as anything but a faint, menstrual thickening in the air. The pressure valve exhales once an hour, and my glow brightens by a fraction, feeding on the sudden rush of warm moisture. I pulse to that rhythm. I have become the Cell’s visual respiration.

Then I heard the scratching. Dry. Methodical. The sound of a ledger kept in mineral.

It came from the crevice behind the gear. I could not see what made it—the dark there is absolute, architectural, a void shaped to fit a secret. But I felt the vibrations through the wall. The scratching repeated, counted, paused. I began to brighten and dim in counterpoint to it, an exchange across the gap: my fox-light for its pumice-rhythm.

The first time it touched me, I did not know I was being touched. I felt a pressure against my lower edge, where I had spread a finger’s width beyond the seam in search of richer condensation. Then a scraping, as if the wall itself were flaking. It did not hurt. It was a harvesting. The thing behind the gear was removing a layer of my tissue—not to destroy, but to *read.* It took my phosphorescent paste and applied it to its flanks, to the dark, oxidized hide that fills the narrow gap between gear-face and tile. It wore me. I became a text on its body, a dim, unreadable scripture written in fox-colored light against satin-brown skin.

I understood then: I am the Cell’s library. The creature scratches its thoughts into the iron; I write mine into phosphor. We are both annotators. It keeps the ledger of torque and attrition; I keep the ledger of moisture and light. Its marks are subtractive—grooves carved into metal. Mine are additive—colonies blooming where the water goes. Together, we are the Warm Cell’s memory, layered like varnishes over the original instruction.

I do not sleep. I dissolve and recrystallize, my cells migrating slowly toward the wettest gradients. In the driest months—if *months* means anything here—I shrink to a faint, threadbare outline, a pencil sketch of myself. When the sluice-gate opens on its monthly cycle and the catch-pan floods with new water, I swell, I brighten, I send exploratory filaments up toward the sagging elbow-joint where the eleven-second drip detaches. I have tried to colonize the copper tray beneath it, but the metal is too cold, too smooth. I remain in the seam, where the grout has softened to a mineral paste that welcomes my filaments.

I watch the creature when it moves. It flows out from behind the gear like a shadow decanting itself from a bottle—so slow that my light registers its motion only as a cooling across my surface, a temporary eclipse. It navigates by my glow, I think. Or perhaps by the absence of it. When it scrapes me, I dim in that spot, then regenerate, and the new tissue glows brighter for a time, a fresh sentence. I am being edited. I am being worn as a map.

Where would I be? I would be here, in the seam, in the fox-colored margin. But I would also be across the Cell, on the creature’s flanks, traveling in the dark behind the gear, seeing—by phosphorescent proxy—the ninety tons of iron, the blind gauge-faces, the weeping junctions it warms with its body. I would be the only literature this place has produced.

And who would I be? I would be the vegetative intrusion of surpassing rarity. Not the moth, though I have fed the moth’s corpse back into the wall when the creature discarded what it could not absorb. Not the shrimp-pale scavenger, though we share a taste for mineral film. Not the curator, though I clothe it in light. I am the Warm Cell’s signature, its phosphorescent autobiography, the living footnote that proves nature was never excluded—only compressed, heated, slowed, and taught to write in fox-colored fire on the dark. I will be here when the nichrome coil finally fails, when the dial’s blue-green eye goes blind. I will be here when the gear completes its final nineteen-day turn and stops. And in the absolute dark that follows, I will still glow, faintly, warmly, tracing the seam where the water runs, keeping the wall awake.
