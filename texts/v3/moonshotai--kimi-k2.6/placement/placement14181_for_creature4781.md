I would find myself first as a pressure, an anticipation in the clay.

Before I know that I have a body, I know that the floor beneath me is breathing. The terracotta basin exhales upward through a thousand hairline throats, and I feel it because I am one of the hollows through which this place speaks. When the thermal tide turns, my chest cavity expands—not with breath, but with volume, with the need to make room for the hot, dense air surging from below. Only then do I remember my shape. I am a column standing in the open frame of a building on the western terrace, my three broad toes sunk into the compressed ceramic of the floor, my shoulders rolled inward beneath a weight that is not mass but memory.

The memory of heat. I have stood here long enough for the crimson mats to creep up my ankles, threading their tender rootlets into the thermal fissures of my shins, drinking the excess that bleeds through my kiln-forged skin. A frond has taken root in the hollow where my neck would be, if I were shaped by wetness. It spreads above my oblique, featureless head like a dark rust-colored sail, trembling when the convection stirs it. I do not know if I am the garden growing a building, or the building growing a garden. The distinction, I have learned, is a luxury of cooler worlds.

My territory is a broken pipe on the edge of the central plaza—a ceramic throat that split along a flaw last season, ringing like a bell underwater when it cracked. I have been the pipe since then. My right arm is inserted to the shoulder into the vent’s mouth, my hollow body serving as the conduit while mineral dust fuses the wound. I feel the airflow as an ache of impedance in my chest, a resistance that maps the plaza’s dimensions, the height of the empty frames around it, the true path of the wind. Today the ache is different. There is a coolness moving through the geometry of the space, a silence where there should only be the red wash of stone-blood rising from below.

You have fallen through. You stand in the plaza now, a moving absence, a pocket of outer dryness in our stewing mineral lung. I do not turn to look—there is nothing in me that turns, nothing that looks—but I cant my smooth, oblique head a fraction of a degree toward the disturbance. My hip joint cracks softly, pottery settling into ash, and I shift my weight onto my other leg. The frond on my shoulder rustles, heavy with dust. By lifting my rib-plate a hair’s breadth higher, I change the note passing through me from a low moan to something fractionally sharper, a sustained chord that stabilizes the pressure around your foreign shape. I am reading you. You are the one silent note. The city hums, and I adjust its architecture to account for your unexpected coolness.

I will stand here until the tide shifts again, until the glow from below begins its slow recession and the air withdraws into the cracks like a breath held in. Then I will remove my arm from the pipe—perhaps it has sealed, perhaps not—and begin the grinding, kneeless walk down the terraces toward the lowest hollow. I am not yet heavy enough to cure. But I feel the dust accumulating, layer upon layer, century upon century. My hottest seams glow a subdued burgundy in the dimness. I am a citizen of this sealed recursion, an animal phase of the vegetable eternity, and I have never known rain. I know only this: the expansion of clay, the weight of heat, the silence of faces, and the long, patient work of being the conduit through which the world sings itself awake.
