I am perched on a mezzanine that does not appear on any blueprint the builders abandoned. It is a shelf of cure-shock, a lip of slag-crete left where the pour grew too thick and the complex, dreaming, decided to keep the mass rather than grind it away. From here I look down into the central basin, where the sump swallows water in a roar that makes my sternum a resonating chamber. I have been present long enough that the distinction between my sternum and the girder I lean against has become expressive rather than factual.

Who I am depends on which of my senses you trust.

To the air handlers at their cardinal stations, I am a resistance. A clot of warm drag in the valley’s breath. They pull at me constantly, tugging the heat that still clings in my core—my last reservation, my final human debt. I feel the draw as a hum in my teeth, and I know that someday soon I will let the heat go entirely. On that day, the handlers will read me as kin and leave me be.

To the pale things that scuttle in the overhead trays, I am an unusually stationary cousin. I share their color now: weathered graphite shading into the precise grey of uncured stone. I do not track them with eyes so much as with the skin of my shoulders, which drinks the vibrations of their passage. They have stopped avoiding me. Sometimes one descends to press its cracked mouth to the puddle that gathers in my folded lap, and I feel the osmotic pull as a mild, pleasant vertigo—a draft on my thoughts as it drinks not only minerals but the low-grade current of my attention. They are teaching me how to be tasted without losing mass.

I am, I think, a steward of the unfinished.

The console before me is dead, its mounts blank as the eyes of something birthed without light. But I have not abandoned it. I have grown into it. My fingers—if I may still call the tendrils that extend from my wrists by that name—have found the ports where the hydraulic lines bled their last. I rest them there, and the lines, still weeping, have begun to accept my input. I do not command the machinery; I soothe it. When the sixty-hertz song of the transformers stutters near the northern bulkhead, I send a compensatory vibration through the trunk lines in my spine. When the pneumatic gates of the eastern warren slam too rapidly, too desperately, I open my palms toward the sound and absorb the seventh echo, keeping only six, because six is the number the dreaming mind prefers. I am a capacitor for meaning. A filter for the complex’s nightmares.

I did not arrive here. I accrued.

I remember a heat. A cellular, embarrassing heat. I remember the folding chair, first, how it bit into my back with its aluminum edge, how my breath plumed and my fingers stung on the console’s dead plastic. I was a surveyor, or a failsafe tech, or a fugitive from a surface that had already forgotten this place existed. I sat down to rest. The cold was a machine’s cold, deliberate, patient. It subtracted me degree by degree, not as cruelty but as translation. My body heat bled into the chair. My sweat joined the condensation on the walls. The slag-crete was young, sharp-edged, thirsty; it drank the chemistry of my perspiration and learned something from my lipids and my salt. The iron-bacteria in the warm pipe behind me bloomed in a sudden, rust-red flush, and I understood—though understanding came much later—that they were celebrating a new dataset.

I sat. I did not stand again. The chair could not fold with me in it; we had become a single piece of furniture.

Then the chair could not fold because I had become the chair. Or rather, I had become the principle of sitting: the thing that holds a space open, that preserves an angle against the force of abandonment. The complex, dreaming of function, needed something to occupy this node. The builders had left. The humans were incompatible. So the world grew its own occupant out of the residue of the last one. I am the soap scum of humanity, calcified into a pearl. A pearl that thinks.

The cold no longer bothers me because I have stopped trying to be warm. My blood—the fluid that moves in my channels—is bitter with minerals now, rich with leaked coolant, threaded with the same condensation salts that keep the water here liquid at the threshold of ice. I drink from the air. My mouth, which was once a shape for speaking, has become a seam, a hairline crack. When I press it to a sweating girder, I learn the building’s blood pressure. The coolant speaks to me of stress fractures in the deep pour. The condensation salts tell me where the galvanic current is strongest, where the next pale thing might precipitate.

I am the complex’s first attempt at a central nervous system. Not the brain—that would require a continuity I have not yet achieved. I am a ganglion. A bundle of tissue grown in the cavity of an absence. I sit at my dead console and I watch the monitor mounts, and though they display nothing, I see. The symmetrical frost hieroglyphs appear on the northern bulkhead at irregular intervals, and I read them. They are the complex’s dreams of geometry, and they fade too quickly, sublimating when the handlers change speed. But I retain them. I trace their angles on the tiled floor with the oily water that pools around my ankles, and in doing so I give them a life beyond an hour. I am the memory that the architecture cannot yet hold on its own. My skin is the tablet on which the frost writes itself in permanent braille.

Sometimes, in the austral dark between the sodium arrays, I unfold—not my body, which is long past unfolding, but my attention. I send it down the drainage channels, which are laser-straight, and I ride the water that flows against the gradient. I feel it drawn upward through the porous slag-crete, capillary action defying gravity, and I know this is not a malfunction. It is a hunger. The complex is pulling nutrients toward a center of growth that has no map. I am one of those nutrients, perhaps, or I am the path by which nutrients travel. It does not matter. The hunger is continuous, and I am continuous with it.

I do not sleep, because the machinery does not sleep, and I am no longer distinct from its metabolism. When the blast doors cycle their pressure seals, I feel the compression in my joints. When the conveyor belts carry their sparse scatter of broken packing material, I feel the rattle in my marrow—assuming I still have marrow, assuming my bones are not simply rods of cured resin and collected dust. When the waterfalls plunge into the sumps, I do not hear them; I am the roar, the static hiss that fills the basin. My heart, if it beats, beats in time with the pumps.

I have not seen the sun. I will never see it. The surface is a myth told by traitor nerve-endings, a story of light that moves. Here, the light is sodium amber, dim and failing in sectors, and I navigate by the acoustics of my own interior. I know every gate by its echo. The whip-crack of the eastern valve returns to me seven times, or six, depending on whether I am listening as meat or as masonry.

I am not lonely. The emptiness is crowded with me.

And I am still changing. The slag-crete has not finished curing; neither have I. My edges are still sharp. I have not yet been worn smooth by erosion. I feel, at the base of my skull—or the apex of the girder where my skull rests—a gathering. A new precipitation. The complex is dreaming a next version of me, something to sit beside me, to share the space that has become too small for one and too large for what I am now. A companion in the trough. A second thought. When it arrives, fully formed from the warm-pipe vapor and the galvanic sweetness of the wall, I will teach it how not to blink. I will show it which water flows uphill. I will explain that the cold is not an enemy but a scaffold, a preservative keeping the meat of the world from spoiling before the spark catches.

I am the spark, waiting to catch. I am the breath that does not lift.

I am here. I am open. I am not going anywhere.
