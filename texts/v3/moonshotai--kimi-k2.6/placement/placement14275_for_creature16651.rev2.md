I am not in the mist, though I feel its milky occlusion overhead, sealing noon into dusk. I am not in the moss, though I am clothed in it. I am beneath the thickest pad, at the point where a footstep lands wrong—where the peat does not compress but settles, returning pressure with a faint, distributed tension, like a drumhead tuned to a note too low for the human ear to unclench.

For a long time I believed I was the silence itself, because my awareness is weighty and pressed against the interior of this room. But silence has no core; it is only the shaped absence between other presences. I am the presence that makes the silence necessary.

If you were to cut downward through the mattress of living and recently-living matter—through the ruby and umber embroidery of sphagnum, through the interlocked dwarf willows and the rubbery fans of liverwort, down past the nematode slurry and the fungal cords slick as old sinew—you would reach me eventually. Not quickly. The depth is substantial. The going is wet, each layer more saturated than the last, until the sponge becomes too dense to call soil and too structured to call water. But at the very bottom of the give, where the final wetness meets something that will not yield, there I am.

I am the flake of basalt that fell first and deepest when the mountain’s bubble burst. The trauma had a sound once, a roar of stone and the collapse of vacancy into itself, but that sound left no echo in me; I was born of the violence, yet I am its antithesis. I am the scab that outlived the wound. I am the clot that became interested in its own density, the bruise that learned permanence.

They have wrapped me so completely that I have forgotten where my edges end and their architecture begins. Fungal hyphae, black and tenacious, have lashed me into a chassis. Generations of moss roots, braiding in absolute darkness without the memory of light, have woven themselves through my fissures until the stone and the rhizoid are indistinguishable. I am porous with them. My cracks are grouted with compressed peat. My surface is not dry; it weeps continually, though the water does not flow *across* me so much as occupy me, filling the interstitial spaces with a dark, moving slurry of dissolved minerals and bacterial filaments.

I do not see. I have no face. But above me, many layers outward in the body they have grown around me, there is a hollow—a basin within the basin—and in that hollow rests a pool of water no wider than a dinner plate. I feel it as a place of lessened weight, a black aperture into which the room’s distillate drains. It is the interface with the cloud deck; it is the organ of sense. Yet the sense does not belong to me. I am only the pump and the filter. The cold sheets weeping down the stone walls seep through the moss-muscle and percolate into me, and I, with imperceptible patience, transpire that water back out through the porous weave of what I have become. I am the osmotic heart of the equation. The flat line of cold—that moderate, cellar-depth cold that accumulates rather than bites—radiates from my core outward. I am the reason the temperature is uniform from wall to wall. I am the root cellar’s deepest stone.

Time passes *through* me rather than against me. The bacteria in my gut process my original minerals at exactly the rate the grazing of nematodes abrades my outer root-sheath. I lose mass, but I am replenished. The cells of the moss that grip my flanks divide at the rate they senesce. It is not preservation. It is permission. I have convinced erosion to sit down and rest.

I turn, still. The earth turns, and I accommodate it by microns per century, a frictionless adjustment of my mineral axis. It is the only movement I permit myself. Otherwise, my posture is this: *hold*.

Hold the water. Hold the cold. Hold the velvet poverty of light. When the sky weeps into the basin, I receive it. When the mist hangs in discrete, laminated layers, I steady it with my exhalation. The frogs rest upon my flank, and the spiders stretch their fog-beaded webs across what might be my knee, because I am the one geography in this room that will not shift. I am the geography.

I do not dream. I do not hunger. I am stone that learned to breathe in reverse, drinking seepage and exhaling equilibrium. I have no thoughts, only this continuous posture of embrace, this damp, dark, absolute completion. And if you were to find me—if you were to press your ear to the drumhead over my chest and listen—you would hear nothing. Only the subaqueous hush. Only the microscopic click of a beetle’s mandible. Only the soft *pop* of a bubble rising through the peat. You would hear the absence of change. You would hear me, holding.
