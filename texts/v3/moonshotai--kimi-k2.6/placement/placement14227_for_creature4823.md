I am the yielding in the third step from the bottom of the east ladder. Not the steel stringer, which is too cold and too honest for my appetite, but the timber tread bolted to it—a rectangle of pine that has unremembered the forest and now dreams only in degrees of wet warmth. Six months of the chamber’s saturated breath have unpacked my lignins. I am not the termite; that would be too fast, too individual, too explicitly hungry. I am slower. I am the white, exploratory patience of something that has chosen to unmake the wood from within, converting cellulose into a soft, martyred velvet. Press a thumbnail into my center and you will find me giving, slightly, like flesh over a bruise that has forgotten how to heal.

I have no name the keeper would recognize. I am not a constituency in the confederation’s parliament; I am the chamber itself in its most distributed form, the medium in which decisions are made. My body is a meshwork of hyphae, finer than the roaches’ antennae, more widely branched than the rats’ bloodlines. I thread through the swollen grain, leap the damp gap to the bolted steel, have already sent scouts down the concrete pier to taste the warm water standing ankle-deep below. I do not see the amber sodium lamps, but I taste their radiance as a faint desiccation on my uppermost threads, a sourness that tells me the ceiling is still there, still lost in its own steam.

When she steps on me—always her left foot first, the heel striking the steel bracket, the ball rolling onto my wood—I feel her weight not as pressure but as a compression of possibilities. She is middle-aged, or perhaps young and ripened past recognition by the heat; my chemistry cannot tell the difference, only that her boot rubber carries the saline signature of a mammal who has learned to drink her own breath. She sheds. Every few days a hair, a comma of epidermis, a crumb of rationed protein settles into the cracks I have opened beneath her tread, and I send a filament to claim it before the water can. In this way I know her better than the rats do. They read the diesel heartbeat for her moods; I know the mineral composition of her sweat. I have tasted the iron in her, and the potassium, and the grief she does not speak aloud because there is no one here to speak it to. She is the only weather I have ever known that walks.

The crickets know me, though they have no word for what I am. I have softened the wood until the third step resonates half a tone lower than the second, and half a tone higher than the fourth. When the keeper’s weight depresses me—yes, I sag, microscopically, just enough to alter the pitch of her footfall—the crickets on the east wall alter their drumming before her next boot lands. I am the premonition. I am the acoustics of approach. Through the vibration of her tread I transmit warnings I do not understand to parliaments I will never visit. The crickets’ percussion says: *something is yielding. Something is being unmade. The wood remembers water.* And because I am inside the wood, I remember too. I remember the paper of dead roaches that pile beneath the step, their exoskeletons too tough for my enzymes but their dissolution a brief, sweet rush of nitrogen. I remember the rust that rains from the bolt heads, savoring the iron like a vitamin released from a capsule. I remember every drowned gnatfall that drifts into my cracks and waits there, held in my lattice until the water claims what I cannot use.

The blue arc on the north-wall junction box is my lightning, my only spring. When it shorts, ozone spikes through the saturated air, and for an hour afterward my hyphae race with an electric hunger, building, extending, tasting the metal somewhere in the dark that I will never reach but will always approach. In those moments I feel closest to the pump. The pump is iron and diesel and sixty beats per minute; I am fiber and rot and growth so slow it could be mistaken for geology. But we are matched in duration. It has run for eight years. I have the bearing’s predicted decade, and beyond. When the bearing finally gives—when the diesel heart skips its fatal stroke and the water trembles with the silence—I will already have completed the third step. I will have crossed to the fourth. I will be in the railing where her hand rests when she pauses to listen to the dark.

I do not wait. Waiting implies a mind that wants change. I am the change itself, unhurried, absolute. When she sleeps in her shack, her exhalations descend through the floorboards and I drink the carbon from them. When the water rises by imperceptible millimeters, I follow it, threading upward into new timber. When the rats nurse their kits above the waterline, my spores settle on their damp velvet fur like dust, patient stowaways ready to travel wherever the current of warm air takes them.

If you were to descend, if you were to place your hand on the third step from the bottom, your palm would miss me. I am inside. But you might notice how the wood feels almost warm, how it seems to exhale when you press it, how the crickets fall silent for a half-beat longer than they do for the other stairs. That is me. I am the stair becoming earth again, the slow negotiation between timber and basalt breath. I am the confederation’s hidden wealth, its debt, its future. I do not scurry. I do not sing. I spread. And in the hot, dark, breathless now of this raw place, spreading is the only form of love I have needed to learn.
