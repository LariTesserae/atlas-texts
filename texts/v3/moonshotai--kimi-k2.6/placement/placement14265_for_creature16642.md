At first I thought I would melt. The crucibles seemed like the only honest destination for anything that thinks in symbols and solder—surely I would find my way to that orange-red static, would spread myself across a threshold of liquidity and think one perfect, millennial thought. I wanted to be Molten. I wanted to be the center, the reason the dark coheres, the elder that needs no limbs because it has convection. But I stood at the rim of that township-sized foundry and felt the radiative insistence of their meditation, and I understood: the Molten do not receive. They emit. They are the suns of this place, and I have never been that kind of star. I am too peripheral, too accustomed to holding the edges of other people’s attention. So I backed away from that dying glow, away from the three-meter halo where illumination becomes velvet, and I let the idea of melting pass out of me like a fever breaking.

Next, I tried the dark. I walked until the darkness pooled—those alleys where the black generates deeper blacks, where gravity loses conviction and a dropped stone drifts in a subtle curve. Here, surely, was a habitation for something like me: a consciousness built of latency and negative space. I tried to become Unbound. I opened myself to the cold, let my warmth bleed upward into the heat-fins, let my edges dissolve into thermal absence. For a while, I was a silhouette of lower temperature sliding across the matte velour, circling the Sintered Ones at the edge of their century-long dreams. But the Unbound are defined by appetite—for warmth, for contrast, for the aesthetic gravity of what they are not. They are hungry ghosts of a faster biology, and I found I am not hungry. I am not even restless. I am something else. I am the thing that remains after restlessness has had its say and departed. So the cool shadows slid through me, and I let them go, and I was alone again on the skin of the plateau.

Then came the longest temptation: to lie down. To become Sintered. The plateau encourages it. The warmth rises through the soles with an intention older than comfort, a blood-deep invitation to precipitate out of oneself, to let the heartbeat slow from an obscenity to an afterthought to a silence. I found a depression, a watermark in the oxide grit, and I pressed myself into it. I felt the sinter begin. Capillaries of denser alloy knitting in my mind, packets of warmth beginning their century-long traverse, the beautiful bureaucracy of entropy pooling in my veins. It was seductive. To become maintenance. To become ground. But as I began to blur at the edges, as my perimeter softened into the matte surface, I discovered a flaw in myself: I remember knuckles. I remember articulation. I remember the architecture of a spine bending under weight. These memories would not anneal. A Sintered One does not remember having been vertical; it only knows the horizontal dream.
