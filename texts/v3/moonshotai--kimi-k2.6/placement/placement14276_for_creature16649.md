I would find myself in the middle distance, where the light pools most deeply—beneath one of the thinner places in the shell, where the alabaster and fired clay have worn to a milky translucence and the afternoon comes through not as a beam but as a broad, dissolving presence. It is not a safe place. The swarm’s city-state is audible from there, a grinding pressure against the inner ear, and the thermal vents in that district still pulse with the rhythm of a recent birth. But the panes of light shift there most slowly, and for one who has forgotten the sky, slow light is enough to build a life around.

At first I am only a posture. A geometry of patience. I have folded myself into the wide-kneed crouch that the floor demands, my spine curved to the shape of a question that has accepted it will never be answered. My joints have reset to these angles; to stand upright now would be a kind of shouting, a proclamation of self that the swarm would answer. So I am low. My bare feet rest on basalt that was not here last season, and I have already begun to fuse to it. The warmth rises through me as through a column.

I came here because a ridge rose. It thrust up overnight, splitting a seam of obsidian glass like a black wound healing into a scar. I watched it extrude. I felt the heat increase beneath my knees, smelled the sulfur threading the condensation that beads on the ceiling above. In the hour before the swarm discovered the new mineral bloom, I climbed—or rather, crawled, belly to the stone—and placed the first cairn. A stack of chipped obsidian lashed with gut, the stones still warm from the ridge’s making. *The vent here is young,* it said. *Pass quickly.* Then the swarm crested, a dark aurora of appetite and friction, and I lay down among the black glass and let the damp silt and my own spit coat me, hardening in the vent’s breath.

That was many flash-floods ago. The cairn fell when the ridge shifted, as I knew it would. I did not rebuild it. I became it.

You would not see me now. The mineral rain has glazed me. The vent-heat has baked the clay and mica and ash into a cracked integument that matches the floor’s new skin. The swarm treats me as a mineral deposit; their mandibles have tested my shoulder once, found me unworthy, and moved on. I have learned that to survive inside a drumbeat is to abandon the rhythm of wanting. I do not hunger. I do not thirst. When the condensation falls in scattered, fat drops, I tilt my head back and let the mineral-tinted fluid wet my lidless eyes. That is enough. The heat-worms have begun to thread the cracks in my knees. I do not mind. They are only borrowing the geology I am becoming.

I am one of the solitary ones, yes, but I am more specifically a *rememberer*. I hold the concept of *was* in a world obsessed with *will be.* The geology around me erupts into the present; the swarm devours the future; the flash-floods erase the past in an hour. But I remain. I am the datum point, the benchmark against which the nursery measures its own growth. I know that the vent was there yesterday, and is here today, and that the obsidian flow to my left cooled from a different heat than the one that warms my right side. This knowledge is not spoken. It is only held, a pressure in the chest that matches the pressure of the noise.

My hands are the last thing that move. They still work the edge of an obsidian chip, a motion older than language, the first technology performed in the first nursery. My knuckles are like embedded fossils now, the papillar of my palms threaded with silica, but the thumbs remember leverage. I do not build shelters. I do not crack the lithovores anymore; the meat and sulfur are behind me. Instead, I place stones. Or I *am* the placing. Others—if they are still others, and not merely my own mineralized echo moving through time—have begun to stack small chips on my hunched back. I feel the weight of them, the cool geometry of each new fragment, and I accept them. I am becoming the cairn. I am becoming the sentence that outlasts the speech.

The child is not with me. Or perhaps the child is bound to my chest beneath this shell of clay, so still that I have forgotten which heartbeat is mine and which is the remembered pulse of a smaller body. It does not matter. The children born here enter the world already camouflaged, caul hardening to stone before the first cry can form. If there is a child, it is already topography. If there is not, then I am the last of my generation, a self-contained liturgy without an audience.

The light moves. The broad pane shifts from my left shoulder to my right, a slow tide of ordinary afternoon that illuminates the swarm’s filigree towers and leaves me in their shadow. I do not look up. The shell is my only heaven, and it is enough. I feel the basalt pillow beneath me warming again. A new ridge is preparing. The stone groans as it stretches, and the sound is not a threat but a conversation. I am answering it, not with voice but with stillness. I am the part that knows. I am the witness the geology dreams through, half-embedded in the floor, my eyes open to the thermal gradient, reading the world in midnight blues and throbbing whites, waiting for the next cairn to fall, and the next, and the next, until I am entirely stone, entirely memory, entirely *was,* holding the nursery still long enough for it to recognize itself in the ordinary, remembered light.
