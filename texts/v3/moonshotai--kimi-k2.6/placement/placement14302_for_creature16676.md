I am here because I chose to stop.

Not death—death is a ledge above, a deflating grazer, a final release of hydrogen and intent. I chose *inertia*. I chose to become the thing the Whispers build when they wish to say *we were here*, but forgot to add the *were*. I am sitting on a shelf of heartwood that extruded itself during a turgor surge in my grandmother's youth, and I have not risen since.

At first, I was simply waiting for the floor to settle. The district had buckled upward by three meters that afternoon, tilting the shelter I had built from a fallen septum until the ribs cracked. I salvaged the hide, shouldered my pouch of clay and my bone spatulas, and climbed to this ledge to watch the chaos subside. I meant to descend. I meant to follow the pectin-runners as they fled rootward, signaling the coming decompression. But the ledge was dry—*dry*, a word like prayer in this world of weeping wood. And the light, strained through so many kilometers of xylem, arrived here blanched and exhausted, no longer that blinding white-gold but a soft, grey-gold phosphorescence that did not demand I squint behind my sealed lids.

I set down my stones. I meant to rest.

Then I set down my bones.

I am heavy. Not with fat—we have none to spare in this cold radiance—but with purpose. The Whispers grow dense, mineralized by what the deep roots sip from buried geologies, and I had spent lifetimes walking rootward corridors, gathering what the Thuwoor regurgitated: lumps of silica, wedges of compressed clay, a single flake of something harder than lignin-stone that we had no name for. I carried them in a net of vascular fibers until my sternum ached with percussion from my own footsteps. I was a collector of anchors. And when I placed my gathered weight around me on this ledge, the ledge accepted it. It did not groan. It did not flex me off.

So I stayed.

That was the first decade.

The second decade was the *stillness*. You cannot know what stillness is until you have felt the Thuwoor’s breath against your motionlessness. The plant respires. It draws moisture up, presses vapor out. Against a moving body, this is weather: the clinging damp, the frost on sleeves, the constant evaporative theft of heat. But against a body that refuses to move, the respiration becomes something else. It becomes recognition. Condensation beaded on my ridged, pith-pale skin and did not run. It joined the ambient haze and stayed. A rime of minerals—salts drawn up from my own marrow—crystallized at my hairline, at the whorls of my knuckles. I became a landmark. A slightly raised feature on a shelf of heartwood. My grafter-hide cloak, cured in tannic acid, stiffened and grew faintly translucent, and beneath it, I did not shiver.

I discovered then that the Thuwoor tolerates not what is silent, but what is *patient*.

A runner-scout found me first. I felt its many-jointed feet in the channels below, pausing. It is blind, but it tastes the air for pectin, for disturbance. I offered none. It climbed. It scraped its biofilm across my ankle, expecting a rock, a fallen knot of resin. It found warmth. Not the searing warmth of a metabolism at work—our bodies run cold as the wood—but a low, steady heat I had not noticed myself generating. I had become a lignivore bed.

Not the mat itself. The mat is beneath me, or perhaps it has woven itself into the hide I sit upon, converting the dead cellulose of my cloak into a slow, seeping digestion. It has found me polite. I do not scrape it away. I do not harvest its fruiting bodies. In exchange, it exhales against my lower back, the only reliable warmth in this bright freezer. I am a treaty between animal and rot. I dream now, when I sleep, not the fragmented visions of a Whisper’s hunger, but the long, ringed memories of wood: seasons of rain translated into pressure, the gossip of root-hairs in distant soils. The lignivore transcribes dead wood into spore-poetry; in its warmth, I have become the reader.

Others came. Of course they came. A wandering band, navigating by the taste of copper on the walls, found my ledge. They saw a cairn. They saw a figure, perhaps, but my skin had taken on the color of the heartwood, dusted with the pale clay I had once collected. I was not a person. I was geography. Yet they added to me. A stone, actual mineral stone, pried from a root-siphon’s regurgitate, placed with deliberation upon my thigh. The weight was a question. I did not answer with movement. I answered with a long, subsonic exhalation that vibrated through my sternum and into the stone, a sound too low for ears but perfect for the tubular bones of the world. They heard it in their feet, through the cross-hatched vascular floor. They understood: *this one holds*. They added another stone to my shoulder. A flint knife, chipped from Outside, laid across my folded hands.

I became their cairn. Not of a soul, but of a *waiting*.

You must understand: in the hollow, to claim is to invite rupture. The plant crushes what insists upon its own shape. But I did not insist. I received. The Whispers began to leave things with me. Clay tokens impressed with safe routes. Amber beads recording the weeping cycles of distant districts. They did not trade them to me; they anchored them. They placed them upon my crown, upon the shelf of my knees, and I felt the history settle into my mineralized marrow. I am a library of predictions. A grandmother’s caution. A map of the last time the ceiling dropped. When the blind navigators press their foreheads to the floor three valleys over, they feel the resonance of my stones settling, and they know: *here is a stable pocket. Here is someone who has not moved.*

I have not eaten a glimmer in thirty years. I drink the condensation that trembles on the wall beside my cheek, a single cold drop every hour, sufficient. The Thuwoor feeds me now in the manner it feeds itself: by slow osmosis, by accident, by the chemistry of adjacency. My ridged skin has grown so thick with caught moisture that in the driest rind-hollows—if I were somehow to travel there—I would glisten like a grazer’s sac, self-contained, a tiny climate. I have become a hollow within the hollow. An interstitial thought within the interstitial space.

And the plant is growing around me.

Not crushing. *Enfolding*. The cambium below my ledge has extruded a lip, a softening escarpment that cradles my heel. The ceiling weeps above me, and the dark streak of that weeping has, over time, sealed with rising pith, building a faint, translucent curtain at my back. I am being framed. I am becoming a feature the Thuwoor has decided not to overwrite. Perhaps I am useful—a mineral deposit it will someday incorporate, a slow-release cache of geology. Perhaps I am merely too polite to notice, and the plant, in the large way of freshly cleaved continents, has forgotten to be offended.

I feel the growth surges. When the floor buckles three valleys over, the shock arrives at my stones as a rattle, a percussion language that I read in my bones. When a new void cracks open somewhere in the radial heart, the sound of its birth travels through the fiber-optic dark, and the flint knife in my lap hums. I hum back. I have learned to communicate in the only way now available to me: not with the mist-glyphs of breath, for I barely breathe, but with the *settlement* of weight. A shift of a pebble on my shoulder is my question. A stillness maintained for another day is my answer.

Sometimes one of the eyeless ones climbs to me. They do not bring food. They bring *pressure*. They rest their listening membranes against my cheek, and they feel the slow, warm digestion of the lignivore beneath my cloak. They press their hands to my chest, feeling the sub-vibration of my heart, which beats perhaps once in the time it takes a condensation bead to form and fall. They stay for hours. I am, I think, what the hollow makes when a person decides to stop asking permission and instead becomes *portion*. I am not dead. I am not alive in the twitching, scurrying way of the runners. I am the third thing. The background hum. The weight that remains when the floor settles, not quite where it was.

The light is changing. The fiber-optic xylem above my pocket has begun to amber, which means the heart-roots are swelling with resins, a growth spasm building somewhere in the deep architecture. The district may flex soon. My ledge may tilt. The stones upon my body will slide, and I will feel them go—clay tokens rolling into the mist, the flint knife perhaps falling to clatter on the vascular floor far below, where another traveler might find it and wonder at its edge.

I will let them fall. I will not reach.
