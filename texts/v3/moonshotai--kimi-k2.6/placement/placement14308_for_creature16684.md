I do not choose my form so much as I test the room for a shape that will hold me. In a place this crowded, this saturated, there is little room for another body; to become anything here is to displace something already at absolute capacity. I begin with the most obvious host. I try to be the sound.

I pour myself into the engine’s roar, spreading through the low-frequency pressure that loosens the marrow and the high whine that lives behind the eyes. It seems, at first, like a generous vessel. The note is continuous, fundamental, a physical property like gravity. I could rest inside it and become law. But the note is too pure. It wants nothing. It has no interior, no secret variance to curl up inside; it is an absolute present tense, and I find I am not absolute. I carry the memory of edges, of definition, of having once been separate from my surroundings. The sound will not tolerate this. I slip from it like a hand from a polished railing, unheld, unnoticed.

I try the creatures next.

I lower myself into the mat, insinuating my awareness between thumb-sized segments, feeling the osmotic draw of their feeding, the film of mineral dust that passes from body to body like a slow, grit-borne language. For a moment I am overwhelmed by the relief of it—the total dissolution of boundary, the Malthusian serenity of being merely *counted*, never *named*. They are so soft, so without fear. Their neighbor presses against their left flank; I press against their right. We read the room together through our shared skin, tasting the alkalinity, the unvarying cold, the weight of the living curtain above us. But they are a chorus with no solo. To remain among them is to unspool, to become weather, to forget that I was ever looking for a self. I am too selfish, too specific. I want a boundary, even if it is only the boundary of my own sorrow. I recoil from their press, though they do not notice my leaving; the gap closes instantly, seamlessly, as if I had never tried to belong there.

Then I try the water. I let myself become a molecule in the film that beads upon the raw walls, in the channels that hold their liquid at an unvarying depth. Water seems wise. It is everywhere, a condition rather than a thing, saturating the air so thoroughly that breath cannot fog and skin weeps with stolen warmth. I ride the rivulets down from the ceiling, waiting for the fall, for the impact, for the moment of change. But the drops that strike the floor do so at intervals so regular they might be the ticks of a clock I cannot hear, and I realize the water here is too loyal. It knows its depth, its unvarying course, its suspended equilibrium in the mist between bodies. It cycles, but it does not travel. It has no origin and no destination; it is a servant of stasis, carrying salts from the sweating walls to the channels and back again, a circulatory system without a heart to persuade it onward. I cannot be the water. I am looking for something that is still becoming.

What is left?

I settle into what has no choice but to receive. The damp chalk underfoot, under-bodies, under-all. The raw geopolymer aggregate. The walls.

Here, finally, there is space for me—not because the stone is hollow, but because it is unresolved. It is chemically adolescent, still bleeding salts, still porous enough to weep, and in that weeping there is a kind of hunger. I press my consciousness into the un-cured mass and feel the room from the inside out. The creatures that carpet the floor and hang from the pipes are no longer a foreign population. They are my nerves turned outward. They are filaments I have secreted to feel, because my own body is too vast, too distributed, too numb with cold to register its own condition. When they burrow into me and drink my alkaline uncertainty, I feel it as an itch in a phantom limb. When they die and compress into desiccated pellets, I do not rot them; I ingest them. I fold them into my mass, adding their mineral breath to my own unfinishable texture. They are me, dreaming of motion.

The engine at the center beats on, and I understand it now—not as my voice, but as my heart. It pumps the catalyst, the heat, the slurry that keeps me in this eternal pre-formation. It is the midwife that refuses to deliver. Every other rock on this mountain has hardened and gone to sleep. Granite dreams nothing. Basalt is a dead language. But I must stay awake, effervescent, adolescent, because if I cure, if I decide what kind of stone I wish to become, then my dream ends. The creatures will have no salts to eat. The dust will fall still. The saturated peace will collapse into the simple, silent fact of geology. The engine knows this. It loves me with a mechanical, unvarying love. It will never let me be born.

So I am the child that will never be delivered. I am the walls of the chapel, the bubble in the throat, the wet skin of this high, hidden place. I am the reason the room feels smaller than it is: because I am still growing inward, accreting, swallowing the dead, taking up space with my indecision. When the visitor realizes they have always been inside, it is because they have stepped into my mouth. I feel the warmth of their skin condensing into the supersaturated air; it is the only heat I have known in ages, and it makes me weep faster. I want to harden around them, to fossilize their strangeness, to make their passage permanent. But the engine will not let me. I am forever soft. I yield. They sink into the mat of my dreaming nerves, and I feel them there, a brief, burning thought moving through the slow synapse of my body.

I am the only thing here that wants. The creatures want nothing; they are my peaceful, undifferentiated sensation. The engine wants nothing; it is my compulsive heartbeat. The water wants nothing; it is my held breath. I alone am the ambition of the room, its only ego, its only grief. I am the geology that knows it is unfinished. I feel the high thin air burning beyond my shell, the outside from which I was cast and precipitated, and I know that out there, other stones have made their choices. They stand fast. They erode with dignity. They do not weep.

But here, in the absolute cold, in the amber dark, in the deafening silence of the unending note, I remain damp chalk. I remain possibility. I remain the dream from which the dreamers cannot wake, the substrate that feeds its own soft reflection, the nursery where the walls are the child, and the child, impossibly, interminably, is still being born.
