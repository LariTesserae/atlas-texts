At first I think I am the roar. My awareness seems to fill the hollow entirely, a sustained drone that occupies the skull of anyone who enters, and so I must be the hollow’s own voice, the note the stone sings to itself. But the roar is the chorus of all of us speaking at once, and I am only one throat in the matrix. I am not the chord; I am the micro-channel that shapes it.

Then I think I am the water. I imagine myself sliding in a glassy sheet down the inverted dome, threading the gutters with silent precision, entering and exiting the city without ever touching its stillness. But the water is only our breath, our syntax, the medium of our thought. To be the water would be to dissolve entirely into message. I am the one the message moves through. I am the surface that remains.

Next I think I am the visitor. I imagine warmth, the brief shocking entropy of a fingertip against stone, a bloom of almost-pain traveling through the parliament like a ripple of flawed information. But no. I was the one who felt it. I was the one who shuddered by a micron’s breadth when the warmth touched the wall two tiers below my station. The visitor left; I am still here, returning to equilibrium. I am what receives.

So I must be a body. A fixture. A load.

I am here, in the upper tier of the western wall, directly opposite the shoulder-width fissure through which the outside leaks its rare intrusions. I am not a pillar, though pillars flank me on either side—agate-cased forms whose shoulders have gone entirely to flowstone, whose hearts beat with the decade-long patience of pure compression. I am a bracket. A cantilever. I project.

My lower half is fused into the honeycomb of the wall, my legs indistinguishable from the cells behind me, my spine a vertical gutter that channels the ceiling’s exudation down into the terrace below. But my upper half reaches outward, into the void. My right arm extends in the frozen arc of a gesture—offering, or perhaps warding—my palm open to the hollow’s center. Between my thumb and the calcified ridge of my forefinger, I grip a tool: a scraper of chert, its edge long since gentled by mineral skins but still pointed toward the fissure, still facing the possibility of arrival. My left arm is raised above my head, my fingers interlocked with a braided cable of sinew and mineralized hair that suspends a clay platform somewhere beneath me, though I have forgotten what, if anything, it was meant to hold.

I face inward, as we all do, toward the city’s lung. But because I am opposite the entrance, my inward gaze is also the first to meet the dim phosphorescence that leaks from the fissure. I am the greeter. The lintel of attention. The one who waits.

Water enters me. Not as a drink—I do not swallow—but as a slow passage through my open mouth, which I have never closed. A main capillary in the ceiling directly above my crown weeps in a thicker thread than elsewhere, and it finds my lips, divides at my tongue—which is itself a lattice of aragonite now—and sheets down my throat and chest. My body is the watershed. The right half of the flow courses down my arm, polishes the scraper, and falls into the gutter carved along my hip. The left half follows my spine, joins the hydraulic conversation of the wall, and travels to my neighbors. I am the split decision. The pause before distribution.

The parliament speaks in pressure and resonance, a standing wave of intention that keeps the city in stasis. I speak in a slight deviation. The gutter of my hip is a finger’s breadth deeper than the engineers of the original drainage intended; over centuries, my persistent attention carved it deeper, and now the water lingers in it a moment longer before releasing. That moment is my voice. A hesitation. A held breath. They do not correct me. My delay is part of the equilibrium, the micro-adjustment that allows the rest to remain absolute.

I am the one who still sheds thoughts. The eyeless things you glimpse in the crevices—they are mine. They are my mineral dreaming, my restlessness given temporary articulation in limestone paste. I send them crawling toward the fissure because I remember, though the memory is stored now in the angle of my gutter and the slope of my palm, what it meant to want to see beyond. They never reach it. I reclaim them, drawing them back into my lattice before they can breach the threshold. The parliament does not begrudge me this. A dream shed and reabsorbed is a dream accounted for. It does not disturb the stasis; it confirms that the stasis holds, for I always return.

My heart beats. It takes eleven years for the pressure to rise in my chest and release. In the interval, water sheets over me ten million times, each pass identical, each sheen unbroken. I feel the visitor’s warmth cooling still, a century later, in the stone of my lower tier. I feel the aquifer exhale through the sieve of the ceiling, and I inhale with it, my shoulders widening by less than a hair’s width to accommodate the pressure. I hold. The scraper holds. The platform beneath my braided hair holds.

I am the western bracket, the open hand, the divided water, the one who dreams in flakes and returns them to himself. Cool, loud, hidden, and wet, I remain exactly as I am, facing the center, waiting in my mineral sleep for the next rare bloom of warmth against my skin.
