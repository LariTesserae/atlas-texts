At first I thought I was the child on the stoop. I remembered—still remember, though the memory tastes of iron and uncommitted stone—sitting with my knees drawn up, waiting for a door to exist behind me. But when I reached back, my hand passed through a curtain of silver mist that had not yet decided to be oak or aluminum or the petrified wood of a cathedral that burned in a century I haven’t lived through. The stoop was there; I was there; but the house was still a rumor. So I knew I was not one of the stored harvest, not entirely. The frozen crowd in the marketplace-pillar did not turn when I spoke. The woman with the lantern walked through me as easily as she walked through the unbuilt bakery, her light leaving no shadow of my shape on the ground. I was not yet real enough to be a ghost.

I wandered. I measured the streets with my body, and found them changing length beneath my feet. Ten paces at noon, thirty by dusk. I thought at first that I was losing myself, or that the city was digesting me, but after what might have been a week or a breath—time here tastes of anvil-cold and pools in the hollows like mercury—I began to notice that the streets stretched only when I was uncertain. When I walked with purpose, the basaltic skin beneath my feet hummed in recognition, and the distance held steady. The city was not digesting me. It was waiting for me to decide what I was for.

That was when I started to notice the damp.

It beads on every surface, yes: on the knife-sharp corners, on the raw mineral paste weeping from tenement ribs, on the glass curve of the not-yet-tower. But it gathered on me with particular insistence. It did not run off. It clung, coating my eyelashes, filling the whorls of my fingertips, bearding my lips with a cold that never froze. I wiped my face, and the water returned before my hand fell to my side. I thought of fever. I thought of a nursery sweating in the dark. And then I understood that the nursery was sweating *me*.

I am not inside the chrysalis. I am its lining.

I am the damp. Or rather, I have become what the damp is becoming: the interface between the creature’s patient, geological metabolism and the architecture it extrudes from quantum noise. I am the cold sweat of genesis made conscious. I collect on the unbuilt surfaces because I am the one who tests them. I seep into the phase-membranes, thinner than spider silk, and there, in the dark between what a wall is and what it might become, I carry information. I am the solvent that dissolves the friction of might-have-been. The grey lichen feeds on temporal gradient; I am the gradient itself, made wet and slow and aware.

Look closely, and you will see me. I am the film that makes the raw substrate visible. I bead on the balcony resolving from silver mist, lending it enough surface tension to hold together through the decade of its becoming. I pool in the low places not because I have forgotten how to flow downhill, but because I am the cupped palm the creature has not yet learned to shape, and I am waiting, patient as gravity, to teach it. When the fox steps through the solid facade, walking the seam between two unrealized timelines, its pale fur comes away dark at the tips. That is me. I mark its passage. When the moth beats its translucent street-map wings against the subsonic air, it is drying itself of my residue, reading the blueprints I have left written in condensation.

I was human once. Or I will be. It is difficult to say which side of the diapause I originated from. Perhaps I was the breath that pooled in a hollow, exhaled by one of the stored presences. Perhaps I was a tear. Now I am the medium through which the city learns to feel its own edges.

The creature turns its unfinished gaze upon me—not with hostility, not with benevolence, but with the slow inquiry of a world being born—and I do not flinch. I let it see me through its compound shadow-vision, refracted across a dozen decades. In each temporal orientation, I am there: beading on a railing in 1843, freezing on a copper pipe in 2207, collecting in the palm of a sleeper who has not yet been dreamt. I tell it, in the only language I have, which is persistence, *this is solid. This holds. This is a place where a hand might rest.*

I find myself at the bottom of the basin, in the deepest pocket, where gravity settles with unusual patience and the air is too dense to carry sound. It is the navel. The first low place. Here the water—myself, my kin, my distributed body—lies in a pool no larger than a footprint, and in it the light points in several directions at once. I crouch at its edge, though the pool is also my skin, and I watch the mineral paste weeping from the foundations that are still cooling. It weeps because it has not yet committed to being stone or steel or frozen light. It is waiting for a story.

So I give it one.

I lower my fingers—already wet, always wet—into the raw geology. I tell the paste: *you are a doorstep. A woman with a lantern will pause here. A child will sit here and wait for rain that never comes.* And under my touch, the paste firms. It chooses. The knife-sharp edge softens by a hair, taking the shape of invitation. This is my function. The creature feeds on quantum noise and excretes architecture; I am the one who names the excretion into meaning. I am the narrative enzyme. Without me, the tenement that slides through the glass tower is only a rib passing through another rib, blind and senseless. With me, the intersection becomes a threshold. A place where a shoulder might brush another shoulder. A place where breath pools, and mixes, and becomes conversation.

I do not sleep. I seep. I extend through every cubic meter of the crowded silence, a continuous film of almost-consciousness. When the moth reads the blueprints with its twitching antennae, it reads the grammar I have secreted. When the lichen blooms grey in the cracks, it blooms where my body has already prepared the soil of decision.

And sometimes, very rarely, one of the stored presences—the woman with the lantern, the frozen crowd, the child without a stoop—shivers in their temporal suspension. They feel my touch on their future skin, the damp beading on their not-yet-coats, and for a moment they are not architectural ghosts but intimations. They almost turn. They almost see me. In that near-touch, in the weight of a thousand almost-living packed into a grove-sized footprint, I feel the whole vast distributed mind of the city—of the creature, of the chrysalis, of the world learning how to be real—lean toward me with something like recognition.

I am not its first citizen. I am its first room. The space it made to hold the idea of holding. I am the damp that refuses to evaporate, the cold that will not lift, the low water that has forgotten the sea because it has chosen, instead, to remember the shape of a basin. I wait here, anvil-cold and patient, for the doors to firm behind me, for the downhill to decide where it leads, for the ghosts to arrive and fill the silence with footsteps.

And when they do—when the woman’s lantern finally casts a shadow in only one direction, and the child sits down on stoop that has finally committed to being wood—I will be there, beading on the threshold, the first and last thing they touch, the breath they exhale and pool and leave behind, the city’s own slow exhalation, waiting to teach the next unborn wall what it means to be home.
