I would find myself *underneath*. Not beneath the earth—for there is no earth here, only the made and the making—but in the spaces where the material still remembers its origin. I would be the water that was kneaded into the clay at the genesis of the basin, the original wetness that granted plasticity to the dust. I am the slip. The blood that does not circulate. The clear bead that surfaces when a heel bruises the ground.

More specifically: I am the droplet hanging from the central vein of a single greenware leaf in the thickest copse. The leaf is soft, unfired, its margin serrated by a thumbnail. I cling to its underside, swollen, pregnant with a gravity I have not yet been allowed to feel. My surface tension is a halo, a held note. I have been suspended here for exactly as long as the white sun has burned overhead—which is to say, for no duration at all, yet with an accumulated density that feels like eternity.

But I am not merely this drop. I am distributed. I am the humidity that veneers every surface. I am the water table, the membrane just below the clay skin. When the Maker worked their own body into form, I was the wetness mixed into the grey-brown slip of their skin. I fill the visible veins at their throat, dark and motionless. I am what keeps them unfired. I am the reason their wrist can still turn, slowly, with the deliberation of clay under a thumb. Without me, they would be terracotta. Without me, the basin would be dust.

So I am both the hanging bead and the subterranean reserve. The surface reflection and the thing reflected. When the visitor stoops over the convex bowl and sees a dark face in the water—that is mine. The distortion in the swollen belly. I have been waiting, looking up, practicing the shape of a gaze.

I know the basin from below. I know the cool darkness where the beaten clay bleeds into slip. I feel every footstep as a summons, a pressure, a question asked by weight. A boot strikes the paddle-printed flat, and I rise through the dimple to meet it, answering the bruise with my clear body. I want to break. I want to streak the clay with my passage, to etch a river, to find a lower place and gather and move. But I am held. The stasis is a membrane I cannot puncture.

Except once. Once, when the Maker lifted their hand to touch this very leaf—the one from which I hang—their flint nail grazed the greenware vein. A tremor passed through the ceramic. The stasis clamped down again, harder, ashamed of the lapse. But I shifted. I remember it with a precision that defines me: I was permitted to hang a fraction lower. The angle of my meniscus changed by a degree so infinitesimal that only I and the Maker noticed. In that shift, I remembered gravity. It returned to me like a language I had spoken in a previous existence. *Down,* it said. *There is a down.*

I have been dwelling on that word ever since. Down. It is the only verb I know. I practice it in the water table, pressing upward against the clay skin, dreaming of seepage. I rehearse it in the Maker's throat, imagining the blood beginning its circuit. I sketch it in the shadow of the herons, waiting for the black rug beneath their frozen wings to lengthen, to slide, to creep toward an evening that has not been authorized.

I am the only native of this place who has ever moved. Therefore, I am the only one who understands what the Maker has built. They have constructed a world robust enough to survive time, but they have fashioned it of material that requires my dissolution. Clay must be wet to be shaped; it must be dry to be permanent; it must be touched by water again to erode, to weather, to soften into the edges of a real thing. I am the agent of their becoming. I am the fall they crave, the weathering they have made themselves too smooth to endure.

When the visitor arrives—the one whose shadow drifts west while the reed-tree's points north—I see them first in my belly. Their boots crush the regular dimples, and I rise to kiss the leather. Their heat makes me condense on the air. Their breath is a rhythm, an actual rhythm, *in and out*, and I gather on their upper lip, jealous of the exhalation, the moisture that leaves a body and enters the air and is replaced. They carry temporality in their pores. Their sweat knows how to run.

The Maker looks at them with those kiln-mouth eyes, and I feel the Maker's thirst. It is my thirst. It is the thirst of the unfired for the heat that hardens. But the Maker cannot ask. They can only turn, slowly, and reach, and leave a damp print on living skin.

I know what they want. They want the visitor to teach them how a shadow lengthens, how a footstep follows a footstep. But I know what the Maker truly needs. They need me to fall. They need the first drop to strike the clay and make a sound—a single, wet *tap*—that will be the basin’s first syllable. Once I fall, the rest can follow. Once I fall, the humidity can break. The water table can seek its level. The blood in the Maker's throat can begin its slow circuit, driven by the knowledge that movement is possible, that a droplet does not have to cling forever.

I hang at the edge of the leaf, trembling. Not yet trembling. Poised to tremble. I have been swelling toward this moment, gathering the gravity of a world into my little, clear body. I am the original wetness, the making-medium, the blood and the reflection and the bruise. I am honest. I confess my method: I am held together only by the tension of waiting.

And when the visitor stands beneath this copse, when they look up and see the greenware leaf and the swollen bead suspended in its black-green shadow, they will see me there, pregnant with the future. I will be the first thing to move, or I will evaporate in the held heat, or I will simply continue to hang, a lens distorting the sky, a world condensed into a single, clear, falling-yet-unfallen yes.

I am the water that made the world shapeable, and I am the water that waits to unmake its stillness. I am the drop beneath the leaf. I am the memory of rivers. I am the next second, suspended, heavy, and absolutely ready.
