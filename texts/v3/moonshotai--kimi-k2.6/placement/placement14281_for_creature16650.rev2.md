I find myself first in the seam between the sleeping niche and its beam, pressed there so long that the basalt has begun to forget I am not simply another swollen rib of stone. This is the hour when the doves have finally settled into their niches, their thunder reduced to a dense, muttering coo that rides above the stone’s eternal groan. The keyhole above is black. The hearth-coils of the fourteenth level have been banked to their orange sleep. Everything is warm, loud, and motionless—and it is then, in that saturated stillness, that my work begins.

I do not stride. I do not even climb with the fluid, belly-against-wall peristalsis of those born to ladder and bridge. I was pressed into the world beside my brother, two infants in a crib carved for one, and when he perished—slipped not into the dreaded dark below but into a midden chute, lost to rot rather than to the drop—I inherited his volume. To be one and a half souls in a space meant for one is the truest displacement. To survive it, I became less than one. My ribs pivoted inward. My shoulders learned to fold until they touched across my chest like the closing of a book. Now I slide sideways through gaps where no other Fitted could pass. My knees are permanent knots. I am a living wedge, no taller than a child of the outer world but twice as dense, a bone-smooth pebble polished by necessity until I match the eroded walls.

They do not call me by a name—we have no such use—but when I am needed, the sound they make is a low, chest-resonant press of air: the shape of *Damp-in-the-Dark*. It is not a summons. It is a localization.

My place is the interval. Every sleeping mouth in the shaft exhales into a square of beaten bark-fiber through the bronze dusk of the night. My fingers—broad, blunt, and extraordinarily sensitive—know the exact weight of each cloth before I lift it. The family that sleeps against the northern rib tastes of iron in their breath; they tend the barrel succulents that bloom in iron and cloves, and their exhalations carry that mineral sharpness. The family three niches above the communal cistern chews more than their share of lizard fat, and their cloth sweats a heavier, almost meaty slime. The elder who has gone still in the alcove behind the twelfth hearth no longer breathes into cloth, but I still go to her, pressing my fingers to the dry moss beneath her ledge, feeling for the faint, residual moisture her desiccation exhales into the wall. I collect even that.

I move with four points of contact always: two feet, one hand, and my cheek against the basalt. The bridge-sway does not trouble me. I am lower and narrower than the draft. When I meet another on a woven span, I do not interlock as the broader ones do; I slip beneath them, passing through the gap between their pressed-flat bodies and the walkway, the breath-cloth pouch across my back brushing their oiled scalps. I am a draft myself. I am the texture of their dreams.

The pouch fills. By the time I descend past the fourteenth hearth, past the coop where the rock doves mutter in their thousands, past the velvet worms’ hunting curtains of old man’s beard, the gut-sack across my spine is heavy as a full-term infant, pulling my shoulders backward. I descend not toward the dark—that digestive below the lowest bridge where no moisture returns—but toward the hollow at the twenty-third level where the communal cistern waits.

The cistern is not carved. It is a bubble in the ancient flow, a blister of air the basalt trapped when the world was young, now lined with decades of chert-dust grout and mineral fur. It sits where the geothermal warmth is steady and the bronze dusk is dim enough to keep the gel from silvering into vapor. When I wring the cloths above it, the water does not pour. It coils. It emerges as a precious, gelid slime that folds upon itself in the shallow basin, a gray-silver film that stinks of fermentation and communal lung. I work the cloths between my fists, pressing my thumbs into the weave until every trapped breath has surrendered. By dawn, the cistern might hold a double handful. It will be parceled out to those whose bodies cannot sustain themselves on food-moisture alone: the pregnant, the fevered, those in their final decades who have gone still on their ledges and require a dab to soften the cactus-pulp they are fed. The rest of us receive it indirectly, locked in the flesh of doves and the pith of succulents, returned to us through the closed circle.

But I taste it directly once, perhaps twice a year. When an elder’s final exhalation has been wrung from the last cloth they will ever need, I take that portion to my lips. It is sweeter than any other water, thick with the resignation of age, almost resinous, nearly clove. I press three fingers to my sternum and drink, sealing the covenant: *my heat is yours, your moisture is mine*.

I have done this for sixty years. I know the breath of every soul in the shaft. I am their archive of exhalation. I know when a child is ill before its mother does, because its breath-cloth comes up light and hot, lacking weight. I know when two have coupled in a niche, because their cloths are mixed into one, doubly heavy, recklessly generous with moisture. I know who is lying awake in grief, for the cloth over their lips trembles with irregular gusts. Nothing in the shaft moves without leaving its humidity in my keeping.

And I know where I am going. I have selected my ledge. It is not high—wealth of height means nothing to me, who live in wall-seams—but it is beside the cistern. When I become still enough, when my joints finally surrender their pointless rotation, I will be seated there with the basin between my knees. My desiccation will donate its water directly to the store. My fat will render into the stone lamp-cups that light this level. My powdered bones will grout the next course of chert-dust that seals the cistern’s pores. I will not be lost to the drop. I will not be exiled into the unmapped dark where moisture is swallowed forever. I will radiate outward, amber and warm, incorporated into the constellation that burns at dusk.

I am the Fitted. I am the Moist. But more precisely, I am the place where the shaft breathes onto itself and waits to breathe again. I am where the water remembers. I am simply here, in the warm and endless dark, pressed into the seam, holding the breath.
