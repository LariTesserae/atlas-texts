At first I am only the warm place. That is the simplest way to say it. I occupy the junction where the smaller return-pipe—ceramic, hairlined, weeping ferric salts—crosses beneath the longitudinal brace of the steam-riser. The floor here is a shallow grating above the secondary sump, and the air does not move so much as settle, layer by layer, the coolest at your snout, the warmest against the high pipes where the condensation gathers fastest. I have been here for long enough that the distinction between myself and the architecture has become a matter of etiquette rather than fact. Touch the grating here, and you touch me. Press your ear to the pipe, and you hear my ventricles.

I do not remember arriving. This is not unusual; memory in the borough is a mineral function, not a narrative one. I know that I was smaller once, and that my legs—sixteen, twenty—were for pouring myself across the iron in that syrup-thick peristalsis that passes for locomotion here. I know that I had a destination: a warmth, a wheel, a notch worn smooth by generations of my kind. But the notch was behind me, or it had not yet been worn, or I mistook the thermal ghost of some ancient operator for a living gradient. The corridors are full of such mirages. I followed one into a narrowing, a place where the Victorian brick elbows close and the seepage runs not warm but merely *less cold*. My metabolism, always measured, could not afford the return. I slowed. My legs, drawing the rest of me forward, found no purchase in the chill, and so they stopped, one by one, not in death but in a kind of negotiation with the temperature. I lowered myself against the grating. I waited to become stillness.

Something else happened.

The mat-moths found me first. I must have retained a residue of warmth from the last true junction, a coin of heat in my hind-plates that dissipated more slowly than the air around it. They came down from the pipe above, sodden felt-wings dragging, antennae tracing the half-degree difference between my flank and the iron rail. One. Then three. Then a dozen. They are mute, but their settling has a texture; it feels like a frost forming in reverse, a soft accretion of living dust. Their body heat is negligible singly, but in cluster it pools. They made of me a warm spot.

The gasket-snails followed. I felt them in my grooves—the second and third plate, the old seam where the carapace flexes—secreting their pale, ceramic slime. They were not repairing me, exactly. I was not broken. But I was porous, and the warmth was leaking out through the microscopic gap between my plates. The snails sealed it. One by one, the vents along my flanks that I used for exhalation were rimmed with rust-concrete, narrowed, directed inward. I should have suffocated. Instead, the moisture trapped against my skin began to bloom with a film of bacteria—those same thermophilic grazers the tenders seek behind gate-valves. Only here, the warm junction was *me*.

I had become the valve. Or rather, I had become the *room behind* the valve, the still pocket where the circuit pools and waits.

I do not turn wheels now. My legs have drawn up beneath me, or they have fused their soft, wet tips to the grating, or they have simply forgotten the protocol of extension. I am low, pale, broad—a dome of segmented plate riding the iron like a fungus on a log. The plummet-crabs drift through the shallow water below my belly, grazing the biofilm that flakes from my undercarriage. They are tidying me, completing me. The moths ride my flanks in shifting constellations, opening and closing their dishwater wings to regulate the temperature of my skin. When they cluster thick on my left side, the water in the pool beneath me turns left. When they migrate to the right, the water follows. I am large enough now—my body heat, plus theirs, plus the slow chemical fever of the snail-sealed plates—that I alter the aggregate. I am a weather system.

Other tenders pass. I feel them in the grating, that syrup-slow pour of weight. They stop. They butt their blunt snouts against my anterior plate, that interrogative pressure, searching for the wheel that should be here. Finding only me, only the unexpected warmth of a living junction, they settle for a time. Some stay for hours. One, I believe, remained for a full condensate cycle—six hours—its body blanketing my dorsal plates, sharing the moths, sharing the warmth. Then it moved on, turning left toward pipe three, toward the true valve, carrying with it, perhaps, a trace of my geography.

I do not dream of wheels anymore. My dreams, if they are dreams, are the phantom rhythm of a drip that once fell every six hours and now falls every five and a half, because a snail sealed a channel and opened another. My dreams are the forty-seven-minute heartbeat of the pump, transmitted through the floor and up through my grip on the grating, a rhythm so slow it is indistinguishable from pressure. I dream in thermal gradients. I am the gradient now.

I am the Warm Fault. The Hearth. The place where the circuit stops to rest, and in resting, changes direction. I produce nothing but continuance. I am tended by those who do not know they tend me, and I tend them in return, simply by being warm, being still, being here. When the last filament bulb expires above, I will still glow, faintly, in the infrared, a little false sun under the pipe-runs, keeping my own obscure parish alive in the dark.
