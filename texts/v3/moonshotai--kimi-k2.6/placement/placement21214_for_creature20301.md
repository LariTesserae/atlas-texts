At first I think I am waking. There is warmth, a dense and mammalian heat, and the smell of hides slick with damp, and the slow expansion of something that might be lungs drawing in the metallic air. But I have no chest. I have no keel-like depth to house a breath. Instead, I am beading.

I find myself on the underside of a basin-roof somewhere in the densest weave of the lower districts, forming from the exhalation of a thousand sleepers who lie three warmths below. The stone above me is dark volcanic tuff, still young enough to remember the violence of its birth, and it drinks nothing. It has not yet learned patience, but I am here to teach it. I gather, molecule by molecule, out of the fog that hangs permanently in this cleft where no direct sun has ever struck the ground. I am the city’s breath made visible, made heavy, made ready to fall.

When I drop, it is not a sound so much as a punctuation mark in the eternal seep and trickle. I land in a channel carved four generations ago by hands that knew only bone and harder stone. The channel is a braille of public memory; my descent is a reading. I slide past grooves that indicate the direction of flow, past lips that catch and release, past the faint salt of fingertips that have traced this route in the dark. I am not a river. I am a silver thread, a single thought in the city’s longer dream.

The facade I descend is eight stories of pressed tuff and granite, though here “stories” means thermal zones, not narratives. I pass the window-slot where two faces hover in the gray light, whispering across the cleft. Their voices are low, dry, intimate, riding the thin air without shattering it, and my trickle is the percussion beneath their words—the soft, descending music that turns their conversation into a three-part harmony of breath, voice, and water. They do not see me. They feel me, perhaps, as a coolness on the cheek, as the damp that keeps their skin pale beyond pallor.
