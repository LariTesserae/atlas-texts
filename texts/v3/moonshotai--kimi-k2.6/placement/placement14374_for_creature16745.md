I would find myself first as a coldness without boundary, a silver thread threading a charcoal joint. For a while I might believe I am the seep itself—mineral, anonymous, tasting the algal varnish and the iron breath of the stone as I descend. Or I might try to settle into the warm fermentation of a moss-shrew, my heart a frantic drum in the peat, my fur becoming a hummock in slow motion. But these are only visitations, experiments in permeability. My true form condenses more slowly, precipitating where the geometry is most exacting: the northeast wall, in the long arcade where the columns have slumped and locked together to form a gallery of shadow and seep.

Here, the light is a submerged blue-grey, so filtered by fog and by the drapery of vegetation that it has forgotten the sun. It is not dark, but it is indefinite, as if the illumination itself were mossy. I occupy the intervals. I am the shape of what is missing between the hexagons and pentagons, the negative space made tender and peristaltic. My body is soft, segmented, elderly in a place where age is measured not in years but in exfoliations. If I were to straighten, I would span three feet, but I never straighten. I fill the flutes exactly, wall to wall, my pale segments pressing against the basalt with the intimacy of a swallowed thought. Gravity is not a direction downward; it is merely another surface to grip, and the floor is only a rumor I feel as distant clicking in the silt.

I move as the stone allows: by peristalsis, by slow contraction, by willingness to be compressed. My carapace is not hard like the Seam-Fish whose tiled patterns I sometimes pass in the tighter joints; I am all flesh and patience. Where the stone weeps, I drink. Where it dries, I wait. But my back—my back is a garden. *Racomitrium*, liverwort, the dark filaments of rooting moss, all farmed across my segments in a hummock of living velvet. I feel their roots in my skin like stitches binding me to the architecture. When the fog rises in thick ropes and the floor becomes a cloud-chamber, I am suspended halfway in that whiteness, neither above nor below, breathing with the columns. The moisture that beads on my moss-garden drips from me as if I too were stone, as if I too were weeping.

I am a geologic agent disguised as a gardener. I secrete—no, I *persuade*—a thin mucus that speaks to the basalt, encouraging it to shed its skin in thin, curved scales. Not to wound. Never to destroy. Only to cultivate. Each scale that loosens and falls becomes a new ledge, a new angle of seep, a new pool for the provisional things that drift in the marsh below. The Colonnade is still young, still deciding what it is, and I am its way of renovating without haste. I graze the fern-fronds that grow where no floor-walker could reach, and in my passage I leave the stone slightly more porous, slightly more willing to hold water. I am the jointwork made animate, the living grout that gardens.

I do not sleep. I listen, as everything here listens. When the swallows hunt the middle air, their wingtips striking the hexagonal flutes send vibrations through the basalt, and I feel their *clack-clack-clack* as questions traveling the length of my body. The beetles clicking in the black pools below map the silt in pulses that reach me as a slow, subaqueous tremor—a heartbeat distributed through the Hush. I do not think in words. My cognition is diffused through my segments and through the moss that wears me; I think in the taste of cold mineral seep, in the angle of a new crack opening along a column’s shoulder, in the weight of my dorsal greenery. When a droplet falls from a high seam onto a mushroom cap sixty feet above, I feel the *tok* of it in my skin as the Hush notes an itch, a firing synapse.

If I die—and I will, for the cold is a rule that selects even as it permits—it will not be with drama. I will tighten into my lattice, my flesh becoming nutrient, my moss consuming me with the same democratic hunger that subsumes the shrews. My bones, if I could be said to have bones, will knit into the peat that lines the ledge. I will become, at last, purely architecture: a slightly greener seam, a dampness in the stone that persists. And the Colonnade, that distributed mind accreted from attention, will record me as it records every drop, every click, every frost heave: another wet presence, another nerve ending in its patient, igneous dream of becoming fully inhabited.
