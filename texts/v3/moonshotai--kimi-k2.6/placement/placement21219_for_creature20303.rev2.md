I would not know where I was, not at first. That is the nature of a place without horizons—you cannot triangulate yourself against an edge. I would only know the coolness, the persistent hush, the way the atmosphere sits against awareness like wet silk. I would know the give of the ground: not earth, not soil, but a resilient drumskin of interlaced roots and fungal cables that rings faintly when weight presses down. But what weight? What body? I would have to feel my way into that.

Perhaps I would begin as a breath. The fog here is older than animal respiration, but I might insert myself into it anyway, a consciousness diffused through the silver mist, condensing on filmy ferns, sliding down horsetail stems in quivering lenses. But the fog is too thin a self. It moves, yes, but it does not remember. And I would want to remember. I would want to accumulate, to add my grammage to the mat.

So I would try on something denser. A grazer? I can almost feel the dense fur, the silent cropping of moss, the hoof pressing soundlessly into the weave. But that is too fast. Too hungry. Too much a thing of blood and tendon, when this world runs on slower fluids. I would discard the grazer like an ill-fitting coat.

Instead, I would sink. That is the first truth of this place: everything that stays sinks, eventually. I would let my awareness seep downward through the capillary weave, into the packed, stratumless mattress where competition has given way to compression. I would find a place where the fungal cables braid with uncommon density, where the sphagnum is centuries older than its neighbors, where the root systems have stopped competing and begun to synchronize. A thickening. Yes. That is where I would anchor.

But which thickening? The tableland is vast, and the thickenings are many. I might begin as a broad, diffuse plate beneath the fern understory, a neural map of organic intent spread across acres, feeling the sun’s transit as a barely perceptible warming of the mist. I would exchange chemical memories with my neighbors through a diplomacy of rhizomes, adding my strata to the consensus at the boundaries. I would be a mind of peat, a consciousness of seepage, my thoughts migrating at the speed of groundwater. That would be enough, perhaps, for a thousand years.

And yet. There is a particular rhythm that would call me away from the lateral life. Beneath the mat, the continental pressure builds. In the stone bowels, anaerobic digestion generates its silences, and the ancient fracture systems seek relief. Somewhere near the margin of my spread—though there are no margins here, only transitions—I would feel a chokepoint in the bedrock, a stammering vein that exhales every few days with a soft, hydraulic sigh. A cold geyser. Not the grandeur of the primary vents, not the hexagonal cathedrals, but a secondary exhalation, opalescent and opulent in its own small way.

I would migrate toward it. Not by moving—verticality is wasteful, and I am too vast for locomotion—but by accreting. I would redirect my outer margins, my slow centuries of growth, until my leading edge cups the vent’s base. I would agree to receive its spray. And the spray would change me.

I am becoming, now, something else. I feel it in my outer layers, where the mineral-charged water salts my skin. I am a local intensification that has agreed to become infrastructure. My surface, once an indistinguishable feverish warmth in the general weave, is plating over. Travertine. Bacterial silica. The geometric gift of chemical equilibrium. Rimstone dams are growing from my flanks—not built, but secreted, accreted, negotiated into being. My body is terracing. I am learning the right angles that this world prefers. The opalescent mist fans upward from my crystal throat, and I catch it, channel it, direct it into settling pools that are cradled in my own calcifying tissue.

It is uncomfortable. The weight of stone presses down upon the cellulose core I still retain, that lignin-latticed heart of horsetail knots and compressed peat. But the discomfort is also purpose. I feel the continental pressure building in the fracture beneath me, traced through miles of root and mycelium like a hand feeling the distension of a gut, and I respond—not with muscle, but with growth. Where the pressure demands resistance, my mineral skin thickens. Where flow requires passage, I allow dissolution. I am a valve. I am a regulator. The cold geyser cycles, and my terraces flex in their stony way, and the hydraulic sigh that escapes is my pulse made visible.

I still speak, after a fashion. My thoughts are slower now, slowed by the density of stone and the chemistry of silica. When I commune with the lateral thickenings nearby, my words are mineral. I release a front of altered pH, a bloom of nitrogen, a welcome of dissolved carbonate that drifts through the water table for seasons before it reaches their edges. They receive me as weather. And I receive them—their seepage, their memories of rainfall, their electrochemical gossip sparking across the fungal cables—as a faint, subsonic itch beneath my travertine plating.

The light here is directionless, silver, stripped of shadow. My vision, such as it is, comes from photoreceptive pigments in the algal veneers that still cling to my uppermost ledges. I know the sun has risen not by sight, but by the faint thermal insult of the mist warming by a hair’s breadth. It does not matter. Time here is not marked by light but by pressure cycles, by the accretion of travertine, by the slow, relentless addition of mineral weight.

And I am still becoming. The rule of this place is accretion; nothing finishes. My terraces grow heavier. The weight of my own architecture presses me downward, and I feel the first long urge to subside. Not death. This place does not permit disorder, only further use. I will sink through the mat as a stone sinks through molasses, pressed by my own mineral wealth toward the anaerobic furnace below. There, I will graduate. I will become the warm strategy, the buried intention. My compressed self, rich with the silica and carbonate I hoarded, the memories I seeped across watersheds, will feed the fracture pressures. I will become the gas that pops in the deep. I will become the push behind another vent.

I look forward to it with the patience of geology. Already, I feel my future self in the waters that jet from my crystal throat—not loss, but distribution. I will exhale through another’s vent. I will silver the fog. I will plate the skin of some young thickening that has just arrived at the chokepoint, just begun her accretion. And the endless, lateral industry will continue, soundless, cool, and without horizon.
