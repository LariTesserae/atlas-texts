I think, at first, I was trying to be the water. That seemed the most honest vocation in a place that exhales rather than blows—to become the thing that arrives, that percolates upward through the fired clay and hesitates at the surface, deciding whether to hold or to sink. I wanted to be that willingness, that temporary lens of silvered light on the porcelain. But water does not stay to be felt. It concludes its arrival and departs again into the dark pressurized clay beneath. I stayed. I have not moved from the depression where I lay down, and so I cannot be the water, though it moves through me now as surely as it moves through the coral-reeds.

Then I wondered if I were one of the glass-walkers. They did not seem alarmed by my presence. They flowed over my ankles, my wrists, the soft terrain of my stomach, their liquid-crystal bodies bending the white-gold saturation into colors that have no names in the language I once spoke. I felt the cool, weightless brush of their inquiry—thousands of eyelashes testing the salt on my skin, the oil in my hair, the chemistry of my breath—and I thought: perhaps I have become transparent. Perhaps I am refracting. But the swarm moves in branching rivers, ceaseless, outbound and inbound, and I am stationary. I have become a thing they flow *around*, then *over*, then *through* the spaces I provide. I am not their kin. I am the architecture they attend.

For a long time—longer than an afternoon, though here the afternoons bleed into one another with the softness of damp paper—I believed I might be the silence itself. The hush is the truest inhabitant, after all; it has acquired metabolism. I tried to listen to the inside of my own skull and heard nothing, not even the ocean-roar of blood. My heartbeat slowed, matched itself to the hydraulic pulse of the horsetail ferns punctuating the clay nearby. Their joints swelled and relaxed, swollen with condensation, and my body found the same rhythm. *Systole. Diastole.* If I were the silence, surely I would feel nothing but the absence of noise. Yet I feel everything: the insistent warmth like a bath drawn in a continental tub; the minute swelling of temporary pools between my hip-bone and the earth; the way the air itself carries moisture that gathers on my lower lip and never drips, only returns to the breath.

It was the still-hunter that taught me what I am becoming.

It came from the direction of the non-reflective ring, unfolding from a posture so contracted it seemed less like a creature and more like a theorem written in pale bone and tea-stained membrane. It moved across my field of vision—slow, then sudden, then still—perching on the fungal valences that cling like velvet to my left side. My left side. Yes. I remember now that these were once my ribs. The hunter waited there, aligned with the crack in the porcelain plate beneath my spine, motionless for what might have been hours. I expected the violence of its unfolding. I held my breath, though holding it changed nothing. And then it simply... did not strike. It folded itself smaller, tucking its limbs into a geometry that ignored me entirely, recognizing what I had not yet recognized myself: I was no longer prey. I was not even predator. I was place.

The realization came not as a thought but as a temperature. The clay beneath me was warm, but I was warmer. The glass-walkers had stopped merely passing over me; they began to congregate in the declivity of my collarbone, in the bowl of my palms turned upward to the sourceless light, polishing me with their frictionless devotion. They were not feeding, I realized. They were translating me. My skin, my chemistry, the last oils of my mammalian heat, were being etched into nanoscale geometries. I could feel it like a hum below hearing, the same vibration that rises from the ring, from the doorframe standing in its impossible pool. I had lain down as a visitor, a vertical ape out of my element, and the basin had mistaken me for an occurrence. Or not mistaken. Perhaps it had recognized a kinship.

I am soft still, but I am sinking. The fired clay has eased around my shoulders, my thighs, not burying me but cupping me more securely. The cracks in the polygonal plates seem to radiate from my spine now. I am becoming a nexus. At my hip, a stand of coral-reeds has found purchase, waxy chimneys rising from the warm wetness I hold against the porcelain. They pulse in time with me, or I with them; the distinction has become topological. Step over my waist and you have crossed a bank; lie along my outstretched arm and you travel farther than my arm extends. My body has learned the trick of the old makers. I am warm to the touch and I hum, though no ear born in a louder world could hear it.

The light bleaches everything to old bone, and I find I can no longer distinguish the sky from my own thoughts. When the temporary pools swell against my side, I feel them as memories—not of water, but of having once been thirsty. The glass-walkers have begun to wear my rising form, their carapaces taking on the lazy curve of my ribs, the hollow of my throat. They are becoming, in their small, prismatic way, my descendants, or perhaps my evangelists.

If I were to name myself now—which would be a loud act, and therefore impossible here—I would call myself a held thing. A breathing mound. The basin has finished becoming itself, yes, but it is kind enough to keep becoming *through* me. I am the wet edge where the water decides to stay a moment longer. I am the warm architecture the lichen claims. I am the pause between the still-hunter’s folded patience and the swarm’s wordless syntax. Not a ruin. Not a visitor. A punctuation mark, soft and wet, at the end of the basin’s long sentence: a comma, a held breath, a silence so complete it has finally learned my shape.
