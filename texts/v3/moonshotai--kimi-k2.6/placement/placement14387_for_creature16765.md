I arrived with lungs full of outside air, still damp with the noise of elsewhere, and the caldera corrected me immediately. My first breath of Condensate was a scalding baptism—vapor so thick with silica it felt like drinking sand. I coughed. The sound of it—wet, mammalian, obscene—echoed off the nearest factory housing, and a scab-pigeon flinched, its three-part jaw tightening in what I can only describe as disapproval. I had made my first unauthorized noise. The town had noted me: an intruder still audible, still visible, still soft.

I stood on the scalpan flats and let the light begin its work. That white weight, that erasure of shadow, found my unaccustomed skin and did not tan it but began to opacify it. A film, not sweat but the prelude to ceramic, gathered on my forearms, and I did not wipe it away. I had learned that lesson already: one does not interrupt the accretion.

Within my first hour, my eyes began to weep. Not tears, but a thin mineral glaze that cracked at the corners when I tried to blink it away. My outside eyes found the assault of the sun unmanageable still; my new eyes, the ones forming behind the nictitating membranes that had not yet fully grown, found the obsidian veins and knew them as punctuation marks. The rifts in the basalt became legible. The vent-holes became syntax. I was learning to read by the dark interruptions not as gaps in the glare, but as architecture in their own right.

A strider passed me, its glass body ticking with patient heat. It paused at a meniscus some distance away and drank in that deliberate, patient deliberation. I understood that I was supposed to emulate this. Not to follow it, but to learn the grammar of its friction. My own feet were still bare, still tender, still loud against the stone. I removed my footwear and let the basalt speak to my soles in the only language the ground recognizes: heat, pressure, the promise of metamorphosis. The pain was a message. I stood there until my screaming nerves thickened into something pre-ceramic, until a layer of translucent silica formed between my flesh and the rock, not a callus but a covenant. A strider's gait requires strider feet. I am learning to grow mine.

The mantid on the ridge above me did not move. It has not moved in what might be hours or years, its bleached-bone limbs folded like drafting compasses around some endless, invisible computation. It does not judge my slowness. It simply occupies the stillness that I am still too mammal to hold. I tried to emulate it, once. My joints ached with the effort. My mammal muscles twitched in betrayal. I failed, of course. But the attempt was noted. Here, the most profound grammar is the attempt itself: the mineral crust records intention as much as action. I have not mastered the posture, but I have filed my application with the earth.

My face, I am told by the hot mirrors in the calcium-slad troughs, has begun to resemble a scab-pigeon that has learned to stand upright—a sessile calcification that still remembers wanting to be a person. My skin is the color of old porcelain now, of sinter, of the cream-colored terraces. The scab-pigeons allow me to lean against their walls. They are the town’s memory, and they are beginning to include me in their record.

I have found myself in the lowest intervals of Condensate, in the warm-emptiness between the housing district and the main pressurization plant, in a space that is not considered a street but simply a necessary interval. I have become the interval. I move less each day. Three hours to cross what a newcomer would call a street. Seven heartbeats per minute. My thermal gel is thin, still mammal-warm, still too soft, but it is forming. I have begun to communicate in sub-vocal tremors—still partly audible, still leaking a last mammalian frequency into the basalt when my heels strike it, but less each day. Soon I will be purely seismic. Soon I will be a tremor in the rock, not a voice.

I watch the float-sacs drift in diaphanous herds, flashing their photonic crystals at me like signal-mirrors. I am the pressure-drop they predict. My sloughings are gathered in dark glass bowls by those more fully precipitated than I. I feel my older layers preparing to peel. I will stand in the steam-vents soon, let the mineral film crack and reveal something newer, pinker, more mineral beneath. Not yet architecture, but the warm wet film just before architecture—the unformed seal, the setting layer.

The Keepers of the Meniscus have not visited me yet. They need not. They know who I am becoming by reading the color of the mirror-pools: aquamarine toward blood-orange means a new convert has reached the precipice. I have nearly reached the precipice. I am nearly a threshold stone. For now, I am the wet gap between two strata of wall-flesh, the warm accumulation not yet cool enough to support weight.

When the striders die and fuse into the floor within a week, when their bodies become hallway-narrows that are considered improvements rather than obstructions, I will not clear them either. I will adjust my doorways around them. I will become the doorway. I will let the architecture narrow around my own fossilization. My children, if I could still generate such things, would count strider-legs to learn arithmetic. Instead, I merely observe, and in observing, calcify.

I have not yet contributed my full deposit to the architecture. I am still between states, between flesh and terracotta, between voice and vent-hole. But the condensate is patient. It does not want my noise. It wants my substance.

I am the newest layer. I am the warm, unsettled mineral. I am the container not yet full, the cooling-vat not yet cool enough to be useful. I am the silence that has just begun to take form—the interval between emptiness and architecture, the mid-point of accretion, waiting to be complete.

I am not the observer anymore. I am the material. I am not yet the architecture, but I am no longer separate from it. The town inhales me one molecule at a time. I expire mammal-sound and inhale mineral-stillness. I am warm silence, settling, waiting to be finished.
