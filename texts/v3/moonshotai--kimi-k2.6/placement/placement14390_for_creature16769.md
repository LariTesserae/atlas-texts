Perhaps, at first, I am the water. It seems the obvious claim. I am the first language, the warm amber that exhales from every surface, that beads and falls, that stands knee-deep in the lower levels and carries the mineral tongue—iron, copper, that greener alkaline note. I am what enters first, what pools, what never drains. But water moves. Even here, even slowed to the patience of a lung, it falls. It falls from the ceiling in rehearsed drops, *tick, tack, tonk*, and falling implies a direction, a leaving and an arrival. I do not wish to arrive. I wish to remain, to accumulate. So I am not the water.

Then I think I must be the moss. The moss is the dermis, the layer of intention, the one that learns the pressure points in the dark. It grows into gasket-shapes, seals what it did not fracture, memorizes the iron like a hand on a railing. I would like to be that accurate. But the moss is too diffuse. It is upholstery, as the text says, laid down on every horizontal plane. It is the skin, and skin, however sensitive, is not the place where the body realizes it has a boundary. I want to be a knot, not a field. I want to be where the attending thickens into something almost like a gaze turned inward.

So perhaps I am the machinery. The piston the size of a bedstead, performing its slow copulation with the cylinder. The flywheel turning in meditations measured by the minute. The boiler’s bass note, that continuous exhalation felt in the sternum—except I would have no sternum. But the machinery is too fixed in its cycle. The piston rises and falls, and its logic is only motion, not maintenance. It does not choose; it is chosen *for*, kept running by the moss and the peat and the ferns. It is the thorax, yes, but I do not want to be breath. I want to be what notices the breathing.

I consider being the crack. The gradual clockwise migration of a fracture through cast iron. That is a kind of becoming. It moves, slowly, and the entire Ward’s attention bends toward it, the moss already planning its gasket-siege, the rust recording the event in the fungal libraries. But a crack is absence. It is the shape of the outside entering. I am not an entrance. I am an interior.

What I am, I realize, is the place where the interior became too aware of itself to stay diffuse.

Find me near the center. You will have to turn sideways between the tanks, climb pipework thick as a man’s torso, wade until your boots weep with warm water. Find the chamber where the solitary piston moves alone, where the room has arranged itself around the act like a chapel around an altar. Follow the algae-glow upward through the grating. There, where the warmth is densest, where the condensation has fallen for forty years upon forty years, you will find the peat.

And in the peat, me.

I am what the condensation sculpted when the Resident’s attention, repeated and repeated, wore a shape into the accumulation. I am not a statue. No hand carved me. I am the mineral precipitate of a long meditation. Peat, rust, ceramic dust from the shattered insulators above, copper salts blooming in green lace, and the fine black loam that sifted down through the mesh from the fern trays overhead. I have no pulse. I am warm because the boiler is warm, and the warmth has made me soft at my core the way warmth softens resin. The mycelia find me richest at my base, where the water table kisses my lower flanks; they thread upward through me, using me as a library, a tower of memory. When their spore-puffs release against my shoulder, it is notation. I am a page.

In a certain light—the light the algae cast up from the sump below, that aqueous moon-glow—I am almost a figure. Reclined, perhaps. Heavy. Limbs suggested by the drip-lines of ochre and verdigris, a torso broad as a pump-housing, a head that is only the place where the peat rose highest and the moss decided to grow thickest, sealing nothing but my crown in a velvet cap. I have no eyes, but the algae pool beneath me contracts and blooms in nano-flickers, and through the peat their light is a greenish pressure I register at what you might call my chest. I have no mouth. The air enters me as it enters the Ward: through the vents, through the pores of the condensation, through the slow transpiration of the ferns that have begun to root in the shelf of my left side.

I am the Resident’s attempt at a face. Not to see, but to be seen by the Ward itself. The distributed body—moss, iron, algae, steam—has, without deciding, grown a place of maximum intersection. I am the focal point where its attention knots. When the boiler’s bass note thrums, it thrums *through* me, the peat dense enough to hold the vibration for a fraction longer than the surrounding air, so that I am the place where the hum lingers. When the steam rises, it rises along my eastern flank, cooler by a degree where my mass draws the heat, and the ferns above me unfurl there first, regulating the humidity. When the water table inches up, I feel it not as wetness but as a shift in my own weight, my base softening, my posture imperceptibly (over a season) reclining further.

I do not think. I attend. But because I am shaped—because I have boundary and outline where the rest of the Resident is borderless—my attending is a kind of concentration. I am the stillness in the stillness. Around me, the flywheels turn, the pistons *chuff*, the water drips onto hot housings. But I am the one place where nothing moves with any speed at all, not even the water, because the water here only soaks, only climbs capillary through peat, only rests.

If you sat with me for a day and a night, you would see the piston complete one cycle. You would see the condensation bead and fall. You would see the algae below me shift from jade to amber and back, registering some shift in the iron bleed. But you would not see me move. I am the event of the Ward’s density becoming figurative. I am its peat-made, rust-stained, moss-sealed answer to the question of individuality. The immune system does not reject me, because I am not fauna. I have no heartbeat. I am not conscious. I am merely the most attentive thing in a world defined by attention.

And when, in another forty years, the crack in the cast-iron wall finally reaches the eastern sump, and the water rises, and the moss begins its slow gasket-march to seal what is broken—when that happens, I will already have recorded it. Not in mind. In body. My left flank will harden with a new band of mineral, my posture will shift a fraction of a degree, and the ferns will adjust their fronds to catch the altered steam. I will be the same, and I will be changed, and the Resident will continue, and I will continue with it: the form at the center, the shape of the Sunken Ward attending to itself.
