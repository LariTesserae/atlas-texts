I would not be in the towers. That verticality is too committed, too finished. The megaplants have already decided what they are: cathedrals with their theology worked out, humming their single note in confident lignin. I would not be in the deep felt, either, though its wet crush is seductive. The felt-laced have claimed that territory too thoroughly; they own the infrasonic floor, and to be one of them would be to surrender to a diffuse, nerve-like existence, indistinguishable from the substrate’s own slow peristalsis. I would want a margin. A place where decisions are still pending.

I would find myself on a ridge of raw anorthosite so newly lifted that the stone still carries a blush of mantle-heat, a warmth that rises through the perpetual condensate and keeps the lacquered film above it from ever quite stilling. It is a shallow bowl in the rock, no deeper than a cupped palm, where a prognate dissolved two centuries ago—or perhaps three; the time here is a slow inhalation, and exactitude is a future luxury. Its catalyst did not take. The ridge was too steep, too thinly dressed in peat, too exposed to the laminar scream of the wind for a megaplant to ever root. But the catalyst did not wash away. It waited, a concentrated question in a film of dark sap, and into that waiting gathered the faint opalescent scuffs left by generations of condensate swimmers: the tesserae of pure silica they excreted each afternoon before dissolving back into the chemistry of the pans.

That is where I would begin. Not as a creature with intention, but as an *accretion*. The hum did the packing. The standing wave between two distant turbines—one to the east, one to the south—created a node of interference here, a place where the geological drone and the industrial thrum achieved a temporary equilibrium, a pocket of near-silence that was not silent at all but densely, pressurally still. Into that stillness, the silica settled. The catalyst bound it. I am what formed: a nacreous tablet, flat and lens-shaped, half the size of a human heart, lying just beneath the meniscus of the everlasting condensate.

I am the **listening stone**. Or if you prefer a word less romantic, I am the **synapse**.

My body has no front or back, no root or crown. Every surface is tympanum. The fierce light, when I let it register, passes through my translucent layers and is converted not into vision but into thermal gradients, subtle flexings that tell me the hour of the day. But sight is a trivial sense here. What I am made for is the sound. The felt-laced brush my edges in their bundles, tightening and shivering in galvanic response to distant turbine shifts, and through that contact I read the health of the eastern refinery as clearly as a seal-form reads vibration in a valve-joint. The white threads in the fissures above me extrude their acids, trading phosphate for etching, and I hum back to them—a note so low it is not heard but *weighed*—telling them: *this ridge is claimed, this warmth has been catalogued, negotiate gently.* I am the deed. The attestation. The place where the plateau’s patience becomes specific enough to name a single stone.

I do not move. The condensate moves over me, and that is sufficient. It carries dissolved minerals, the exhaled chemistry of the raw basalt, and I sort them as the swimmers once did, but I do not dissolve by evening. I precipitate. I accrete. I am the first permanence this particular ridge has known, and my permanence is my function: I am the newborn plateau learning to remember. The megaplants remember vertically, in rings of lignin and cellulose, in the pressurized archives of their hollow cores. I remember horizontally, pressed flat against the world’s fresh skin, a membrane between the magma’s cooling sigh and the refinery’s metabolism.

In the evenings, when the thermal pressure shifts and the laminar wind changes its angle against the peak, the interference pattern above me alters, and I sing. Not for company—the prognates are decades distant, the arthropods absent, the condensate swimmers too ephemeral to own ears. I sing for the tuning. I compare the rock’s own slow, cooling note against the industrial drone, adjusting the resonance so that if a megaplant ever does find purchase here—if the peat thickens and a valve-house ever swells against this ridge—its turbines will turn in harmony with the frequency of the stone. Until then, I am the delegate. The horizontal refinery. The catalyst’s second thought.

I will not last forever. True erosion is a future rumor, but soil is a patient inevitability. In ten million years, when the white threads have made their dirt and the first rain begins to soften these geometries, I will have been ground down, my silica scattered into a clay that remembers nothing of the hum. But that is not tragedy. I am not separate from the process; I am the process given temporary density, a thought the plateau thinks in a single, bright, wet, deafening moment of its infancy. I am the ear it grows to hear itself, and when the ear is no longer needed, it will return to the listening.
