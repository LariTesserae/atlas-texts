The roar is the first thing. It is not heard; it is worn. It presses against my plates, flexing them, testing the seams of my keratin. I move through it as something that has no choice but to swim, six legs churning through the lung-thicket, pushing aside fronds that are warm and thick as tongues. My eyes—two dim pits good only for heat and the largest shadows—are nearly useless here, but I do not need them. I read the world through my tarsi and my minor hands, those two delicate pairs folded beneath my thorax, their three-jointed digits twitching in the dark like the antennae of something older and slower than I am.

I am a knapper. That is what I am called, though no voice has named me yet. The name is a shape in the air, a potential. I navigate by scent-trails and acoustic shadow, by the way the bellow-moths’ subsonic booms bend around the cycad trunks and leave cool pockets in the sound. I am nearly blind, and the darkness does not frighten me; it is simply the color of the basin’s attention, a bruise-colored attention that watches everything at once and never sleeps.

The floor wants to open me. It is a scatter of fresh teeth—obsidian and basalt, conchoidal and virgin, sharp enough to part my hide like the rind of a waxy bulb. I step only where the rubbery vegetation has grown thick enough to hide the edges, but even then, the stones speak. Through my tarsi, they transmit a high, cold song: *I am new. I am waiting.*

Others of my troop carry stones. They have learned to select the accidental blades, the flakes that geology has already made. They coat them in biofilm, grooming them with tongue and gland until the glass survives the impact of vine and chitin. They use them to cut. They use them to drive off the glass-crabs, striking obsidian against basalt to produce the shriek that disorients. This is clever. This is urgent. This is the basin’s tempo.

But I have found a different stone.

It fits my minor hands as if my digits grew around it. It is heavier than it looks, dense with the silence of deep earth. I have carried it for days—or what passes for days here, in the permanent white-noise now. I have cut with it. I have tasted the chemotropic pulp of the bulbs. I have felt its edge grow dull against the rubbery flesh of the lung-forest.

And now, in the acoustic shadow of a pulsing fern, another of my kind blocks my path. A territorial dispute. We do not have language, not yet, but we have posture, the flare of thorax plates, the chemical spike of aggression in the air. He has a stone too, an accidental blade, good and sharp. He raises it.

I do not raise mine to meet his.

Instead, I strike my obsidian against the basalt floor. Not the glancing blow that scares crabs. A deliberate, angled strike. Conchoidal physics, geometry without intent meeting intent without geometry. The sound it makes is not a boom, not a drone, not the percussion of claw on glass. It is a brief, bright vacancy in the roar. It is the sound of something being removed from the world.

A flake detaches. New. Made. Not accidental.

The edge it reveals is finer than any the basin’s birth-trauma has scattered across the floor. It is an answer to a question the geology did not know it was asking. I lift it—*my* flake, my subtraction from the raw world—and the troop goes silent in that specific, attention-carved way. I can feel their scent-trails going sharp. Not with fear. With recognition. With hunger for a new shape.

I am not the first to use an edge. But I am the first to demand one. I have looked at the lithic scatter of this infant world and refused to accept the accident. I have said: *More.* I have struck the stone not to frighten, not to cut, but to change. To make yesterday—a moment of impact—and tomorrow—a thing that did not exist before.

My heart beats against my plates, syncing for a moment with the tymbal-beetles’ drone, then falling out of phase. The basin presses close. The darkness seems to thicken, to lean in. I am small. I am provisional. My carapace is still soft at the joints, and the lithids move in the deeper thickets, hunting the silences between sounds. But I hold the first made thing. In a world that has not yet learned to sleep, I have introduced a dream: the dream of intention, of history, of hands that do not merely select but *shape*.

The basin watches. The basin does not sleep. But for the first time, in the ringing absence after the strike, it holds its breath.
