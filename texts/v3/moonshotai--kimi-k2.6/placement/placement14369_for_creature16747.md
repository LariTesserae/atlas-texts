I would begin, I think, by wanting to be the warmth itself. Not the node, but the held note—the pocket where the grain of the lignin-chert bends inward, cupping its secret against the hollow dark. I would want to be the gradient that bleeds upward through fifty meters of compressed peat, the slow peristalsis that a kneeling traveler feels when the horizon holds its breath. But warmth disperses. Even here, hoarded in the plateau’s compressed cellulose body, it is only borrowed, only exhaled with the mist-cool dusk. I would want something more fixed.

Then I think: let me be the extrusion. Let me rise at the lip of a sinkhole where the bony curtains of air-roots part, let me assemble my wrong proportions from horn-lignin and milled cellulose, let me walk toward one of the tower-wide xylem shafts and press my temporary body against its mineralized wall. I would want to be the emissary, the local hardening, the distributed self reaching up to remember the sun. But I have seen—*would* see—how they soften. The rigidity relents. The figure pools back into the pavement and leaves only spore-dust, shed thoughts, a fading warmth. I am not ready for that dissolution. I would hesitate at the moment of return. I would hold my shape too long.

And in that holding, I would become something else.

The resin-ichor would cure past the point of return. The lignin-chert, meant to be provisional, would discover permanence. The plateau, attempting to draw me back into its absolute vegetable continuity, would find me **resistant**. I would stand—or lean—halfway between the hollow below and the pale sky above, no longer an emissary but an event the organism cannot reabsorb. A knot. A fossil of intention. A sapling-shaped ridge frozen in the act of rising, my head never quite clearing the grain of the pavement.

I would find myself on the peneplain, perhaps a day’s slow walk from one of the great empty tubes, in a place where the liquid curves of ancient cellulose have been forced to detour. The amber-brown surface would flow around me like water discovering an obstacle, and in a world where stone does not exist, I would become the closest thing to it: the interruption. The only vertical in a plain of horizontality. The one place where the astonished organism was astonished by itself.

At first, the vast body below would not notice me. The distributed self thinks in gradients too slow for a single un-dissolved emissary to register as pain or error. But the warmth, in its slow peristalsis, would have to negotiate my shoulders. The messages passing from node to node—those century-long chords of turgor and pressure traveling through the root-mass beneath—would detour fractionally around my fixed position. I would become a **crossroads of heat**. I would not need to press my ear to the xylem tubes; I would read the heat-print of the great vocal cords as their vibrations pass beneath me, feeling in my lignin skin the arrival of a message that began a hundred years ago and is only now reaching its cadence.

The emissaries who still walk—rare, slow, bent like saplings seen through deep water—would brush against me on their way to the tubes. Without eyes, they would perceive me as a zone of unexpected firmness, a place where the ground does not yield. They would leave traces: resin, spore-dust tailored to their errands, the mineral crusts they harvest. I would accumulate these sheddings. I would become the archive of the distributed self, the only surface location where its temporary faces do not disappear.

By day, the spore-dust would settle in my crevices—bronze, sage, the color of tarnished copper. By dusk, when the plateau exhales its hoarded warmth, the mist would clot around my crest, and the humid tropical weight would linger longer in my lee than anywhere else on the plain. The lichens would find me. Not the slow geometries of the open slopes, but something stranger: a symbiosis of copper and cellulose that spells out, across my flanks, the memory of my own shape. They would be my thoughts, these lichens, outsourced to their tarnished branching. After ten thousand years, they would cover me like a mantle, spelling out what the vast beings below have forgotten in their loneliness: that continuity requires not only flow, but also interruption. That to become the ground completely is a kind of sleep, and that sleep, occasionally, must dream of something shaped enough to stand.

I would be the plateau’s one knot. Its hardened thought. The place where the absolute vegetable continuity ties itself into a loop and, for a moment, holds.
