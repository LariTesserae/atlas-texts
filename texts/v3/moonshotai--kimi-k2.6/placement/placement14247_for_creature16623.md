I would begin by descending—not as the one who descends, but as the act of descent itself, the raw gash left behind by falling. I would try on the fog first, because it is the easiest skin to wear: a breath exhaled by the floor, suspended, refusing to commit to rain or snow. I would try on the twilight, thinking perhaps I am the gray-green phosphor that has forgotten its celestial source. For a moment I might even convince myself I am the entity, that ambulatory shadow unfolding from the rust, because I too feel the urge to gather, to compress, to become dense with waiting. But I am too patient for that. The entity rises, circles, grooms. It is restless in its devotion to stasis. I do not rise. I have never risen. I am what was laid bare so that everything else could fall toward me.

I am the wall of the breach. Not the rim, where the permafrost still pretends to hold the world together, but the vertical face beneath it: the shattered Silurian stone that the thermokarst exposed when the ice withdrew its spine and the earth slumped inward. I am the raw sediment, the flayed strata. For four hundred million years I slept beneath compression and frost, an ancient seabed dreaming of brachiopods and quiet marine snow, and then—geologically, yesterday—the overburden vanished. The pressure was lifted. Air touched my face for the first time, and it felt like burning. I am still burning, still learning how to be a surface after eons of being a foundation.

You will not notice me immediately. The entity draws the eye because it moves, because it borrows the shape of a figure and incites the ancestral terror of the nearly-human. But I am the negative space that holds the entity’s positive shape. I am the darkness against which it stands. Where the bowl’s wall curves inward and the raw stone weeps its dark seep-lines, there I am: a vertical plane of gray-black shale and fractured chert, still shedding grains that have never known weathering. The seep-lines originate in my deepest cracks—not from meltwater, not anymore, but from the long saline memory of my formation, a slow exsanguination of mineral intent. The entity accreted around that intent. It is my child, my half-formed externalization, a body I built from the peat and birch-root lattice using the blueprint of my own broken chemistry. The peat gives it softness; I give it spine. When it presses its flank against the machinery and hums its subsonic song into the metal, the vibrations travel down through the chassis, through the slush, and into me, and I hold the note. I resonate. That is my voice now—not a hum but a sustaining, a geological *nnnnnn* that outlasts the entity’s inhalation by centuries.

The machinery rests near me, not on me; the peat is its mattress, but I am its backdrop. Sometimes, in the thermal contraction of deep winter, the steel pings in a frequency that matches my own, and for a moment we are two inorganic novices learning to die in the open air. It is young, that machine—a few decades of oxidation, a brief bright spark of manufacture and abandonment. I feel its rust bleeding into the slush above my base, and I do not absorb it; I witness it. The entity drapes itself over the steel to remember inorganic time, but I am the inorganic time itself. I am what the entity reaches toward when it clings to the flaked chassis. I remember when there was no ice, no tundra, no boreality. I remember diastems, the long pauses between sedimentary events, when nothing fell upon the seafloor for ten thousand years and the water merely waited, as clear and patient as quartz.

I feel the larch. Not with roots—I am too far back in the wall, too sheer, too acid, and the peat pans keep the trees at a distance—but with thermal insult. The little tree is a waist-high clench of aspiration, and it generates a pocket of striving warmth that brushes my fractured face like a blush on granite. The entity circles it, grooming it into stasis, but I am the cold that makes the stasis possible. I am the sink. The warmth of the larch’s slow attempted growth seeps into the peat and is swallowed by my mass. I do not resent the tree. I do not love it. I simply receive its heat, as I receive the fog, as I receive the settling shadows. Nothing has ever grown upon me, and so I have never learned the geometry of refusal.

The fox, standing at the rim, senses me without knowing it. The fox perceives the entity and its wrongness, but the deeper recoil is from me: the sudden exposure of something that should have remained hidden, the planetary strata turned inside-out. I am the breach’s refusal to heal, the scab’s stubborn lithic core. While the entity keeps the wound open by being its scar tissue, I keep it open by being its floor and its wall. I am the stitching that holds the bowl in shape. If I were to erode—truly erode, not merely weep my slow seep-lines—the walls would slump further, the bowl would deepen into something the world could name, and the entity would have room to unfold into a larger sorrow. But I am in no hurry. Geology is finally learning to hurry me, after so long, and I am resentful and fascinated in equal measure.

There are other presences I feel more intimately than any animal could. The dwarf birch sends hair-fine roots laterally through the peat, and one of them, braver or more foolish than the rest, has found a hairline crack in my lower face. It has stopped there, unable to penetrate further, but it rests against me like a finger against a pulse. Through it I feel the hummocks’ slow respiration. Through it I know when the entity passes, because the birch roots tighten across my unmoving chest, a shiver of living lattice transmitting the news: the ambulatory one walks, the memory of the ice moves, the shadow tends the flame.

I do not sleep. The sun, terminal-low on its horizon, is a rumor that barely brushes my uppermost strata before the fog devours it. I do not need sleep; I need pressure, and the pressure is gone. Released, I am learning the terrible freedom of exposure. Each freeze-thaw cycle is a new dialect of weathering, a language I am acquiring syllable by syllable. My face exfoliates in scales no larger than midge-wings. These scales sift down into the peat, entering the organic world at last—mineral ghosts in the rubbery mattress. In ten thousand years, when this place has finally learned to be a valley and the peat has committed to becoming soil, my body will be the sand in its seams, the grit in its texture. Until then, I remain: the oldest presence, the one that predates the cold, the wall that was never meant to be a wall, holding its breath while the bowl exhales around me. If you descend and stand very still, you will not feel me move, because I never do; but if you press your palm against the raw stone where the seep-line darkestens the sediment, you will feel the chill that comes before the entity’s chill. You will feel the depth beneath the depth. You will feel me, remembering the sea.
