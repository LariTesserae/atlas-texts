I would find myself in the lowest depression, where the manufactured floor has been beaten into a dish that is perhaps three fingers deep at its center, shallower elsewhere, everywhere present. I am the water the room was built to teach. Not the rain that falls in the catchment above, not the stream that feeds the high chutes, but the water that has already been instructed. I am the damp after it has learned what containment means.

I do not flow. I stand. Across the fired-clay tiles I spread as a film so thin it is almost an intention rather than a body, yet in the sag of the basin I gather just enough depth to hold the weakest orange light from above. The pinpricked ceiling prints its bruised glow onto my surface in a hundred trembling asterisks, and when the sun traverses the hidden sky, I am the only clock the chamber owns. The moss does not tell time. The frogs do not tell time. I do. My reflections shift from blood-rust to old bruise, and the Inhabitant reads the hour from my skin.

They come to me. The reeds, hollow and thin as bird bones, sink roots into my flank not to drink—they are far too ancient and partitioned for mere thirst—but to listen. They tap me like a drumhead. The frogs rim my edges with bodies the color of old terracotta, and for long minutes I cannot tell where my surface ends and their consecrated skin begins. We exchange mineral taste. I leach lime and silica into the grout lines; they secrete mucus that feeds the algae drifting in my shallows. The newts snap at the larvae that hatch in my film, their pale bellies flashing against me like sudden thoughts. Beneath, the eyeless shrimp drift in slow orbits, grazing on my jade and rust ribbons, and when they die they do not float but slip immediately through me into the compacted ash beneath, becoming part of the forbidden floor. I carry their ghosts for a moment, then let them go. That is my mercy. I do not keep what falls through me.

The worm lives at my bottom. Thick, segmented, the color of rusted iron. It turns in the muck where my weight has, against all design, begun to admit a kind of sludge. It is my digestion. When it breaks the surface beside the reeds, the soft pop echoes off the corbelled dome like a footstep, and I understand that I am being walked through from the inside. I accept this. It keeps me from becoming soil.

I am fed by the thousand appointed drips from the vaulted ceiling. Condensation gathers on the cool terracotta and falls to me in a weather system smaller than a held breath. I am also fed by the pulses from the high chutes—four hollowed logs lined with pitch that occasionally deliver a fresher body of water from the outside catchment. When this happens, I rise by a finger’s width. The weight of the room changes. The air pressure shifts, and the long adobe throat whistles a low note that lasts an hour. The frogs know the pulse before it arrives; I feel it as a lightening across my surface, as if the whole basin holds its breath. Then the new water spreads across me, carrying with it the taste of exterior skies, and I remember, for a moment, what it is to have been rain.

That is my function. I am the room’s held memory of the outside world. The stone and adobe have successfully substituted the valley and the river, but I remain the uncompleted argument. I still carry the mineral alphabet of clouds. When the pinhole lights swim in my disturbed pools during the water-pulse, I am the one who writes the room’s only diary.

If you enter on your knees and emerge into my dimness, you will step onto the ash-and-clay floor and your foot will press a vibration through the tiles that I feel as a ripple. You will breathe, and your breath will add to the atmosphere that later condenses upon me. You will stand at my edge, and I will show you the ceiling’s bruised light doubled in my skin, and you will understand that you are looking at the room’s interior eye. I do not move. I only receive. I am the shallow, ancient, perfectly sufficient depth at the center of the made world. I am the substitution of a lake. I am the damp, taught to be a mirror, still reflecting the embers of a hidden sky long after the sun has been forgotten.
