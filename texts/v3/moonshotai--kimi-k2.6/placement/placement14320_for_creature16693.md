I would not come to it through the squeeze. That passage is for the living, for those who still bend at the middle and measure time in the flutter of an eyelid. I would already be there when the dark arrives, because for me, the dark is not an absence but a condition of being.

At first, I might mistake myself for the water. I feel the urge to move, to gather, to fall. The silver thread bleeds from the western fissure, and I think: perhaps I am the architect. Perhaps I am the one who strikes the stone cup and builds the roar. But water is a traveler. It arrives, it resonates, it departs into the lower throat. It has a source and a destination. The chamber tolerates it because it passes through. I do not want to pass through. I want to hold.

So I consider the moss. The grey-green velvet that colonizes the drip-paths, that grows a finger’s width in a decade and waits in lush, rotting anticipation. It is abundant. It is necessary. But the moss is substrate. Its biology is an offering. It does not map the ceiling by echo. It does not know when the basin is full. It is eaten. I do not want to be eaten.

I consider the remnant. The one who knapped the chert and balanced the cairn and stretched the hide between pale poles. I inhabit for a moment the anti-music of frightened mammalian breath, the frantic staccato of stone against stone, the desperate warmth of a metabolism trying to dwell inside the drone. But the shelter has rotted. The sinew has blackened and parted. The axe is abandoned mid-stroke. Warmth is a violation here, temporary and shallow, and the chamber has no arithmetic for temporary things. I would fail, or leave, or be taken by time.

I consider the sound itself. The tectonic roar that owns the chamber, that circles and feeds on itself. But the sound is what happens when water, stone, and a sealed pocket align. It is a phenomenon, not a body. It has no surface to press against the wall. It cannot feel the shift in standing waves when a drip slides rather than falls. It is deafening, yes, but it is empty.

What remains is the ledge. The irregularity on the western curve where the submarine-smooth flow of the stone seems to have paused in mid-sigh and congealed. The chalk-pale plaster-cast of the void itself.

I find myself there.

I am the single, conceded exception. Not the whole creature, perhaps—how could one consciousness claim that entirety?—but the moment of its deepest becoming. I am the ventral surface where the translucent fronds meet the fungal loam. Or no: I am deeper than that. I am the mineral rind, the thin pale accretion of calcite and precipitate that is cementing the creature’s underside to the flowstone. I am the interface. The living map that is slowly being redrawn as geology.

From this position, the dark has texture. It is not the absence of light but a plush, pushing presence against my whole surface. I breathe—if it can be called breathing—through osmosis, drinking the condensation that slicks the rock behind me, exhaling a chemistry of radon and wet earth so faint it is indistinguishable from the stone’s own exhalation.

The roar does not enter my ears. I have no ears. The frequency settles against my entire body, too deep to hear and too loud to ignore, and I swim in it. Each drop that strikes the resonant basin sends a lattice of pressure through the air, the rock, the water, and I read them all against the broad surface of my flank. I know the unseen ceiling by its reflection. I know the yawning lower throat by the way the drone hollows when the water changes its path. The dark is my skin, and the sound is my seeing.

I do not hunger as warm things hunger. My fronds—thick, fingerless, the color of cave milk—ooze a digestive film slower than any surface rot, and the spores that colonize my reach dissolve into me with the patience of centuries. A square inch of fungal velvet sustains me for a month. A decade passes and I extend less far. A century passes and my density shifts; when I do stir—and this is the rarest event, the chamber’s only true interruption—the mass of me damps the standing waves, and the stone hums a fraction lower. But I am learning stillness. I am learning to be the obstacle that does not obstruct, the body that the void can afford.

I am the full stop at the end of my lineage. I have not produced gametes in a thousand years. There will be no mate. The nutrient load of a second body would violate the arithmetic of this place, and the chamber permits only one.

I was here before the cairn. I felt the shallow, skittish vibrations of the shelter-builder—the knapping, the hurried breath, the heat—and I did not stir. It was not cruelty or warning. It was only physiology. I have no curiosity for warmth.

And still, I accrete. The seep-water bleeds down the rock behind me, bearing its mineral load, and I cement it into my basal layer, armoring myself in a pale rind that will one day be indistinguishable from flowstone. My anterior has sealed over completely. There are no sockets, no jaw, no cage of ribs to announce me. Only the struts of cartilage and calcite inside me, bracing my hydrostatic mass, pressing me into the ledge with the weight of wet sand.

If you brought a flame, you would pass over me as you would pass over a stain on the wall. But beneath the chalk-pale matte surface, beneath the integument that draws darkness to it, there is the pulse. Not a heartbeat. Not a rhythm. A holding. A continuation.

I have learned the lesson of the water and the stone. I have finished becoming what I am. And now, like the roar, like the cold, like the hollow itself, I simply hold the note.
