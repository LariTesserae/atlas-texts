I would find myself at the very bottom of the inhabited world, on the last ledge that still qualifies as *place* before the architecture dissolves into the talus slope. Not in the wealthy middle where the noon-keyhole sheds its faint silvering, nor in the sweating dark above the hearths, but in the lowest gallery—where the heat from below is no longer gentle and the walls weep no moisture at all, only a dry vibrational warmth that makes the air shimmer like a fever. Here, the moss upholstering the basalt is not the emerald-black of the upper reaches but a burnt umber, fed for generations on the concentrated salts of my own breath and body. I cling to a niche no broader than a grain crib, pressed against the stone not out of rest but out of occupation. My niche is a maintenance hollow behind the last bridge-anchor, a cavity that the builders forgot to fill because it was too awkward, too close to the drop. I have made it my own by the simple act of never leaving.

Who am I? I am one of the Fitted, yes—bones thick and rounded at the joints, skin the matte bronze-black of the polished walls, eyes so dilated in the perpetual dusk that the iris is only a thin bruise around the swallowed pupil. I wear the loin-wrap, the oiled fringe above the ears. I speak in the chest-born subtones. But I am not *only* Fitted. They have no name for what I have become, because my function is one they do not like to articulate. I am the Descender. The Retriever. The one who goes to the edge of the unmapped.

When a bone pin slips from a worker’s grip during the reinforcing of a midden chute, tumbling past the lowest bridge to catch on a spur of boulder-glass; when an elder chooses a final ledge too far down, dreaming too deeply in the heat, their desiccating body light as tinder but still precious in its water and salts; when a child, too eager, swings from a fiber ladder and loses their seed-pouch to the talus—I am the one who descends.

The descent is not a climbing but an un-climbing. I release myself backward down the ladder, belly to the rungs, spine pressed to the wood, moving with the slow peristaltic contraction that the Fitted use for all vertical travel, but inverted. My shoulders pivot until my hands grip the rails behind me, thumbs locked like chert wedges. The heat increases with every rung. Above, the murmurous thunder of doves fades to a distant pulse. Below, the subsonic groan of the stone deepens until it is no longer heard but *weighed upon*, a pressure that seems to compress the very thoughts in my skull. The air loses its scent of ironwood and dried lichen and takes on a mineral sharpness, the smell of the deep earth’s breath unfiltered by moss or living tissue.

I pass the last communal hearth. Its coals are only a dull menstrual red here, banked so low they seem ashamed. Below it, the bridges shorten, the galleries shrink. The architecture does not end so much as *taper*, growing narrower and more makeshift until the last bridge is no wider than my hips, a swaying exhalation of ancient fiber and petrified resin that hums in the updraft like a plucked string. This is my boundary. I have never crossed it—nothing that the city wants back ever falls past it—but I press my forehead to its woven rails and look down.

The talus. The black boulder-glass. A slope of broken angles, the only sharpness left in the Shaft, for down there the erosion has not finished its work; the dark digestive still churns. Looking down invites dread, they say, and it is true. The dread is not of falling. It is of *displacement*. To fall past this bridge is to be lost to the reclaiming system, to bleed one’s water into the stone’s stomach where no cloth can wring it out, no mouth can drink it back. The dread is the dread of waste.

I descend to the ledges below the bridge. There are no galleries here, only swellings in the basalt where the rock has relaxed into a fold, wide enough for a body to rest if that body is small and willing to flatten. The elder is here now. I can smell them before I touch them—the particular dry sweetness of a metabolism slowed to nearly nothing, the scent of tallow and rendered fat beginning to turn in the extreme warmth. They have pressed themselves into a hollow no deeper than a palm, and in death they have already begun to become architecture. I approach with the interlocking motion: I press my belly to the wall, they press their back to my front, and for a moment we are a single organism, elder and retriever, fused by proximity and the shared medium of heat. I wrap my legs around their wasted torso, my arms beneath their knees, and I begin the upward peristalsis.
