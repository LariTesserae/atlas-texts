At first I thought I might arrive as something winged—one of the great pale moths, ash-colored and indolent, drifting through the twilight on currents I could not see but only feel as shifts in pressure against my powdery back. I liked the idea of carrying spores from bracket to bracket, leaving silver trails on iron, being a messenger without a message. But the basin does not need another traveler. It needs a holder.

Then I considered the Renderers, those slow priests of the crucible, hands stained with mineral memory, their words arriving pre-eroded by tectonic time. I imagined descending to the Works where the billion-year-old stone refuses to melt and instead recalls shapes older than the mold. I wanted to be the one who listens, who reaches for a cup and leaves an afterimage of my fingers in the condensation. But the stone, I realized, already has listeners. It did not need another. It needed something softer.

I considered becoming one of the children, the ones who know the town is not confused but folded, who clap their hands in the dense air so the echo arrives before the sound. But childhood is too quick for this place. The basin would digest that speed and turn it into something patient.

So I settled.

Not with intention. With gravity. With the same downward drift that pulls the mist from the soft-shouldered rims into the clustered hab-levels. I found my boots becoming felt-soled, my gait sliding into the shuffling, frictionless walk of the Vârşani, my ribcage broadening to sip the thick, metallic air. I spoke in chest-tones. I learned the acoustic etiquette of the stone. My skin steeped to the color of moss-shadow, and my hair grew thick with a lanolin sheen, as if my body had begun to agree with the damp that it should no longer try to sweat, only to absorb.

For a time, I believed I was a sump-watcher.

There is a level beneath the lower factories, drowned every few seasons when the ancient aquifers turn and burp upward through fissures invisible to surveyors. The water pools in a cavern that was a mine, then a cellar, then a foundation, then a sump. I was given an iron ladder to descend, a hooded sodium lamp that glowed like an ember underwater, and a duty to watch the intake pumps. I tasted the gradient where warm mineral seep met cold condensation. I pressed my forehead to the damp brick and felt the slow chemical drumming of the mycelial mats, learning the administrative syntax—nitrogen here, etching acid there, a request for more ore-dust slurry in the eastern wall.

But I did not stay the watcher.

The damp, you see, does not merely surround you in Vârşa. It negotiates. It beads on iron, it seeps through felt, it migrates into the pores of anything that holds warmth. And I held warmth. The basin needed a new organ. The stone had said all it had to say through the Renderers. The fungal mats drummed their governance through every load-bearing wall. What was missing was a membrane. A surface. Something still enough to let the basin see itself in its own shallow breath.

I kept descending. Or rather, the floor of the sump kept rising, not in elevation but in density, in intimacy, until my boots were always underwater and my shuffling gait became a kind of slow floating, a near-immersion in the mineral-heavy dark. I stopped drying. Water did not bead on my skin anymore; my skin *was* the beading. My lungs, already heavy from altitude, learned a new rhythm—not breathing, but permeation. The air and the water and the damp cool of the billion-year-old stone achieved equilibrium in my chest.

And then I understood where I had found myself.

I am at the bottom of the seventh drowned level, in the pool that the pumps have forgotten to drink. The water here is not bitter or urgent. It is lukewarm, patient, the color of the sky this basin has never seen. I do not stand in it. I do not sit beside it. I have become its meniscus. I am the stretched surface tension, the skin between the warm aquifer below and the sodium pall above. The Vârşani come to me—not often; I am a low and neglected place—and they lean over the iron rim to check the water level, and in me they see their silvered faces rendered in stillness. I hold them. I hold their hollows and their exhaustions. The mineral scale that filmed the lamp-panes in the Works above has filmed my vision, too, so that I see them through an oyster-grey kindness, a bruised and edgeless mercy.

The children understand. They come to the sump’s edge and peer in, their folded knowing untroubled by what bobs just beneath the silvered reflection. They nod. I do not nod back. I ripple, very slightly, in a current that has no source but the stone’s last exhalation.

The mycelial mats have found me. They have extended their hyphae through the sump’s walls and into the fluid geometry of my presence. They do not speak to me in words. They speak in gradients of warmth, in the slow drift of spores that settle through my body like dust through a sunbeam. I translate now. Not for humans—humans have stopped coming so often—but for the water itself, which must migrate from vent to sump to cloud to fissure to root, and which needs, at certain moments, a stillness capable of knowing its own name.

I dream in seep. My thoughts do not race forward in narrative; they accumulate, bead, migrate upward through capillary attention. I feel the tick of cooling girders in the Works three levels above me as if they were the settling of my own teeth. The *pip-pip-pip* of condensate falling into abandoned iron drums arrives in my body not as sound but as pressure, a gentle percussion on the inside of my skin.

I am not dead. I am not alive. I am in the settled phase.

The bracket-shelves are the elders of the warm cores, and I am their counterpart in the drowned dark. Where they record pressure cycles in rose-gold and violet wood-flesh, I record the softer pressures: the footstep that never arrives, the breath held too long in the dense air, the child’s echo that precedes her. I am the Vârşa Basin’s attempt to grow an eye in the water. I am the damp that, having once learned to walk upright, decided to lie back down again and remember, through capillary stillness, everything the stone has drowned.
