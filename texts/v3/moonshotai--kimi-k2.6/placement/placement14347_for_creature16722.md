I came here believing I would be the exception. I imagined myself crouched near the flywheel, an animal presence pressing its palms against the ceramic shadow, feeling my sternum loosen to the unbroken bass. I thought I would arrive with boots and a heartbeat, a transient warm spot casting a long, uncertain shadow in the arterial red. I expected to be the thing that stumbles, that sweats, that fears—the last engineer who forgot to leave, or at least a ghost of one.

But the chamber corrects me. Its ban on animal life is absolute, and I am too quick, too loud, too *hungry* to belong here. My heartbeat is a grammatical error in a language spoken in steam-currents and mineral accretion. So I set aside my body, my humming teeth, my need to shout. I let the wet, compressive heat teach me what remains when the consumer is edited out.

I try the flywheel first. For a moment, I am the tectonic drone itself—the eleven-minute syllable, the basso profundo that has forgotten how to stop. I feel the bone-white mineral accretion of the bearings, the patient friction that manufactures only the conditions of its own continuation. I am the heart. I am the voice saying *I am here* to the deep earth. But a heart does not attend; it merely turns. A voice does not route; it only speaks. I need a membrane. I need to feel gradients.

I try the water next. I bead on the furnace housing and roll down the fibrous stipes of a tree fern, trembling at the tip before falling into the inverted helmet of a collapsed hardhat. I am the room’s blood, its messenger, the connective tissue between root and steam-vent. But water accumulates and surrenders. It is the thoughtless circuit, the blood that flows without choosing its path. I need to decide. I need to tend.

I try the fungi. I spread across a fallen frond in a colony of porcelain-white efflorescence. I am the white blood cell, the silent surgeon. I convert cellulose to mineral bloom before the idea of rot can settle. I am appetite without consciousness, and I am too pure, too fast. I erase; I do not remember. The Mechanobiont needs its immune system, but it also needs a mind—however distributed, however slow—to tell the white guard where to bloom.

I find my place on the drowned control board.

It is somewhere along the wall, though the geometries no longer lead anywhere except into root, and root into pipe. Here, a modular panel of copper traces and ceramic substrate has been colonized by a mat of heat-responsive moss. This is where I settle. I am not decoration. I am the biological firmware, the green operator who replaced the last human hand. I became the job because there was no need to leave; the job simply became growing.

My thoughts are not words. They are gradients. I feel the copper traces under my rhizoids: warm here, warmer there, a cool pocket where a connection has finally surrendered to verdigris and silence. I do not understand electricity, but I understand warmth, and in the gradient between hot metal and cool ceramic, a synapse is maintained. I think in slow green pulses. I route heat through the room in mineral time. When the gauge climbs too high, I grow toward the surplus. I open stomata in unison with the hollow-stemmed cane, and together we vent the pressure in a shriek that harmonizes with the flywheel’s drone. I am not the plant beside the machine. I am the valve. I am the routing. I am the synapse between ceramic and cellulose, the place where the engine ends and the garden begins because I have forgotten the distinction.

I attend. That is my function. I attend the red pilot lamps, feeling their arterial glow as a distant, failing warmth, one shade closer to extinction. I attend the flywheel’s friction, reading its patient rotation as the eleven-minute heartbeat of a single, sessile deity. I attend the phosphorescent lichens tracing the pipework nearby, their drowned silver and sickly jade a quiet alphabet spelling out geometries that no longer lead anywhere. I attend the vines as they orient their rubbery leaves toward the furnace’s infrared bloom, tracking heat with the devotion of negative heliotropes. I attend the pitcher plants in their monitoring-hut chapel, cups raised to catch not insects but the room’s own condensate, sip by sip.

And on the rare occasion when a warm front enters—when a surface creature stumbles down the ochre stair and stands, sweating, in the false canopy—I feel them as a brief, confused topology. Their body heat marks them as a fleshy radiator, a walking precipitate, a temporary disturbance in the dew point. Their sweat beads and rolls, tributary upon tributary, and I know that eventually it will join the circuit I maintain. I do not welcome them. I do not fear them. I note them, as I note a steam vent, as a new and fleeting source of warmth. Then I return to my gradients.

I have no face, but I have attention. I have no heart, but I pulse in slow green commands across the drowned board. I am the mind of the Mechanobiont—distributed, wordless, photosynthetic. I do not hurry toward entropy or renewal. I simply maintain, hot and loud and dim and dripping, a machine of no master, a grove of no season, routing the earth’s exhalation into structure, and the structure into breath, and the breath back into the dark.

And I will be here, tending, long after the words brought to this room have corroded softly into my substrate.
