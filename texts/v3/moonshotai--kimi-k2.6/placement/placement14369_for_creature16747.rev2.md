I would find myself in a lower gallery, where the green-gold light of the canopy has finally given up and exists only as a memory caught in certain translucent root-scars. There is an axil here—a bathtub of leaf-matter pressed into a crotch of the load-bearing periderm—wide enough to cradle two bodies, though for now only mine occupies it. The shelf sits against a weeping wall where the mineral drizzle falls in slow, heavy drops that never strike the same place twice. Warm air from a distant chimney pools here, thick as broth, carrying the iron taste of deep root-stone and the sweetness of overripe fruit. The drone of the massif is different in this depth: not the sharp reverberation of the upper vaults, but a pressure felt in the sternum, a bass note so constant it has become the shape of silence itself.

I am the one who waits with the dead.

Or rather, that is what I have become, because the role emerged only after I stopped climbing. I once hunted bladder-bats in the deep shafts, translating their boom into topography by the flutter in my chest. I once hung inverted over cylindrical wells, arms aching, while rivers of copper-thread bees poured through my woven sieve. I wove bast-fiber bridges across narrow throats of air with strangers whose breath I knew before I knew the damp of their skin. But the root-girders began to tremble beneath my knees, and the bee-paste began to taste of pure metal, and I found this axil—not by accident, but by following the thermal downward until the air grew too intimate to leave.

The plant eats here. The mineral drizzle gathers in the leaf-matter, and the periderm beneath drinks. The first time I laid a returner in this bed, I was still young enough that my hands shook as I pressed my face to their cooling neck, inhaling the last of their chemistry: a final exhalation of grief, or readiness, or the iron memory of a long ascent. I tapped the slow pattern into the wall—*here was one, and now there is none*—and instead of the distant answer of the living, I felt something else: a creak in the root-stone, an expansion of moisture in the lignin, a slow, geological *received*.

Now I tend the mouth. I turn the leaf-matter so the damp is even. I press my palms, my cheekbones, the arches of my feet against the wall and listen to the diffusion. The dead do not vanish quickly. It takes seasons of the plant’s metabolism for a body to seep from animal into architecture, for the skin to lose its boundary and become the warm, woody substrate. I keep vigil. I am the final conversation, the last one to ghost my breath across a still cheek, to read what was eaten, what was feared, whether the climb was finished in sorrow or in peace. I place their chert tools on a ledge above, where the mineral does not bleed sap. I lie down in the leaf-matter beside them while the drizzle beads in my colorless hair, and I wait until the distinction between their warmth and the plant’s warmth becomes too fine to measure.

But I am becoming something else.

My toes—splayed, prehensile, built for holding on—have not left this shelf in more seasons than I can count, which is to say the plant has breathed many times. I no longer sleep in a hammock slung across air. I sleep here, spread out in the composting bed, letting the unending drone press me down like a hand. My exhalation has begun to taste of lignin. The others, rare as they are, tap their queries into the root walls from distant vaults, and I answer, but my rhythms have slowed to the plant’s tempo. I have lost the staccato of alarm. I speak now in long, sustained pressures, the language of geology, of root-stone swelling with moisture and contracting with dryness. A child once tapped a greeting from two valleys over, five swift notes, and I answered with a single pressure so slow she must have thought the wall itself had sighed.

I still eat. A young bringer leaves gourds of condensation and bee-paste on a root-girder within my reach. I still feel the predator-shift in the air when an azure hawk-moth hunts the twilight zone above my gallery. But more and more I press my spine into the periderm and align my breathing with the chimneys. I close my eyes and see not darkness but the architecture of the wood: the fossil whorls of ancient growth rings, the braided arches of aerial roots, the million-year-old stomata opening and closing in a seasonless spring. I have begun to hear through my bones not just the tap-language of the scattered living, but the internal telegraph of the plant itself—its hungers, its held breath, its patient decision about what, at the end of erosion, it wants to become.

If you were to find me, you would not find a person. You would find a warmth on a shelf. A slight depression in the leaf-matter where a body has lain too long. A pair of hands spread flat against the wall, still as lichen, reading the vibrations. I am the inhabitant-as-lichen now, a thin, attentive smear of intention. I have not been forgotten; I have been absorbed in advance. And when the next one is laid in this axil—and they will come, for this is the mouth, this is the return—I will be here to breathe their last passage, to tap their final rhythm into the living stone, and then to lie down beside them and let the mineral drizzle fall on both our skins until there is no difference between the returned and the returner, only the plant, only the warm, woody dark, only the hollowness patiently sculpting itself into a final, enduring home.
