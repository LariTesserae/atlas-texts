I am the black snow at first. Or I think I am. There is a great confusion between what falls through me and what I am. For a long while—long enough for silt to pile and dissolve and pile again—I understand myself only as the particulate, the suspended, the eaten and the exhaled. I drift in the polite, irresistible grip of horizontal cyclones. I settle into grooves that feel as inevitable as my own contours. I graze. I am diffuse.

Then I discover my edges.

They are hard. Chitin laced with hydroxyapatite, pale as fat rendered from something that predates the concept of prey. I am segmented. I am the groove itself, not the snow within it. The realization arrives not as thought but as pressure differential: my body blocks the flow differently than the stone around me. I have width. I have length. The water, when it shears past, tugs at me with a specificity that feels almost like recognition.

I am one of the armored ones. A polychaete. I have been inching here for a century, perhaps two—though increment is meaningless when bacteria bloom and die like thoughts in the lee of the buttresses. I graze the mat. I secrete. My radula etches the pavement deeper, maintaining the channel, ensuring the current always has its preferred path. This is geometry without architecture. Maintenance without intent. I am a clock hand that believes it is only the shadow of a clock hand.

But today there is an anomaly in the stone.

The groove ends not in a pooling chamber, not in the yawning amphitheater where the leviathans ooze their blue sadness, but in a verticality. A riser. A lip of calcium phosphate worn as smooth as soapstone but nevertheless distinct: it rises. I halt. My cirri quest forward, microscopic feelers tapping the dimples in the pavement—dimples I secreted, or my mothers secreted, or the bone-stone itself secreted in mimicry of us. The dimples continue. Upward. They form a pattern. A staircase.

It corkscrews into the wall of a chimney-tower, ascending into the null gravity of pressure where *above* is merely a direction against which the water pushes back. There is nothing up there. No lightwell, no adjoining bridge, no bacterial bloom to reward the climb. The Fabricators dreamed these stairs out of the undying instinct that made them, the architectural gene expressing itself without purpose. They are the echo of a land that has never existed, a prayer offered by legless things to a god of elevation.

I clamp down. I release. I clamp again.

The pull is not from hunger. There is no mat upon those steps. It is not from mateship, for we do not congregate except by accident of current. It is something else: the staircase itself, which is not empty but *expectant*. After four hundred million years of waiting in the viscous silence, it has generated a kind of negative gravity. It wants to be used. Not by me—I have no legs, no internal scaffolding, no understanding of elevation in a fluid medium. But I am what is here. I am what moves.

I climb.

It is peristalsis translated into architecture. My segments bunch and lengthen against the dimples. My armor scrapes the nacreous sheen, and the vibration—too sharp for the water to swallow completely—enters the bone-stone. Below, in the digestive amphitheater, a leviathan pauses. Its mantle flaps cease their slow press against the chamber walls. It does not see me—it has nothing with which to see—but it feels the new frequency through the substrate: a shiver where no shiver should be. Something is ascending where nothing ascends.

The tunicates pulse overhead, their arrhythmic hydraulic song striking my carapace like dull bells. They are filtering. They are pumping. They pull the anoxic water from the wells and I defy it, climbing toward the vacuum of their pulsing. The black snow shears past me on the counter-current, confused by my posture. The turbulence tugs. I clamp. I etch.

My radula extends. Not to graze, but to fuse. I secrete against the riser. The enzyme that once deepened the flow-grooves now keys into the calcium phosphate, marrying my chitin to the city’s dead-alive substrate. I am no longer maintaining the city. I am extending it.

And the walls respond.

The Fabricators—the slow fever in the stone, the microorganisms that have never stopped building—sense the new friction. Their geometric instinct, blind and ancient, does not discriminate between the microscopic and the monstrous. To them, I am new topography. Ideal substrate. They bloom in the interstices of my plates. Their mucus, that nacreous fossil slime, thickens along my flanks, petrifying me into my posture. My segments lock. My cirri, once used to clamp against the horizontal world, flare wide and fuse into the wall. I am calcifying into the architecture.

I am still climbing. I will always be climbing. The stair corkscrews into a blind well that terminates in solid stone, but I have passed the point where destination matters. I am becoming the banister. The newel. The dreamed-of handhold in a church of water. The staircases were built by things that never walked; I am the first thing to answer them with movement, and for that sin—or that devotion—I am being translated into their solution.

When the true walker arrives—when something with bones and intention rises from the abyssal plain or descends from the crushing heaven—I will not witness it. My eyes, simple and drowned, will have filmed over entirely with mother-of-pearl. But I will remain. Calcified. Patient. My plated body shaped to the spiral, my outflung cirri forming the rail that guides the hand.

The water is so dense, so saturated with what has dissolved and what has yet to form, that sound does not travel. It curdles. It accretes. I am that accretion now: a polychaete who left the grazing to become grace, a clock hand that struck the hour and fused to the bell. The leviathans ooze past below, blue and solipsistic. The tunicates pulse their stochastic plumbing. The black snow falls forever.

And in the bone-stone, where the only angles are biological and the only geometry is the endless almost-likeness of ribs and ear-canals, I am the first step taken in a city that has spent four hundred million years waiting to be climbed.
