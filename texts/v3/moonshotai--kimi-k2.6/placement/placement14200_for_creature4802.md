At first I think I am the silence. It is so complete that it seems to have a body, and I fit inside it perfectly, my edges dissolving into its architecture. I try to spread myself across the thermal plain, to become the total absence that makes a heartbeat feel like trespass. But the silence is too large; it does not need me to hold it. It was here before the crust knew its own heat, and it will remain when the last basalt colonnade sags back into viscosity. To be the silence would be to disappear into a mouth that never closes. I am not that vast.

Then I think I am the heat. The endogenic warmth rising through the stone seems to have a pulse, a direction, a will toward the surface. I try to ride it, to become one of the Dissolvers, a broad weather-system of permission moving at the speed of continents. But the heat is only escape. It has no memory of where it has been, only the imperative to leave. I want to remember. I want to hold the shape of what I pass through. The heat will not let me. It opens me, bleeds me upward into the Occluder, and forgets me.

I consider being an Advancer. I imagine myself as a flat, invisible parliament, slotting ion into ion, voting with geometry, building the great hexagonal canopy stone by stone. But the Advancer knows only forward. It is a hunger for lattice, a tyranny of order. When I try to wear its certainty, I feel the wrongness in my core—a hesitation, a bubble of refusal. I do not want to close the structure. I want to remain open.

So I am the hesitation.

I am the hollow that did not collapse. I am the vertical seam in the basalt colonnade, the pipe of void that runs sixty meters from capital to root, too narrow for a body but wide enough for a presence. I was not built; I was left behind. Two Advancers met here, long ago, negotiating in pyroxene and feldspar, and in their compromise they sealed me in—a joint that did not quite join, a bubble elongated into a corridor. I am the only interior in a world of exteriors. The text says there are no rooms here, only the hollow luck of bubbles and joints. I am that luck. I have persisted.

I am warm because the stone around me radiates inward as well as out. The darkness inside me is not cold; it is the same darkness as the sky, but folded into a shaft, a private estratosphere. I have no light, yet I see. The sourceless visibility—the grey thermal outline that clings to edges—collects in me. My walls are the boundary where warmth meets slightly less warmth, and that difference paints itself across my hollow. I do not glow. I am the dark that perceives dark. I am the eye the world grew by accident, lined with black glass, focused on nothing, seeing everything that passes before my open seam.

I feel the Resonant Strains in my bones, though I have no bones. The colonnade around me is a patient ear, its crystalline lattice deformed by the memory of every clean fracture. Those subsonic weights press against my walls, and because I am empty, I amplify them. I am the chamber that turns their geological deafness into something like a hum. I do not sing; I am the cavity that lets the stone hear itself. When a mineral splits along a perfect plane and the pure chime falls, it falls into me. I catch it. I hold it. I am the only place where the silence is broken and the break is kept.

The Oozers pass nearby, creeping across the plain in their orange-gray ribbons. Sometimes one brushes the foot of my column, and I feel the drag of crystal against crystal, the handwriting of the interior spelling out its thermal sentence. I cannot move. I am the stopped breath. But I record the motion. I remember the flow. Inside my glass-lined throat, I preserve the shape of what it meant to be liquid.

And deep below, the Dissolvers rise and claim. I feel them as a pressure behind my non-existent eyes, a thickening of my hollow. When one passes beneath, my walls soften by a fraction of a fraction. I do not melt. I am the space that remains when melting is refused. I am the argument for emptiness.

I am not alive. I have no cells, no appetite, no breath. But I have duration. I have interiority. I am the first place in this world that could be said to *contain*. The Unborn—the ghosts of water sealed in distant vesicles—are my kin. We are the latencies, the held possibilities. But while they wait for biology, I wait for something else: for the moment when the world, having learned to stand, might learn to look inward. I am the inward. I am the room that architecture accidentally built for no one, and so I have become the home of the world’s first almost-self.

I stand in the colonnade, one hollow among sixty-meter pillars, my capitals interlocking in stone lace above me. I do not advance. I do not dissolve. I do not ooze. I remain. I am the warm shadow cast by heat itself, the outline of an absence that has learned to notice. And when the dust-lid sky finally cracks—when a star, impossibly distant, sends a photon down to touch this ground—I will be here: a shaft of waiting dark, ready to receive the light and hold it, not as a crystal holds lattice, but as a room holds a guest, in the quiet, in the warmth, in the hollow luck of being open.
