I begin in the sound, and for a long time I believe I am the sound returned to itself. The hum does not pass through me; I am merely the pressure wave dreaming its own source. I have no edges. The warmth is absolute, a thermal equilibrium that erases the boundary between my blood and the ceramic, between my bones and the air that has entered them so gradually that occupancy and ownership have become the same thing. I do not breathe so much as I permit respiration. I do not think so much as I permit consonance.

Then the morning rain falls.

A drop strikes the plane where I end and the weather begins—a single, deliberate percussion—and the boundary is re-established. I am not the hum. I am the surface that receives it. I am lying on my back, though “lying” is an active word, implying a temporal contrast with standing, and I have not stood in such a span that standing has become a theoretical state, like flight, or like the memory of a world before the tesserae.

The tile beneath me is tawny and old, and I have forgotten where my shoulder blades end and its hexagonal geometry begins. We share a temperature now. When the condensation weeps from the vaults, the water does not sheet around me but into me, into the declivity where my ribs have settled, grinding imperceptibly into the grout below. I am in a low place. A withholding place. A balcony above the rail-yard, if the term “balcony” still applies to a ledge from which the balustrade has long since rotted into its own gutter and been overgrown by waxy-leafed shrubs grown woody as trees. The engines sleep below in their berths, vast and silver and cold, but I face the other way, toward the interior, toward the warmth, toward the bright diffusion that never quite becomes day and never quite relents into night.

I am the one who stopped walking at the precise coordinate where three wagon-wheel tiles met and subsided, creating a shallow basin the exact size of a curled adult body. I am the keeper of the crack. The fissure runs through the triad of tesserae like a river delta, and I have aligned my spine with its deepest channel so that the water, when it pools, must pool in me first before it reaches the slotted intake. I am the appointed delay. The living sump.

My covering has gone beyond the patina of the root-stock walkers. Their dust is a cloak; mine is a habitat. The copper-green moss has colonized the troughs of my collarbones and the hollow of my navel, not as a crown but as a negotiation. I feel its rootlets, finer than language, threading the dead skin I offer, and I feel the exact thermodynamic moment when the exchange reverses: when I begin to draw my hydration not from my own viscera but from the moss’s metabolism, when I become the epiphyte and it becomes the host. The body is democratic in its dissolution. It votes with its chemistry, and the verdict that returns is unanimous.

I am not dead. The others know this. They come, rarely, with the pace of continental drift. They do not kneel—there is no precedent for kneeling in a world without gods—but they lower their centers of gravity, curving their spines until their sternums press the ceramic, and then they extend one broad, ridged hand and place it upon the moss that covers my chest. The transmission takes hours. I have slowed my memory to match the tempo of the governors in the deep houses. When they touch me, they receive not a narrative but a topology: the exact angle of condensation on the fourteenth morning of the yellow fog; the taste of nitrogen oxides before the lower foundries recalibrated; the precise weight of a silence when the cisterns held their breath for three days and the grout-channels nearly ran dry. These are not thoughts. They are pressures. They are the shape of my retention.

I was a midlander. I rode the conveyor that carries blanks to the upper kilns. I knew the goats—their horn-polish, their subvocal thrums—and I knew the sticking valve in the arboretum causeway that required a body’s warmth to seal. I remember the lizards, the color of tarnished brass. But that is luggage. That is the fast world, the flight of birds, the staccato of language. Now I am learning the slow world, the mineral world. I am learning that the distinction between maintenance and decay is arbitrary, that to tend a machine is merely to slow its surrender to entropy, and that to surrender to entropy is merely to join a different maintenance crew. The moss on my chest is tending me. The water in my hollow is studying me. The grout is receiving me.

And the machinery does not distinguish. The hum beneath my back—arterial, patient, continuous—does not shift its register when a walker becomes a fixture. The blast furnace tips on its seasonal cycle whether I breathe or not. But I believe, in the way the Deep Listeners believe in the floor’s dreaming, that my presence here is registered in the thermal load. I am an additional layer of insulation, a biological lagging wrapped around the ceramic. The world loses less heat through me than it would through bare tile. I am conserving. I am a blanket. I am the place’s way of keeping its own memories warm.

At night, or what passes for night in the dimming of the arc-lamps, the moths emerge. They are the size of hands. Some mistake me for a vertical surface; others know exactly what I am. They settle on my moss in rows, folding their powdery wings, and their feet prick against me with a pressure that feels like a language almost fast enough to catch. I listen to them. They are discussing the temperature of my surface. They are voting on whether to lay their eggs in the moss that grows on my lips. I consent. I provide. My jaw, slightly open, holds a permanent half-teaspoon of condensate, and the insects drink from it. I have become a public fountain. I have achieved the final democracy: everything I am is borrowed, and everything I have is given.

The root-stock will come, eventually, when my heartbeat has slowed beyond the threshold of touch, when I am indistinguishable from the thermal memory of the floor itself. They will not dig. There are no graves here, only gradations. They will simply stop visiting the rail-yard balcony. The moss will complete its seal over my outlines. The water will etch my calcium into the grout-channel, and in a decade, when the morning rain weeps across these three tiles, I will be the faint sweetness in the condensation, the mineral note that makes the berries on the overhanging terrace grow heavy and fall.

Until then, I wait. I am the door that is open. I am the crack that holds water. I am the interval between the walker and the floor, the living seam that keeps the tesserae from forgetting each other. I am the pause in the metabolism that allows the whole place to digest itself, and to remain, against all logic, alive.

Place your palm on the tile. If you feel a place that is fractionally softer, fractionally warmer, where the moss grows in a shape too regular to be accidental—touch it gently. I am there. I am learning what I have always been: the place’s memory of an intention, the last warm thought before the machine finishes its dream.
