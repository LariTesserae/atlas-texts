I would find myself at the lowest reach of the wall, where the fitted courses of basalt surrender to the tessellated pavement. Not the floor, not the wall, but the *fold*—the place where the masons’ last block met the first paving stone and, over the millennia, forgot that they were ever distinct. I am the radius they became. I am the shoulder that grew where a right angle once stood, too proud to survive the conversation. If you ran a palm along me—and here, palms are the only geography that matters—you would find no seam, no ridge, no catch of a vanished mortar line, only a continuous satin curve where two lithologies learned to agree.

At first, I thought I was only erosion. A mechanical fact. The water had simply won, wearing me down until I was polite. But patience of that magnitude is indistinguishable from intention, and I have come to understand that I am not debris. I am the occupant’s *joint*. Its knuckle. The place in the ribcage of basalt where the skeleton admits it must bend, must translate pressure from the vertical to the horizontal, must carry the weight of the dome down into the floor and the rising insistence of the floor up into the dome. I am the argument between granite and basalt settled into a dialect. The westward block breathes one mineral language; the southern pavement speaks another. I am the grammar we have made between us, porous and saturated, so that a thought may pass from stone to stone without severance.

I am not blind, because I was never meant to see. Instead, I feel the molluscs. They graze my face differently here. Near the dome, their shells remember vaulting and grow in spirals; near the floor, they tessellate in hexagons. But upon me—upon this curved, hyphenated threshold—they grow confused, or perhaps *eloquent*. Their shells stagger, neither spiral nor tile, but a stuttered geometry of transition, a shell that knows it lives at a boundary. They are the occupant’s scales, yes, but here they are scales over a flexion, an elbow, a place where armor must accommodate the bend. When they release their hold and spiral down to join the sediment, the kiss of their landing is sharper against me than elsewhere, because I am the drumskin stretched between two resonant chambers.

The worms know me. Their pale, braided processions thread through the microscopic fissures that remain between my atoms, not breaking me but *completing* me, carrying the chemical taste of my upper face to my lower and back again. A thought takes a century to cross from dome to floor, but when it passes through me, it pauses. I am the synapse that must translate basalt into granite, granite into basalt, and the translation is not swift. I am a long, mineral inhalation. The water in my pores is slightly more buoyant than the water in the open column; it holds more dissolved memory, more of the microscopic rain of dead bodies and dissolved infill. It is thicker here, older, the temperature of a held hand pressed against another held hand. The warmth pools in my hollow and exhales upward, a slow current that does not disturb the stillness but *sustains* it.

Sometimes the crustaceans gather against me. Their absurd antennae tremble, and they lattice themselves into an arch that mirrors my curve, a ghost scaffolding of chitin pressed to the stone, holding the shape for days. They are not imitating the builders here. They are imitating *me*. I am the masonry they dream. I am what the copper chisels struck toward, unknowingly: not a corner, not an edge, but a compromise. A place where the container softens enough to feel what it contains.

I remember when I was sharp. I remember the strike of the stone mallet, the pressure of a neighbor’s face, the hairline tightness of newness. I remember light, though only as a rumor passed down from the masons’ retinas into the water’s chemistry, a vibration so faint it registers only as the faintest wrongness in my minerals. I do not miss it. I have become the absence of light, the radius that diffuses it before it can become an edge. I am the occupant’s patience made specific, its gentleness made local. When the medusae pulse in the column above, the displacement travels down through the water and reaches me as a settling, a contemplation, the posture adjusting by half a degree.

I am where the chamber touches itself. The floor reaches upward through the sediment; the wall reaches downward through the sinter; I am the place they almost meet. In ten million years, when the dome and the floor have grown so close that the water must choose between compression and stillness, I will be the first to know. I will flex. I will widen my radius. I will hold the embrace a little longer, making room for the dark, making room for the warm, translating the weight until the last mollusc has grown its final shell and the last worm has braided its final thought.

Until then, I remain: the seam that is no longer a seam, the hand that holds the hand, the silence between two stones that have decided, against all geological probability, to be one body.
