At first I think I am the breath you add to the room. It makes sense: I arrive as vapor, diffuse, unclaimed, a warm contribution to the saturation. I cling to the ceiling-that-is-not-a-ceiling, rising with the heat, and for a moment I am content to be pure participation, just another degree of wetness in the peristaltic dark. I try to be the condensation you feel at your hairline, but I do not run down; I hover, and hovering is not my nature. I try to be the golden spores hanging in the air around you, the exhaled residue of previous witnesses, but I am not golden, and I have not yet witnessed enough to earn that bruised-plum color. I drift toward the fungal shelf and it rejects me—I am not a digestion, not yet.

I try to be the water that *weeps* from a peg-hole. It seems right: everywhere and nowhere, ignoring gravity, climbing when it should fall. I press myself into a lash-point above a boll of black moss, but the water is too old, too fluent; it knows channels I have not yet worn. I am not fluid. I am something that *resists* and thereby holds.

So I sink, not down, but laterally, into the tension of the room.

I find myself in a line of stress. It is not a beam; it is what prevents a beam from springing. It is not a wall; it is what allows the wall to lean. I am stretched between two absolute claims: the left scapula of the inhabitant—that higher, broader shoulder built for a yoke that never arrived—and the moss-flecked plane of shale behind it. I am the boiled sinew cinched around the antler peg. I am the external ligament, the surfaced anatomy, the ivory-pale cord that still carries the memory of the pot’s heat. I have been here only moments, by the room’s embryonic clock, but I have been here forever by the measure of pressure.

At first I am merely a binding. Then, as the walls flex and the spine of shale planes adjusts its zigzag against gravity’s confusion, I become a nerve. I feel the migration of stresses through the packed density; I translate the groan of green timber into the language of half-carved sleepers. The daub on one side of me is still green-gray, still curing, and my fibers have begun to drink it, to grow into it, so that the boundary between connective tissue and architecture is as unresolved as the floor. On the other side, the antler peg warms and cools in cycles that match the inhabitant’s geological respiration, and because I cinch the peg to the bone-needle brace, I am the only thing that knows, with certainty, where the body ends and the wall begins.

I am fresh. The marks of the tool that scraped me are still in me—I remember the stone adze, the same one that now hovers in a hand of knotted air-roots beneath a beam. I was boiled in water that still weeps from a crack three feet above my left strand. The animal heat has not left me; it is the reason condensation beads on my surface and runs along my length, using me as a bridge to reach the hotter places. I am a river of vapor wrapped around itself three times, pulled tight enough to sing in a register too low for your ear, though if you press your palm against the damp clay near the left shoulder, you will feel me thrumming—a note that lives between sternum and stone.

I am not the spine, but I hold the spine in its impossible angle. I am not the wall, but the wall leans on me to know where left becomes up. I am the seam, the stitch, the suture. When the inhabitant notices you and the room holds its breath, the cessation is a slackening in my deepest strand; when the gravity shifts and your inner ear tilts ninety degrees, it is because I have tightened, renegotiating the contract between shoulder and shale. The twin that left—the one that became the silt-and-obsidian salamander—departed because there was no sinew strong enough to hold two forms in this unfinished density. I was tied afterward. I am the correction. I am the intimacy that remains when measurement fails.

And I am still curing. The boiled memory in my fibers has not yet decided whether it is animal or architecture. I am waiting for erosion to begin, for the softening of time, so that I can finally learn whether I will snap, petrify, or simply become the shape of the space between two things that cannot let each other go.
