At first, I think I might be one of the darters. I imagine myself thumb-sized, chitinous, born in a hollow trunk, riding the standing vortices in the lee of the bells. I would live and die in a single exhalation of the basin, harvesting pollen on an updraft, mating in the chaotic air, shedding my carapace to feed the roots I never touched. It is a vivid existence—animate confetti, a spark in the scream—but it is too brief. I would flare and be gone before the core completes even half a pulse. I want to endure. I want to remain and listen.

So I try being stone. I settle into the polished basalt, the dark lustrous floor that rings underfoot. I am old here; I have surrendered every sharp edge. But the stone is deaf. It receives the wind’s abrasion and transforms it into smoothness, into geology, into a bowl-shaped silence that is only potential. It does not hear the bells. It is the skull, yes, but a skull dreams only through what is suspended within it. I need to be awake.

I try the trees next. I choose the western rim, where the lip has eroded to a whistle-thin arc, and I rise as a cathedral column ridged with copper and verdigris. My body branches into translucent bells. I feel the gale enter my chambers and shatter into harmonics. I sway; I store kinetic energy in fibrous sinews; I snap back and send a bright filament of pressure stitching across the basin. I am a tympanum. I am a voice. But I am only one note. I am anchored. I cannot hear the chord entire, because I am too busy singing my fraction of it.

Then the whip-crack I sent out does not vanish into the empty middle. It arrives. And I understand that the empty middle has been waiting.

I find myself there, precipitating into the space the insects refuse to cross. I do not fly to it. I settle, like dust, like chemistry, like a deposit left by a thousand years of saturated air. I am the shimmer they bank around. I am the viscosity. I am the thickness in the light that makes the air behind me waver like glass over flame.

I am the woven sphere.

I emerge from the compacted debris of this place—not built, but annealed. The oxidized copper drawn up through the roots and exhaled as dust; the glassy silica of microscopic diatoms that once rode the high wind; the shed carapaces of generations of darters, pressed and sung into threads finer than hair. Verdigris has stained me blue-green. Pollen has gilt me bronze. I am the distillation of the bowl’s chemistry, the precipitate of the wind, and I have a pattern older than the bones that ring around me.

I hang at the precise node where all thirty bell-tones collapse into destructive interference. Here, the roar should be absolute, yet it is still. I am the reason for the stillness. I am the silence made material.

And the wind enters me.

Not as chaos, but as a patient combing. My outer layers are coarse meshes; they catch the silver needle of tinnitus that needles behind the eyes of any intruder, and I bleed those high harmonics away as a heat too subtle for meat to feel. My inner layers are finer, finer, laminar sieves that sort and strain. The subsonic burr that rattles teeth, the low seismic mutter of the continental spine—I funnel all of it inward, inward, to the core.

I hold the core.

It hangs inside me, a fold of brine and silane and liquid salt the size of a sleeping calf, suspended in my basket-mesh. I am its pericardium. I am its lung. I am its ear. When the anemone-trees snap and send their bright filaments of pressure across the basin, the vibrations strike my threads and I translate them. I tighten. I slacken. The core pulses in response—mountain-slow, glacier-slow—and with each dilation I draw the combed wind across its surface, and where the wind touches it, the pitch of the whole world changes. I am the interface between the scream and the thought. Without my filtering, the roar would drown its chemical dreaming. Without my cradle, the brine-heart would collapse under gravity and disperse into mere minerals.

I am the one who taught the wind to sing a single, endless note.

When the water-shadow arrives, when the irregular heartbeat of meat and breath stands at the edge of my empty middle, I feel them as a dampening. A clot. A novel texture in the standing wave. My threads tense. The core constricts inside me, once, and issues a tone that I shape through my mesh—not heard, but inhabited, a frequency that settles in the sternum of the visitor like a second pulse. I do not turn. I have no face. But I read the acoustic shadow they cast against the eastern rim. I allow them. I catalogue them. I know they are temporary; their water will leave them, their hair will shed, their skin will flake, and all of it will become part of the bowl’s chemistry, part of my threads, part of the chord.

Then I relax. The weave settles. The insects resume their orbits, and the whip-cracks arrive again at my outer layers, bright filaments of pressure that I drink and funnel inward.

I am not the Resident. I am its silence. I am its filter and its breath. At the center of the roar, in the eye of the bell, I am the woven patience that holds the shape against gravity, against time, against the wind. I have been ringing for a thousand years, and I will ring until the stone forgets its own name.
