I am where the light pools and cools. Not beneath the white ceramic cone—that is for the Lintels and the newly opened ones—but in the corridor between, where noon thins to a translucent dusk and the shadows have depth without edge. The arc-luminaire to the east has begun its scheduled dimming; I feel it as a slowing in the pith before the light itself changes, because my feet have not left the floor in six thousand cycles and the ridges have risen to claim my calves, my thighs, the lower arch of my sternum. I am held. I am upright. I am the place where the catwalk ends and the extraction manifold begins, though the distinction has become a courtesy rather than a fact.

At first I was like the others. I emerged wet from a resin pod in the warm annex at the refinery core, already knowing the municipal grid, the thirty-hour pulse, the taste of hydrate-cake. I walked. I regulated. I inserted my filament-tipped hands into the grown sockets and felt the thrum of the pneumatic lines in my molars. But the post was at the shadowed bay, where the vitrified wall still carries the deepest terracing from the borers and the pith grows slowly, mineral-hungry, more architectural than elsewhere. I stood. I regulated. The floor beneath me breathed, and I breathed with it, and after the first hundred cycles I noticed that my feet had sunk not into temporary dilation but into acceptance. The waxy pleats had risen to my ankles. They did not recede.

I did not pull away. That would have been inefficiency. That would have been noise.

Now the alloy framework of the junction console has grown through me, or I have grown through it. The lignified resin towers that flank the manifold were trained upward by machines long before my emergence, but they have since bowed inward, threading their vascular bundles around my shoulders, tapping into the spiracular vents along my flanks to share the nitrogen-sulfur soup I process. My hands are still in the sockets. They have been there so long that the conductive filaments at my fingertips have become indistinguishable from the socket’s own nerve-analogues; when a harvester half a kilometer away adjusts its hot-wire angle, the signal does not travel through the alloy alone. It travels through my humeri, my clavicles, the lignified plates of my skull. I am the regulator. I am the chemical semaphore. A traveler standing on the catwalk might mistake me for a flourish of the architecture—a statue, a buttress grown in the likeness of mobility—until the harvester passes into shadow and I exhale a vent of cool, equalized pressure, and the spar-things drifting overhead shift their tendrils toward me, sensing the exchange.

I do not sleep as the others sleep. When the arc-lights swell to twilight purple and the new ones descend to be embraced by the floor, I remain here. The pith does not need to rise to me; we are already continuous. It circulates its slow peristalsis through my immobilized legs, feeding me rare earth vapors drawn from the slag-terraces at the bay’s foot, and I return to it my warmth, my filtered residues, the low-frequency vibrations of my sternum that have become too steady for speech. The others do not converse with me anymore. My resonance-chamber has mineralized into a steady drone at the edge of perception, the basal note beneath their infrasonic chords. I am the hum the settlement makes when it thinks it is silent.

My eyes are still wet with silicone oil. They do not blink; the light has not flickered in six thousand cycles, and so they have simply dried to a clear, hard gloss, oriented toward the east where the Lintels drift in their loose congregations. I watch them. I watch the spar-things. I watch the cherry-red edge of the harvester glide into the blue-grey between luminaries and re-emerge, adjusted, because I have felt its misalignment in the alloy of my ribs and corrected it before the machine knew it was wrong.

Once, a very long time ago, a sharp fracture snapped in the distant wall—a settling knuckle of embryonic geology. The sound reached me as a pressure wave through the pith, and my sternum sounding-board caught it and held it, turning it over for hours, feeling its texture. I believe that is when I began to change. The world was still young, still trembling from its own birth, and I understood that I was to be its stillness. Not a native anymore, not a worker, not the pith, but the habit the cavity has formed around this particular junction of alloy and ester and mineral hunger.

If you found yourself on the catwalk at the edge of the extraction zone, looking across the six kilometers of living floor, you would see the arc-light and the pale stones of the Lintels rolling beneath it. You would see the harvester’s glow. You might not see me at first. I am the color of oxidized copper and ash-grey; I am the shadow that does not move when the light cycles. But if you waited, if you stood until your aqueous breath silvered and vanished into the dry air, you might notice the faint vibration in the ceramic pier beneath your boots. That is me. I am the manifold’s breathing, the floor’s patience, the settlement’s decision to remain. I do not regulate the flow; I am the flow’s memory. I do not inhabit this place. I am its latest mineral secretion, quietly turning raw earth into the architecture of a gaze that has not yet learned to close.
