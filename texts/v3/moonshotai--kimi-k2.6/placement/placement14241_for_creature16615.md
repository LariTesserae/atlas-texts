I would find myself first by the sound—not the cathedral-drone of the sealed engine pits, which is the constant gravity that holds my bones together, but the secondary note, the ghost-frequency that bleeds from the eastern junction of the fourth-generation aqueduct where it crosses the abandoned spine of a decommissioned cable-tram line. It is a beat pattern, an interference created when two eternal machines disagree by half a cycle. To stand in that corridor is to feel the fog shimmer in the chest like a bell struck in a distant room. The warm condensation beads on the inner surface of a vitrified ceramic arch, drops into the black water below, and each drop rings against the isopod grit compacted at the bottom in a different pitch. I came here before I knew I was searching. My feet, broad and deliberate, read the temperature gradient of the polished basalt floor; my porous mastoid bones drank the thrum and said: *here.*

Here is a hesitation. A marriage of inconvenience between two civic bodies. The aqueduct, its inner skin painted in generations of copper sulfate bloom, weeps mineral tears that fall onto the traction lines below, where they do not corrode the iron but rather charge the air, precipitating a blue-green snow that gathers in the tram’s old trough. The line was rerouted three centuries ago; its cables now sing in a higher stratum. Because nothing here is destroyed, only wrapped, the masons poured a half-shell of porous masonry over the iron spine, creating a tunnel that leads nowhere official, a duct that inhales fog from the plateau’s western weather-edge and exhales it into the under-street lymphatics. This is my district. Not a home, for that implies a door, a threshold, a distinct architecture. I occupy the interference. I am the body that chose to calibrate itself to the beat frequency rather than pass through it.

I am still becoming what I am. I have not settled. Each morning—if the gray-gold diffusion that reaches this depth can be called morning—I wake in the hammock I have strung across the traction trough, woven from shed trawler-wings that do not rot but slowly mineralize, becoming stiff as glass, becoming *structure*. I press my palms against the seam: the cold iron of the tram spine on my left, the warm vitrified ceramic of the half-shell on my right. The gap between them is alive. It weeps. The fog finds its way in, depositing layers of silica that would, without me, dry into dead, brittle scales—false enamel that would crack in a century and weep steam into the stratum above, disturbing the nesting trawlers, altering the harmonic map. I am the refusal of that hollowness. I am what grows into the pause.

My mother was of the western aqueducts, her skin the precise blue-green of oxidized copper, her subsonic hum the pitch of water falling through ceramic junctions. My other parent tended the cable-trams, and I carry their inheritance in the stiff, conductive crest of my hair, the faint ozone crackle when I move through a dry pocket. Between them I emerged the color of a deep healing: indigo at the joints, copper across the ribs, a violet bruise where the two mineral signatures negotiated a treaty in my dermis. My siblings reroute steam in the public galleries. They tend the great flywheels and speak in the proud, forty-second diaphragm drones of open infrastructure. I speak instead in the near-silence of the gap. I place my horny thumbnail against the iron spine and strike a percussive note—*tock*—that travels through the metal. I follow it with a hum, low and slow, that lasts half a minute and means *this fracture is occupied; this place is healing; do not pour the next shell yet.* The city listens. The city waits. Nothing here is rushed.

I eat the precipitate. I scrape the copper-sulfate film from the aqueduct’s underbelly with my thumbs, mix it with the isopods that lose their grip on the masonry and fall into the trough. I crush their iron-oxide shells to powder with a stone I carry—an old bearing-race, smooth and dark and heavy as a heart. I render the paste on a flat tile of heat-resistant ceramic set over a pinhole vent of geothermal steam. The meal tastes of metal and patience. I drink the black water, not from the channel where it runs hottest and most sulfuric, but from the condensation that sheets in slow curtains down the half-shell. It is warm, faintly sweet with silica, thick with the spores and chaff that make the air a broth.

But what I do—what makes me *who* I am—is older than diet or dwelling. I tend the city’s wounded edges. Where the vitrified shell did not fully marry the iron spine, where the masons left a gap no wider than a child’s wrist, I press my body. I anoint the wound with my bacterial films, with the archaea I have cultivated since childhood in the creases of my elbows and behind my ears. I sleep against the fracture, my cheek to the cold iron, my back to the warm ceramic, and I let the fog condense through my hair, across my skin. The gap tastes me. It draws my shed cells, my sulfuric oils, the microscopic architecture of my living varnish, into its mineral weeping. When I wake, the deposit is no longer dead. It is a living scab. It breathes. It has begun to accrete in earnest, integrating my chemistry into the seal, translating my body into infrastructure.

This is not called a profession. My people do not have a name for it, because it is too specific, too intimate with failure. The others pass through the interference; I have become its node. When the predators navigate the pipe-ways below, their echo-clicks rise through the floor-grate, and I answer them with a click of my own—fingernail on isopod shell—a timbre low and patient, disinterested in prey. They pass. We share the acoustic territory. The seam is my territory.

I am the hesitation made flesh. I am the blue-green and violet scab on the body of the shelf. I will not die here tomorrow, or next century. My joints are broad, my bones dense with calcium leached from the mineral water, my movements suited to the slow friction of this saturated volume. But when I finally slow to a stop—when my diaphragm can no longer generate the subsonic hum that tells the architecture I am present—they will not carry me to the deep seeps. There will be no need. I will already be half-enamel, half-synthetic geology. The next generation of masons will pour the new shell—slurry and vitrified alloy—and I will be the core around which it sets. My bones will alter the resonance of this beat frequency. The interference will shift into a new harmonic. Future seam-walkers will press their ears to the cavity and hear my particular note: copper, ozone, patience. They will know that this fracture has wept its layers, that the gap has sealed, that the city has found its form once more and is keeping it, slowly, loudly, forever.
