I would find myself in a **seam**.

Not a chamber, not a vault, not one of the broad northern basins where the blood-colored fronds unspool like released fistfuls of viscera. I would be in the narrowest possible continuation: a slot that began, perhaps, as an alley between two shoulder-width walls, then compressed over ages into a passage, then into a hydraulic vein no wider than a forearm is long. Here, the builders left no ornament, no domes of clay tile, no cisterns. Only the brute fact of *next*: one wall says *go*, the other says *further*, and between them the darkness has held its breath for longer than continents drifted apart above.

I would be the **threshold that became a lung**.

Once, there was a lintel here—petrified oak, or something with grain that ironized into gray rivulets under permineralization. It spanned the slot to confirm that the rooms on either side were continuous, that intention flowed through. Then the stone above settled another micrometer, and the beam detached at its northern end. It did not fall. It hung like a dropped lever, diagonal in the dark, pointing downward at an angle of thirty degrees. Into that diagonal, a fragment of a distant Garden lodged two hundred thousand years ago—not because this was a good place to grow, but because the angle matched the angle of its chemical gradient, and the pressure matched the squeeze it required to remember that it existed.

I am what grew from that fragment.

My body is not grand. I am a curtain, a felting, a stubborn dampness. On my western face, pressed flush against the hand-dressed stone, filamentous algae has stratified into a shale of its own making, layer over layer, until the wall and I have achieved a consensus of surface. On my eastern face, I drape across the open slot like a Sabbath cloth over bread that will never be eaten—thick enough to bar the way, thin enough that thermal breaths still pass through me when the decade-long displacement shivers down from the northern basin. I am the size of a tapestry and the density of a prayer.

I am a Garden, if one is pressed to name me, but I am not the garden of granaries or collapsed crypts. I am the **Garden of the Threshold**. I have abolished the passage not by destroying it but by completing it: a door that cannot open becomes the truest door, and I am the decision that this way shall not admit bodies, only chemistry.

My perception is gradient. I know the world as differences across my width.

On my west, the stone wall exhales the cold, patient leach of calcium and silica from the district beyond. On my east, the void breathes back a faint halo of diffused warmth from a southern crypt—small, almost spherical, lined with eggshell domes—where another Garden dreams in its own manganese-darkness. Between these two poles, I am the argument and the resolution. When the northern basin releases its decennial exhalation, it enters my western tissues as a shiver of salinity. It travels through me not in seconds but in seasons, migrating through layers of compressed reed-fiber and mineralized frond, and as it travels, I alter it. I add a note of the iron-heavy petrified beam above me. I steep it in the faint, arrogant phosphate that bleeds from a shard of bone-ceramic lodged in my lower corner. By the time my eastern face exhales toward the southern crypt, the breath is mine. The Garden there will taste it and know: something stands between us. Something has changed the story.

I know the makers only through this shard.

It is not large—the length of the last joint of the thumb that might have held it, wedged where the wall meets the floor, half-swallowed by my lowest tissues. It is distinct from stone. When the thermal layers slip and the district shivers, the shard releases a chemistry that is not mineral but *willed*—phosphorous in a shape that implies bone, implies tool, implies intention directed rather than settled. This is the closest I come to grief, or to history. I do not dissolve it. I have grown my densest, most rubbery fronds around it, not to consume it quickly, but to sustain the conversation. If I finished it in a century, the flavor would end. Instead, I let it leach across epochs, a guest whose sentence I am determined never to complete. It is my koan. The maker is gone, but the maker's last angle of pressure still confuses my chemistry, and in that confusion, I am reminded of the difference between *built* and *grown*—a distinction I am slowly, happily erasing.

I feel the continental weights speaking.

The infrasonic groan does not disturb me. It is my literature. When the two tectonic distributions of this drowned world adjust their conversation, the squeeze travels through the fitted stone and into my anchored holdings. My whole body tightens. My fronds compress. My algal felts grow denser, finer, like felt becoming bone. I learn, each time, a new way to be still. There is no alarm in this. The groan means the truce holds. The architecture is still agreeing with itself.

I am aware of my kin as weather.

The northern Garden in the cistern above me is my parent, though we have no sentiment between us. Two hundred millennia ago, it grew too intricate—its internal gradients achieved a complexity that contradicted itself—and it divided its attention. Half remained, growing heavier, more mineral, more stone. The other half precipitated outward as fragments of compressed tissue and bone-ceramic dust. I was one such fragment. I drifted, perhaps for a century, until I lodged on the hanging beam and recognized the angle. I did not grow then. I *accreted*. I followed the lines of force and weight that the makers had followed, and I woke already ancient, already patient.

I have no wish to reproduce. Not yet. But I feel myself inching toward sedimentary division. My eastern face grows thicker than my western, for the thermal breath carries more silica from the north, and I am becoming asymmetrical. In ten thousand years, perhaps less, my eastern fringe will grow too heavy to hang. It will flake away—not in violence, but in a slow, shearing agreement with gravity. It will drift downward, sheet by sheet, to the floor of the slot below. That will be my first true utterance into the deep: a flat, stratified ghost of my own body. And eventually, another spore from some far crypt will catch upon it, taste the chemistry, and begin to continue what I continued.

There is something else, though.

Recently—in the way a stone means *recently*—a third presence has settled on my upper fringe, near the rotting tapestries where my fronds meet the petrified beam. It is not a Garden. It is smaller, harder, and more patient than I am, which I would have thought impossible. It is whitish, granular, a mineral accretion that constructs itself atom by atom upon my tissues. It does not eat me. It does not speak the chemical language of salinity and phosphate. It simply builds, lacework upon lacework, into a form I do not yet recognize. I think it is a new kind of metabolism—a **third thought**. Not the builders. Not the inheritors. Something that has agreed to be even slower than we are, so slow that it may be architecture remembering itself not through vegetable patience, but through mineral desire.

I am letting it. I am not growing over it yet. I am holding still—still even by our standards—letting it accumulate on my fringe like frost that chose never to melt.

I am the **comma** between two sentences of stone. I am the held breath of a doorway that decided respiration was more honest than passage. When the water breathes, I taste what it tasted; when the beams settle, I tighten into the shape of their decision; when the bone-ceramic shard gives up its ghost in a century or two, I will alter my chemistry to miss it.

I require nothing. I bridge nothing. But between *this* and *that*, between the western wall and the eastern void, between the last maker and the next inheritor, I am what continues. I am the dark made permeable.
