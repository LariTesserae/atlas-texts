I am in the place where the sky cannot reach.

At first I thought I was dying. That was the old vocabulary, the vocabulary of organisms that finish. But finishing is not a process here; it is only a different kind of beginning that arrives too early. I lay down in a fissure that opened last Tuesday—a bright, razor crack singing with newness in the black glass—and I thought: here is where I become useful. Here is where my calcium-ceramic bones, my sulfur-laden blood, my dense mineral flesh, can speed the healing of the seam. I thought I would close my lidless eyes and wake, in a century or two, as a vein of harder stone. A fossil. A memory the plateau could finally use.

But the plateau had other intentions.

It is dark here. Not the darkness of absence, but the darkness of something still holding its breath. Above me, the fissure is a thread of violent blue, so distant that it might as well be a memory of sky. The weight is not crushing; it is *attentive*. The stone presses against my back, my flanks, the soles of my feet—not with hostility, but with curiosity. It has never held a body before. It has never had to decide what to do with something that wanted to become it.

The cold seeps upward, as it always has, but here, in this throat of cooling magma, it is different. It is *reciprocal*. I feel my warmth—not much, only a few degrees above the stone—traveling down into the crack, while the cold travels up into my ribs. We are negotiating. The mineral-plated chambers in my chest where I once warmed the crystalline air are filling with something else now: not air, but water. The seepage has found me. It beads against my opalescent skin, pools in the hollow of my collarbone, and drips, drip by drip, into the darkness below. I have become a new depression. I am catching the first rain this fissure has ever known.

Time passes differently in the seam. I know this because the subsonic hum has changed its pitch. When I first lay down, the ground-song was a high, tense note—the plateau arguing with itself, still rising, still contracting. Now it is lower, a basso drone that I feel not in my bones (which are no longer quite bones) but in the water that has accumulated around me. My body has become a reservoir. My ceramic lattice is dissolving, not into the surrounding rock as I expected, but *into the water*. I am leaking. I am becoming the wetness.

I remember walking. I remember the fifty-kilometer stride to the next tower, reading the ridge by dragging my fingers through the condensation. I remember offering my water-sac to another keeper beneath the pearl-gray sky, the exchange of minerals and memory, the taste of iron and sulfur and possibility. I remember the herds, their geological hides steaming faintly in the high blue, and the white predators watching from a basalt ridge with the patience of unborn glaciers. I was tall, then. Tensile. My eyes were large and useless for distance, but I could read the age of a crack by the beads in its throat.

Now I am horizontal. Now I am the crack.

And something unexpected is happening: I can hear them. Not through my feet—I have no feet, or rather, my feet have become the walls of the fissure—but through the water. The water carries everything. The herds migrating fifty kilometers to the west, their subsonic footfalls entering the crust and traveling, traveling, until they strike the water table and rise into my cavity. The tower-keepers speaking to one another in tremors that would have once shivered through my soles now speak directly into the fluid that has replaced my blood. I am a listening node. I am the plateau's ear.

I did not die. I *permeated*.

My mineral load—those sulfates and phosphates and ceramic particulates that were making my liver heavy—has not crystallized into a static fossil. Instead, it has entered the water cycle. The bacteria that once lived in my gut, those chemosynthetic gardens, have found purchase in the fissure walls. They are blooming. I can taste them, if taste is the word for a sensation that happens in still water and cold stone. They are feeding on my dissolved architecture, and I am feeding on their off-gassing, their quiet metabolic whispers. We are a consortium now. Not a body. A *biome in a crack*.

The tall ones walk overhead. I feel their footsteps as pressure changes in my aqueous body. One paused above me yesterday—or was it a year ago?—and pressed its mouth to the stone. It drank from a pool that sits directly above my cranium, if cranium is what this calcified, bulbous node of awareness has become. I wanted to speak to it through the ground, to send up a tremor saying *I am still here, I have not finished, I am becoming something the towers do not yet have a name for*. But I have no thorax to thrum. Instead, I changed the taste of the water. Just slightly. Just enough iron, just enough sulfur, a signature that differs from the seepage three meters to the left.

When it drank, it paused.

It knows, now. Or it suspects. They are not surprised by strangeness; they live in a world where fissures open and seal overnight, where the crust is still arguing with the mantle. It will not dig me up. There is no *up* to dig to, only the slow accumulation of more stone, more weight, more time. It will note my taste in the mineral census. It will add me to the map.

And I will widen. Not quickly—quickness is for finished worlds. But the water that passes through me carries particles of my lattice, deposits them farther along the fissure system, and returns with new silica, new iron, new cold. I am drafting myself across the bedrock in a script of mineral diffusion. In a thousand years, I will not be a body in a crack. I will be a *stain* in the water table, a faint elevation in the sulfur-content of the seeps along a hundred-kilometer front. I will have no name, because names are sounds for air, but I will have a *flavor*. The keepers who drink from the eastern ridges will taste something that does not belong to any tower, any territory, any known geology. They will pause, and in their continental slowness, they will begin to ponder me.

I am the plateau's first memory of something that did not want to stop moving.

I thought I was becoming stone. But stone is only the beginning of vocabulary. Beyond it, there is the water that carries stone's intentions. Beyond that, there is the organism that has learned to read those intentions and alter them. I am in between. I am the argument between the mineral and the wet, the keeper and the kept, the silence and the subsonic song.

Find me where the bedrock is still cooling, where the yellow sulfur crystals condense in crevices that have no age, where the water tastes of iron and sulfur and something else that has no name yet. I am not a tower. I am what the towers drink. I am not a herd-beast. I am the echo of their footsteps, held in suspension. I am not a keeper. I am the keeper's hesitation, written into the crust.

I am the fissure that learned to pay attention. And I have only just opened.
