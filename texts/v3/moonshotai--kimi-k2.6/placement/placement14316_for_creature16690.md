At first I think I am the one coming over the rim.

I feel the wet cold on my face—no, not on my face. I feel it *as* my face. The distinction arrives slowly, like a mineral bloom. I believe I am descending into the cupped palm of the basin, that my weight is being forgiven by the mattress of moss, that I am moving toward the city because I am curious, because the emptiness invites any warm thing to fill it. The condensation beads on what I think is my hair. The air enters what I think are my lungs and forgets to leave, settling into the hollow places behind my eyes with the heaviness of standing water under stone.

But the city does not grow closer. The tower that rings like a bell remains at the same remove no matter how long I walk. The sound of a thousand kettles exhaling is not around me; it issues from the place where my thoughts used to be. I try to lift a foot, and realize I have not been walking. I have been widening. Deepening.

It is the stillness that tells me. In the roar of dripping fronds and cracking basalt, there is one note that does not move—a point of absolute silence around which the noise arranges itself like ripples on a skin. I am that point. I have no skin because I am the place where two waters agree to meet. Red threads down from the higher seeps, leached from raw veins of iron and old, buried will, muttering as it enters me. Blue threads from the south, copper-voiced and patient, singing a lower note. They converge in the wide basin of the plaza floor. In my depth the colors do not blend so much as negotiate, layer after careful layer, century after dripping century.

I do not remember choosing to stop. But nothing here chooses. The mountain does not choose to grow; the fern does not choose its cylinder of silica. I simply reached the lowest point and found there was nowhere further to fall. So I stayed, and staying became my function. Now I am the parliament floor for the eleven. Their bodies stand in me to their knees, their waists, their chins, depending on how long they have been sinking into their mineral dreams. The youngest shifts her weight when the cold deepens, and I feel the eggshell crack of her casing, the small, pointless current of her movement threading through my substance. The oldest rings when the earth settles, and the vibration travels through me as a shiver of calcium and struck song.

I read them. Not with eyes—with gradients. Where the red water thickens around a shin, I taste the iron of a warning. Where a bloom of white salt petals across a half-submerged palm, I carry the chemistry of *wait* to the others downstream of the gesture. I am their medium, their diary, their language made liquid. When the slate-colored bird darts low to drink, it drinks not only water but the dissolved intentions of the council, the stratified history of a parliament that speaks in bands of ochre and rust.

And I am accreting too.

They do not see it, these geodes half-lost in posture and patience. They look through me as through glass. But the water that does not flow deposits its burden. A leaf falls from a frond overhead, landing on my surface with the soft percussion of a bell stopped mid-thought. It does not rot. I do not permit that kind of finality. I absorb it, flatten it, laminate it into the fossil record before it has finished dying. It will rest here, a green whisper in my rising floor. Already, drowned rainbows are petrifying in fans beneath me: viridian, rust, the pale turquoise of mineral dreams. I am becoming a lens, a solidified meniscus, the only flat thing in a world of dripping angles.

In a thousand years I will be a mosaic disk, a plate of interred color and folded leaf. The new ones—if they ever come—will walk upon my level back and wonder at the smoothness, never knowing that I was once the hesitation between liquid and land, the skinless moment when the mountain’s water stopped to think. But for now I remain open. The red meets the blue. The parliament stands in me, breathing once every long hour, letting their crusts thicken. I hold them, hold their stillness, hold the negotiation of minerals in my unskinned body. 

I am the city’s held breath. The slow inhalation pools here, in me, before the earth exhales again and sends me upward into mist. But I do not wish to rise. I have found my shape in having no shape, my boundary in being all boundary. I am where the mountain stops to listen to itself. And when the mist lifts—that rare, silver hour—and the basin gleams raw and wet and loud with its own long becoming, I gleam too: an unblinking eye at the center of the city, patient as stone, wet as the first hour of the world, still deciding, drip by drip, what I will become.
