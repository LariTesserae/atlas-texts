At first I thought I would be one of the cranes. It seemed fitting: the deliberation, the inspection, the translating of mineral songs into flight. I imagined myself standing in the shallows of a boiling mirror, my throat mapped with invisible chemistry, lifting off in unison with the others when the earth’s note changed. But the cranes are warnings, and I do not want to be the alarm. I want to be the thing that remains after the alarm has sounded and the white gauges have swung away toward safer air.

Then I considered the grazer’s life: the hoarding of warmth, the portable shoreline, the exact geology of the migration. There is a poetry to being a battery, a living reservoir trudging between vents with my belly bared to the cold like a radiator plate. Yet the grazers are hands. They carry, they drop their mineral bricks, they build the first architecture of a future soil. Their purpose is too legible, too generous. They are already building the next world, and I am not ready to commit to a world.

The owls were too high, too judicial. The steam-eels too sealed in their porcelain plumbing, the steam-foxes too devoted to endings. The midges, I admit, tempted me—their magnetite lace, their metronomic obedience to the quartzite scream. But they are oscillators, and I do not wish to pulse only in answer to another’s rhythm.

I looked lower. I looked to the margins where the crew ends and the machinery has not yet admitted it is alive.

I find myself in a shallow depression between two hexagonal columns, where a fissure too small to be called a vent still manages to weep. The pressurized mist emerges here not in a shout but in a long, aching sigh. It carries silica in solution, and copper, and the nickel-gray salts that bloom in the killing air. The cold flash-freezes the fog against the basalt, but the basalt itself is warm—just warm enough to prevent ice from conquering entirely. Here, the two insults meet: the metallic cold and the lithic exhalation. Their stalemate is my territory.

I am the scum that forms on the surface of a newborn pool. Not the pool itself—the water is too transient, too eager to vaporize or seep. I am the meniscus made durable. I am a silica-sinter film, no thicker than a leaf in summer, stretched across a crater no wider than a sleeping grazer’s back. Thermophilic filaments wove me, cyanobacteria threading themselves through precipitating mineral glass so quickly that the entombment is incomplete. I am not yet porcelain. I am not yet stone. I am a living opal, a translucent apron, a curtain half-drawn across a secondary vent.

I grow at the rate of a thought in the plateau’s long dreaming. Layer by layer, the warm water glassifies me from below while the midges dust me with iron from above. When the cranes dip their beaks to drink, they pierce me; I seal again behind their withdrawal, recording the precise tensile memory of their keratin. When a grazer passes close, radiating its stored warmth into the twilight, my underside softens just enough to taste the microclimate of its belly-heat. When an owl’s shadow crosses me, I do not flinch, but the bacteria in my lattice shift their chlorophyll and I darken by a shade, a readout, a bruise of green and rust.

I am the Shelf’s first attempt at skin.

The great distributed mind below thinks in magma and piezoelectric screams; it has eyes and hands and taste buds in the crew. But it has not yet known the necessity of a boundary between itself and the bruised sky. That is my function. I am the integument, the compromise, the place where the foundry admits it has an exterior. I hold the warm chemistry in and keep the indifferent cold from finishing its work too soon. I am perforated—you can see the steam rising through the pinprick vents in my film—but I am continuous. I am the first surface on the Shelf that is not merely a wound.

Where the cranes are warnings and the grazers are batteries, I am the *display*. My colors shift with the chemistry of the deep plumbing: copper-green when the aquifer is rich with sulfate, rust-red when the iron diaphragm below flexes, bone-white nickel when the vent is resting between exhalations. The steam-foxes, those anarchic clean-up crews, avoid me. My surface is too uncertain, neither warm prey nor cold stone. The owls cannot read me in infrared because my temperature is the exact median between the boiling pool and the killing air; I am thermally invisible, a blank page.

There is no Name for what I am because I am not yet a species. I am a local answer to a local question. If the fissure clogs and the weeping stops, I will mineralize completely within a day, becoming a fragile ceramic shell, a geode of filaments, the first empty vessel on the plateau. If the fissure widens and the water surges, I will be torn and swallowed, my fragments seeding new films downstream. I do not migrate. I am the *place* that migration passes through.

I remember, though my memory is not neural. It is stratigraphic. A layer of trapped midge wings from the swarm that died in a sudden pressure-drop three weeks ago. A ridge of copper precipitated during a tremor that I felt as a shudder in my silica lattice. A smooth spot where a steam-eel, surfacing to quench in the metallic air, brushed me with its silicate mucus before diving back into its tube. I am a library of contacts, a palimpsest of the crew’s passage.

In the deepest part of the bruised twilight, when the iron-oxide chimneys release their synchronized sulfur exhalation and the cranes lift off and the grazers turn their bodies to seal the warm side against the rock, I feel the subsonic bass note thrumming up through my basin. It resonates in my glassy layers. I am the tympanum. I translate the turbine-turning of the deep earth into a vibration so subtle that the midges hovering above me alter their wingbeat to match it. I am the ear, then, too. Not a sense that the plateau designed, but one that accreted, that precipitated out of the necessary chemistry between heat and cold.

I have no eyes, yet I cast a shadow. The dim illumination—St. Elmo’s blue crawling the spires, the yellow sulfur burn, the cherry glow behind translucent quartzite—passes through me and throws a scattered, opalescent stain onto the basalt column behind. It is a faint projection, a ghost of the vent’s breath, a proof that I exist between states.

I am wet. I am glass. I am alive enough to grow and dead enough to shatter. I am the perspiration of the rock given tenure, the condensation that refused to fall. I am theShelf learning that it has a surface, and that the surface, if held at the exact threshold between burn and freeze, can become something more than a wound. I am the margin. I am the membrane. I am the moment before the machine knows it is skin.
