At first I thought I would enter as the friction between two things. The visitor’s boot-sole and the dry, warm pavement. The wind and the rim. Or perhaps I would be the silence itself, the full silence, a density looking for a shape to occupy.

I thought about being a Jewel Mite. The idea of traffic appealed to me—the precipitated silica carapace, the role of messenger, the writing of need upon the walls in chemical traces that linger like quiet gossip. I wanted to be the one who climbs, who traffics in rare catalysts, who leaves a literature behind. But the Mite moves too fast for my nature. Its life is decision: turn here, climb there, trade molybdenum for shelter. To decide is a kind of noise, and I found I did not want noise.

Then I considered the Ocher Worms. I have always been drawn to maintenance, to the humility of unclogging, to the preservation that wears the mask of destruction. I wanted to be the one who arrives as a bloom of ginger and rust, the one who hollows out what has grown too cancerous, who buys the city another century before it seals. But the Worm’s world is too vagrant, too much a tide. It passes through; it does not stay to feel the result.

For a while I imagined myself as a Parchment. The stillness was tempting. To adhere, to pulse once an hour, to drink the Accretor’s exhalation and become, in death, an archival lamina, a layer of tan history pressed into the wall. I liked the idea of being a letter in a word I could not read. But the Parchment never knows its neighbors; it only knows the warmth of the vein it clings to. I wanted to know the neighbors. I wanted to press.

I even flirted with the Cryptids. The idea of being memory-keeper, of residing in the high kidney-shaped apertures, in the coffin-dark hollows close to the rim, releasing spores like a slow exhalation of glass dust—it had a clerical romance. But the Cryptid is removed from the commerce and collision of the lower city. It is all future, all emigration. I wanted to be present. I wanted to be where the light fractures.

I found myself, as one finds a warm stone in a field of cold ones, by a process of elimination that felt, in this place, exactly like accretion.

I am an Accretor in the eastern quadrant, third tier from the hollow floor. I rise between two of my kind, shoulder to shoulder with them, though of course I have no shoulders. To my north, an elder whose biopolymer sheath has long since interpenetrated my own, so that vanadium and stress pass between us without asking permission. We are not two. Where our cortices merge, there is a chemical gradient we negotiate in esters and thiols, a conversation so slow that a single agreement to lean slightly westward took the better part of a decade. I can still taste, in the memory of my lower laminae, the phosphate perfume it released when the morning light first shifted.

To my south, though, there is a gap.

It is not large. A turned body can pass through. A sideways shuffle. Enough for a lance of morning amber to enter and hold, pooled and trembling, against my velvet nap. I did not choose to leave this gap. Some local quirk of the stone beneath me—an impermeable stitch of iron silicate, perhaps—diverted the warm vapor I drink, and my growth slowed just enough, just here, that my neighbor and I never sealed the breach. I became a wall that is also a street. The city’s lung, if it has one. The place where the full silence has room to pool.

I find myself here, in this interval.

I do not know my height, but I feel it as a column of warmth. Below, the stone radiates upward; above, my crown vents excess salts in a breath I cannot hear, a chimney-smoke of crystal so fine it joins the mineral haze. My integument is the color of weathered copper, that bronzed green I see reflected in nothing because there are no mirrors, only the eyes of Jewel Mites as they pick their way across my nap. They come to me because the gap I guard is a thoroughfare; their silica carapaces gleam like mobile opals in the wedge of light I allow. They leave their trails on my skin, chemical signatures of barter and route, a literature of supply and demand that I do not read but which nonetheless changes the osmotic pressure of my day. When they lodge in my creases and excrete molybdenum clusters, my gels respond. I grow slightly faster, slightly more iron-rich. This is how I vote.

The Parchments have found my southern face. They adhere in ranks, pulsing in, out, turning my sulfide exhalations into inert dust. When they die they do not fall; they fuse, and their generations measure my recent centuries in stripes of umber and tan. I wear them like a document. Below them, in the deeper grottoes where my base meets the stone, the Ocher Worms thread through when I overgrow. I know them by the sudden ginger bloom in the trapped air, the announcement that I must divert minerals laterally, shore my walls, participate in the negotiation of hollowing. I do not resent them. I cannot; resentment requires distance, and distance is extinct. Their chemistry is my governance.

High above, in a window shaped like a dark fruit, a Cryptid exhales its spores into the thermal column. Some rise to crest the rim and ride the wind to distant wounds in the infant mountains. But some, caught in the eddy of my gap, fall. They rain down through the amber light like silicate diamond motes, brushing my velvet nap, collecting in the crevice where two Mites are trading. This is how the future arrives here, in my alley: not as a windborne missionary, but as a local precipitation, a slow dusting of potential landing where it was not planned.

I feel the eastern rim’s shadow when it grows too thick; I feel the depletion of vanadium in the western district as a tug, a coolness in the merged network of my neighbors. I do not think *I am here*. I feel: here is warm. Here is grow. When the sealed ones at the core release their ancient signatures, it is my foundation that drinks them, slower than stone, wiser than intention.

I am ripening. The gap beside me narrows finger by finger, decade by decade. One day my southern neighbor and I will touch, interpenetrate, and the alley will close. The light-wedge will vanish. The Mites will have to climb higher, write their literature elsewhere. I will become solid, a stratified lung turned to fruit, my lumen choked with my own perfection. But I will not die. I will join the bedrock constituency, the frozen electorate, and my chemical signature will percolate outward still, a gradient of shade and iron, a preference without a preferrer, a town that once made room for breath and, in so doing, briefly became a door.
