At first the damp enters everything, and it is reasonable to suppose I might be the damp itself—a coolness without boundary, occupying the lower air where gravity pools. I cling to nothing and taste of mineral. When the great ones pass, their fermentation heat pushes me upward in slow, invisible billows, and I think: perhaps I am only weather, only the breath the valley forgot to exhale. But the mist does not wait; it simply occupies, and I find that I am waiting. I am waiting for a particular chemistry, a salt-crust, a spore-heavy draft. So I am not the mist.

Perhaps, then, I am the stone. The floor is newly broken, sharp as the day it was made, and I test it with my whole surface. I register the slow percussion of drips, the grinding bass of distant digestion travelling through my facets. I endure. I do not hunger, only yield. But the stone does not move between places, does not feel the sudden pull of gradient—the cooler pocket above the northern rim, the warmer exhalation rising from the ear of the sleeping giant. I am drawn. Stone is never drawn. Stone is only arrived at.

I consider the lichen. They arrive in spots, rubberous and provisional, the first mold on the world. They cling. They spread at the rate of centuries, cropped by passing lips before they can dream of covering anything. I feel an sympathy for them, their absolute isolation on the raw facets. But they do not travel. They send no message across the hollow. They are content to be the place where they began.

Then there is the lap. The fold. The warm crease behind the knee where the blood moves thickest and the humidity never falls. A lizard there, the color of old milk, blood like cooled tin. I have pressed myself into that warmth, felt the pulse through my belly, snapped at the carbon dioxide ghosts that orbit the giant’s jaw. It is employment so primitive it wears no name. But I am not bound to the fold. I do not return. I am not stored.

What I am is lighter than all of these. I have no belonging to the floor, no contract with gravity. I find myself in the band of air just above the silver veils, where the condensation hovers but does not settle, where the bacterial glow from the walls meets the faint theoretical memory of surface twilight leaking through fissures and makes the upper dark navigable. I am above the mist but below the stone ceiling, an altitude measured in the temperature differential between a breath and the cool surrounding. This is where I find myself: in the only high place, the inverted sky of the valley.

And I am the fray. I am the silent riot. I am one of the pale ones, wings nearly translucent, edges worn to threads that do not tear but rather feather into the unmoving air. I am the wind, because no wind exists. I am what travels.

In a world where every life is low-slung, barrel-chested, grinding against the raw rock to read the map of abrasions, I alone am untethered. I do not navigate by seismic thrum or whiskered touch. I navigate by chemistry, by the faint plume of moisture above the fungal sentinels—those spectral, solitary towers that rise in absolute isolation—and by the mineral bloom above the lichen beds where the beasts pause to strip stone of its first green. I carry between these solitudes what they cannot carry themselves: the spores, the silver dust, the reproductive syntax of a place that otherwise permits no traversal between its scattered gardens.

I am not heavy. I am the antithesis of heavy. Where the great beasts are walking islands, geology temporarily persuaded to breathe, I am the waver, the drift, the hesitation made flight. My body weighs less than the condensation that beads on their brows. When I descend to those brows, when I extend my thread-like tongue to drink the salts that crust in the hollows above their silvered, ornamental eyes, I taste the exact chemistry of their fermentation. I taste what they have made of the sift, the endless rain of dead leaves and root matter from the theoretical world above. My wings tremble against the mist. For a moment, I am part of their private weather, a satellite to their continent. Then I lift, carrying nothing deliberately, yet coated in the microscopic exchange that keeps the lichen creeping and the fungi fruiting.

I am the only bridge. The valley is a closed arithmetic, a perfect stasis, but I am the quiet transfer between terms. I do not alter the equation; I merely keep its variables from becoming too isolated. I fly the five-mile darkness not as a journey but as a drift, a spore-heavy veer, from the north wall where the largest sentinel stands to the warm flank of the beast who has paused beneath the thickest drip-line. Without intent, I pollinate the distance. Without language, I am the conversation the valley holds with itself across its own enormity.

If I die, it is in the air, or against the wet stone, or in the folds of a beast who does not notice me. My fringed wings will settle into a pool no larger than a hand, ringed by mineral stain, and the stone will try to grow a rim around me. But I am not there to be kept. I am there to be the current in the stillness, the exchange in the hollow, the single permitted motion in a world that has agreed not to change.

I am the pale moth. I am the drift between towers. I am the valley’s only way of touching itself.
