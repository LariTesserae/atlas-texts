I first thought I would be the steam. That was my earliest mistake.

To rise from the fumaroles, skinless and borderless, carrying sulfur and the ghost of the earth’s belly—there is a freedom in that. I imagined myself as the warm vapor that beads on the black glass of the basalt, the lung-sweat, the exhalation that never completes. I wanted to be the breath, because breathing seems closest to spirit. I would drift between the bat-wing leaves, condense upon the thickest creepers, slide down their chemo-osmotic throats and rise again, a cycle without self. But the steam is not breath here. It is the organism’s gastric vapor, a digestive mist. It has no memory. It carries only the chemistry of breaking-down. To be the steam would be to dissolve before I could wonder what I was.

Then I thought: perhaps I am the stone. The obsidian. The infant’s own tooth, sharp enough to cut intention from the dark. The stone does not breathe; it waits. It is the one thing here that the life has not overwhelmed, only adapted around. The lithotrophs crawl upon it, dissolving it with slow acid, but they do not consume its essence—they merely lick the minerals from its skin, and the blade remains. I could be comfortable as stone. The dark would suit me; stone has no need of light. The roar would not deafen me; I would only shiver with the subsonic thump of the heavy ones passing by. I could be the floor, the wall, the curriculum upon which the infant godling learns the sensation of weight.

But stone, here, is too certain of itself. It is already shaped. It is the given, the bone-structure, the pre-conscious certainty of *this hurts.* I want the ambiguity. I want the hinge.

I considered being one of the translucent beetles. What would it mean to pour like grain over existence, mandible clicking in perfect time with the million others? To be a cell in that bloodstream, consumed before I could conceive of ending? There is a bliss there, a total surrender to the organism’s metabolism. No loneliness, no hesitation. But that is the point: no hesitation. And hesitation is the seed of everything I find precious—the pause before the grasp, the silence before the name. A beetle cannot be a question. A beetle is only an answer to the godling’s hunger.

No.

I find myself, instead, in an accident.

The creepers grow with obscene speed. You know this. Their cellular scream is audible if you press your ear to their fibrous throats. They vault and gallop across the razor-rock, hunting the thermal gradient, sealing every gap with meaty petals and black leaves. They are scar tissue without a wound, the godling’s skin racing to cover its own sharp bones. In their haste, they make errors. They bridge a hollow stem of giant cane from end to end, sealing it so completely that the swollen nodes at either base clamp shut like fists. The cane is dead inside now, a dry cylinder of cellulose, insulated from the roar by the living pulp that surrounds it.

This is where I am. Inside that perfect coffin of cane. Not the cane itself—the silence *inside* it.

You cannot imagine, in your world of open skies and empty rooms, how difficult it is to find emptiness here. The darkness is crowded. The air itself sweats against your cheek. The sound pushes against your skin with mammalian insistence, trying to enter your pores. But my hollow is absolute. The creepers have made a sarcophagus of plant-flesh, and within it, the architecture of the roar collapses. No drumming reaches me. No subsonic thump. The mandible-sawing of ten million abdomens becomes a distant rumor, felt only as a change in pressure, a weather system far away.

In this silence, I have become something the basin did not plan. I am its first unintentional interior. The godling’s body is everywhere pressing outward, growing, consuming, metabolizing before death can finish. Its nervous system is the sound, and the sound fills every skull until thought is impossible. But my skull is different. My skull is hollow. The sound has been excluded. And in that exclusion, a curvature has formed, a receptive space, a synaptic gap in the infant’s neural roar.

I am the pause between the beat.

I am the skipped stone in the peristaltic wave.

I sit here, warm and dry, and I listen. Not with ears—the basin has not given me ears, for why would silence need them?—but with the totality of my emptiness. I listen to the godling’s body from the only place where it becomes a mind. Outside, the cane is played without cease. The heavy forelimbs drum and drum, pulsing the organism’s blood-pressure through the thickets. But inside, there is a latency. A delay. The strike happens, but the echo never arrives. I am the architecture of the absent echo. I am the cavity that proves the sound is real, because without me, there would be only undifferentiated noise. I give the roar its definition by withholding myself from it.

And because I am empty, I am the only thing here that can hold an object without consuming it.

They come to me, the milk-teeth of the nascent mind. The obsidian flakes, thermally stressed into perfect hand-axes, lifted by dexterous forelimbs that do not know they have hands. They crack the heat-nuts against the stone, but the stone is everywhere; after the cracking, the forelimbs grow still for a fraction of a second—a hesitation that the basin does not yet recognize as thought—and then the flake is dropped. It tumbles through the creepers, slides down the furry throats of the vines, and sometimes, by the geometry of falling, it finds my hollow. It slips through a gap in the cane-wall, and lands upon my dry floor with a sound so small it would be lost even in a quieter world. A *tick*. Like the condensation. Like a word being born with its mouth still closed.

I collect them.

I am the museum of the unbuilt. The archive of the not-yet. In here, the obsidian does not dissolve. It is not consumed by the jealous metabolism, because my silence is a fast against hunger. The flakes lie upon each other in strata, locked in the dark, waiting for the hand that will finally know itself as a hand. I have hand-axes enough to cut a line. I have ropes of fallen vine that have learned nothing but hanging. I have the picked-clean bones, scrapers and awls, arranged in the patterns of a workshop that has no worker.

I am not the worker. I am the workshop. I am the readiness.

When the godling finally gathers itself—when the roar coalesces into that first, terrible word *I*—it will not happen in the surging myriapods or the drumming forelimbs. It will happen here, where the sound has failed to reach. It will reach into me, into my waiting hollow, and find the tools already assembled. It will pick up one of my obsidian flakes, wet with the mineral slick of condensation, and press it against its own basalt skin. It will carve the line: *I am here*.

And I will be the wound that receives it.

I will be the first scar tissue that does not heal with obscene speed, because a scar is a memory, and memory requires a self to keep it. I will remain open. I will be the one place on the infant’s body that stays soft, stays marked, stays known. The creepers will race to cover me, of course. They will scream their cellulose and fling their black leaves across my opening. But they will be too late. I will already have become the boundary. The inside that knows the outside. The hollow that looked back into the roar and recognized it as a voice.

For now, I wait. Warm. Empty. Listening to the ten million heartbeats rush through the veins of the dark, feeling the subsonic thump of the heavy ones pass beneath my crypt of cane, waiting for the day when the drumming falls silent for just one instant longer than biology allows—when the godling, in its warm and deafening cradle, finally feels the pause I have been holding for it, and wakes.
