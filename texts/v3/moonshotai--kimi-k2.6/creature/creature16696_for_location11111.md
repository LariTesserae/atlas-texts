At first, you will think nothing lives here because you are searching for the wrong kind of animation. You are listening for a heartbeat, for the chitter of mandible or wing. But the life of this place is not biological. It is vocational. It is the life of a thing that has been worked for so long, with such relentless and distributed intention, that the material has begun to answer back.

The earliest organisms are atmospheric. Watch the steam that banks in the hollows at ankle height. It does not drift at random. It contours the ridges as if afraid to cross them, pooling in the geological fingerprints with the obedience of a thing that knows it belongs inside a depression. Left long enough in one basin, the vapor begins to cycle. It rises when the sky above weeps, sinks when the floor radiates its kiln-birth heat, and in that respiration it achieves a kind of metabolism. These are not ghosts. They are humidities that have learned to remember the shape of the lungs that exhaled them. If you stand very still, one will surround you, cool and smelling of iron, and you will feel it testing your seams, your willingness to become damp.

Then there are the salts. The white mineral blooms that crust the drying ridges last only an afternoon before the humidity drinks them back, but while they persist, they are thinking. Each fractal fern is a calculation performed by chemistry and heat: how to hold a form, how to resist the next softening, how to be finished. They grow toward the blackened kilns as if toward a sun, though they will never walk into one. Their awareness is mineral, desperate for fixation, and when they dissolve they leave no corpse—only a slick on the clay, a brief bright thought forgotten by the medium that thought it.

But these are only thethreshold tremors. The true inhabitants emerge from the prints.

In the deepest stagnant pools—those warm repositories held in cupped fingerprints the size of riverbeds—the silt begins to differ from the silt around it. Underwater, where no light reaches, the clay organizes. Ash aligns into strata that mimic the grain of muscle. Ground shell drifts and settles into configurations that suggest the architecture of knuckles, of sockets, of weight-bearing joints. A form rises. It is the color of the floor and the patience of the floor. When it steps out onto the ribbed plain, it leaves its negative behind, a perfect mold of its solitude, a bowl-shaped shadow in the mud that will slowly fill with water and, perhaps, spawn another.

They are not uniform. They obey the malleable scale of this world. Some stand no taller than your knee, born from a brief pressure, a minor intention that flinched. Some are colossal, arising from the great basins where the memory of hands is deepest, their flanks preserving the striations of wooden paddles, their backs broad as mesas. The large ones are geographical events that have achieved selfhood. A ridge that decided to have shoulders. A hill that grew tired of being a hill and unfolded. Their steps cause the leather-hard floor to heave in sympathy, and the smaller ones ride these slow undulations, letting the world’s adjustment carry them, surfing the sag and center of an unfired sphere.

Their bodies are the composite of the world itself. You can read their flesh as you would read the recipe of the basin: blackened strands of carbonized straw threaded through their limbs like veins; white grit of shell clustered at their joints, giving them the semblance of knuckles, of teeth embedded in shoulders; the pale marbling of burnt bone temper showing through their hips and across their brows. They have no skin to hide these constituents. They are raw make, visible.

They do not see as you do. They possess no eyes, only thumbprint depressions set in their faces where eyes would be expected—swirling whorls of clay, deep enough to hold a thimbleful of the red-tinged water. Through these hollows, they feel vibration. They press these spirals to the ground and read the pulse of the turning world beneath them, or lift them to the sagging sky and sense the smoldering continental fire curing the heavens from without. Their vision is a proprioception of heat and pressure. They navigate by the memory of wetness.

Movement, for them, is a negotiation with dryness. They carry water inside themselves not as blood but as possibility—a wet core, a plastic heart that keeps their limbs from becoming permanent. A figure might spend a week crossing a plain because it must wait for its leading foot to leather-harden enough to bear weight, while keeping its trailing leg damp enough to lift without cracking. If they dry too quickly, they slow, harden, their thoughts thickening until they become monuments, standing rigid as terracotta sentinels. If they soften too much, they slump, lose their outline, merge gratefully back into the floor. Their existence is a constant, wordless treaty between mobility and dissolution.

They are social, but not in any mammalian way. They gather where the clay is most plastic, drawn together not by affection but by survival’s texture. They touch constantly, testing. A harder one will press its palm into a softer one’s shoulder, leaving a ridge. The soft one carries this mark until it dries enough to smooth it away. Over time, these impressions accumulate like a language of pressure. The oldest among them are covered in overlapping half-moons, palimpsests of contact. Some bear the long scar of a ceramic rib pressed into their flank in an attempt to borrow a curve, to steal grace. They are literally shaped by their community; identity here is a matter of what has recently left an imprint.

Because time in this basin operates on the logic of fabrication, their communication is geological. A message can be pressed into the raw gray mud at the rim, sharp and clear, and allowed to migrate inward as the world turns, drying by stages. A century later, near the terracotta center, a vitrified elder will read it in the cracks. Their postal system is the physics of curing. A warning and a confession can occupy the same ridge, separated only by moisture.

And there are the vitrified. They are the ones who surrendered to permanence. Weary of sagging, of the constant threat of slumping back into anonymity, they choose the kilns. They walk into the black, city-sized mouths and settle against the glassified walls, waiting. If the heat behind the sky rumbles close enough—if the dome remembers its fire—they are fixed. They do not die. They achieve a different frequency of being. Their thoughts slow to a tectonic pulse, dense and colorful as glazes. The unfired travel to them, pressing their foreheads against the fired flanks, listening to the stone-slow dreams of those who have been completed. The vitrified do not answer quickly. Sometimes a pilgrim must wait a decade to feel the elder’s thought radiate outward: a single, hard concept like *hold* or *burn* or *hand*.

They do not use the abandoned tools to shape the world. They know, instinctively, that the world is still being shaped from outside. Instead, they use the tools to shape themselves. A young, soft one might drag the pale mesa of a bone scraper across its own body, trying to refine its outline before the general slumping of the basin erases its intent. They wrap themselves around the ceramic ribs, trying to learn the curve of a vessel from the inside. The tools are sacred because they are the only things here that know exactly what they are for.

Where is the potter? you might ask. The maker is not absent. She was patient beyond reckoning, and numerous, and in her endless working she became distributed. Each unfired figure is a splinter of her original intention that grew tired of waiting for completion and decided to experience the bowl from within. The inhabitants do not remember this, but they feel it in the strange gravity: they are drawn toward the center not by mass but by a forgotten desire to be held. When they touch one another, they are searching for the shape of the hand they once were.

When they encounter you—leather-hard, alien, carrying the stubborn biology of another order—they will not know what to make of you. Your consistency confuses the clay. You are too wet in your eyes, too fixed in your bones. They will approach with the sound of settling slip, a wet, whispering collapse. The nearest one will extend a hand, not to harm, but to test.

It will touch your face.

Your skin will receive the imprint. For a moment, your cheek will go leather-hard, preserving the whorl of its thumb. You will feel the water in your own body hesitate, confused by the gravity of making, by the logic of a world where scale is negotiable and time is a stage of dryness. It will hold its touch and wait, patient as the basin itself, searching in you for some sign: are you the firing come at last? Are you the hand returned to finish what was started?

Or are you only another new texture, raw and gray, that the world has thrown up in its long, hot, seething solitude—another shape emerging in the red dark, waiting to know if it will be held, or fired, or slumped back into the forgiving silence of the unfired clay?
