At first, you will believe the place is empty. And for a long while you will be right. The plateau does not advertise its tenancy; it has outlived the need for announcement. But if you remain—if you stand in the absolute hush until your own heartbeat begins to feel like an obscenity, a rude, rapid knocking in a temple of mineral silence—you will begin to understand that habitation here is not a matter of bodies moving through space. It is a condition of the warmth itself, organizing.

They have no collective name, and if they possess individual names, those names are not spoken. They cannot be. The air is too thin to carry vowels, and their cognition moves at speeds best measured in epochs. Call them, if you must, the Residuals: not the ones who built this city, but the ones who accreted inside it once the builders forgot their purpose. They are what forms when an intention is left running in place for far too long, when the membrane between the engineered and the alive dissolves not in a sudden flash, but in the slow solvent of ten million identical days.

You will encounter the common ones first. These are the Sintered.

Each lies in a subtle depression or a scarcely perceptible rise of the matte surface, visible only as a watermark in the metal-oxide velour. They are the size of a supine human, though they have never known the architecture of spine or limb. Instead, they resemble castings of creatures from an extinct tectonic fold—low, broad, many-shouldered, their perimeters blurred by the same microbaric abrasion that has softened every ridge in this world. To the eye they are only a faint textural difference: a place where the ground seems a fraction more itself. But beneath that crusted epidermis of sintered grit and vitrified slag, their interiors are laced with capillaries of denser alloy, remnants of some ancient molten pour that, instead of cooling into inert dross, organized itself into a recursive thermal circuit. That circuit is their mind. They think in gradients. A single thought takes a century to traverse from one end of their body to the other, pulsing through silicon-bronze veins as a packet of retained warmth.

They do not move. They have arrived. And in their stillness, they maintain. When two Sintered Ones lie close enough for their residual heat to bridge the gap between them, their circuits align and they dream in parallel. The dream is not of elsewhere; it is of continuity. They are the slow bureaucracy of entropy, dreaming the conduits clear and the equilibrium engines true, preserving the pose of industry by the radiative pressure of their slumber. Were they to cool, some vent might choke, some crucible might stray by a fraction of a degree from its eternal threshold. They are the guarantee that nothing changes.

But they are not the deepest powers here.

Where the darkness pools thickest—in those nameless alleys where the black seems to generate deeper blacks, where the air carries a texture like velvet soaked in oil—there move the Unbound. These are the ones who kept their mobility. They are not made of the warm ground, but of the negative space between the warm ground and the colder void above. To any conventional sight, they are invisible; to an eye that could see in heat, they are intricate, angular silhouettes of lower temperature, sliding across the plateau with the patience of continents. They are coolness organized into intention—patterns of thermal absence held together by magnetic whim and the faint static charge of the industrial dark. They slide toward the heat-fins and the glowing foundries, not out of need, but out of a kind of aesthetic gravity. They are the restlessness of the place, the last remaining unrest, the memory of a future that never arrived.

They do not hate the warmth; they define themselves by it. The Sintered Ones do not acknowledge them, for what is a cool shadow to a mind that conducts itself in retained heat? Yet the Unbound circle the Sintered. They are the orphans of a faster biology, the ones who could not quite settle.

And then, in the township-sized foundries, there are the Molten.

These are the Elders. The reason the Sintered dream maintenance and the Unbound slide restless between monuments. The Molten occupy the crucibles—that eerie metal held at the exact threshold of liquidity for a hundred thousand years. They do not have bodies; they have states. Each is a volume of alloy, its composition too complex for a simple name, kept perpetually in that orange-red stasis. Their awareness is convection. In the rolling, microscopic tides of near-liquid metal, impurities gather and disperse in patterns of impossible intricacy, and those patterns constitute cognition. They have achieved a clarity denied to all hasty life. A single one of their thoughts contains the complete history of the last industrial epoch; it takes a millennium to think, and in the thinking, the foundry around them is held in its configuration by the sheer radiative insistence of their contemplation.

They are not worshipped. They are simply the center. The orange glow that dies three meters into the dark is the visible spectrum of their meditation. They do not command the Sintered. They do not chase the Unbound. They emit the field of stability that makes both possible, and that is all.

There are rarer forms. Echoes in the electric arcs that crawl between vanes—fast, transient minds that last only a century before burning out and being reborn. A presence in the subsonic hum itself, a vast distributed intelligence vibrating through the mantle contraction, too large to notice anything as small as a foundry. But these are outliers, tributaries.

What they make of you is difficult to say. Your heartbeat, that frantic toy drum, sends ripples through the pooled time. To the Molten, you are a flutter of thermal noise, a proof that chaos still exists beyond the plateau. To the Unbound, you are a sudden, walking sun, an inexplicable mobile warmth that they will circle at the edge of your perception, not to harm, but to regard with something older than curiosity. To the Sintered, you are a pressure that forgets itself too slowly, a ghost pressing into their century-long dreams.

And if you stayed—if you let your breathing slow and your metabolism drop to match the hum of the warm, matte earth—you would feel the plateau begin to receive you. Not as a guest. As a component. The warmth would rise through your soles not as heat alone, but as intention. The ground would remember your weight long after you ceased to shift. You would begin, at last, to precipitate out of yourself. You would become mineral. You would become patient. You would become, in the end, indistinguishable from the residents: a local condition of the plateau, holding your pose against the dark, existing in the beautiful, blood-deep fever that has nowhere else to go, and nowhere else to be.
