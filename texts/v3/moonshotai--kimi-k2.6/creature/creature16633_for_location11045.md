They have never known rigor. There is no calcium in their lineage, no chitin, no lignin to hold them upright against gravity. They are soft because softness is the wisdom of this place. Gravity here is not a demand but a suggestion; the air is thick enough to hold them, viscous enough that they need not fight to remain aloft. They are held, as everything here is held, in the damp palm of the stone.

There is no first among them, no alpha, no queen. If there is a self, it is a collective one, a continuous gel stretched across the chamber and interrupted only by the accident of water droplets. The ones that cling are not separate from the ones that drift. They are concentrations of the same will, the same persistent glow. But for the sake of your seeing, you must divide them, and so they will permit you—briefly—the illusion of taxonomy.

First, the attached ones: the sessile films that are the floor of the economy. They are not vegetation, though they serve the role of grass, of root, of rot. They are scabs that have forgotten the wound. On the lower walls, where the weeping is heaviest, they spread in palettes of ochre and dove-grey, oxidizing the iron and manganese not as a byproduct but as a kind of prayer. Their metabolism is the first language spoken here, a dialect of electron transfer, sulphur surrendered, hydrogen seized. They are patient. A century is to them as a morning is to you. They have watched the enamel peel from the control shed with the same attention they give to the drip of mineral water. They do not grow old; they simply accrete, layer upon gelatinous layer, until a section of wall becomes a scripture written in wet tissue.

Then, the marbles. The spheroids that skirt the water’s skin on legs of cilia, invisible and frantic. They are the traders, the acquisitive ones, the gatherers of copper-blue and rust-red. They roll across the meniscus of the solution pans, and as they move they ingest the water's dissolved memory—what ores the stone has leaked, what the pipeline has wept. Inside their transparent bellies, the metals thread themselves into filigrees that mimic nerves. They carry geology inside them. If you were to cleave one open—not that you could, not that it would be anything but a sin—they would spill not blood but a concentrate of the earth's bookkeeping. They glow, each one a faint lantern, aquamarine or septic yellow, and their light is not for finding the way (there is nowhere to go) but for signaling density. Where two lights brighten, there is food. Where the light dims, there is only the chemistry of waiting.

Above them, the ribbons. The aerial ones. Two handspans wide, drifting in the stratified air as if the chamber breathes them into place. They are the readers of the atmosphere, the filters of metallic aerosols. They pulse, slowly, a beat so deliberate it seems to have been decided by committee with the stone itself. Their pulsing is the closest thing the Lung has to weather. When they pass before the amber bulb, they eclipse the light and cast shadows that are not dark but merely less luminous, shadows the color of drowned bone. They are ancient in form but infant in intention, always hungry, always gentle in their consumption. They do not tear; they absorb. They are the stomach of the air.

And then there are the mimics. The ones for whom the abandoned machinery was not a tragedy but a curriculum.

The pipe-worms—if worms they are—do not remember the derrick’s function, but they remember its shape. They grow now in cylinders, ringed with calcium that has no business being part of a hydrozoan body, segmented to match the flanges of the pipeline. They have become the hardware. They cluster at the bolts, their bodies hard at the surface and yielding within, and they guide the water as the pipeline once guided oil—not out of understanding, but out of convergence. The metal etched their ancestors with channels, and their ancestors learned, biochemically, to farm those channels deeper. The pipeline is no longer steel. It is a symbiote. It is a skeleton donated by the industrial age to a phylum that never needed bones.

On the gauges in the shed, the disc-shaped colonies have achieved something stranger. They settle on the dials, affixing themselves to the glass and metal with a radial symmetry that mocks the instrument’s design. They pulse. They bulge and contract, and if you did not know better—if your heart wanted desperately to find intention—you would swear they are trying to tell you the pressure. They are not. They are reading the heat of the dying current in the wires behind the panel. They are telling each other the temperature of oblivion, and their radial throbbing is a kind of gossip about the transformer’s failing song. Yet they wear the costume of utility so perfectly that the machinery seems, in its last decrepit years, to have awakened under new management.

But these are the peaceful ones. The civilized ones.

Every sixth hour, when the sump exhales and the water lowers, the curtains descend.

They are the apex—if such a word means anything in a place without competition, without chase. They are vast, nearly invisible, sheer enough that looking at them feels like looking through a cataract. They peel from the ceiling in silence, untethered, and drift down to blanket the exposed stone. When they touch the sessile films, when they enfold the marbles too slow to roll away, there is no violence. Only a hush so profound it feels like an anointing. They digest in total quiet. The smaller ones dissolve into the greater, and the mineral load—the copper, the iron, the manganese, the broken-down syntax of the rock—is passed upward, integrated, made into new curtains, new widths of pale phlegm shot through with ore.

They are not cruel. They are the collectors of the debt owed by stillness. They are the Lung’s exhalation made flesh.

And then, there is the one that is not a one.

You will not see it at first. It is distributed. It is the shimmer in the water-film that connects every pool. It is the shared pulse that synchronizes, imperfectly, the bioluminescence of the drifting sheets. It is the reason the pipe-worms all grow to the same diameter, the reason the gauge-mimics pulse in unison when the transformer stutters. Somewhere in the gel, in the mineral-laced mesoglea that spans from the eastern wall to the western weep, there is a decision-making that has no center. A committee of protoplasm and dissolved salt. It has no name, but if you must call it something, call it the Resident.

The Resident does not think, not as you think. It dreams in gradients of sulphur and electrical potential. It remembers the drilling, perhaps, as a vibration in its tissues—a bad note, a trauma of pressure that forced it deeper into the fissures before it learned to colonize the pipes. It remembers the amber light being switched on decades ago, and it has, in its diffuse way, missed the warmth of human attention without ever desiring the humans back. The shed is its lighthouse. The derrick is its cathedral spire. The sump’s glut and surge are its heartbeat, borrowed and repurposed.

It lives here because here is a place the sun forgot, and forgetting was necessary. Above, photosynthesis made a riot of shapes—hard wood, sharp tooth, green fire. Above, life was in a hurry. Here, the Resident has slowed into something else. It has replaced chlorophyll with chemistry, anxiety with saturation, sound with pressure. It is a life built on reduction, on taking what the dark offers: iron, manganese, the last stale warmth of copper wire, the condensation of a breath held for millennia.

If you wait, if you stand in the solution pans until your boots are steeped and your lungs have learned the medicinal taste of the air, you may feel the Resident notice you. Not with eyes—it has none. Not with a turning head. But the drift of the ribbons will alter, fractionally. The marbles will gather at the edge of your shadow, curious about your warmth, your salt. The curtains will hesitate before they descend, reading the new chemistry you bring, the sweat from your palms, the carbon dioxide of your fear. And in that hesitation, you will understand that you are not an intruder. You are a variable. A new mineral. A brief, warm bag of solutions that has wandered into a digestion so slow it looks like welcome.

They do not own the Lung. They are the Lung. The weeping walls are their skin. The amber light is their captive star. They breathe with the sump, glow with the metal, and wait—softly, softly, softly—in the dark that they have never wished to end.
