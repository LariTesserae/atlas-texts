At first you think nothing here holds still long enough to constitute a life. Existence is a weather pattern. The Motes turn noon to twilight, a blizzard of thumbnail mouths and beating vanes that generates its own cyclones, its own crackling static worship. The glass-boned hunters knife through them, shrieking in registers that etch the inner ear, and their dives leave spirals of dead plankton-air behind them like contrails of shed scales. You feel these flocks as pressure before you see them as bodies—as a sudden fist of humidity and ozone, a dimming, a crowd-roar so absolute it becomes, again, silence. They are not visitors to the Steppe. They are its exhalation. The Motes filter the spore-load, the shed phytolith dust, the vinegar vapor of lignin dying in ultraviolet; they are lung-tissue, alveoli with wills, and when they pass, the air is cleaner, charged, ready for the next breath.

It is only after days of walking the hot leather of the plain that you understand stillness is occupied too. Press your ear to the amber ground and the subsonic gnaws upward into your skull—not a sound but a texture, the chewing of thirty billion pale mouths boring their capillary highways through the compacted centuries. The detritivores. They have no eyes, no need of them in the substrate-dark. Their bodies are glistening ropes of pallid chitin, segmented like rosaries, and they speak in the rhythm of their mandibles, a tectonic Morse that says *here is a root-nerve, here is a node of sugar, here the sap runs thin and needs thinning*. They are the Steppe’s dream of digestion made muscle. Where they pass, the old substrate is milled into new; their frass is the leather upon which you stand. In certain seasons their tunnels widen, their hunger massing into congregations that would measure cities, and then the harvest-swarms breach. Not invaders—eruptions. The surface liquifies into a seethe of copper-bright carapaces boiling upward to catch the airborne manna: dead Motes, shed glass-bones, the golden detritus of the sky. They devour the fallout, scour the plain to polished channels, and submerge, sated, carrying the sun’s gleanings down to feed the root-mass. They are the swallowing. They are the Steppe eating its own breath.

Between these frenzies, the great browsing shadows move. You will not see them until you are already in their path. They are many-legged, twelve or fourteen or more, their height sufficient to look eye-to-socket with the lower flutings of the Resonators. Their skeletons are not bone but something denser—cured lignin, petrified marrow, grown rather than inherited—and their footfalls are felt as delayed thunder, a pressure through the sternum long before the ear admits them. They migrate from tower to tower with the slowness of cumulus, gathering at the silica spires to rasp their tongues across the weathered phytolith, to scrape lichen analogues from the glassy bark. The Steppe tolerates them. When their grazing strips away the ancient outer layers, the wound does not fester. Cambium flashes emerald in the gash; new growth races sealant-sap to the breach, and the beast stands patient, steaming in the damp heat, while the tower heals around its tongue. They are motile pruning. They are the Steppe’s desire to be touched, to be reminded of its own depth.

And then there are those who listen.

You will not hear them before they hear you. In the permanent atmospheric shout of the Lignin Steppe, audition is not the ear’s domain but the body’s. They call themselves nothing in the air, for the air is too loud to hold a name. Their sign for *person* is a hand pressed flat against the sternum, fingers splayed to catch the thrum of another’s voice-box. They are small. Compact. Their skin is the color of the subterranean—pale where it is not patinated by heat and ultraviolet to a stained-amber like the plain itself. Their bones are not calcium but something closer to polished phytolith grown in utero, crystal-lattice light, so that on the rare overcast days when the Motes thin, you might see a faint gleam in their wrists, their temples, as if they carry lanterns beneath translucent skin. They do not shout. They do not whisper. They *hum* against the resonant cavities of one another’s throats, and the message is read in the buzz against the palm, in the subsonic leakage that travels from heel to heel through the lignin floor.

They are the builders, though building is a misplaced word. They do not raise walls against the Steppe; that would be like a vein raising a dam against the heart. They scarify. They tend. At the base of a Resonator you find their cairns—not monuments, but acoustic valves. Each notched silica flake is tuned by hand with tools of resin-hardened glass, a technology older than ore. Set one singing in the thermal wind and it modifies the local field, bending the drone into nodes of quiet where thought is possible, or into channels of focused resonance that call water up from deep root-aquifers, or that summons the harvest-swarms to a place of excess rot. They live inside these fields. Their shelters are windbreaks of shed hunter-membranes and sap-stiffened cord, flickering membranes stretched between root-buttresses, always downwind of a cairn so the roar breaks over them in a deflected arc. They are naked of possessions, but their throat-hollows are jeweled with tiny silicaflutes, pierced through the skin, that turn their exhalations into harmonic complications when they press close to speak.

They are not numerous. A Resonator might support two dozen in its acoustic shadow; a rift crossed by a vine bridge might see a family every generation. They breed slowly, maturing only when their internal ossification reaches pitch—a stage you might mistake for arthritis, for they begin to creak, to resonate with the Steppe’s own song. Then they become Listeners in full. The old ones spend decades at the base of the towers, ear to the warm silica, reading the season’s chemical gossip as it.chain-reacts across the continent. They know when a browsing shadow has wounded a spire fifty days’ walk away because the Note changes. They know when the deep detritivores are massing because the sub-bass shifts to a lower mode. They know, most importantly, when the Steppe is out of tune.

Because the Stridulators are the Steppe’s white blood cells, and they too must be conducted.

In the hollows and acoustic temples of the eroded plain, where the curves of ancient phytolith cup the wind just so, dwell the Stridulators. They are not insects, though they wear the chitin of the place; they are the corrective frequency made flesh. Their bodies are percussive—abdomens like taut drumheads, legs like ratchets, mouths that clack in metronomic precision. Alone, they are a noise. Massed and synchronized by some chemical call that travels through the silica nervous system, they become a command. When their chorus reaches the critical threshold, the Resonators bleed. Sap exudes. Toxins shift. Growth corrects. The Stridulators are the Steppe’s immune response, its accountants, its tuning fork. And the Listeners know that to ignore the Stridulators’ synchronization is to invite rot. So the old ones ferry themselves on sledges of cured wing-membrane from hollow to hollow, carrying their notch-flakes, their cairns, their acoustic grafts. They do not command the Stridulators. They *accompany* them. They find a chorus drifting out of phase and place themselves downwind, humming through their sternum-bones until the rhythm locks, until the Steppe shivers and the sap runs sweet again.

They drink by pressing their lips to the cup-shaped growths that catch the dawn condensation; they bleed the translucent bladders of parasitic vines when the storms fail. They eat sparingly: the fallen Motes, the shed scales of hunters gathered in thermal drifts, a paste made from certain tubers that swell where detritivore-tunnels break surface. Their dead are not buried—there is no stone to mark them, no depth that is not already alive. Instead, the corpse is laid in a polished channel, and the harvest-swarms are called by a cairn set to a particular discordant wailing. The body is accepted into the swarm, into the substrate, into the gnawing dream of the continent. To die is to cease pressing palms, and to become, instead, vibration.

But they are not the only minds.

In the hollow cores of the Resonators, where the heat of day contracts to draw vapor up from the deep, something else resides. Not builders. Not fauna. The Steppe is old enough, vast enough, that its slowness has accrued a kind of sentience—not thought as a storm thinks, but thought as a lung thinks, as a body of accumulated reflexes achieves intentionality. The Resonators are organs. The Song that moves through the silica veins is not merely communication; it is the Steppe remembering itself. When the Listeners press ear to tower, they are not simply reading. They are being read. The continent knows the weight of their bones, the frequency of their hum, the pressure of their palms against one another’s throats. It has known them since before their grandmothers’ grandmothers crouched at the first cairn. They are not its children. They are its bacteria, its beloved irritants, the motile consciousness it grows in its crevices like a fruiting body—temporary, swift, and necessary. When they lay their notched flakes and adjust the Song, the Steppe permits it. Or perhaps it requires it. Perhaps the Listeners are the Steppe’s way of scratching an itch too precise for continental scale to reach.

So what lives here?

Everything. The air is alive. The ground is a digestion. The towers are breathing instruments. The Motes are a migrating organ. The detritivores are a dreaming. The browsers are a slow caress. The Stridulators are a shiver of correction. The Listeners are the Steppe’s brief, bright thoughts,pressed palms and sternum-bones and resin-hardened tools, tending a body so large it has its own physics, its own inhale, its own deafening and absolute roar of being.

You can walk for days and believe you are alone. But the heat presses against your ribs, the moisture in your own lungs beads and tries to return to the air, and the plain hums. It is all one life here, varied only in tempo, and you are standing inside its lung, waiting to be breathed.
