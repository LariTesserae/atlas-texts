What lives here is a citizenship of cold.

The water boatmen hold the main offices. With their backs pressed to the root-bark ceilings of the roundhouse, they traverse the submerged chambers on four oar-legs, rowing inverted through the green-gold dimness. They know every lintel by the taste of algae on its underside. They lay eggs in the seams where the hazel ribs have begun to honeycomb, their larvae hatching into the tick of mandibles that forms the place’s idle clockwork. They do not sleep so much as anchor, drifting in the weak currents beneath the thatch, tiny trapped buoys in the architecture.

Below them, the caddisfly larvae enact the only masonry still underway. Each is a hermit engineer encased in a portable turret of grit and willow-twigs, grazing the lignified walls with the soft abrasion of a tongue on wood. They are not deliberately repairing the settlement, yet their grazing keeps the root-bark clean of the bacterial dark that would otherwise fatten and blur the channels. They preserve the negative spaces by their appetite. In the sleeping niches, where root-bolls have been hollowed into basins the size of a curled dog, the salamanders rest. They are the true occupants of the beds. Their skin is the same temperature as the meltwater. They do not breathe fast enough to disturb the silt. A pebble dislodged by a newt’s webbed foot—*tok*—against a hollow chamber is their only punctuation, and the salamanders tolerate it with the patience of furniture.

The alleys belong to the char. They nose through the straight channels with the copper flanks of minor deities, moving not with the scatter of wild fish but with the deliberation of locals who understand property. They know which seams are cooler, which root-walls harbor midge larvae in silver quorum, which corners shelter the phantom midges’ dry whisper. Trout pass through too, but they are tourists, snapping at striders with the abrupt, soft *clap* of jaws, always surprised by the acoustics. The leeches are permanent fixtures, clustering in the dark seam-berries along the peat, passive shareholders in the enterprise, absorbing the slow commerce of blood and time.

None of these creatures built the town. None of them remember the antler wedges or the bone spades. But their bodies fit the negative spaces too precisely to be accidents. The salamander curls into the root-boll basin as if cast for it. The char’s lateral line is tuned to the cataract’s thunder the way a parishioner’s ear is tuned to a bell. The water boatman’s legs are exactly the length required to clear the moss-thatch without snagging. The place has educated them, generation by generation, until their shapes are replies to its architecture.

And then there are the ones who do build.

The living willow is the acting authority, the continuation of a civic project it never named. Its pale new roots thread through the dead hazel ribs of the roundhouse, seeking downward, binding the lintels with patient increment. It does not remember the geometry of plazas, yet it thickens exactly where a post should bear load, arches exactly where a doorway should hold its shape. It grows in the obstinate logic of the settlement, a clerk copying a manuscript in a language it does not speak, faithfully reproducing the angles. The sedges stream from the wall-tops, ribbon-thin, their submerged leaves writing amendments to the town plan in photosynthetic script. The algae are the varnish of consensus—jade and ochre declaring every surface public property.

But the oldest resident, the one that holds the lease to everything, is the peat.

It is not a thing so much as a presence: a buoyant, black, breathing coalition of centuries. Alive at its edges, dreaming in compressed cellulose at its core, it is citizen and city both. It creaks like a hull in a long swell, ancient fibers grinding in their laminations, and that sound is its voice at council. It settles a finger-width per century, adjusting the plaza’s concavity with geological deliberation, voting on the elevation offloors by the mass of its own body. The sphagnum at its surface are the peat’s sensory organs, its lobbyists, drinking the glacial melt and swelling to hold the shape of the walls against the water’s patient push. Without them, the alleys would deliquesce. With them, the hamlet remains a knot of intention.

There is no decay here, only transliteration. A diving beetle trapped in a root-channelexhausts its air and does not rot but becomes a dark punctuation in the peat’s text. A char dies and its phosphorous enters the sedge, its calcium into the caddisfly’s next tower. The water is cold enough to slow chemistry to a civics. Time is syrup, and the inhabitants are suspended in it like pollen in honey.

What built this place, no one now living can say. No ash, no bone, no shard. But absence is also an architecture. The doorways are preserved with the too-clean precision of molds, and the current residents move through them like inheritors who have forgotten the will but keep the house in perfect order. It is possible the builders never left. It is possible they slowed, thickened, lignified, exchanging their single voices for this communal creak and tick and whisper. The settlement does not remember them. It remembers *for* them, using the only memory available: root seeking root, beetle clicking in the dark, peat settling into the shape of a chair that no one will ever need to sit in again, and yet remains, exactly the right size, forever.
