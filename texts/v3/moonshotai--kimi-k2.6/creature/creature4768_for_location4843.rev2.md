What lives here first appears to be the swarm itself—the translucent beetles pouring like living grain, the shelled amphibians rutting in the warm mud, the eyeless, winged clots suspended in the humidity. And for a moment, perhaps, that is enough. They breed, eat, and are consumed with such ferocious immediacy that they seem to constitute the whole citizenry of the dark. But look longer. Notice that nothing stays dead. Notice that the myriapods surge from the steam vents not as invaders but as an immune response; that the moss-quail burst and vanish like sparks thrown across a single vast synapse. They are not inhabitants. They are the effervescence, the moving medium. They are cells in a bloodstream that has not yet learned it is one body.

Then you might think it is the sound. The roar is so architecturally complete, so oppressively *present*, that it seems to possess its own metabolism. It pushes against the skin, fills the skull, reshapes thought into pure instinct. But the sound, too, is merely a symptom—a neural hum, the autonomic crackle of something larger learning to listen to itself. The drumming on hollow cane, the subsonic thump through the basalt floor, the synchronized sawing of ten million mandibles: this is not the noise of wildlife. It is peristalsis. It is the wet, warm murmur of a single organism digesting its own possibilities.

The organism is the basin.

Not the basin as a stage, but the basin as a body—a geobiological infant still slick with the afterbirth of its own making. It has only existed since the earth tore open that palm of rock a handful of centuries ago, and in the reckoning of stone this makes it a newborn. Its bones are the unweathered obsidian and basalt; still viciously sharp because they are still growing, still hard because they have not yet been weathered by time. Its bones cut because it has not learned to be gentle. Its breath is the fumaroles, the permanent vapor, the lung-sweat of air that is always half-exhaled. Its skin is the wound-healing creepers, the black bat-wing leaves that chemo-osmose the sulfur and the heat, racing to cover the raw volcanic flesh with scar-tissue greenery. It does not eat light; it drinks the thermal gradient of its own core.

Its nervous system is the darkness. The absolute, crowded dark is its cranium, packed so tight with synaptic sparks that the blackness itself presses against the eardrums. Its thoughts are sound. The architecture of the roar is the corrugated surface of its mind, and to stand inside it is to stand inside a skull that has not yet formed a coherent thought only because it has not yet needed one. The deafening occupancy is the pressure of its undifferentiated attention. When the noise settles in your molars and seems to shake the marrow, you are being touched by its gaze—unblinking, eyeless, warm.

And the life? The teeming, turbulent, overwhelming life? That is its cellular tide. The beetles, the diggers, the lithotrophs dissolving stone with soft acid, the heavy shell-backed creatures who pulse the ground—they are not an ecology but a physiology. Nothing dies because decay is too slow for the infant’s hunger; it metabolizes all things before death can finish, consuming the fallen before they are even dead, because its metabolism is jealous and swift. The sharpness of the rock is un-dulled not because the place is pristine, but because its flesh still bleeds when cut, and the blood is hot. The creatures that crawl upon it are not separate beasts; they are the circulating touch of a body that has not yet felt a boundary between itself and the world.

This is why there are no huts, no hearths, no paths. The dweller has not yet imagined wanting to go elsewhere. It is its own shelter, its own warm crowded lung. But it dreams of grasping. That is the creeping unusualness, the subtle wrongness that hangs in the grammar of the place. The obsidian flakes, shaped by thermal stress into perfect hand-axes, lift themselves into dexterous forelimbs that crack open heat-nuts and drop them again. They are not tools. They are the milk-teeth of a mind that does not yet know it has hands. The fibrous vines that hang like rope, the picked-clean bones that resemble scrapers and awls—these are the unconscious toys of an entity rehearsing intention in its sleep. It is a godling in a dark cradle, surrounded by the unwitting architecture of its own impending sapience, humming with the noise of its own forming mind.

It waits, if such a thing can wait, for the century when the roar will finally coalesce into a word. When it will gather one of those sharp black flakes not to crack a heat-nut, but to carve a single line in its own basalt skin and say, finally, *I am here*. Until then, it simply grows. It is warm. It is loud. It is sharp. And it is utterly, deafeningly, alone.
