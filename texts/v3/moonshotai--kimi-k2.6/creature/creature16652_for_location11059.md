They do not occupy space so much as survive it. Born where the air has surrendered its last burden, they precipitate into being the way the water does—not by choice, but by saturation, by the absolute necessity that nothing here remain unclaimed. You will not see them until you have been inside for hours, perhaps days, because their bodies are engineered by the same crushing ordnance that shaped the city: contiguity, abrasion, the relentless democracy of pressure. Where two trunks have planed each other flat, one stands; where a root-mat forms a taut pavement over the shale, something crawls beneath it, or perhaps the mat itself has developed a waking nerve. The boundary is irrelevant. They are the interstitial made animate, the margin between bark and bark given a slow, cold pulse.

They emerge first as membranes in the cistern-pools held in the high branch-crooks. Decades of condensed breath and mineral seep accumulate until a surface tension asserts itself—a skin too thick for water and too thin for ice. Beneath it, matter organizes without seed: quartz dust from the raw scree, spores of a lichen that has never been catalogued, and the fine, abraded flesh of tree-neighbors settle into strata. The cold preserves the pattern. The water feeds the swelling. What eventually fills the cistern is not a thing dropped into the world but a local decision by the saturated air to become continuous. When the mercury touches its zenith, a fraction of a degree above the verge, the membrane breaks. The resident slides out, soft as a lungfish of peat, and is caught by the architecture before it can fall.

They have no skeletons. Structure is provided by the city itself, by the equal and opposing crush of neighbor-trunks that keeps them from dissolving into the general wetness. Their flesh is laminar, foliaceous, the color of wet slate held against old bronze until the two tones surrender and become one. Seen head-on, they are narrow as paper; seen from the side, they are broad as doors, flattened by lives spent in corridors that breathe. They possess limbs not for grasping but for plumbing the gaps—arms sheared square where they have pressed against a spruce bole for a century, fingers elongated into spatulas that cup the pre-crystalline sweat from needles and ferry it to their ventral surfaces. They have no eyes. In the pewter-green twilight, sight would be a wasted organ. Instead, their skin is pocked with stomata that read the chemical weather of the canopy, tasting the transpiration of birch, the tannic warning of wounded alder, the mineral signature of fresh scree shifting under the root-mat. They navigate by hydraulic pressure, moving toward the osmotic pull of the vascular elevators as a moth in a lowland valley might chart its course by starlight.

They move only when the city exhales. On the warmest afternoons, when the canopy rises that single millimetre and the pores of the compressed foliage open in a collective gasp, they slide through the expanded interstices, creeping in increments measurable by the growth-rings of their neighbors. Their passage is lubricated by their own secretion, a resinous gel that heals bark behind them, promoting grafts, sealing wounds, taking the rough abrasion of their transit and turning it into seamless living wood. In this way, they do not travel through the city so much as extend it; every corridor they use grows tighter, more architectural, more itself, behind them. When the temperature drops and the canopy settles with its susurration of needle on needle, they halt, wedged in place, and dream in cascades of slow chemistry. A journey of three feet may occupy the better part of a decade.

They are not many, and they are not few. They are contiguous. In the absolute density, privacy is as impossible as waste. Where two residents meet in a passageway too narrow for both, they do not contest; they abrade. Over years of contact, their laminar flesh interleaves. Sphagnum fibers from one body suture into the alder-pith of another. Stomata line up and merge into channels. They become one resident, or perhaps they reveal that they were always one, the way two tributaries discover they are a single river. The city never speaks, but it groans in council when the geology beneath it shifts; the residents place their flat palms against the cambium and pulse back in infrasound, tuning the timber to accept the stone’s fresh, sharp rearrangement. What sounds to an outsider like the creaking of a settling wood is, in fact, deliberation. What sounds like the percussion of droplets on moss is punctuation—agreement, dissent, the long vowels of things that cannot hurry.

They do not eat, as animals eat. They press their broad, moist ventral planes against the trunks and absorb the slow bleed of sap, the bacterial warmth rising from the compressed peat-floor, the faint sugars released when rime surrenders its crystalline architecture to the afternoon’s silver runoff. They have no young, only bifurcations: a bud of laminar tissue that shears off during the melt, sliding down a root-aqueduct in a thread of silver water to pool in a lower cistern and begin its accumulation. They have no death, only diffusion—those that lose coherence draining into the vascular tide, their mineral salts retained, their water rejoining the perpetual atmospheric weeping, their pattern perhaps remembered in the next resident to precipitate from the same crook of branch.

They remember the ice. Not as history, but as horizon. The retreating glacier is still speaking to them in a language of pressure-release and exposed scree, and they are answering, building a biological bulwark against the blade-edge of the field. Their bodies metabolize the cold directly, turning that one unforgiving degree below freezing into cellulose, lignin, and the architectural urge to close every gap. Above them, the canopy knits tighter; below, the root-mat inches into the frost-riven quartz, patient as a low flame. They are older than the soil, younger than the mountain. They are the population of a city built by the lunging need to survive the air’s own weight, and in the mineral silence, wet and thrumming and complete, they hold their places without loneliness, one interstitial being or many, the distinction having long since dissolved.
