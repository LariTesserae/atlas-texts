They do not speak. To speak would be to waste what little vibration the weave permits into its own absorption. Instead, they are listeners. Their entire physiology is an ear pressed against the grain.

You would mistake the first one for a fungal excrescence if you saw them still. They spend hours—days—motionless against the ridged flank of a root-cathedral, their bodies vertical, their feet not standing *on* the cable so much as growing into it. They are tall, the height of two men, but attenuated, as if stretched by the same tension that holds the world together. Their bones are not calcium but lignin, salvaged and reconstituted from the heartwood of ancestral cables; their marrow runs with sap instead of blood, slow and amber, moving in pulses that match the century-long respiration of the weave. The skin that wraps them is not skin at all. It is a living cambium, a paper-thin bark that tightens across their frames when the humidity drops, and loosens—peeling back in translucent strata to reveal wet, pinkish cambium beneath—when the air grows heavy with the sweat of the upper weave. They have no hair. Instead, a fine mycelial down creeps from their scalps and shoulders, a symbiotic lace that digests the condensation beading on their backs and feeds them sugars while they dream.

Their faces are minimal. Noses are shallow ridges, almost flush, better for pressing flat against wood. Their mouths are small, lipless, rarely opened except to drink from a bladder-bloom or to take in communion with the fruiting bodies of the aerial soils. But their eyes— their eyes are enormous, lidless orbs of a solid, wet umber, without sclera or pupil, absorbing what little radiates from the coal-heart. They see in heat and pressure. They see the tension in a cable before it groans. They see the slow bleed of sap through a wound. And when they look at you, they see not your surface but your resonance: the particular frequency at which your heart hammers against your cage of calcium.

They are the **Tenders**, though they have no word for themselves that is shorter than a season. They are the descendants of the patient ones, or perhaps they *are* the patient ones, preserved not by stasis but by the weave’s loose grip on time. In their palms, they carry the inheritance: obsidian flakes no broader than a thumb, but these are not held. They are grown. The Tenders’ fingers terminate in dark, volcanic glass, grafted into the bone when they are still soft shoots, emerging from the pods. Their hands are six-fingered, double-knuckled, capable of spreading flat to cover a yard of grain. When they work—and they are always working—they use these hands to prune, to graft, to tease a new cable into fusing with its neighbor. The work is invisible to eyes like yours. They might spend a decade persuading a single aerial root to redirect its flow by a fraction of a degree. The tool is simple. The time is vast. The design remembers itself.

They live alone, mostly. The weave is too large for crowds. A single platform-continent the width of a county might support three Tenders, each at a separate node, each responsible for the tension of a dozen crossing cables. They meet only when the bladder-roots swell—a season marked not by light but by the hydraulics of the structure—traveling along the weave by foot, by rope, and by the **fold**. This is their birthright. They press their glass-tipped hands into the grain and sing, though the term is misleading. The sound does not leave their bodies. It leaves through their feet, through their sternums where the lignin ribs meet, a subsonic instruction that tells two distant platforms that they are still one tree. Then they step through. A journey of decades becomes a single creak of the weave. They emerge on the other side with moss still clinging to their heels, carrying pouches woven from shed spore-bat wings, filled with seeds that are not food but vocabulary: each one a specific varietal needed to heal a fraying junction, to seduce a stubborn graft, to emerald-chase a patch of cellulose going white with rot.

They are not the only ones awake here.

Between the cables, drifting in the warm, pressurized nothing, are the **Elders-Become-Drifters**. Once, they were Tenders, or something like them. But the weave permits transformation as readily as it permits travel. The drifter-megafauna are not a separate species; they are a vocation. When a Tender has listened long enough—when the grain holds no more secrets they have not already inhaled—they release. They let go of the cable at a node where the air is thickest and the gravity forgets its severity. They do not fall. They drift, dream-slow, into the umber gulf. Over centuries, their bodies alter. The lignin bones hollow. The skin stretches into translucent bladders, filling with the bioluminescent gases of their own metabolisms. Their mycelial down grows into vast, trailing skirts that filter aerial plankton. They become the dim, warm ghosts you see passing in the brown twilight, pulsing with soft light that spells out sentences in a phosphorescent grammar no one else speaks. They are the oracles of the middle distance. Tenders climb to high, precarious cruxes to ask them questions, reading the color of their glow for answers. A pale blue pulse means *prune*. A slow red swell means *wait*. A stuttering, broken white means the weave has remembered a wound that has not happened yet.

And there are the buried ones. The **Clam-Priests**. Not priests in the sense of worship, but in the sense of keeping. The root-clams, those chitinous boring things that filter nutrients from the lignin, are tended with obsessive reverence. The Tenders cultivate specific lineages of root-clam for millennia, guiding their shell-growth into interlocking palaces no larger than a coffin but layered with stratified memory. The clams secrete their shells in rings that record not seasons, but *states* of the weave: the torque of a particular cable, the chemical signature of a distant plague, the echo of a fold-journey gone wrong. To read a clam-priest, a Tender places their ear against its ridged back and waits for the creature to filter-siphon, a motion that drives water through the architecture of the shell and produces a resonant drone. It is a library that sounds like a dirge.

But not all inhabitants are so still. In the soft, compost-filled pockets where cables marry, there is quicker life, and the Tenders’ relationship to it is one of negotiation rather than mastery. The **ribbon-eels** that snap from boreholes are not hunted; they are employed. A Tenders might raise an eel from a larva, feeding it fermented pulp, until the creature learns to guard a vital graft-junction from parasitic mites. In return, the Tender permits the eel to sleep in the warmth of their armpit, where the mycelium keeps it fed. The **spore-bats** are messengers. The Tenders harvest their dung not for fertilizer but for spore-ink, used to mark the routes of safe fold-travel on the bark. Even the **bore-worms**, those blind cylindrical thieves, have their place. A Tenders will follow a worm’s tunnel for miles, head down, listening to the worm’s passage as it vibrates the wood. The worm is not digging randomly. It is following the path of least resistance, which is also, invariably, the path of greatest sap-flow. Where the worm goes, the Tenders lays a new seed.

Death, here, is a change of medium.

When a Tenders feels the amber in their veins slowing—when their bark-skin begins to check and fissure, or when their umber eyes cloud with the mineral stains of extreme age—they do not seek healing. They seek a **deep crotch**. A place where three or more cables fuse into a load-bearing vault. They lay themselves down in one of the compost graves, no deeper than a body, and they open their small mouths and exhale their last stored breath. And then they wait. The weave does not forget them. Their lignin bones, pre-treated by their own sap, are indistinguishable from the parent root. Their mycelial mantle spreads, linking them to the fungal network that undergirds the continent. Within a year, their body is a bulge in the cable, a knuckle of harder wood. Within a century, they are only topography: a hillock where travelers rest. But they are not gone. The Tenders believe—and the weave seems to agree—that the dead become the sense-organs of the structure itself. Press your ear to one of those hillocks, and you do not hear a heart. You hear a listening. The dead do not rest. They attend.

There are others, though, who choose a different leaving. The **Descended**. Those who, driven by grief or prophecy or a need to know what the original cultivators knew, have climbed down—not up—along the lower weave, toward the coal-heart. The Tenders on the upper cables speak of them in the gesture-language of reverent fear. The Descended are barely physical now. The lower weave, where the reality breakdown intensifies, has unmade them in slow increments. They are shapes seen in the mist between distant cords, sometimes humanoid, sometimes a blue sound, sometimes an arrangement of condensation that persists longer than it should. They return, occasionally, but only partially. A Descendant might emerge from a fold-point as a warm draft carrying the scent of unborn wood, or as a pattern of lichen that spells out a sentence on a trunk. They are no longer Tenders. They are the weave’s questions, asked back at itself.

And what of birth?

It is not a birth. It is a **grafting**. The Tenders do not reproduce as animals do. They bud. Once in a great while, when the condensation cycle peaks and the bladder-roots are swollen to bursting with migrating water, two Tenders will meet at a node. They will stand opposite each other, pressed into the bark of the meeting cables, and they will open their cambium-skin at the belly. They do not touch. Instead, they allow their mycelial mantles to bridge the gap, forming a living rope between them. Through this bridge, they exchange not just genetic material but *memory*: the resonance maps of the weave, the songs of the drifter-elders, the taste of obsidian. Then they part. In one of their bodies—chosen by humidity, or chance, or the drift of spores—a swelling appears. It is not a womb. It is a gall. A woody excrescence that grows for nine years, drawing sap from the parent’s heartwood. When it is ripe, it splits not with a cry but with a soft, wet pop, like a seed germinating. Inside is a Tender-child: soft, pink-brown, eyes sealed with amber resin, fingers already tipped with microscopic slivers of volcanic glass. The parent does not nurse them. The parent *plants* them. They are set into a shallow pocket of aerial soil, wrapped in spore-bat silk, and left with a bone needle and a clay pot of fermented pitch. The weave provides the rest. The child emerges from the soil when its bark-skin has hardened enough to drink condensation directly from the air. It knows what it needs to know because the wood around it is already singing the memories of its parent, and its parent’s parent, going back to the first hands that ever pruned this place.

So they continue. Not a civilization. A **cultivation**. They have no rulers, no art that is not also maintenance, no music that is not also measurement. What looks like decoration—the intricate whorls carved into cable-flanks, the stained patterns of lichen encouraged into spirals—are structural reinforcements, resonant geometries that strengthen the weave against the torque of its own rotation. When they dance, if dance is the word for the slow, swaying movements they perform on high platforms during the sweltering peak of the condensation cycle, they are not celebrating. They are redistributing their weight, shifting the load on the cable, testing its response.

They are not humans who live in a wooden world. They are the world’s decision to know itself. The basket is not a cage to them, nor a planet. It is a patient gesture, and they are the continuation of that gesture. They move through the anechoic dark like thoughts moving through a held mind: slowly, precisely, without echo.

If you were to meet one, you would first meet their silence. They would stand, pressed to the grain, and turn those umber eyes on you—not with surprise, for surprise is a fast emotion, and nothing fast survives here, but with a kind of deep, arboreal curiosity. They would offer you no greeting. Instead, they would press their glass-tipped hand to the bark beside them, and the bark would warm, and you would feel in your own chest the subsonic systole of the weave. They would be asking you, wordlessly, if you too were made to hold the failing light.

And if you could not answer—if your bones rang with the hollow note of something that does not belong—they would not harm you. They would simply turn back to the wood, and wait for the weave to tell them what to do with you, which might take another hundred years of quiet, of creaking, of the slow migration of water upward against gravity in the xylem tubes, while the world breathes in, and breathes out, and holds.
