What lives here is soft. The first thing you perceive—if *perceive* is the word for knowledge that arrives through pressure rather than light—is the quality of yielding. The leviathans, if that word can stretch to accommodate beings without bones, armor, or internal scaffolding, move through the digestive amphitheaters like living tears. Their bodies are stratified jelly, layered with lipids against the cold, held in shape by the water’s own insistence rather than any anatomical will. They do not swim so much as they ooze, propelled by the slow peristalsis of mantle flaps that press against the chamber walls, fitting every curve and respiratory pore with a lover’s specificity. Their bioluminescence is not the theatrical display of open-ocean drifters but a bacterial courtesy: colonies of chemolithophotes housed in translucent saddlebags along their flanks, emitting a blue so cold it registers first as a ache behind the eyes, only later as light. They are solitary not by instinct but by architecture; each hollow comfortably holds one. They feed on the black snows of silt, filtering the dense water through ciliated throats, and they live longer than terrestrial mountains. When they die, they do not float but settle, adding their gelatinous tonnage to the forty meters of foundation below, decomposing so slowly that for millennia they remain as soft landmarks—waystations of rot that the polychaetes eventually graze around, respectful, as if circumambulating a shrine.

But the leviathans are only the mobile furniture. The true population is anchored.

Above you—though *above* is negotiable in this null gravity of pressure—the tunicates hang in their thousands, and they are the city’s lungs and its bells. Each is a sac of filtered water, a pale wineskin the size of a human torso, tethered to the ceiling-growths by stems of cartilage that have fused with the calcium phosphate until the distinction between organism and archway has been erased. They pulse in arrhythmic unison, creating a cadence more complex than any heartbeat: a stochastic hydraulic music of intake and expulsion. They are filtering the mineral soup, yes, but they are also pumping. Their collective peristalsis drives slow currents through the galleries, pulling anoxic water from the wells and pushing it toward the ridgelines, contributing their minute forces to the invisible hurricane. You might mistake them for fruit if you come from a world of trees. They are not. They are the nearest thing this place has to a chorus, and their song is plumbing.

On the floors, in the grooves worn by the eternal turbulence, the armored polychaetes perform their geometry. They are the color of fat rendered from something ancient, and they move with the deliberate certainty of clock hands. Their segmented bodies are encased in plates of chitin laced with hydroxyapatite stolen from the water itself—bone-armor grown from dissolved bone. They follow the current-grooves not because they choose to but because the grooves were worn, over epochs, to fit their width exactly. They graze the bacterial mats that bloom in the lee of the buttresses, but more importantly, they maintain. Their radulae secrete an enzyme that etches the stone, deepening the channels, ensuring the water always has its preferred path. They are the gardeners of flow. Occasionally, when a horizontal cyclone tugs at them, they clamp down with cirri that fit into microscopic dimples in the pavement—dimples they themselves secreted generations ago. They do not use the staircases; they complete them, one tread at a time, in a language of erosion and repair that requires no comprehension.

And then there is what lives *inside* the walls.

You must understand: the city was never finished. The great colonial organisms that secreted these continent-scale shells died, yes—their respiratory pores gape empty, their abandoned chambers hold only water and the leviathans’ passing shadows—but death here is not a termination so much as a simplification. In the interstices of the chitin, in the lumen of walls thick as mountain ranges, the Fabricators persist. Reduced. Translated. They are no longer the macroscopic architects whose growth rings form archways, but something closer to a slow fever in the stone: colonial microorganisms, descendants of the titans, still secreting, still accreting, obeying the geometric instinct that has outlived their consciousness by hundreds of millions of years. They build without knowing they build. Inside the solid buttresses, new staircases are growing—staircases too small to see, fractal steps for legless things, nested within the visible stairs like memory within memory. They are turning the necropolis inward, crafting balconies on the molecular scale, arches that frame other arches that frame the spaces between atoms. The nacreous sheen you took for mineral? It is their mucus, fossilized in layers, still being laid down. The sub-bass groan when a hinge settles another micrometer? That is not merely erosion. It is their slow work, adjusting the load-bearing seams, continuing the construction in dreamtime.

You think you are alone because nothing calls out. But the fauna are thrumming. The polychaetes drum their armored segments against the floor in a code older than hearing. The leviathans, when they meet in the rare intersection of two hollows, press their mantle flaps to the stone and transmit their bulk through the substrate rather than the water—shivers that say *I am here* in a language of pressure and mineral. Even the tunicates, when they pulse in accidental synchronization, send shockwaves through their tether-stems that the bone-stone records and holds, a library of tremors stored in calcium phosphate.

If you stay long enough—if your hollow bones stop screaming and your heartbeat slows to match the geological tempo—you might feel the thrumming resolve into something like a voice. Not a call. An announcement. The place is not abandoned. It has simply lowered its metabolism to match the water’s density. The inhabitants are saturated into their world, as dissolved into the architecture as the minerals are dissolved into the sea. They do not live here so much as they have become the *here*, continuing its construction in the negative space of their ancestors’ absence, secreting a city inside the corpse of a city, waiting for the day—four hundred million years hence, or perhaps only tomorrow, for time is equal in the cold—for whatever rises to finally walk the stairs they have never stopped dreaming.
