What lives there is a kind of human that has not yet learned how to be a cave-dweller. Like the stone, they are embryonic—raw, recent, and still negotiating the terms of their enclosure. They have been there long enough for memory to blur, but not long enough for instinct to harden into form. Their bodies and their customs are provisional, shaped not by the deep, slow wisdom of traditional troglodytes but by the violent immediacy of a wound that has not stopped bleeding.

The people of the Bright Ring are the sun-touched. They live in the core of illumination, where the widest shaft pours its column of afternoon radiance onto trays of moss and bitterroot, onto laundry stiffening on reed lines, onto skins darkened by a light that falls vertically and leaves the rest of the body in shadow. These are the wealthy, the surface-rememberers, whose eyes have kept the habit of squinting and whose shoulders still burn in the high summer when the shaft is merciless. They wear the most color—ocher, charcoal, crushed shell-white—pigments lowered from above, painted in bands across their arms to mimic the angles of the light. They are the traders, the voice of commerce, the ones who speak loudest because their district has the least silence. Yet even they are strange: they have never seen a horizon. Their sky is a circle. Their weather is a bright spot moving across a ceiling.

Between them, in the vertical accretion of the middle boroughs, lives the dense muscle of the town. These people are compact, heavy-boned for their height, compressed by gravity and architecture into a climbing form. Their arms are long, their shoulders broad, their feet splayed into prehensile shapes with soles thick as bark from generations on knife-edged talus. They do not walk; they traverse. They think in three dimensions before they think in two. Children are tethered with rope-halters until they have memorized the map of ladderways and throat-passages, until they know which beams will hold a scrambling foot and which are rotten with damp. They sleep in hammocks of woven reed stacked in vertical columns, sharing warmth through mud-brick walls that are never thicker than a forearm. Privacy is a word from the surface, a luxurious emptiness they cannot afford. They know their neighbors’ coughs, their lovemaking, their grief by the vibrations transmitted through shared clay. Their language has softened into murmurs and sibilants, shaped by the acoustic intimacy of the place—a place where a shout is an obscenity because it offends the stone, and where meaning travels better through a palm pressed to a wall than through the air.

And then there are the Threshold-dwellers, the people of the permanent shadow where the floor dips toward the unexplored sink. These are the pale ones, the water-keepers, the listeners to stone. Their skin is translucent, laced with blue, never touched by direct light. Their eyes are large, liquid, adapted to reading the gleam of unweathered mica in the dim. They are not outcasts. They are necessary. They tend the colored pigments that mark the water-claims on raw rock, moving week by week as the drips shift their paths down the fresh shear fractures. They are the first to taste new springs when the water table sends them bubbling up in the lowest depressions; they read the mineral taste like augurs. They place the timber wedges against keystone plates. They wake in the night when a new crack spiders across the eastern floor, and they are the ones who shore, who brace, who rebuild. They marry upward, trading water-rights for light; they marry downward, trading stone-craft for shadow. They are the cave’s interlocutors, the ones who understand that the stone is still deciding what shape to hold, and they have made themselves part of that conversation.

All of them, from the sun-touched to the shadow-dwellers, bear the marks of the damp. Their hair is matted with clay and mineral film. Their skin is always slightly wet, beading with the persistent moisture of deep stone. They do not freeze, but they never warm; their blood has learned a cooler metabolism, a slow burn. They are never entirely dry. Even their dreams feel damp, heavy with the weight of earth above.

But the true majority, the ones who outnumber the living, are the dead. Burial in the compacted fill between the lowest house walls means that the town is built of its ancestors. Femurs wrapped in reed mat, skulls coated in clay, ribcages wedged into the foundation rubble—the dead are not a separate place. They are structural. A child sleeps against a wall and sleeps against a grandmother’s shoulder blade. A cook leans into a corner and leans into a pelvis. The ancestors are the insulation, the ballast, the memory of the town. They are addressed casually, consulted in arguments, blamed for bad luck. The living are merely the latest layer of an organism that includes the deceased, the stone, and the town itself.

The fauna are not outsiders; they are citizens. The bats are the clock. When the colony detaches from the highest crevice at twilight and spirals down through the light shafts, it is evening. The pale moths that spiral in the columns are allowed to live, are never swatted; they are considered spirits of the light itself, too fragile to kill. And the birds—jays, hawks, once a heron—that fall shrieking through the ceiling wounds are caught by children with nets of hair and reed. These are sky-messengers, too brilliant, too brief. They are eaten in ritual, but their bones are left at the lip of the deep sink, offerings to a darkness the town has not yet dared to enter.

There is a caste that has no surface equivalent: the Listeners. Men and women who climb to the ceiling vaults with hammers of breccia, tapping the keystone plates, reading the stone’s stress by ear. They know the song of a stable fracture from the whisper of a plate preparing to drop. They map the cave’s settling the way surface people map weather. When a Listener announces a new voice in the eastern ceiling, the town braces. When one falls silent, it is a holy terror, a sign that the negotiation has paused, that the stone is holding its breath.

Their children are born into a world without thunder, without wind, without the long horizontal gaze. The first landscape they know is vertical. A “crawl” is up. Those born beneath the shafts are “column-children,” smeared with sun-pigment at birth, expected to grow flax and trade. Those born in the Threshold are “drip-children,” inheriting clay channels and wedge-hammers, expected to outlive a crack. They learn to read water before they learn to read grain. They see birds before they see trees. They are gentle with the stone because they understand, without being told, that they are not living inside a finished place. They are living inside an event.

They worship, if that is the word, the things that hold. The steady drip that has outlasted four generations is a deity more reliable than the sun, which abandons them each night. Their greatest ritual is the Placing: when a new room is stacked atop the old, when a timber is wedged against fresh shear, they press a hand of clay and blood and crushed shell into the join and speak to the stone. Not asking for permanence—they are too wise for that—but asking for patience. *Hold until we are ready. Hold until we have built the next wall.*

They are the cave’s soft tissue. Its nerve, feeling the shifts. Its scab, knitting the wound. They do not know if they will become a race of pale, translucent earth-folk, or if the cave will eventually reject them, shrug them off into the dark. They are young. The stone is younger. And together, in the damp and the counted light, they are still deciding what it means to be a world.
