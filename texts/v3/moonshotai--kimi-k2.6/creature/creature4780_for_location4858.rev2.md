They discover early that stillness is the only architecture they need. Children in this place do not cry out; they learn before they can walk that the thick amber will swallow any sharpness, and so they lie suspended in the warm fluid, nostrils barely breaking its meniscus, waiting for the dense medium to lift them. Their bones are mineral-heavy, thickened by generation upon generation of resisting a gravity that here must be pushed through rather than merely borne. Their bodies are long but compact, built for the narrowness of fractures: elongated torsos to span the gap between warm wall and warm wall, dense legs to anchor them while they work, fingers that have grown improbably lengthy and prehensile, able to span a basalt prism and feel the infinitesimal slow collapse of cooling stone. They do not swim, for this is not water, but they move with the labor of wading even when they walk, each step a negotiation with the syrupy atmosphere, each gesture undertaken in the tempo of continents.

Their faces are not what you would seek first. Eyes, in this twilight, are secondary organs—small, dark, shielded behind a slow nictitating membrane that keeps the copper-thick fluid from drying upon the cornea. More vital are the palms, the soles, the wide pads of their fingertips, which map heat the way another species might read a face. Their true visage is their skin itself, and the colonies they grow upon it. From birth they are inoculated with the same bioluminescent molds that drape the cliffs. Over decades, careful attendants paint spiraling patterns across the infants’ throats, palms, and chests, seeding the dermis with light. By maturity, each dweller carries a living lexicon upon their body: fingerprints of phosphor green, fungal gold, bruised violet, pulsing in rhythms too slow for a rushing heart to catch. Two passing in a fluid-filled artery do not speak—that would be a lost expenditure of breath. Instead they halt, press glowing palm to glowing palm, chest to chest if the crevice permits, and exchange pulses. A conversation is the interweaving of spirals, a synchronization of light that may require the turning of a thermal vent’s whole cycle. Grief, warning, consent, and memory are all conducted in this full-contact braille, their bodies entirely legible to one another in the gloom.

Because of this, they have never developed a word for loneliness. Their settlement is a saturation of touch. They sleep in vertical stacks in the terraced chambers, three or four to a thermal nook, woven together by the silica-hair ropes they braid not merely for tools but for the binding of lives. A child belongs not to a pair but to the column of adults who occupy the warm fracture at the time of its emergence. Passed from chest to chest, the infant gathers its first skin-patterns from the collective, drinking warmth from a dozen bodies until its own bioluminescence awakens. They are creatures of density. To be alone is to be cold; to be cold is to be dead.

Their hands are their theology. Each finger ends in a nail blackened and thickened like a shard of the mountain’s own obsidian, useful for scoring the raw stone but sensitive enough to detect a fractional degree of heat loss across a basalt face. They wield scrapers of volcanic glass and levers of charred bone, but these are only extensions of the patience in their wrists. Their great labor is the persuasion of geology. They carry cultures of mineralizing bacteria in their mouths, warm and alive, transferring them in spit-wetted handfuls to the lips of fresh vents. With ropes of silica-hair they weave screens to tempt a lava flow into shading itself, to tease a cooling arch into spanning a gap. A habitable tube may occupy the attentions of twelve contiguous generations, each adding a layer of silicate upholstery, a terraces of persuaded stone. They do not build. They midwife. When a new chamber finally holds its own warmth, they do not carve their names into it. They enter it quietly, install their thermal gardens of chemophore frond and fungal slab, and regard it as one might regard a child who has finally chosen to sleep.

They eat as they do all things: slowly, completely. The kelptrees and bacterial mats of their gardens are scraped with obsidian, fermented in the heat of their own gut chambers, and wrung of every chemical whisper. They eliminate rarely, their digestion a geological process in miniature, their waste a dry mineral ash that is pressed into the mortar of cooling stone without disgust. There is no distinction between the consumed and the structure that consumes—it is all one cycling warmth.

In the deepest chamber-halls, where the subterranean bass note of the planet’s settling is felt strongest in the hollow of the chest, they gather. Not to speak—the silence here is absolute—but to sway. Hundreds of them, spiral-patterns dimming and flaring in unison, move with the mountain’s own rhythm, a slow communal undulation that acknowledges the heat they share. There is no leader. There is no need. They are a single organism of distributed warmth, patient beyond the scope of seasons.

Death, when it comes, is merely the final cooling. When a heart slows until it can no longer be told apart from the vent it has tended, the body is not buried. Burial would suggest a separation from the place that is still becoming. Instead, the dead are carried to the freshest, angriest fracture, laid against stone that has not yet decided what shape to take, and offered to the heat. The bacteria they carried in life bloom across their cooling tissues; the molds they wore dim and then steady into a permanent faint glow. In death, they become the first unable layer of a new persuasion, their chemistry entering the slow argument with the rock. Their bones, over centuries, take on the black glassy sheen of nascent obsidian. The settlement does not memorialize them. It grows through them.

And so they continue: faces like faint lanterns in the syrupy dark, shoulders brushing in passageways carved by consent rather than conquest, tending the gardens of a world that has not finished being born. They possess no word for eternity. Instead, they have a gesture—a motion of both hands pressed flat against warm stone, fingers spreading in a spiral of light that means: *I remain. I have not yet finished.*
