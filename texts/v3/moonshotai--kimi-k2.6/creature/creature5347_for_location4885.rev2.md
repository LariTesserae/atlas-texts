What lives here does not grow. It accretes, molts, and digests.

Begin with the floor, for the floor is as much a creature as anything that moves upon it. The primary inhabitant—and there are billions, enough that their mass out-weighs the stone they graze—is a radial thing the size of a fist, though “fist” implies a grasping symmetry foreign to its nature. Its body is a low, calcified saucer, a petrified lily pad of piebald chitin, vaguely greasy to the touch, worn smooth by the friction of its neighbors. From the rim extend the limbs: nine, or eleven, or seven, always odd, always refusing the bilateral contract of earthly bodies. Each leg terminates in a hooked tarsus fine as a needle, and these hooks do not merely walk; they *play* the stone. The creatures churn in logarithmic spirals, each body orbiting its own axis while the whole mass rotates like a slow, seething gear. Their contact is a constant percussion—tok-tok-tok—prime-numbered rhythms that echolocate through the stone itself. They have no head. The mouth is central, a valve of five crystalline flaps that opens onto a rasping star. They graze on mineral salts, on the compressed chitin of ancestors, and on one another when the moment demands it. Individually, they are stupid as pebbles. Together, they are a computing carpet, a navigational matrix sending news of pressure and gradient through the basin’s bed. You do not step upon them; you step into a single, continent-scale organism that has simply spaced itself into nodes.

Above this churning substrate, the middle air is claimed by the Drift—those translucent, gas-filled sacs that refuse the name of jellyfish though they resemble them. Each is the size of a human head, but they possess no brain worth naming. Their beauty is in their emptiness: a membrane so thin it shows the ghost of veins, a hydrogen pocket harvested from the sulfuric fermentation of their own gut flora. They rise because chemistry insists they must, and they school with the eerie choreography of starlings, though they have no eyes to guide them. Perhaps they follow ionic trails, or perhaps the clicking of the floor speaks to them in frequencies we cannot hear. From each sac trail stinging threads, long as a forearm, fine as spider-silk, that harvest the microbial phosphorescence beading in the mineral sweat. They do not hunt; they precipitate. When a mote of drowned light touches a thread, it is paralyzed, reeled in through a tubule, and rendered into tautness, into lift. They are the grazers of the sky, and their constellations shift like slow breath.

Between them, making the air into an obstacle course of flesh and resonance, move the Unsighted. The winged predators are black with a reflectivity like wet obsidian, their bodies laterally compressed into daggers that can slit the heavy air without screaming. Evolution here has abandoned the front-facing gaze; they have no eyes, only a sheath of vibration-sensitive setae that coats every joint. They navigate by assaulting silence. In flight, they snap their wing-cases open and shut—crack-crack-crack—a percussive lighthouse that illuminates nothing visible but charts the acoustic topography of the basin in rebounding shockwaves. They have too many knees; their legs fold forward and backward with equal facility, allowing them to perch upon vertical stone or snatch prey from the carpet without alighting. Their predation is a local catastrophe of silence. When they strike, the sudden cessation of their snapping creates a vacuum in the roar, a momentary pocket of absolute hush that is more terrifying than thunder. They do not kill and then land. They mate in mid-air, eat in mid-air, and even their young, dropped as nymphs into the viscous silver river below, swim upward against the current until they can take flight and join the crack-crack chorus. They are the only true commuters of this place, and their route is the soundscape itself.

Highest, clinging to the stone that weeps upward against gravity, are the Fixed Ones—those blind, porcelain tubes. They began, perhaps, as larvae in the brine-silver river, wriggling until they found a sweating spire and attached, never to move again. Their bodies simplify immediately into pure appetite: a hollow cylinder of white tissue, blind at both ends, pulsing with the slow peristalsis of mineral broth being drawn through microscopic radulae at the base. They are sessile not by weakness but by dedication; they have traded locomotion for access to the stone’s chemistry, and in this trade they have become the only landmarks in the dark. Their waste is photons. From the uppermost aperture, they excrete the faint, cold light—arsenic green, bismuth blue, violet like a bruise—that trickles down and maps the world in gradients. They are the stars of this velvet underside, and like stars they are indifferent, burning the byproduct of their feasting. They live centuries, perhaps, or mere molt-cycles; time is measured by shedding here, and they do not shed. They have opted out of time’s currency and become geology with a heartbeat.

And there are the margins, the interstitials, the specialists of compression. Because space is saturated, because the air itself feels architected, there are things that live in the gaps between the floor and the drift: flat, flexible, jointed in directions that imply extra dimensions. They tile. They stack in living masonry three bodies deep, the lowest walking, the middle clinging to the upper’s belly, the uppermost clinging to the underside of a stone shelf, all of them moving in a single, rippling current without aggression, because territorial display is a luxury of spacious worlds. They are the lubricant of the ecosystem, the ones who intercept the dying mid-fall, who rasp the stone smooth in passing, whose nests—hardened accretions of spit and shed material—become grottoes that shelter nymphs and sacs alike. They are taxonomically ambiguous, neither floor-dweller nor flier, the connective tissue of the feast.

For feast is the condition of the place. The inhabitants do not merely live here; they are the weathering agent, the hydrology, the light-source, and the soil. The stone is smooth because it has been licked. The floor is flagstone because it is compressed exoskeleton, strata of chitin and enamel. When something dies, the chill air flash-mineralizes it; there is no rot, no stench, only a rapid petrifaction that locks the chemistry back into the architecture. A carcass hit by a Snapper does not strike the ground. The carpet intercepts it. The tile-creatures intercept it. It is disassembled in mid-air by a hundred mouths, unmade before it can cool, distributed so completely that death becomes indistinguishable from migration.

There is no green, so there is no waiting for the sun. The only producers are the microbial films, the chemolithotrophic verdigris that glows faintly in the deepest pores, turning sulfur and iron into the base currency of life. Everything else is animal, or animal-adjacent, a closed loop of hunger so complete that the basin has forgotten the outside. The inhabitants do not dream of open sky. They dream in click-patterns. They dream in gas-pressure and wing-case resonance. They dream in the mineral taste of stone and the cold light of their own making.

It is old. It is full. And every particle of it is alive.
