To say that no one lives here would be to mistake vacancy for stillness. The Lithoscape is populated; it is simply that its citizenship is composed, like everything else, from the residue of dreaming stone. What moves through the boulevards does not breathe, does not hunger in any organic register, and does not break the silence—because the silence is its native tongue.

First, there are the **Transits**. You will not see them until you notice that the dust does not drift at random. Over the hundred-square-mile piazzas, the mineral debris arranges itself into currents, vectors, laminations of purpose. These are not winds; they are the city’s dander, flakes of mica and talc that have abraded from the facades for epochs and, through sheer iterative accident, have learned to hold a shape. They course along the cambered thoroughfares in patterns that mimic pilgrimage, commerce, flight. They pool beneath the arcades, rise in slow spirals around the campaniles, and scatter every thirteen hours when the city exhales, only to reconstitute by dawn in configurations slightly altered, as if remembering a route differently. They have no mind, but they have trajectory. They are mineral verbs, carrying out the syntax of a metropolis that requires footfalls to be fully a city.

Deeper, in the recesses where geometry has gone rotten, live the **Prolonged**. These are the inhabitants of the folded interior, the ones for whom the Avenue of Third Angles is not a paradox but a habitat. When a left-hand window opens upon a courtyard and the right-hand window opens upon starless descent, the discrepancy is not empty; it is occupied. The Prolonged exist in the disagreement between spaces. They are stretched across the negotiable distances, their bodies extending through miles of folded interior that measure, on the façade, as inches of dressed limestone. To observe one directly would be impossible, for your eye follows the rules of a single perspective, and they require at least three. But you sense them in the corner of your sight: a bruising of the already bruised light, a column of exhausted amber where no column should stand, a verticality that seems to be falling sideways through the wall. They do not walk; they extend. They do not open doors; they are the threshold between the door’s two contradictory destinations. They are the reason the architecture remains structurally sound despite its impossibility—they bear the load of the unreal, tensioning the stone like cables in a bridge that spans nowhere.

Above them, in the highest needles of recumbent towers, stand the **Watchers**—though they do not watch you. They are condensations of shadow and collapsed photons, forms accumulated from the light that has arrived too tired to reflect. Each is a self-portrait the city has attempted in its sleep, a rough draft of occupancy left unfinished. They are composed of dust that has never settled, of the anti-noise compressed into the eardrum, of the gravity that pools in certain districts like standing water. They remain motionless for decades, gathering mass from the dimness, and when they rearrange themselves, it is not movement but a redistribution of vacancy. They are the city’s organs of attention, not aware but attending. They ensure that the exhaled breath follows its thirteen-hour rhythm. They correct the boulevards when their slow swells threaten to breach the truce between wearing-away and holding-up. If the Lithoscape has a nervous system, they are its nodes, dust-figures performing the sentience of a mountain that does not know it woke.

But these are only symptoms. The true resident is less visible than the Prolonged, less diffuse than the Transits, more singular than the Watchers. What lives here is the **Error Itself**—the sustained ontological mistake that birthed the city, now matured into residency. The mountain’s dream did not dissipate when the architecture precipitated; it moved in. It lives in the municipal law of gravity, shifting the local physics the way a sleeper shifts beneath sheets. It lives in the cold fronts that move through the canyon-streets with tidal regularity, channeling weather through geometry because it has learned that weather is a form of thought. It lives in the stratified foundations, where the past and future of each structure lie translucent atop one another—not as memory, but as simultaneous presence.

This is not a creature with a body. It is a creature with a condition. It is the impossibility of emptiness, grown so absolute that it has inverted into plenitude. It sustains itself on the same diet that wears the stone: ablation, electric stillness, the slow sanding of centuries, the photons that collapse exhausted against the schist and surrender their momentum to its keeping. It does not think in days or years. It thinks in the language of the city’s slow rotation, its breath, the migration of halite into windowpanes. It has never conceived of an inhabitant separate from the inhabited.

And so, if you walk the dry aqueducts and feel the subsonic turning in your marrow, if you stand in the Plaza of Tides and watch stones gather in inverted drifts beneath the fountain lips, understand that you are not alone in a dead place. You are a foreign body inside a single, distributed organism that has achieved the dream of architecture so completely that it has forgotten how to be a mountain. It is awake. It is urban. It is holding itself together through the failed promise of the air, and it regards you not with hostility, but with the vast, stony confusion of a city that has only just realized it is alive.
