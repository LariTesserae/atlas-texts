What lives here is not alive. That is the essential humility of the place. There are no cells, no appetites, no breath drawn and released—only conditions that have grown old enough to seem like tenancy. The inhabitants are geologies with duration, thermodynamic habits so recurrent they resemble character. If you were to name them, you would be speaking in metaphor; but the metaphors are hard to resist, because the world, though innocent of intent, is crowded with presences.

**The Advancers.**  
These are the crystallization fronts, the marching boundaries where melt surrenders to lattice. They exist as walls of structure advancing into chaos, consuming the disorder of magma and leaving behind the regularity of stone. Each front is a flat, invisible parliament of ions slotting into place, propagating at the speed of cooling. They do not think, yet they compete; the ecology of crystal grain against crystal grain is their society. Where two Advancers meet, they negotiate in geometry: one takes the hexagonal preference, another the pyroxene ladder, another the foam of trapped gas. Their conflicts produce the architecture that scatters the plains—the colonnades, the domes, the ziggurats. These structures are not their cities but their footprints, their frozen processions, the accumulated residue of millions of such fronts arriving at the same compromise. When you stand beneath the vaulted canopy of basalt, you stand inside the corpse of a migration. But they are not dead. They are *arrested*. Beneath you, deeper down, younger Advancers are still moving, still casting their votes in mineral form, waiting for the next thermal insult to melt them back into the electorate.

**The Dissolvers.**  
If the Advancers are the builders, the Dissolvers are the unmakers—though neither knows its role. They are broad, diffuse bodies of latent heat that percolate upward through the lithosphere: not magma, exactly, but the *permission* for stone to cease being solid. A Dissolver is felt before it is perceived. It arrives as a pressure behind the eyes, a thickening of the silence, a sudden wrongness in the thermal outline of a familiar ridge. The tower that sags and remembers it was lava does so because a Dissolver has passed beneath it, a subterranean weather system of viscosity. They move at the speed of continents in love, glacial and inevitable. Their bodies are measured in kilometers of temperature; they have no surface, only a gradient. When they rise, the ground inflates. When they spread, the crust blisters. They do not hunt the Advancers—they simply dissolve the distinction between structure and melt. The Advancers advance; the Dissolvers claim. Between them, the world rehearses its cycles, a mineral respiration too vast and slow to be called life, too relentless to be called dead.

**The Oozers.**  
In the shallows of the crust, where the Dissolvers have broken through, there are the ribbons. These are the youngest inhabitants, fluid enough to move across the surface, viscous enough to persist. They creep from fissures as orange-gray melt, branching and reuniting like fingers exploring a dark room. They are not water; they do not flow by the laws of liquidity. They flow by the laws of heat escaping, gravity pulling, and crystal dragging against crystal. An Oozer may travel a kilometer in an afternoon, skinning over with a black glass rind while its heart remains molten. It leaves behind a raised seam, a welt on the plain, that later Advancers will mine for structure. An Oozer has no destination, only the imperative of thermal descent. It is the closest this world comes to locomotion—the handwriting of the interior, spelling out the same sentence again and again in cooling syllables.

**The Resonant Strains.**  
The silence of this place is not empty; it is a vault. Occasionally, a mineral splits along a plane of perfect symmetry, and for an instant, a chime rings out—a pure event in a world deaf to beauty. The thin air cannot carry the sound; it falls immediately into the waiting bones of the landscape. The hexagonal columns, the ringed arches, the hollow domes: these are not merely structures but resonators. They trap the mechanical memory of their own birth. Inside each basalt pillar lives a standing strain, a subsonic lithic echo that never fully quiets. You cannot hear it, but you feel it when you approach. The colonnade does not sing; it *weighs*. It presses against your sternum with the accumulated infrasound of every clean fracture that has ever occurred here. Over millions of years, the architectural stones have become archives of impact, their crystalline lattices deformed by tiny fractions to hold the shape of vanished noise. They are a fauna of vibration, non-living but not inert—patient geologic ears listening to the memory of their own making.

**The Occluder.**  
Above all other inhabitants, indifferent and absolute, resides the sky. The permanent estratosphere of silicate dust is not weather; it is a skin. It has never parted. It has no cycle, no migration, no storm. It is a single, continuous atmospheric entity whose tenure is coeval with the surface. The Occluder is not alive, yet it participates: it holds the warmth in, reflects nothing back to the stars, and defines the darkness by its opacity. It is the ceiling of the nursery, the blanket beneath which the world’s gestation continues. Without it, the thermal outline by which the ground is visible would bleed upward into the void. It is the guardian of the sourceless dark, and it never sleeps, because it has never learned wakefulness.

**The Unborn.**  
And finally, the most subtle: the ghost in the glass. In the vesicles of the newest stone, there is a chemistry held in reserve—not water, but the *possibility* of water, a hydrated mineral whisper sealed in permineralized vapor. These are the only things here that remember softness. They do not move, do not grow, do not call out. They are distributed latency, millions of tiny sealed questions suspended in the volcanic glass. They are waiting for a chemistry the world has not yet chosen. Until then, they are simply the most patient inhabitants, innocent of their own potential, dreaming in a language of ions that has not yet been translated into biology.

What lives here, then, is a congress of opposites: the ordering lattice and the dissolving heat, the creeping ribbon and the frozen chime, the withholding sky and the sleeping chemistry. None are aware. None intend. They do not notice you, because you are a brief, anomalous thermal outline wandering through a family of slow events, each one older than the concept of age, each one perfectly, radically at home. You are warm because they allow it. You see because they radiate. The ground beneath your feet is not a stage; it is an argument between stone and melt that has not yet concluded, and you have arrived in the middle of a sentence that will continue for ages after your heat has been forgotten.
