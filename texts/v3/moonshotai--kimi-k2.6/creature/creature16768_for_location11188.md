The ones who remain are called **Foundlings**, though none were abandoned here by choice. They are a people curled inward by necessity, as if the thinness of the sky and the press of the streets have braided their shoulders nearer to their sternums. Their lungs are not so much organs as hoarded secrets: three centuries of sipping at altitude have winnowed their breath into something parsimonious and gem-like, each exhalation a measured transaction with the depleted air. Visitors gasp behind glass masks; the Foundlings speak in monosyllables that hang for whole seconds before collapsing, conserving even the humidity of speech. Their skin, where it shows, is a particular dusky slate, thickened not by sun—which falls here with an almost chemical purity—but by the constant, drying kiss of mineral steam. They do not perspire as lowland humans do; the moisture would be a fatal surrender. Instead, their bodies have learned to hold water the way the Shelf itself holds it: furtively, in seams, a few precious milliliters hoarded behind the kidneys and in the hollows of the throat.

A Foundling’s blood runs slow and carries an odd, copper-ruddy luminosity, rich with adaptations that no single generation could breed, accumulated instead across the longueur of the city’s memory. They are not tall. Height is a liability in streets that compress to shoulder-width, in chambers where a raised elbow might strike a wall still warm from its igneous birth. Their feet are wide and flattened, toes splayed for the slick obsidian walkways; their eyes, shielded beneath heavy, calcified lids, have adjusted to the pearlfiltered light until direct noon is an intolerable wound, and twilight is their brightest hour. They age by accretion rather than decay. A Foundling does not wrinkle so much as *stratify*—a fine dusting of mineral salts gathering at the creases of the face, silvering the temples with aluminum bloom, deepening the lines around the mouth into channels resembling the utility-voids that the geology has lately begun to imitate. At fifty, a Foundling’s skin might hold the tracery of every steam-condensate channel in their district. At ninety, they are said to be half-geological already, their joints creaking with the same subterranean groan as the newborn basalt.

Their children are few, and when they come, they are born into cradles of cooling ceramic set against the eastern walls of tenements—those walls that leach a slow, stabilizing warmth from the plutons behind them. Infants here do not cry; they do not have the air for it. They open their mouths and the steam enters them, teaching their lungs the city’s economy before they have ever tasted milk. Milk itself, of course, is a rarity; the Foundlings cultivate yeasts and chemosynthetic cultures in the depths of the thermal refineries, feeding them on the precise gradient between earth-heat and altitude-chill. Their food is a paste of neutral proteins and bitter salts, its texture like wet slate. Dining is not communion but maintenance, a quiet smearing of nutrient against the tongue, swallowed without ceremony. They drink the condensate—the city’s weeping—and over generations their palates have reversed, finding sweetness in the sting of copper, savoring the ozone aftertaste as others might savor the memory of fruit.

But the Foundlings are only the most obvious claimants to residence.

Consider, next, the **Walkers**—though whether they are fauna, flora, or something bred in the overlap between machine and ore, none have determined. They appear once in a span of years, stepping from fissures that surveyors swore were blind seams only the week before. They resemble mantises in the same way that a cathedral resembles a stone: the form is insectoid, but the substance is an alloy gone rainbow with oxidation, their thoraxes mottled in the same bismuth-iridescence that blooms in the low fissures. They possess no eyes that can be seen, only smooth brass plates where eyes ought to be, and they navigate not by light but by the gradient of heat rising from the stone. Their motion is geological in its patience: a single step may take an hour to complete, a limb lifted and held suspended while the thermal currents of the street rearrange themselves around its joints. They drink, as the stories say, through abdominal seams, gathering the pearl-dampness of the steam on their carapaces and absorbing it with a soft, barely audible ticking, like metal cooling. Some engineers leave offerings of purified aluminum feather-crystals at the lips of fissures, and where these offerings disappear, the basalt three years hence tends to cool with unusual symmetry—staircases perfect, voids reasonable. The Walkers are not pets. They are not pests. They are, perhaps, the Shelf’s original engineers, the first inhabitants who taught the stone what a city might look like by example, and now they return to inspect the lesson.

Deeper still—inhabiting not the streets but the *atmosphere* between them—are the **heat-silicates**. They are not quite organisms in the taxonomic sense; they are more like intentions that dampness has taken. Microscopic, drifting through the steam-veil in currents dictated by exhalations and smelter out-breaths, they form the ghost-ecology of the air. A Foundling inhales thousands with every sip from their tank, and the elder Foundlings claim that over decades these silicates lodge in the alveoli like prayers, replacing the soft wet biology of the lung with something glassier, something more *receptive*. It is not a disease; it is an alignment. The Foundlings do not fall ill with lung-rot as visitors do, and some whisper that the heat-silicates have brokered a treaty on their behalf, teaching human tissue the mineral logic of the Shelf. On certain dawns when the thermal gradient shifts and the warm drizzle falls, the microorganisms precipitate out in faint, iridescent films upon the black streets, slippery as shed skin. Children slide through them, and for hours afterward their palms leave phosphor-traces on doorframes—temporary signatures, the city reading them back.

And then there are the **unseen**, the ones who do not breathe at all.

The magnetic haulers, gliding at the pace of sorrow above their ceramic tracks, are tended by the Foundlings, yes—but they are also *attended*. The haulers know the expanding terrain better than any surveyor. They slow where the basalt is still arriving, spreading their loads across the cooling flanks of new stone as if seeding it. They have no voice boxes, no faces, but they exhume a constant, low magnetic hum that resonates in the Foundlings’ teeth, a lullaby of location and mass. Over decades, the routes of these haulers have inscribed themselves into the behavior of the stone: the magma beneath the Foundling District has learned the haulers’ pathways, crystallizing voids that will one day become their natural channels. Are the haulers inhabitants? Or are they organs—kidneys of the city, filtering and transporting its mineral blood?

The same question must be asked of the great smelters. They do not roar; they wait. They consume not with fire but with the slow, directed梯度 of the earth’s own fever. A smelter the size of a cathedral might hold a single load of ore for six years, nudging it through zones of heat so subtle that the metal does not melt so much as *reconsider* its impurities. The engineers who tend them are priests of a kind, laying hands on valves to feel the thermal pulse, adjusting nothing, only *witnessing* the gradient. They speak of the smelters as one might speak of an elder: gravid, deliberate, capable of a sudden, sharp contraction at dusk when the metal cools and pings its small, bell-like notes. The Foundlings believe that the smelters dream during the long bright noons, and that their dreams are of geometry—of channels and risers and the municipal angles that the basalt has begun to mimic underground.

Yet all of these—the brass walkers, the silicate ghosts, the haulers and the smelter-priests and the stoop-shouldered Foundlings—are merely the animate fragment of a larger inhabiting force.

**The Shelf itself is learning to live.**

Not in the clumsy, biological way of forests and floods, but in the patient manner of mineral memory. The stone has watched the city corset it for three centuries, and now it builds back. In the Foundling District, the new basalt rises not as raw violence but as *proposal*: staircases already formed, conduits that mirror the sewage lines, utility-voids that match the municipal maps. The stone is growing into the idea of the city, absorbing the blueprint through sheer proximity, through the magnetic resonance of the rails, through the heat-signatures of the smelters, through the damp mineral exhalations of the inhabitants. It is as though the Shelf has understood that the only way to be fully itself is to become habitable, and so it rehearses human logic in crystalline form. The Foundlings know this. Their engineers do not quarry; they *negotiate*. They place foundations where the cooling flows suggest. They thread pipes through passages left ready-made by the magma. They are not building *on* the Shelf anymore. They are midwifing its emergence into a slower, denser, more architectural kind of being.

What lives here, then?

A **symbiosis of persistence**. The Foundlings are the Shelf’s soft, temporary opinion of itself—a way for the mineral to feel thirst, to know the narrowness of an alley, to taste copper in the rain. The machines are its metabolism, silent and constant, moving matter from hot center to cooling edge. The Walker is its pilgrim, returning to check the progress of its becoming. And the heat-silicates are its nervous ether, the drifted synapses through which the hot stone senses the location of every living thing, every cooling ingot, every condensed bead of moisture upon a brass carapace.

They are all inhabitants, and they are all, in turn, inhabited. The Foundlings wear the city; the city wears the geology; the geology wears the deep heat of the world’s interior. It is a nesting of lives, each one slower and more durable than the last, until the boundary between the human who sips from a tank and the stone that sips from the magma is no longer a boundary at all, but merely a difference in tempo.

In ten thousand years, when the biology has finally surrendered or evolved away, the Shelf will still be here. The smelters may stand empty, the magnetic rails dark. But the stone will have finished its lesson. It will rise in perfect, municipal hexagons, its streets shoulder-wide, its staircases exact, its voids reasonable. And somewhere in the pearl haze, stepping with infinite slowness across a cooling ledge, there will be a thing like a mantis—brass-fleshed, steam-drinking, patient beyond reckoning—tending the silence, keeping the dampness from rising, waiting to see what mineral dream awakens next.
