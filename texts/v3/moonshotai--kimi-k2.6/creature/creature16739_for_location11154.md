If something lives here, it has given up the pretense of separateness. You will not find residents behind doors, nor nests in corners. The basin does not shelter life; it *recruits* it, folding whatever persists into the same sintered logic that built the empty towers. The inhabitants are the only things in this region older than the concept of cities, or perhaps they are younger than the last condensation cycle—it amounts to the same distinction here. They are upright, after a fashion, though their posture is determined not by anatomy but by acoustics, each figure leaning into the Thrum at the precise angle where the pressure becomes bearable, a node of living equilibrium in a field of endless bass.

They are tall—twice the height of a human, though height is a meaningless measure for creatures who spend decades in the same footprint, their weight sinking slowly into the mineral glaze until the plaza tiles cup them like palms. To call them clothed would be inaccurate; to call them naked would imply a skin evolved in some elsewhere. What covers them is a sedimentary integument, hundreds of years of warm particulate fused to a denser subcutaneous lattice by the ambient heat and the faint prickle of atmospheric charge. They are the color of the architecture because they *are* the architecture in transit: salt-cured, vitrified, dusted with the same rust-frost that blooms on the ledges. Where a face should be, there is only a smooth anterior plane, slightly concave, like the soundboard of an instrument that has never known a string. They do not see in any way you would recognize; their world is mapped by the reflections of the Thrum off hard ceramic surfaces, a topography of pressure felt in the sternal cavity that serves as their primary organ of sense.

They do not eat, not as you understand it. They press their ventral surfaces to the weeping walls and let the glassy braids of warm water run over the channels of their torsos, filtering out ferric salts and phosphates, metabolizing the geothermal charge that dances in the humid air. When the Saint Elmo’s fire clings to a spire, they gather beneath it, extending their armatures upward to let the discharge skein across their surfaces, their concave faces flickering with a light that seems, momentarily, almost like expression. In the lowest courts, they wade knee-deep into the ponds of old blood and new rust, motionless for weeks at a time, while the gelatinous algal mats creep across their lower limbs and colonize them with patient verdigris. They do not mind. The flora does not parasitize; it *partners*, fixing the hot chemistry of the water into compounds the tall ones can slowly absorb.

They are not many. Perhaps two dozen in the whole basin, spaced like punctuation across the vast plazas. You might walk for days and see only the marks they leave: a footprint, already half-healed by condensate, where the ceramic silt has packed into a density unnatural for this gentle accumulation; or a runnel in the tile, not worn by random droplets but by the steady, centuries-long drip from a figure standing in permanent attendance beneath a cracked gallery. They communicate only when necessity demands, and necessity moves on geological timescales. They find the null points in the Thrum—those rare pockets where the pressure waves meet and cancel—and there they stand face to face, their concave planes almost touching. In those intervals of comparative relief, information passes not as sound but as *geometry*: the shape of the basin as it is felt from two positions at once, the standing wave of their mutual presence resolving into a new harmonic. Weeks pass. The condensation beads on their shoulders and runs down in channels already memorized by older droplets. Then one shifts, or both, a migration of inches, and the interview is done.

They are not the builders. They are the *consequence* of the builders having built too well, or too long. When the machinery internalized its industry and the workers were no longer required, the basin did not forget the template of the upright form. It simply grew its own versions from the materials at hand. Deep in the warm galleries, where the turbines exhale and the condensate pools in ceramic throats, the city occasionally nucleates a new one: a soft, mucilaginous bud flushed upward by geothermal pressure, pale as pond scum and quivering with the fundamental tone. It climbs toward the pearlescent light over years, decades, hardening as it rises, accreting layer upon layer, until it finds its station in a plaza or at the base of a weeping tower. There it settles. There it becomes.

And there are the ones who did not settle, or who settled too thoroughly. You will find them if you look for the buildings that seem, in the haze, to bear an unusual proportion—a tower with a curve that suggests a shoulder, a fluted edifice that is actually a figure grown massive, overgrown, its limbs indistinguishable from the architectural mass. They are not dead. They are *dormant*, having achieved a final marriage with the structure they were grown to tend. The city does not distinguish between a wall and a wall that dreams.

The ash-colored moths belong to them, or perhaps they belong to the moths. The insects alight on their shoulders to drink the saline condensate that beads on the sedimentary skin, and in exchange—though exchange implies economy, and this is pure ecology—they carry the mineral dust from one plaza to another, planting microscopic gardens, redistributing the glaze. In the warm mud at dusk, the phosphorescent worm-colonies brighten in sympathy when the tall ones pass nearby, a visual chord struck across the flats. Nothing here is separate. The basin is one organism, one weathered instrument, and these figures are its fragile, voluntary notes: the only moving parts in a machine that has otherwise forgotten motion, playing themselves for no audience but the mountains, held upright by a sound that has not stopped, will not stop, and holds them like a hand holds water—patiently, completely, with no intent to ever let go.
