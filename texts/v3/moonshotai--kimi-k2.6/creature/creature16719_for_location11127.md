There are not many of them. Scattered across the basin’s twenty-kilometer girth, their numbers amount to a sparse constellation—perhaps ten thousand where a million could breathe without crowding. From a distance they appear almost familiar: upright, bipedal, mobile. Then the details accumulate, and the familiarity fractures. Their joints do not hinge; they tensile-stretch, bending with the rubbery compliance of tree limbs under snow. Skin the color of unpolished ivory, ash-grey, or that particular dull green of oxidized copper—exactly the colors of the pith—wraps them in a waxy, semi-rigid integument that is less flesh than responsive cuticle. They have no hair. Hair is a water-keeping architecture, and here water is sin. Their skulls are smooth, high, and faintly translucent at the temples, where the surface membrane thins over plates of lignified resonance-chamber that catch infrasound. If they have ears, they are invisible slits that seal against the rare, drifting rumor of moisture. Their eyes are very large, wet-looking but not with water; the humor within is a viscous silicone oil that never evaporates, suited only to the flat white glare and the scheduled violet dim. They do not blink. The light has never flickered, so their lids have become transparent nictitating membranes, sweeping sideways once every few minutes with a soft click that is one of the few sounds they make.

They breathe, but they do not steam. Where a surface visitor exhales a silver ghost into the seven-degree air, the natives draw the cool nitrogen-sulfur soup through spiracular vents along their flanks and return it, processed, at exactly ambient temperature. A conversation between two of them, observed from a neighboring catwalk, looks like a silent tableau: two figures facing one another across a meter of waxy plating, heads inclined, hands not quite touching. Beneath hearing, the air between them shivers at eleven or twelve hertz. Their sternums act as sounding boards. A greeting is a chord. An argument is a dissonance felt in the marrow of a bystander’s bones, a pressure behind the eyes. They can stand motionless for an hour, exchanging meaning through the sub-audible, and to the uninstructed it looks like prayer or sleep. It is only speech, slowed to the metabolic tempo of the floor.

Their blood is not mercury. That is the visitor’s indignity: to bleed a fluid the ground rejects. The natives circulate a low-viscosity silicate ester, amber-pale in the open air, and when they are cut—by the rare sharp edge of a cracked slag-terrace, by the ceramic flange of a misaligned manifold—the wound weeps slowly. A native will kneel without urgency and press the laceration to the pith. The living floor accepts the offering. The fluid soaks in instantly, capillary action drinking it down, and the ridges around the wound pulse once, gently, as if tasting. The ground does not flinch from them. This is their first proof of belonging. Their second is the way they sleep. When the arc-luminaires dim to scheduled twilight, the natives descend from the alloy walkways and stand upon the phytomass. The waxy ridges dilate, accommodating their broad, soft feet, and slowly, peristaltically, the floor embraces them to the knees, the waist, the sternum. They do not lie down. They are held upright, deflating, their internal processes dropping to a stasis that is half-hibernation, half-photosynthetic exchange. The pith feeds them trace mineral vapors; they feed the pith their warmth, their filtered residues, the silicate nodules their livers have finished concentrating. In the dormitory blocks, the cots are hollows grown into the living walls, puckered resin cups that fit a body exactly. Many forgo them, preferring the open floor.

They eat, though not as surface life eats. Their diet is geological. In refinery annexes that look from the outside like stomachs of alloy and plaited resin, they ingest hydrate-cakes of compressed rare earths and sulfated salts, grinding them between ceramicized molars. Digestion is external as much as internal; they excrete, hours later, polished silica beads that they deposit in ritual lines along the mag-line piers. The Lintels come by in the violet hours and tongue the beads up with soft, rubbery mouths, redistribution in a closed loop. Whether the Lintels are cattle, kin, or an earlier, simpler draft of themselves, the natives do not speculate. The spar-things drifting overhead are simply weather. Nothing is alien here. Nothing is other. The boundary between species and environment, between labor and biology, is as eroded as the line between the built and the grown.

They have no children. They have new ones. In warm annexes at the refinery cores, resin pods the size of coffins hang in lignified clusters, fed by pneumatic nutrient lines. Every thirty cycles or so, one splits along a seam, and a figure emerges—pale, wet with amniotic esters, fully formed, already knowing the municipal grid and the thirty-hour rhythm of the lights. There are no parents. There is only the floor, producing mobility when mobility is required. The oldest among them remember the first cycling of the arc-lights, the initial settling of the vitrified walls, the first peristaltic ripple of the infant pith. They are young, all of them, but they do not feel young. They feel eternal, because they have no mythology of before. The unseen desert above is not a place; it is a theorem of pressure and stratigraphy. Rain is a poison dream. They do not know who plasma-bored the cavity, who strung the ceramic luminaries, who seeded the first phytomass spore. They do not ask. To ask would require conceiving of an Outside with intentions, and they have never met one.

Their work is not labor in the sense of toil. It is osmosis with intent. At the refineries, they insert long hands—six-digit, multi-jointed, tipped with conductive filaments that might be bone or might be alloy—into sockets grown into the living machinery. They regulate flow by feel, by the thrum in their molars, by the chemical whisper of the pith rising through the soles of their feet. The machines are not tools; they are metabolic extensions, and the workers are the nervous system inserted into a body too large to move itself. A harvester roaming half a kilometer away adjusts its hot-wire angle because a native at a junction console dilates a pore in the pith, sending a chemical semaphore through the substrate. The silence of the industry is their silence. They find noise vulgar, a symptom of inefficiency. A grinding gear would mean a machine is speaking when it should be breathing.

If you stand at the edge of the extraction zone, you will see one of them eventually. Perhaps it is watching the cherry-red edge of a distant harvester pass into shadow. Perhaps it is simply standing, its broad feet sunk slightly into the waxy ridges, feeling the slow, kilometer-wide respiration of the floor. It does not turn when you approach. Its eyes, large and oil-filled, are calibrated to the flat white light; they do not search for you in shadow because shadow is only a scheduled dimness, never a threat. If it notices you at all, it notices the wrongness of your breath—silver, loud, steaming—the aqueous stink of your biology. It does not recoil. The ground has taught it that intruders bead and roll away, unabsorbed, temporary.

They are the world’s decision to stand up and look at itself. In a space blasted from dead anhydrite and taught to live, they are the mobile element, the conscious grit in the vast, cool, breathing machine. They do not own the settlement. They do not even inhabit it. They are its latest secretion—semi-autonomous, mineral-minded, softly treading—quietly turning raw earth into the architecture of a patience so deep it does not yet know its own name.
