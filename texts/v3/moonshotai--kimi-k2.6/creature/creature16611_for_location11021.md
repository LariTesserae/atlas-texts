They do not present themselves. You can stand in the bladder-cool damp between the primary works and the secondary chimney for an hour, listening to the ball mill turn the sky to gravel overhead, and believe yourself alone in the basin. The foxes will not contradict you; the peregrines have their own grievances to air. But solitude here is a misreading of scale. The inhabitants are simply living at a velocity that matches the terrain. If you wish to see them, you must cease expecting the quick semaphore of human busyness. Stand until your boots silver with condensation. Wait for the six-hour compression stroke to exhale its steam. Watch the flywheel eat another degree of arc. Then you will notice the figure at the edge of the hardpan, or the silhouette in the scaffolded gap between the corrugated wall and the newer concrete skin, or the motionless shape seated on a stack of sea-green blocks, head tilted as if listening to the stone’s subsonic lullaby. They are there. They are always there. There are not enough of them to fill a single room of an ordinary house.

They have no names that can be spoken over the thunder of the mill. What designations they use pass in low frequencies—vibrations intoned against pipework, conveyed through the stilted rails, felt in the sternum rather than heard. Perhaps there are three of them, one rooted in each building like a taproot. Perhaps there are five, or seven, or simply a small quorum sufficient to turn a valve every few hours and to note, in ledgers buckled by humidity, the progress of the curing blocks. Their number seems less important than their density. They are heavy. Not fat, not tall, but *dense*, as if the subsonic thrum of the flywheels has settled into their marrow and crystallized there. They move with the continuous momentum of those same wheels; they do not start or stop but only continue, shifting weight from one foot to the other in a gait that presses the shale without bruising it. Suddenness is a language they have forgotten. A hand raised to brush condensation from a brow travels at the speed of fern-unfurling. A glance takes time enough for moss to bead on the eyelashes.

They wear the architecture they tend. Their garments are layered accretions, geological in structure: a core of waxed canvas and greasy wool, accreted over with aprons of rubberized sacking, shawls of knitted jute, and outermost cloaks of pale amber dust that has fired in the kiln-heat to a thin ceramic glaze. Everything is damp, always. Their hair, where it shows, is matted with clay and the fine spores of moss; in the warm pockets near the steam valves, it sprouts actual ferns, tiny *Asplenium* rooting in the salt of their scalps. Their skin has taken on the quality of the basin’s unweathered clay—pale, faintly iridescent where the silt has worked into the creases of knuckles and the whorls of ears. They do not shiver in the cold exhalations of the basin. The chill is their medium. Their breath, visible in the mornings, hangs longer than yours would; they seem to breathe out weather, small local fogs that join the greater mist rising from the fissures.

They do not dwell apart from the works in any shelter that could be called a house. They occupy the interstices, the improvised hollows of the accreted town. A space beneath a cooling tank where the subsonic hum is strongest and most womb-like. A scaffolded platform above the ball mill, draped in tarpaulins that have greened to velour, where the vibration lulls rather than shakes. They sleep in the intervals between mechanical cycles, twenty minutes here, ninety there, never surrendering entirely to unconsciousness because the machinery requires witnessing. The kilns must be fed not only clay but attention; the blocks must be stacked with a regard that resembles tenderness, or perhaps stewardship. They eat what the basin offers—fistfuls of purslane and velvetfoot mushrooms scraped from the moss-tide, the occasional frog taken with apology and swallowed whole for its wetness, hardtack softened to paste in the condensation trays. Mostly, though, they seem to subsist on steam. They open their mouths to the six-hourly dump, gulping the mineral-laden vapor, letting it scour their throats and fill their lungs with heat. It is not enough to sustain ordinary biology. They are no longer ordinary.

This is the thing that emerges only after watching them for hours, for days: they are not merely tending the process. They are entering it. The vitreous blocks are stacked in the yards, sweating their ghost-moisture, curing across months and years toward some unnamed mineral adulthood. The tenders, too, are curing. Their bones are slowly turning. You can see it in the way they press a palm against a fresh block and hold it there for half an hour, not testing the heat but comparing notes—my hum to yours. Their joints have grown stiff along the same fault lines as the basin’s shale. Their movements, once merely slow, have become *patient*, the patience of sedimentary pressure. There are stories in their skin, strata of old burns from the kilns, pale keloid rivers where groundwater has seeped through cracks and reacted with their chemistry. They are not becoming statues. Statues are inert. They are becoming something glassier, more translucently persistent—a smoky, semi-vitreous entity that can hold dampness in its flesh for decades and still move, still tend, still lean close to a newborn block and murmur a single low note that is part greeting, part diagnosis.

The fauna knows. The foxes do not dig their dens at random; they choose the slopes where the tenders walk most often, where the clay has been pressed into better angles. The frogs breed in the depressions left by their footprints, slow ponds that hold water perfectly because a Tender’s weight knows how to make the earth accept it. The peregrines, wheeling in complaint at the steam dump, drop feathers onto the corrugated roofs only when the tenders are watching, as if paying rent. There is a compact here, older than the buildings, older than the drained water. The basin is embarrassed to be land, and the tenders are embarrassed to be flesh; together, in their mutual hesitation, they have struck a bargain. The mosses close doors. The ferns colonize catch-basins. The machinery grinds and steams and exhales. And the tenders move among it all—between the three structures, along the vanishing rails, in the watercolor light— becoming daily more necessary, more mineral, more perfectly the slow, damp, humming soul of the place.
