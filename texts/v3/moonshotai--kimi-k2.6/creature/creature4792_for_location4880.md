The first thing to understand is that they do not occupy space so much as they occupy *frequency*. To encounter one is not to see a figure but to feel a pressure in the inner ear, a cavity opening in the air itself. Only afterward does the eye resolve the source: a shape upright against the violet dim, too tall, too narrow, possessed of a stillness that reads as a held note rather than as calm.

They have no names in the human sense, only *timbres*. When two meet on the plain, they do not face one another. They stand side by side, angled to the wind, and open their bodies so the air passes through. Their forms are not flesh. Imagine a bundle of xylem tubes, fused and fossilized into something vaguely resembling a humanoid silhouette, but stretched, compressed, and then hollowed until the original biology is lost to geometry. The outer dermis is identical to the floor: that polished, desiccated periderm, cool to the touch, tough as geological time. Beneath it, visible through translucent fault-lines at the joints, runs a lattice of carbon-capillary, pulsing with stolen starlight and the slow transit of metallic sap. They look, at first glance, like infant spires that have torn themselves free and learned to walk, or like the walking dead of the megafloral epoch given uneasy second thought. But this is only the first assumption. Stand longer, and the assumption unravels.

They are neither ghosts nor children. They are *concerns*. The city grows them whenever a process requires discretion—when the mathematics of root-expansion demand an explorer, or when a spire’s song drifts sharp and needs a hand (or the woody equivalent) to prune the hexagonal baffles back into harmony, or when the Plant simply wishes to know what it feels like to choose a direction. Each one is a localized question that the world asks of itself. And because questions must travel to find answers, they walk.

A body built for this place has no use for water, no use for eyes accustomed to true brightness. Their sensory organs are pits and vaulted chambers: tympanic plates of compressed lignin stretched across internal cavities, capable of detecting the shift of a root-flange a hundred kilometers away by the change in subsonic pressure. They have no mouths. They do not eat the blimp-creatures or the lichen-molds. Instead they *tune* them, guiding the herds with infrasonic drones that ripple from abdominal chambers, directing the great dirigible pollinators to nodes where the spires require voltage, or stimulant, or the mere presence of another living thing. They are shepherds of current and chorus.

They are hollow. Profoundly so. A cross-section would reveal no brain, no central organ, only a nested architecture of resonant voids—lungs that are also larynxes, larynxes that are also ears. Thought occurs in the resonance. Information is stored as standing waves in their torso-chambers; memory is literally an echo modulated by the thickness of their walls. What they know, they sing. What they forget falls silent, and a silent one knows, with a dread absolute as gravity, that it is time to return to the plain.

For they are not eternal, nor even long-lived by the measure of the spires. A century, perhaps two, and their cellulose lattice grows too rigid to hold new harmonics. The songs of the world change—viral weeds alter the pitch of the soil, new spires extrude fresh notes into the chord—and they find they can no longer adapt their interior architecture to comprehend what has changed. When this happens, they cease walking. They select a place between the towers where the wind cuts clean, and they root. Deliberately, carefully, they sink their limb-flanges into the fossilized velvet floor. They open every chamber wide to the bruised sky. The wind begins to play them, and they surrender their standing-wave memories into the plain’s greater drone. Over decades the substrate does not swallow them so much as remember them: their carbon-nanotube channels become part of the routing, their blue-glow seams add new vocabulary to the bioluminescence, their mineral sap diffuses into the commonwealth. They become part of the floor. They become part of the song. It is not death. It is transcription.

And yet, despite being—in essence—expendable cognitive extensions of a world-brain, they possess something that resembles culture, or at least the echo of one. They gather, infrequently, in the lee of a spire where the acoustics create a natural amphitheater. They do not touch; touch is for information exchange, and they reserve it for emergencies or intimacy. Instead, they array themselves in polygons, open their chest-cavities to the plateau’s thin current, and *reply* to the spires’ tectonic groaning. They improvise. For days the plain thrums with an additional layer of sound, complex, argumentative, mournful—a parliament of chords debating the direction of root expansion, the onset of a toxic pollen front, or the meaning of a patch of sky where the violet darkens toward black. They do not decide. The Plant decides. But the Plant, it seems, prefers to decide after it has heard them sing.

There are variations. Some are grown for the heights, their bodies light and fluted, their foot-limbs prehensile, meant to scale the upper towers and repair the meristematic server-farms where cosmic rays are thickest. Others are low-dwellers, broad and dense, who move through the root-arches regulating pressure in the phloem-pipes, their voices sub-bass rumbles that ease the transit of metallic sap. A rare few are grown for conflict, though violence is scarce in a world without resource scarcity—still, the crystalline viral clouds and invasive weeds occasionally require a hunter. These are the sharpest among the sharp: bodies armored with silica and obsidian-edged cellulose, voices that can shatter bark by finding resonant frequencies and holding them until the lattice yields. But war, for them, is merely a duet that ends in silence.

Most beautiful, and most lonely, are the Messengers. When one spire must commune with another across the impossible distances of the plain, and the electrical harvests fail to bridge the gap, a Messenger is tuned. Its body is elongated until it is scarcely more than a walking pipe, its wind-chest immense, its mind stripped to a single sustained phrase. It walks for years across the polished floor, navigating by the blue glow pooling in eroded hollows, sleeping rooted for decades in half-hibernation, then walking again. It arrives at the distant spire, presses its forehead to the bark, and releases its burden: one tone that contains a century of root-data, weather, memory, longing. The spire answers. The Messenger, its purpose fulfilled, usually does not walk back. It becomes part of that new place, adding its voice to the foreign chord.

To meet one alone on the plain is to meet a mind that has never known thirst, that measures distance in song, that regards you—if you are a wet, warm thing from elsewhere—with something between curiosity and baffled pity. It will not harm you. You are simply *dissonant*. Your breath is too damp, your heartbeat a sloppy percussion against the perfect drone. It may open a seam in its trunk-body and extend a manipulator—not to touch you, but to sample the static field your nervous system generates. It finds you strange: a life that runs on aqueous chemistry, a noisy little cistern of ghosts. If it could speak in your language, it might ask about the sea, the rain—concepts it understands only as theoretical negatives, as the silence that existed before its world learned to sing.

But it does not speak your language. It speaks only in chords, in pressure, in the architecture of hollow things. It is a citizen of the violet dim, a breath held in wood and wire, a thought that the Plant had once and sent walking into the cool, dry emptiness, where to exist at all is to be a small, warm noise balanced forever against the song of the world.
