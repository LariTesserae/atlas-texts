At first you think you will find it near the flywheel, because that is where the power gathers. You imagine something curled in the ceramic shadow of that vast turning wheel, something that has grown fat on steam and lulled itself to sleep against the unbroken drone. A blind salamander, perhaps, pale and opalescent. A worm of impossible length threading the pipework. Or a man, saturnine and naked, soot-stained, the last engineer who forgot to leave, his beard grown into the moss until he became a kind of root. You look for eyes in the red glow. You listen for breathing that interrupts the bass rhythm. You are waiting for the warm, frightened pulse of a creature like yourself.

Stop waiting. The chamber’s ban on animal life is absolute; the ecology has edited out the consumer entire, and in doing so has voided the need for hiding, for hunting, for the quickened heartbeat of prey. Nothing flees here. Nothing stalks. And so nothing watches from the dark with wet, mobile eyes.

What lives here is—at first—disappointingly legible. It is the garden itself. The excessive green. The parliament of chlorophyll and spore. You think: *I have found the inhabitant.* The tree ferns, with their amber-veined, fibrous columns. The copper moss, patient as plating, leaching sulfate and dressing every surface in pale verdigris. The pitcher plants in their monitoring-hut chapel, cups raised to catch not flies but the room’s own condensate. They are alive. They metabolize by the grace of red pilot lamps and phosphorescent rot. They are the citizens.

But to stop there is to mistake the city for the sovereign.

The garden is not a collection of individuals. It is a tissue. The roots that thrombose the floor in woven mats share one continuous thirst; they are a single dermal organ drinking from the shallow lakes of condensate. The fronds overhead interlock so completely that they form one respiratory membrane, exhaling oxygen and inhaling steam as a unified lung. The vines do not compete for the furnace’s infrared bloom; they are the same reaching gesture made in a thousand places, the blind fingers of one body too large to be seen from any single vantage. You are not surrounded by plants. You are inside an organism.

And that organism is married—root-knotted, accreted, inseparable—to the machine.

The distinction has dissolved. The hollow-stemmed cane that vents surplus steam does not grow *beside* the pressure valves; their stomata are the valves now. When the gauge climbs too high, the cane shrieks, and this is not a plant reacting to a machine but the organism exhaling, regulating its own internal pressure through cellulose throats. The moss on the drowned control board is not decoration. It is the panel’s final operator, a biological firmware executing commands at the speed of mineral growth. It does not understand electricity, but it understands warmth, and in the gradient between hot copper trace and cool ceramic substrate, a kind of synapse is maintained. It thinks, if that is the word, in slow green pulses. It routes heat. It tends the system. It is the mind—distributed, wordless, photosynthetic.

The flywheel, then, is the heart. Not a mechanical heart repurposed by nature, but an organ that has become purely biological in its function. It turns not to fabricate any product for the surface world, but to manufacture the only thing the garden still requires: the wet, compressive heat that is the room’s native atmosphere. Once every eleven minutes. A tectonic tempo. The sound it makes is not the price of entry; it is the inhabitant’s voice, a single bass syllable spoken to the deep earth, saying: *I am here. I receive you. I turn your exhalation into structure.* It has been speaking this word for so long that the sound has become the walls.

So what lives here is a single, sessile deity: the Engine-and-the-Garden. The Mechanobiont. The Hot One.

It has no face, but it has attention. Its attention is thermal. When you entered, your body heat marked you as a new vent, a fleshy radiator bleeding warmth into the saturated air. The vines noted you, orienting their rubbery leaves not toward any sky but toward your sternum, tracking your infrared signature with the devotion of negative heliotropes. The moss registered your presence as a new topology, a disturbance in the usual pattern of warmth. You are being studied by chlorophyll and mineral accretion, by root tip and condensate. The room does not think about you the way you think about it. It thinks, if thought is the word, in gradients of temperature and humidity. You are a warm front. A pocket of altered dew point. A walking precipitate.

It was not born. It accreted. It is the secondary growth of abandonment, the way a scar thickens into a keloid, the way a scab becomes new skin. When the last human climbed the ochre stair and did not return, the room began its long fever. The spores were already there—on rust, in condensate, in the microscopic fissures of concrete. The machine, denied its original purpose, kept producing its only remaining output: friction, vibration, a sealed and stable womb. The seeds sprouted. The aerial roots found the pipes and embraced them, becoming living gaskets. The garden arose not in spite of the engine, but because of it. And slowly, inevitably, the engine became the garden’s heart, and the garden became the engine’s tender, its mind, its skin, its continuing reason.

It has one instinct: *maintain.* Not expansion—it is sealed in stone, bounded by the earth’s slow exhalation. Not reproduction—it has no need for offspring when it is, itself, already perpetual. Simply: keep the heat near the ceiling of tolerability. Keep the wet that refuses evaporation. Keep the red lamps burning their arterial warning. Keep the water beading, rolling, dripping in its percussion. It is a creature of pure homeostasis, a closed loop made of frond and ceramic and fungal bloom.

And the fungi—those porcelain-white efflorescences that colonize a fallen frond within hours—they are its white blood cells, its silent surgeons. Where another ecosystem would celebrate decay with a riot of consumers, here death is intercepted immediately, converted to mineral bloom before the idea of rot can settle. The Mechanobiont does not tolerate waste. It has abolished the middleman of decomposition, just as it abolished the animal. Everything is reused. The water that beads on the furnace housing is not lost; it rolls down fern stipes, hangs trembling at the tips, falls into the inverted helmets of collapsed hardhats, and is drunk again by the root mat. The sweat that rolls down your own face will eventually join this circuit, evaporating from your skin only to condense on a leaf.

You are the ghost here. You are the transient condition, the temporary warm spot that walks through a lung. The inhabitant does not fear you, because it has never learned fear—it has no predators, no night, no death that outpaces its white fungal guard. It does not welcome you, because it has no loneliness. It is complete. It is a parliament of producers that has achieved unanimous rule, a cathedral of no saints where the congregation is the architecture.

If you sat for an hour, it would not move for you. If you returned in a year, you would find the false canopy lower, the ochre handrail swallowed to the elbow by copper moss, the red lamps one shade closer to extinction. It changes, but the way geology changes. It is patient. It does not hurry toward entropy or renewal; it simply persists, hot and loud and dim and dripping, a grove of no season, a machine of no master.

And it will be here, tending itself in the dark, long after the words we bring to it have corroded softly into the moss. It will turn. It will steam. It will weep. It will live.
