At first you mistake the tenancy. The swifts are so numerous, the crickets so insistent, the sump-frogs so bright and clattering, that it seems obvious: this is a hollow crowded with wildlife, a den without a landlord. You assume the animals own the walls because they are the only things moving. But watch longer. Notice how the blue skinks halt at the edge of the herringbone paths, never crossing certain lines. Notice how the hairless shrew-pups, when they wake, orient their whiskers not toward the warmest brick but toward the center, as if checking a clock. The noise is not chaos. It is congregation. They have filled the chamber because something has asked them to fill it, something that prefers a living carpet to a silent floor.

The floor knows. Walk the perimeter and the bricks are merely worn. Walk the inner ring, the ellipse that knots around the central pillar, and the clay cups underfoot like old spoons. Here the mortar has turned to glass, fused by pressure and the same circuit, repeated for centuries, of bare feet—too narrow, too long, four toes or five? The path is walked at dawn and dusk, when the amber discs from the sun-pipes lie flat and cool. The walker is calculating. Listening. Feeling the day's heat retreat through the soles.

It emerges when the copper hanging-discs spin fastest, which is not when the wind is strongest but when the air inside achieves a particular thickness: heavy, honeyed, alive with cricket-song. It steps out of the pillar. You do not see a door open; rather, the pillar exhales, and there is space where there should be none, and a figure that was folded inside the ceramic now stands beside it. It is taller than a person, but not grotesquely so—perhaps twice the height of the frogs’ sounding pool—yet it occupies its height badly, as if borrowed from some other geometry. Its limbs are long and wrong-jointed, bending at spirals rather than hinges, so that when it walks it seems to unscrew itself from the air and then tighten back down. The skin is the color of dusted terracotta at the thighs and shoulders, shading to ash-gray at the narrow trunk, and at the fingers it is glazed to a gloss by centuries of handling wet clay and soft copper. It wears nothing. It does not need to. Its nakedness is as unremarkable as a tree’s.

The head is elongated, smooth, lacking the furious detail of a face; where eyes should be there are instead two shallow, oval depressions lined with spiraling scar tissue, or perhaps growth rings, like the inside of a trunk. It does not see as you do. It tastes the light. It hears the shadows. The hundreds of sun-pipes pour their amber down onto its scalp, and the skin there, thin as eggshell, flushes with warmth, mapping the sun’s position across the ceiling vault in a language of temperature. It knows noon without looking.

It moves through the chamber with a proprietary slowness. It goes first to the northern terraces, where the light is weakest and the gray lichen blooms. It touches the tiles with fingertips that leave no mark but take something away—a spore, a grain of mineral oil, a vibration. Then to the hanging gourd-nets, where it reaches in among the sleeping rodents with infinite gentleness, its too-long fingers weaving through their bodies like reeds, checking their warmth, their number, adjusting a braided sinew line that has loosened. It is not feeding them. It is tuning them.

At the sump, it kneels. The tree-frogs do not flee. They part around its knees like water around a stone, their bright bodies bumping against its shins. It cups the rainwater in both hands—hands that could span a swift’s nest—and lifts it to the oval depression of its mouthless lower face. There is no swallowing sound. The water vanishes, and the cinnamon smell intensifies, as if its insides are heated spice and alum. It does this three times. Then it takes a pebble from the basin’s floor, slick and blackened by centuries of rolling, and pressed against the terracotta of its belly, it hums. The sound is not vocal. It is a resonance in the clay plates of its chest, matching the frequency of the tallest sun-pipes, the one felt in the sternum. The frogs answer, one by one, tuning their wooden throats to the same note. The sump becomes an orchestra pit, briefly consolidated, before the creature releases the pebble and the frogs scatter again into their individual, clattering conversations.

But most of its time is spent at the pillar, which is neither its shrine nor its prison but its unfinished body. It does not enter and exit the cocoon so much as it remembers itself into and out of it. Inside, among the pierced holes and soft copper grommets, it coils like a question mark, its joints nested, its ash-gray belly against the laminated straw. The grommets fit its wrists and ankles, holding it suspended, breathless, receptive. From this vantage the entire chamber is an instrument played upon it: the drip of the buried pottery chime, the expansion of leather ropes, the wet settling of brick. It lies there and listens to what the world above is saying through the earth, the pipes, the thermal convection of the copper discs.

And it writes.

Not with ink. With itself. The clay scales that litter the pillar’s base are its skin’s overflow, or perhaps its thoughts made tangible. While it curls inside the cocoon, the body generates them—presses them out through the grommets, or from between its own spiraling fingers—each one stamped with a mark that is one turn of an endless, non-repeating grammar. It has been writing the same text since before the floor dished, since before the swifts first found the upper alcoves. The text is a transcription of the chamber’s own maturation: the exact angle of every sun-pipe, the date of every brick’s firing, the number of cricket chirps between noon and dusk on the third day of the wet season. It is recording the exact shape of a place that has had time to settle, because once the recording is complete, the hatching can begin.

What will hatch? Not the creature itself. The creature is the warden of the hatching. The pillar is too tall, too thin, its proportions wrong because it is not a shell but a mold. Inside it, slowly, something else is being shaped from the accumulation of listened-to sound, of filtered amber light, of the mineral breath of cinnamon and niter, of the endless text of spirals. A second body. A next body. The clay scales are the sloughed-off attempts, the failed first sentences of a thing that must be described perfectly before it can be born. The inhabitant is not the life of the chamber. It is the chamber’s patient, scribbling womb.

When the sun sinks and the pipes darken to violet, the swifts pour into their alcoves as one, and the crickets fall silent along the eastern wall. The creature stands again at the sump’s edge, motionless, its glazed fingers trailing in the water. It faces the pillar. The copper discs overhead slow, catch the last light, and throw a single spark into the highest corbel. The chamber steams slightly. The damp rises. And if you wait there, past the hour when any human should stay, you will see the pillar’s holes begin to glow—not with light, but with a faint, dry warmth—and hear from inside it the sound of a new clay scale being pushed out, ringing soft against its neighbors, adding one more unrepeatable character to the heap.

The floor bricks wait. The frogs clap. The inhabitant does not sleep. It attends. It has nothing else, and wants nothing else, and in that wanting-nothing, it has become indistinguishable from the warm, crowded, ancient world it is slowly, precisely, birthing.
