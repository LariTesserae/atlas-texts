The ones here do not cry at birth. The cold would punish the vibration, and the fog would swallow it whole before it reached another ear, but it is more than that: they are born knowing that sound is a currency without value in this economy. Their first act is not a scream but a held breath, lungs learning to hoard the thin, iron air. They emerge wet, for the womb is dug into the ice where the seep runs warmest, and their skin carries the same black gleam as the schist, polished by the dense fog and by generations of water passing over them. They are small at first, compact as knot-stones, because the place tolerates no wasted motion. But they grow long in the spine, in the fingers, in the reach required to climb ladders of sinew and scapulae and to cantilever their weight across the wet glacier plug without slipping. Their bones are heavy, mineralized by the ancient melt they drink; when they walk, their footfalls carry a dull, subsonic thud felt through the ice long before any notion of sound could travel in the air. Their eyes are dark—not merely brown or black, but obsidian-dark, without white or iris, a single pool of pigment evolved to drink the absolute light without blindness. They do not blink often. The glare here is not braved with lashes but with a nictitating caul that freezes in the outer air and thaws against the warmth of the cheekbone, a thin slickskin of ice that washes the retina clean. Their ears are reduced to fur-sealed holes, like the foxes they move beside, and they hear as the foxes do: through the pads of their feet, through the hinge of the jaw, listening to the glacier’s arguments with itself rather than to any lover’s whisper.

And there are no whispers. There is no spoken tongue. What would be the point? The silence is not an absence to them but a presence, a thick felt presence that presses against the face like a damp palm. They communicate by thermal exchange—pressing wrists together so the chill blood moves in sympathetic rhythm, or breathing onto each other’s necks so the fog crystallizes in glyphs of rime that can be read before they collapse. They speak by touch, by the angle of a shoulder in a bone doorway, by the arrangement of obsidian flakes on a ledge that catches the violet halo. Their language is editorial, carved into the things they build and the things the ice replicates after them. A bridge of scapulae says *we crossed here*; the ice bridge that replaces it days later says *I remember*; the repair, the new lashings of sinew, the shoring with basalt says *but you misremembered the tension*. The city is a long, wordless manuscript written by two hands—one flesh, one water—and they read it together, brushing elbows in the fog.

At first you might think they build to survive. Watch longer, and you will see they build to teach. They are curators of a tutorial. Bone is stubborn; it holds its shape against the seep, provides a model the glacier can copy, a grammar of load and span that water, left alone, would never invent. When a ladder of vertebrae collapses and an identical ladder of hoarfrost rises in its place, they do not mourn the failure; they climb the ice ladder to see if it holds, recording where the replication is true and where it wavers. The totemic markers they drive into the zones of greatest turbulence are not warnings but lessons in topology. The cisterns of frozen hide are templates: this is how a bowl holds. This is how weight rests. They are showing the ice how to remember, and in exchange the ice shows them what it is to be fluid, to erode, to archive without sentiment.

Their hearths are few, shallow, and precious. There is no wood to burn, no peat, no green drying into kindling. They feed the flames with compressed cakes of pink algae scraped from swallow-guano pools, with the dried and powdered viscera of the pale fish, with rendered midge-fat that burns with a blue, smokeless tongue. The fires are arguments against the wet authority, dryness held in the mouth like a secret. The smoke does not rise; it creeps along the ceilings of their burrows, restless as a cat, until it finds the walls and blackens them over generations. They tend these flames with the same devotion they tend the seep-channels—not because they expect to win, but because the argument itself is the point. Cold fire, warm light. Each is a different response to the sun’s indifferent barrage, and both are necessary.

They do not age in years. They age in phases. A child is born when the seep runs highest, and is considered a youth while the hoarfrost spires are in their building season. They reach maturity when they can stand naked in the killing light for the length of time it takes a cornice to detach and fall—the slow, tumbling waltz—without shivering, their blood thickened to syrup by the chemistry of the fish they eat. Their elders are those who have been replicated. Once, perhaps twice, the ice has raised a perfect spire of their fallen body before shattering it into diamond dust. They eat the midges in dark, protein-rich mouthfuls, and the midges’ antifreeze enters their plasma until their veins run opalescent and slow. They drink the melt until their bones begin to fluoresce in the violet halos, faintly, from within. They press their bellies against the densest crevices where the springtails breed, feeling the static shiver through their skin, a lullaby felt in the sternum where words cannot go. They are becoming the medium. They are becoming worthy of replication.

And perhaps that is all they are. Not a people, but a persistence. Not inhabitants, but votive objects left as reminders that shape matters.

Watch one of them in the center of the basin, during the hour when the sun hangs lowest and the light is arterial. A woman is working. She is replacing a collapsed platform, threading new sinew through basalt anchors and the ribs of something larger than bears. Her movements have the soft, scuffing precision of the white foxes. Around her, the air churns with its unseen business: water feathering into rime, ice needles collapsing, the fog resettling in a saturation of phases. She does not hum. She does not curse when the wet soaks her frost-fur lining. She works in the liquid silence, and the silence watches. When she finishes, she climbs to an aerie-room and sits beside the ember-bowl, and she scrapes obsidian against obsidian to make a flake that will cut the next piece of whalebone. The strike produces almost nothing—a *whuff*, a breath against velvet. Then nothing. But the glacier feels it. The vibration travels down the bone ladder, into the ice plug, into the slow argument.
