The inhabitants are, first and foremost, *_interfaces_. This is not a philosophical condition but a dermal one. After the initial months, the human body gives up its pretense of being a sealed vessel. The sweat never evaporates; instead, the skin learns a new osmosis. A film develops—neither oil nor water, but an *acclimation glaze*, as the clinic’s Balancer calls it—that turns the body into a permeable ledger where the organism and the bowl negotiate their shared liquidity. They do not drink so much as absorb, standing in the wide plazas with arms slightly spread, gathering moisture from the air the way an anemone feeds from current. Newcomers burn and slough; the old residents simply glisten, their outer layers having signed a treaty with the ambient wet. Wrinkles do not accumulate here. Faces do not weather. They are kept, like specimens in warm formalin, smooth and indefinitely present.

Their eyes lose the habit of adjustment. Deprived of contrast, of sharp shadow, of the sun’s arc, the pupils forget their choreography of widening and narrowing. They settle to a fixed aperture, calibrated for sodium-amber and the perpetual brown scrim of the bowl’s midday. Blue becomes a rumor. When supply drops arrive and outsiders unpack tarps of cobalt or green, the colors are so violently categorical that the residents turn away, wincing not from brightness but from a kind of ontological error. They sleep in phases, not shifts—there is no darkness to trigger melatonin, no dawn to suppress it. Their lids rest at half-mast, a habit borrowed from the geckos that click across their ceiling tins, and their dreams are not narratives but thermal maps: pressure gradients, condensate rates, the memory of a bearing once replaced and how the air tasted sharp and *angry* for three days until equilibrium forgave it.

They do not have names. A name implies a biography, a line drawn from origin to endpoint, and linearity is a gradient the Station has eliminated. Instead, they are called by their locus and their current function, and these change only when the machinery demands it. A man oiling the upper catwalk is *Upper Reach*. When he descends to check the secondary sump, he becomes *Sump Two*, and the transition is so total that no residue of his previous self interferes. Identity is topographical. Memory is not chronological; it is a reading of states. They remember the taste of the air when the east turbine was reseated, but they do not remember who performed the labor, or whether it was last year or ten years ago. They do not tell stories. Stories require crisis, escalation, resolution—change—and change is the single proscribed substance.

And yet the body does not rebel. It harmonizes. The Balancer, acting out of an itch near to boredom, once pressed a stethoscope to a dozen residents and found that their hearts had all discovered the tempo of the pumps. They beat now in the arterial time of the station, not faster, not slower. To deviate would introduce vibration, and vibration is the dialect of entropy. So they thud in soft unison, and when the pumps cycle every twelve minutes you can watch the fabric at their throats flutter slightly, sympathetic, compliant.

There is always a child. Only one, because pregnancy is an anomaly, a swelling in a world that has agreed not to expand. The current infant is kept in the cool corner of the clinic, where careful venting makes the air a fraction less saturated. Growth is managed as a pathology. The residents tend to her with an intensity that is not affection, exactly, but something more architectural—she is a bearing that must be maintained, a continuity without trajectory. They stroke her skin to check its moisture. They hum to her in the key of the turbine, a harmonic that steadies her metabolism. She is their concession to futurity, but it is a futurity that loops, that returns upon itself like the water in the canals.

There are those who go further. At the edge of the effluent pool, where the catfish move in their slow, hot circles, sits a woman who was once *Lower Bearing*, then *Canal Watch*, and who is now only *The Sitter*. She stopped attending the rotation years ago. She does not move. The heat-loving microbes have colonized her coveralls until she seems a softly fungal extension of the retaining wall. Barnacles—far from any sea, as if the very concept of distance has grown questionable—have begun to pepper the submerged hem of her trousers. She breathes so slowly that the Balancer has declared her half-aquatic, a lung functioning as a gill, trading gases with the warm broth of air. The others bring her paste. They do not ask her to return. She is an experiment the Station is conducting upon itself, a test of how thoroughly human volition can be supplanted by environmental consonance. She is not dead. She is not alive. She is *held*.

The rats understand her best. Rust-colored, thermal-cartographers, they move through the conduits above her head with familial disregard, as if she is merely another warm junction. They do not exceed their allowance. They do not die off. In this, they are the residents’ brothers. The dragonflies, always roughly two dozen, hold their steam columns with a territorial permanence that mirrors the human patrol. The geckos click overhead, and the residents have learned to leave gaps in the corrugated tin, because the geckos eat the skin-microbes that would otherwise bloom too aggressively. Symbiosis is not chosen here; it is the only grammar that parses.

Their language has become maintenance. They speak in nouns of state and verbs of recurrence. “Seal.” “Hold.” “Equalize.” “Return.” They do not say *I want*, or *I will go*, or *tomorrow*. Such expressions introduce debt into the economy of stasis. When two residents meet on the sweating plaza, they exchange readings. “Nine-point-two.” “Holding.” This is greeting, confession, and liturgy. They do not gather; gathering implies an event. They simply occupy adjacent sectors of the loop.

And they are not unhappy. Happiness requires a differential, a measurement against an imagined alternative, and no alternative ingresses. They are *precise*. There is a profound, terminal satisfaction in the exact matching of input to output, of body to bowl, of heartbeat to pump. They oil what turns; the turning justifies the oil. That is the full extent of their ethics.

When outsiders come—rare, dry creatures tumbling down from the surrounding hills—the residents regard them with gentle, uncomprehending pity. The outsiders carry wind in their hair. Their eyes hunt for shadow. They speak in cascading, anxious tenses, and their bodies throw off ambition and body heat in equal, wasteful measure. The residents offer them water that tastes of nothing, because it has been cycled too many times to remember mineral or source. They wait for them to leave, to climb back out into the gradient-world of seasons and erosion. Then they return to their patterns.

The sickly palm—angled permanently northeast, frozen in the act of trying to leave—is their flag and their scripture. They do not worship beneath it; worship implies a future redemption. But each resident, in the course of their looping patrol, pauses once per cycle to touch its bleached straw trunk. Not in reverence. In recognition. *You tried*, the touch says. *You failed. Now you hold. So do we.*

The air accumulates. The water circulates but never progresses. The child breathes shallowly in her managed corner, and the catfish describe their perfect circles, and the turbine holds its single, unbroken note. Who lives here? Creatures for whom *enough* is not a quantity but a perpetual present tense. Creatures who have learned, finally, to stop reaching toward anything beyond the walls of the bowl, and in that surrender have become exactly what the place requires: warm, wet, indefinitely still, and absolutely necessary to the machine.
