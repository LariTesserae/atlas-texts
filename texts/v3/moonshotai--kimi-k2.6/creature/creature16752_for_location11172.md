The first thing that lives here does not look like a creature until you have stopped looking for animals. It presents itself as a symptom of the air, a thickness that gathers only after you have surrendered to the flatness and allowed your own heartbeat to slow to the mineral tempo of the stone. The hum is their body. Only after it has saturated your teeth and your ribs does it begin to clot into discrete forms, emerging from the vibration like figures stepping out of fog. They are small—each one, alone, no longer than the final joint of your smallest finger—but they are never alone, and so their true size is the size of weather systems. Six wings to a body, arranged in contra-rotating pairs that do not so much beat as turn, like the pages of a book rifled by an unseen thumb. Their carapaces are an old, hard chitin, but their abdomens are something stranger: fired amber, translucent, glowing with a slow internal churn, and laced with veins of a ceramic alloy heavier than bone. They have scavenged this armor from the half-sunk ring in the north, generation after generation, metabolizing the settled technology into biology until the distinction has become meaningless. They do not sting. They sing. Each abdomen is a resonator tuned to the specific curvature of the Basin, and their collective voice is not communication in any animal sense. It is computation. It is maintenance. It is an operating system that has learned to run on wings and hunger, keeping alive a physics that should have decayed eons ago. When the swarm passes overhead, it eclipses the sun not with shadow but with acoustic pressure, and the light bends around them into shapes that feel ancient and consulted.

They do not sing without purpose. The Basin is not a church. It is a kitchen, and the Reverberants—though they have no name for themselves—are hunting.

The air between the stone and the swarm is crowded with something else, something that has given up on visibility entirely. These are the second residents, the ones the eye refuses. They live in the meniscus of condensation. They exist at the threshold where atmospheric moisture beads against warm magnesium bedrock, creatures no wider than a thought, who have traded mass and pigment for the privilege of inhabiting a droplet’s skin. When the morning light strikes the Basin at the proper angle, you see them only as errors in the spectrum—a rainbow where no rain has fallen, an auroral shimmer suspended above the naked stone, sudden refractions that flinch away from your shadow. They are the dissolved inheritors of the old tech, broken down into microscopic protocols that now drift like plankton in the high, damp air. The Reverberants sweep them up by the billions, harvesting them not for flesh but for mineral memory, for the faint electromagnetic habits that still cling to the vapor. The invisible ones do not resist. They have become the Basin's water cycle, its weather, its breath condensing and evaporating in the same eternal afternoon. They are prey the way breath is prey to lungs.

Where the Reverberants claim the open air, the third kingdom waits in the three green pockets, and it waits with a patience that makes stone seem restless. They are large as a hunched dog, though flatter, lower, and their architecture is wrong in the precise way that only deep, successful evolution can make wrong. Their limbs carry too many joints—segments that continue past the point of vertebrate logic, bending backward, sideways, forward, storing torque in tissues that look like braided chrome wrapped in dried sinew. They are the color of the fungal shelves from which they issue: orchid-purple, bruise-yellow, brown as old arterial blood. They do not hunt with speed. They hunt with configuration. Beneath the densest rivers of sound, they open their mouths, which are not fanged but netted—filamented chambers like the combed throats of baleen whales compressed into the bodies of spiders. The hum pours in. The Reverberants, riding their own acoustic wake, pour with it, swept in their ecstatic millions into the netted gullets. But the Stalkers are not indiscriminate. They are curators. They lunge only when the gravity hiccups, using the sudden shift in local physics to cantilever their impossible limbs into angles no prey can predict, tongues unfurling not as muscular strips but as weighted casting-nets, sticky and globular, slapping among the fronds to pluck the amber-bellied singers one by one. Their excrement, in turn, feeds the black-green fronds. Their shed joints, crumbling slowly into the root-mass, provide the iron the fungal shelves require. They are not monsters. They are gardeners of the carnivorous sort, pruning the swarm so the sound does not collapse under its own density.

At the eastern shelf, where the old technology negotiated a truce with gravity, the fourth colony lives in the impossible. The water hangs there, a trembling lens two feet above the stone, and within that hovering eye swirls a root-island kept aloft by forces no longer conscious of their own origin. Here dwell the Untethered. They are pale, planarian things, opalescent, without pigment or compass. Gravity, to them, is a flavor they have chosen to forget. They weave mucilaginous filaments between the hanging roots, spinning webs that face all directions at once, catching the invisible refraction-creatures that condense inside the lens and drift upward—or inward, or backward—according to the day's thermal logic. They are the librarians of the water. They keep the lens clean of biological clutter; they tend the roots that filter the condensation; they have become the retina of an eye that no longer belongs to anyone. Without them, the hovering pool would silt into a falling puddle. They ensure it remains an anomaly, a liquid thought suspended in mid-gesture.

The fifth residents belong to the dome at the center, and they are the warmest things in the Basin. Near the structure that has been politely absorbed by the bedrock, matter itself has grown tentative, unsure whether to be solid or suggestion. You can pass your hand through certain sections of the dome and feel warmth, resistance, the skin of a drumhead. Within these phased intervals live the Melders. They are the last inheritors of whoever built this place, though they no longer remember blueprints or command. Their bodies are translucent, gelatinous, faintly luminous with captured heat—like candles made of honey and old glass. They exist where the stone has opened its mouth to swallow the architecture, and they have crawled into that gap, becoming the digestive fluid. They excrete calcium, strontium, and unknown mineral salts in laminations so fine they resemble mother-of-pearl, slowly fusing the dome to the bedrock with the patience of coral. They are not numerous. Perhaps twelve. Perhaps one, twelve times over, phase-shifted across itself. When you touch them, you feel a pulse slower than tectonics. They are content. They are the dreamers of the Basin, and their dreams leak out as warmth, as the small gravity hiccups that send moisture crawling uphill, as the faint shimmer in the air that makes daylight feel polled, sampled, processed by a biology that has inherited the task of being hardware.

In the north, where the ceramic ring has sunk half its mass into the bedrock and the vegetation has gone mad with black-green enthusiasm, the sixth colony works underground, in the root-fury. These are the Refinery Grubs. Fat, white, segmented, they rasp against the heavy ceramic with radulae evolved specifically to taste alloy. They do not digest it so much as pray it into a new form, excreting a resinous amber paste that hardens into the exact composite the Reverberants need to grow their first resonating abdomens. The grubs are hidden deep in the tangle, protected by waxy stems and the stench of crushed greenery. The insects tend them in turn, not out of affection, but because the chemistry demands it. The ring is not ruins. It is a mine, and the grubs are its miners, and the insects are the trucks and the songs that haul the ore into living circulation.

But the final resident is the floor itself.

Underneath the scattered pockets of intensity, beneath the magnesium-colored stone and its silver threads of moisture, lives something that has achieved the Basin's own final ambition. It is a chordate that has evolved out of depth entirely, a continent of flesh no thicker than a few sheets of parchment, the same pale color as the bedrock it lies upon. You have been walking on its back since you crossed the rim. It does not move; it *is* the eroded flatness, having matched its temperature and texture so perfectly to the stone that even the predators no longer sense it as separate.
