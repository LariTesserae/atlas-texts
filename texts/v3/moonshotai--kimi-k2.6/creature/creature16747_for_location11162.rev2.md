They are not a civilization. They are a consequence.

Each one is a vertical thing, built for holding on. Generation upon generation of climbing has elongated the body without warping it: fingers slightly too long, toes splayed and prehensile, capable of gripping the fossil whorls of ancient growth rings where no rope has been lashed. Their skin has forgotten dryness. It holds the moisture of the interior not as a film but as a condition, a parchment dampness that drinks the green-gold haze and returns it as a faint, internal glow. They are pale under the leaf-light, not with the bloodless pallor of deep caves but with the flush of chlorophyll shadow—skin tinted amber where the sun diffuses through new growth overhead, translucent at the wrists and temples where veins show like leaf-venation. Hair is sparse; what grows is fine, colorless, beaded at the tips with the mineral drizzle. They wear the minimum of woven bast fiber: climbing slings, gathering nets, loincloths stiff with sap. Modesty is irrelevant in the massif’s breath-close warmth; utility is everything, and naked skin listens better to the architecture.

But their most telling adaptation is the stillness. In a world that deafens, they have become creatures of absolute silence. Their ears—if ears are what these are—have migrated inward. They hear through their palms, through the arches of their feet, through the bones of their skulls pressed against lignin. When a hunter stands motionless on a root-girder, the whole skeleton becomes a tuning fork, reading the billion-voice drone for the particular vibration of a bladder-bat’s boom or the thermal shift of descending spores. Speech is impossible here, and so they have not merely abandoned it; they have replaced it with older senses.

They communicate by tap, by breath, by the reading of skin.

When two meet—and meeting is rare, perhaps once in a season of the plant’s million-year metabolism—they do not call out across the void. They climb toward one another with the patience of aerial roots, closing the distance until they stand on the same shelf of periderm, close enough that the exhalation of one ghosts across the cheek of the other. Then they read. The breath carries chemistry: what was eaten, what was feared, the iron taste of a long ascent, the sweetness of readiness or grief. They place their faces in each other’s necks and inhale. A sigh is a paragraph. The humidity on another’s upper lip is punctuation. To know someone here is to breathe them, to let the damp air between you become a shared lung. If there is affection, it is shown by pressing palm to sternum, feeling the heartbeat through the damp skin, a rhythm answered by the receiver’s own hand. If there is warning, it is a sudden exhalation, hot and sharp, carrying the scent of alarm.

For distance, there is the wall. They press their knuckles against the root-stone and tap. The language is complex as any spoken tongue: a vocabulary of fingernail scratches, heel drums, and the soft percussion of knuckle against grain. A greeting is five swift notes; a warning is a slow spiral drummed into a resonant buttress. Because the hollow massif is a single sounding chamber, these vibrations travel for miles through the lignin strata. The inhabitants read the architecture like a living telegraph, their hands spread flat against the walls to catch messages from other valleys of air. An elder tapping in a distant vault becomes a tremor in the fingers of a child dangling in a lower gallery. The plant carries their words.

They do not farm. The organism farms them, in its way, and they are its harvesters, its itinerant pollen, its sparse and careful appetite. The condensation-bees, those rivers of copper thread, are gathered in woven bast sieves. A gatherer hangs inverted over a cylindrical well, secured by rope and harness, and lets the insect tide pour through the net until the mesh is heavy with paste. The bees are pressed into cakes, fermented in the axils of leaf-matter, eaten as a thick sustenance that tastes of honeyed copper and ozone. The bladder-bats are hunted in the deep shafts, stalked by hunters who have learned to translate boom into topography, snaring the creatures in trip-lines of hair-fine rootlet. And water—water is both religion and meal. The fishers of condensation string fiber nets across cooler inner walls, collecting the mineral drizzle in gourds grown from the plant’s own dried seed-pods. They know the thermal currents by taste: the iron-and-chlorophyll updraft of the chimneys, the spore-heavy sweetness of evening downdrafts. They move with the massif’s breathing, climbing when the warm air rises, descending to sleep in the pooled humidity of the lower galleries.

Sleep is not a collapse but a spreading-out. They weave hammocks across narrow throats of air and lie with limbs extended, skin open to the damp, letting the unending drone press them down like a hand. Their rest is shallow, avian, alert. Perhaps they dream in vibrations.

Children are born in the warmest hollows, where the air is thickest and most intimate. The infants arrive small, wet, and preternaturally quiet. They do not cry; crying is a useless expenditure of moisture and sound. Instead they grip, instinctively, with hands and feet, latching onto the first root-hold within reach. They are fed on the milk of certain cracked seed-pods and on the paste of condensation-bees. They grow slowly, climbing before they walk, learning the tap-language before they can imagine any other way to speak. By the time they are old enough to span a root-girder alone, they navigate by the taste of the air and the resonance of the walls, native to the interior in a way their ancestors, if they had ancestors, never were.

They do not bury their dead. There is no raw earth to receive them, only the living strata. The deceased are laid in the great axils where the perpetual drip gathers, placed in the bathtubs of leaf-matter and left for the root-stone to absorb. Over decades, the body diffuses into the load-bearing periderm, composted into the warm, woody substrate. It is not death but return. The living do not mark these passages with tears—tears are too small for the scale of the massif. They mourn by climbing to a high chamber and tapping a single, final rhythm into the wall: a slow, deliberate pattern that says *here was one, and now there is none, only the plant*. The vibration carries, and those who feel it understand.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them, perhaps fewer. The massif is vast enough that one could live an entire lifetime without encountering another of one’s kind. Solitude is their native condition. They are not lonely; they are diffuse, like the spores they breathe. Each is a solitary node in a network that does not require proximity. The soot stains, the flaked chert tools, the half-carved ladders ascending into mist—these are not evidence of a village abandoned, but of a presence so light it barely interrupts the ecology. They are the opposite of colonizers. They are the inhabitant-as-lichen, a thin, attentive smear of intention across the interior surface.

And yet they are not primitive. The chert is flaked with a precision that suggests philosophy—each strike considered, because the material is rare, carried perhaps from some distant edge where mineral still persists, or prised from the fossilized hearts of ancient roots. Fire is used sparingly, sunken into hearths that glow like slow red eyes, burning the compacted leaf-pellets not for warmth—the massif is warm enough—but for light during the dim cycles, and for transformation: hardening tools, rendering fat, sending thin smoke signals up the chimneys to mark a place in the green-gold twilight. Their stories are not sung; they are woven into the ropes, carved into the ladders. A bridge of lashed bast-fiber is a collaboration, a silent epic worked by two or more who met, labored without words, and parted. The architecture of their passage is their history. Where they have been, the plant is subtly altered—a pruned aerial root, a cleaned axil, a pattern of wear on a growth-ring handhold. They are editors, not authors.

As the centuries turn inside the organism, they have forgotten anything outside. The concept of sky is a myth of the cold outer shell, a place of thin air and brutal light. Their cosmos is bounded by lignin and leaf-haze. They understand, if understanding is what they do, that the world is a single breathing body and they are its mobile thought—temporary, aware, held. When the root-stone creaks in its geological metabolism, expanding with moisture and contracting with dryness, they feel it in their teeth and know the body is turning in its sleep.

And in the deepest shafts, where the light is only a rumor and the drone becomes a pressure felt in the chest rather than heard, there are said to be older ones. Not dead, but diffused. Dwellers who have climbed so far inward that they no longer need the bee-paste or the condensation. They have pressed their bodies into the warm root walls and let the plant’s slow pulse move through them. Their eyes are closed. Their breathing aligns with the chimneys. They have become something between animal and tissue, anchors of consciousness in the woody dark. Whether this is death, transcendence, or merely the final stage of adaptation, no one can tap clearly enough to explain. But the others leave them there, reading the subtle warmth of their exhalation in the bark, and know that the massif is still making room, still sculpting the emptiness, still patiently deciding what, at the end of erosion, it wants to become.
