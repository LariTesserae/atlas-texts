They are narrow. Not merely thin, but narrowed—compressed by altitude and by architecture into a physiology of clefts and slots. Their chests are deep, almost keel-like, housing lungs that have grown patient with the metallic air, drawing each breath in a slow expansion that would seem, to a lowlander, like a deliberate sigh. They do not pant. They do not shout. Their voices have settled into registers that ride the thin cold without shattering it: low, dry, intimate, carrying through the vertical gaps between dwellings with an ease that makes privacy a shared fiction. They speak as they move, with an economy that matches their gait—no gesture wasted, no word loose enough to tumble into the fog and become noise.

Their skin is pale beyond the pallor of sunlessness; it is a damp pallor, a complexion fed by condensation rather than by light. In the rare moments when direct sun strikes them on high bridges or at the rim of a basin-roof, they do not bask; they turn away, blinking, their eyes adapted to the perpetual gray twilight of the lower floors, to the phosphorescent dimness where the only reflections come from water trickling in basalt channels. Their pupils are perhaps too large, their night-sight too keen, so that full daylight hurts them like a loud sound.

They live by descending and ascending. A life here is plotted on a vertical axis. The infants are born in the warm, dark, ammoniac depths where the flocks huddle and the fungal cellars breathe, swaddled in hides against the damp that would otherwise slick their skin to dangerous chill. As they age, they climb—slowly, as if the city were a ladder made of generations—seeking cooler air, sharper angles of light, until the old withdraw to the high, dry ledges to sleep their final sleeps in the bright cold, and the dead are laid out in the highest ossuaries to become part of the desiccating wind. To grow old is to ascend toward silence and light; to be young is to burrow in the warm wet dark.

Their culture is hydrothermal. They read the city not by maps but by gradients. A destination is described as a temperature, a humidity, a quality of drip. *I am going to the cooler fog,* one says, or *She lives three warmths below the swift-nests.* Their navigation is dermal; they feel the subtle shift as a cistern-breath gives way to a ridge-wind. They know the architecture by touch in the lower halls, fingertips tracing the carved channels that have become a braille of public memory—grooves indicating the direction of flow, the age of the stone, the generation that last deepened the basin above.

They are carvers, not builders. With bone levers and harder stone, they do not conquer the basalt but negotiate with it, working at angles that follow the plateau’s own fractures. A single facade records a century of hands, each generation adding another layer of channel, another lip, another catch-basin, so that a family’s history is written not in portraits but in hydrology—in how their wall gathers, retains, and releases the fog. To own a wall is meaningless; one belongs to the water that passes down it. The labor is so slow that a person knows they will not live to see the completion of any major work; they are merely a single exhalation in the city’s longer breath. This has dissolved the lowland fever of individual legacy. They move as cells in a tissue, content to be the intermediate hand between the quarry and the great-great-grandchild who will finish the stair.

They are warm-blooded in a place that punishes warmth. Every body is a furnace, every exhalation a contribution. The millions of them, breathing together, create the low cloud-ceiling that sustains the lichens, the insects, the birds. They understand this not as metaphysics but as engineering. A crowd gathering in a lower court is a deliberate act of meteorology, a seeding of moisture. They tend their flocks and their fires with the same attention a lowlander gives to irrigation canals, knowing that the heat of the animals, the smoke of the tallow, the press of bodies in the damp cellars—all of it rises, condenses, falls, and feeds the trickle-music of the walls. They are gardeners of breath.

The animals are theirs, yet not domesticated in the lowland sense. The pale moths that breed in their condensation pools are welcomed; the beetles in the interstitial walls are heard like a heartbeat; the swifts that knife through the clefts are messengers, their arrival a seasonal note more reliable than any calendar. The commensal rodents are not pests but silent housemates, their warmth in the walls another layer of insulation. The people have bred themselves into the ecology so completely that they no longer imagine a boundary between the city and the wild. The wild is the cleft; the city is the cleft. They are the wild made warm.

They are quiet, but not because they are somber. Sound is simply a different medium here. In the thin air, a shout would tear itself apart; a song would fall flat. Instead, they have developed arts of proximity—music played on instruments of bone and hollow stone that resonate in the chest rather than the ear, theater performed in whispers across windowsills, literature that is scratched into the wet walls to be read by fingers trailing through condensation. Their greatest art might be the conversation across a narrow slot, two faces framed in stone windows, passing words back and forth until they are not two speakers but a single circuit of thought.

They do not dream of horizons. The concept of open land, of a boundary between settlement and wilderness, is as foreign to them as the ocean. Their world is complete, contiguous, dense. A month’s walk in any direction still passes between thresholds, under lintels—there is no exit, only deeper interior. This has made their cosmology inward and downward. They believe, if belief is the right word for a thing so self-evident, that the center of the world is not a place but a temperature—the perfect point where warmth, damp, and darkness achieve a balance so exact that time itself slows to a drip. They seek this center not by pilgrimage but by living, by descending to the deepest cisterns where the water is black and the stone sweats, and there, in the fetal dark, they know they have arrived.

They are not a tribe. They are not a nation. They are the metabolism of the plateau—a slow, cold, damp combustion of breath and stone. Each one is temporary, a warm punctuation in the architecture, but together they have become the thing that outwaits the mountains. They do not wait for change. They are the change—the patient, collective, breathing change that turns raw black ridges into a habitable dusk, one exhalation, one whisper, one stone on stone, at a time.
