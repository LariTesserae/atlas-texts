The silver-bodied filter-feeders do not know themselves as survivors. Introduced as a protocol, escaped as a probability, they move through the Compensation Sea with the intimacy of a component inside its housing. Their scales are not bright with the quicksilver flash of ocean fish but dulled by precipitation: metalloid sulfides plate each minute scalelet in a patina of industrial bronze, so that a school seen from below resembles a sagging net of tarnished coins rather than flesh. They number in the millions because death here is a slow sink rather than a snap of teeth; they perish when their gill-rakers, overgrown with the very filtrate they harvest, can no longer pass water. Then they founder, rotating leaf-like into the haze beneath the light, still heavy with the poisons they stripped from the effluent.

In life, they are pure synchronization. When the shift-lights gutter in their sequence across the ferroceramic sky, the vertical migration begins—not a panic, but a scheduled withdrawal. They drop through the grey-green noon, a reverse snowfall of living metal, descending to meet the particulate load that bleeds down during the smelting cadence. Forty meters passes, then fifty, until they exist in a world where the false twilight has never been. There they drift in the scheduled currents, rakers spread like sensitive fingers, catching the fine metallic snow. They are the sea’s first act of digestion, and they obey the factory clock more faithfully than any technician on the dry side.

Nearer to the basal plate, where outflow vents bleed a modest heat into the four-degree cold, the bottom-crawlers make their claims. These are the armored ones, each about the mass of a terrier, plated not with flexible scale but with interlocking osteoderms that have the matte finish of kiln-fired ceramic. They are colorless where the light strikes them, grey-green where colonized by the hairline algae they graze. Their locomotion is hydraulic, segmented legs pushing in staggered sequence, while the feeder mouth—a piston-ringed aperture lined with masticating plates—grinds continually against the substrate and the stunted, engineered kelps. They are not graceful. They are engines of attrition, converting waste heat and engineered vegetation into a slow fecal sediment that will, in centuries to come, form the first honest mud of this place. For now, they are the only creatures that touch the poured ground with something like affection, hoarding the warmth discarded by machinery, clustering in steaming piles where outflows cross, so that from above they register not as animals but as ochre stains on the angular seabed.

Between the swimmers and the crawlers, clinging to the verticality of the pylons and the knife-edge rims of settling basins, the thousands of crustacean analogues make their sound. They are the punctuation of the place: click, scrape, click. Smaller than a hand, chitinous, and narrow-bodied, they traverse the weld beads and moldlines with an apparent purpose that owes nothing to the migrations of light or heat. They eat oxidation. They eat the biofilm that dares to colonize the reinforcement. They are the maintenance crew that was never invited but which arrived in the genes of the package, carrying in their segmented bodies a mandate to keep surfaces clean. The workers in their heated suits sometimes mistake their clicking for thermal contraction in a cooling pipe; the sea knows better. The analogues are the nervous system of the infrastructure, tasting rust, reporting by their presence where the poured walls are weakest, where the chemistry is advancing beyond the tolerance of the design. They do not swarm. They simply occupy, patient as rivets.

Then there are the humans. Descending in lock-step shifts, they arrive as warm intrusions, their suits humming with battery-drawn heat, their exhalations silvering the inside of polycarbonate faceplates. They carry waterproofed clipboards because the bureaucracy of the dry side extends even here, even into the absolute mutability of the water. They replace anodes. They inspect intake grilles.
