At first you think the basin is uninhabited, save for the sour yellow and jade veins licking across the overhead stone—those chemolithotropic films, patient as rust, converting the void’s chemistry into a thin, cold soup of energy. Animal life seems impossible here. No water, no pollen, no echo of wings. Only the mineral haze, the slow dunes wandering like the ghosts of seas, and the architecture: those low, eroded walls, the fallen avenues, the foundation pads scattered across the pale floor like the vertebrae of some continent-sized spine. You assume these are geology’s accidents, basalt intrusions or ash-flow tuffs worn by million-year winds into parodies of colonnades and plazas.

But then you spend more time in the twilight. You walk the floor until the dust works its way into the weave of your boots, and you begin to notice that the ruins are too rhythmic. The angles are wrong for pure erosion—there is a repetition, a spacing, a logic to the fallen. The knee-height dunes drift against certain walls and then stop, as if respectful, or as if the walls exhale just enough to barricade the sand. You place your palm on a threshold of pale bone-meal grit compacted into something like concrete, and the pressure in your teeth changes pitch. The threshold is humming. Not resonating with the dome’s drone, but *shaping* it.

You look closer. The architecture breathes, but so slowly that your eyes must borrow minutes to see the motion. The walls are stratified with bands of silica and dust, yes, but threaded through them are networks of pale jade tubules—chemolithotropic descendants, perhaps, grown macroscopic, or symbionts encased in their own mineral coffins. The stone is not stone. It is biology that has abandoned softness.

They do not reveal themselves all at once. You meet the first one near the eastern vents, where the thermal smolder warps the haze. It stands in the lee of a broken “tower,” a figure that at twenty meters might be a pillar, at ten meters a monolith, and at two meters—when the twilight finally resolves its edges—clearly *body*. It is taller than you, assembled from the basin’s own vocabulary: compacted talc and crystal, wind-rounded ash, the sulphur-yellow dust of the upper vault. It has no face. It has no need of one. Its surface is a continuous, matte skin of fused particulate, the color of old teeth, and it is shaped like a humanoid only in the loosest sense, the way a termite mound suggests a fortress, or a hoodoo suggests a robed saint.

It does not move as you approach. It is moving, but its tempo is measured in years. You realize, standing before it, that your footsteps have disturbed the floor’s particulate harmony; the subsonic drone from the dome’s perforations thrums differently here, warped by your mass. The figure’s “head” tilts—not quickly, not toward you exactly, but toward the *disturbance* you cast in the field of sound. It hears with its entire mineral body. The constant pressure that aches in your jaw is, for this thing, the medium of perception. It is a creature of infrasound, living inside the drone the way fish live in water.

You retreat, and over hours—or days, time blurs in the twilight—you begin to see them everywhere. The migrants. They drift across the basin floor at the speed of the dunes, sometimes indistinguishable from them. They are the dunes, or rather, the dunes are their larval states, their shed skins, their weather. A young one—if youth means anything over spans of millennia—is a low, humped accumulation of dust that inches toward the thermal vents, following the bacterial veins like a root follows water. It has no limbs in motion; instead, it dissolves its windward face and precipitates its leeward one, a slow conveyor of crystal and secreted silica binder, never rising above the knee-high horizon of drifting sand.

The adults have anchored. They are the architecture. The “neglected city” is not a ruin in any human sense; it is a metropolis of voluntary stillness. When one of the migrants has fed long enough on vent-gases and lithotrophic film, when it has stored enough mineral capital and mapped enough of the resonant field, it chooses a site and begins to grow upward. It accretes. It pulls the dust from the moving dunes and fuses it into extensions of its own mass, becoming wall, becoming buttress, becoming tower. Eventually it stops moving altogether. It becomes geography. Its hollows and chambers, its archways and cavities, are not rooms but *organs*—resonance chambers tuned to specific frequencies of the planetary exhalation above. Standing among them, you feel your teeth ache not from the raw drone, but from the complexity of it, the harmony. The city is singing. The buildings are the choir.

There are no young and old, only mobile and sessile. The rooted ones are not dead; they are concentrating. From their stations, they modulate the basin’s pressure, tuning the perforations in the distant dome like a vast lung drawing breath across a flute. The migrants carry spores of the jade and yellow bacteria in their dust, seeding new veins, tending the vent chemistry, maintaining the sparse garden that feeds the rooted. When two migrants meet at a crossroads of bacterial shadow, they do not speak. They stand for decades, leaning into one another, and their body-cavities alter the drone between them, trading information in standing waves. Sometimes they exchange mass, fusing and fissioning, and the floor where they meet retains a new, harder scar of compacted silica—a fresh foundation pad, a future wall.

They have no eyes for the dim twilight, no mouths for the absent water. They have the vibration in the stone, the chemistry of dryness, and the long, patient project of becoming landscape. They do not fear the cold. They are the basin’s response to itself: life that looked at eternity and chose not speed, but *weight*; not variation, but the drone. Watch a migrant dissolve its leading edge into the bone-meal sand, watch a rooted elder shed a film of dust that will one day be a dune, a pilgrim, a cathedral wall, and you understand that this hollow is not empty. It is crowded with a civilization that has traded flesh for resonance, and motion for the architecture of waiting.
