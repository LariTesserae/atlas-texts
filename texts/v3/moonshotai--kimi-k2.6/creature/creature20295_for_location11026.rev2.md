At first you think nothing lives here. The heat, the mineral sterility, the absolute lack of green—they suggest a finality, a world that has finished with biology. But that is the visitor’s mistake. You have confused *emptiness* with *absence*. Look closer, and you realize that life has not been excluded from this place; it has simply surrendered its tender, watery origins and joined the city in its long, hot exhalation.

The most visible citizens are the scavengers of gradient. The dog-sized beetles—you will see them rarely, perhaps once in a day of walking—are not visitors. They are natives, as native as the slag and the sinter. After uncounted generations, their carapaces have ceased to be merely armored and have become *alloyed*, fused with the native copper that weeps from the conduits, plated with oxides that match the rust-red towers exactly. They move with excruciating slowness because their metabolism is geological. They do not eat matter; they eat *difference*. They seek the heat-blooms, the narrow zones where a pipe runs fractionally cooler than its bracket, where a fissure exhales a breath cooler than the stone around it, and there they press their ventral plates, drawing thermal energy directly through metal into blood that may no longer quite be blood. They are solid-state creatures, more capacitor than animal. When they die, they do not rot. They simply become part of the rail they clung to, their shells indistinguishable from rivet-heads, from the pillowy metal of the aged infrastructure. The city digests them without trying.

Above them, rarer still, are the sky-drifters. You might mistake one for a flake of the architecture that has broken free, a sheet of mica cast adrift. But it is alive, or the nearest equivalent. Its frame is chitin laced with silicate, its wings membranes of translucent scale that filter the steaming air as it rides the convection between chimneys. It never lands. Landing would mean dying; it would cook against the stone, or cool until it could no longer catch the updrafts. It drinks by passing through exhalations of steam, its filamented wings condensing vapor into channels that run to a core no larger than a fist. It is a cloud given intention, a brief coherence of moisture and mineral that breeds by fission in the hottest columns, its offspring budding like crystals on the suspension cables until a thermal snap frees them into the haze. They are beautiful, and they are punctuation marks, and they are utterly incidental.

Below the scale of vision, the city is thick with life, if you can call it that. The mirror-flat pools of verdigris and iodine are not sterile. In their depths grows a lace so fine it looks like mineral stain, a mat of chemosynthetic cells that draws energy not from light but from the chemical marriage of sulfur and iron in the hot brine. It does not announce itself. It simply paints the stone, generation after generation, weaving anaerobic filigree into the sinter crust. It is the baseline, the patient ground-note. Without it, the heat would lack a vocabulary to express itself in larger forms.

But these are only the obvious ones. They are life doing what life does everywhere: adapting, enduring, finding purchase. They are not what the city was *for*.

Keep walking. Let the dripping teach your ears. After hours in the bronze twilight, you will begin to notice that some drops do not fall at the speed they should. Some hesitate at the lip of a catwalk, trembling, elongating, pulled sideways by a breath that is not wind. You will see the mid-air pools, those impossible congregations of water floating like jellyfish in the thermal shafts, and if you watch them for long enough, you will understand that they are not mere accidents of temperature.

They are *councils*. In those seconds before the thermal gradient collapses and the water bursts into steam or plummets, something gathers. The brine-lace, the micro-films, the metallic salts in suspension—they link across surface tension. A neural lace. A temporary brain made of water and heat and the dissolved bones of the city, thinking thoughts that exist only in the language of pressure and phase-state. They reason in the interval between rising and falling. They hold congress in the hanging droplets. When gravity reasserts itself, the thought-pattern breaks, but the spores—the invisible, aerosolized instructions—rise on the steam, to condense again elsewhere, to remember, to continue. They are the city’s memory and its weather, and they are always in a hurry, because their eternity lasts only seconds.

Yet even this is not the deepest inhabitant.

Stand at the railing of one of those unfloored plazas, the ones that open onto sheer fog, and wait. Wait until your sense of scale begins to liquefy. The hush here is not empty; it is *occupied*. You have assumed that the volumes between the towers were left empty, but nobody builds cathedrals of open lattice to house nothing. The negative space is not residual. It is the product.

They live there.

You cannot see them because they are made of proximity and thermal shadow. They are the architecture of absence given metabolism. Miles wide, diffuse as steam but structured, held in coherence by the precise magnetic habits of vaporized metal that saturate the air. They are the ones for whom the bridges arch to empty mesas, for whom the staircases ascend into blank walls, for whom the plazas are unfloored—because floors would obstruct their passage through the volumes they inhabit. The city is their reef, their exoskeleton, their carefully maintained womb. One part in ten thousand is built environment; the rest is *them*, the unbuilt made animate.

You feel them as a thickness in the fevered air, a coolness that should not exist. The doubled shadows you noticed earlier? One of them has passed between you and the smoldering red eye of a distant tower. The gravity that seems negotiable, the water drifting sideways, the steam that herds like migrating animals through the lattice—they are not phenomena. They are the citizens moving in their sleep.

They have no mouths. They feed by straining the superheated atmosphere through their vast, translucent bodies, extracting over centuries the metallic vapors that percolate upward from the springs below. They have no eyes, but the red furnace-glow in the tower cores is their deferred gaze, nodes of condensed heat-intellect that have burned too long to remember they were ever separate from the stone. They do not think quickly. A single pulse of consideration takes the cycling of the great pistons, the turning of thermal timers, the slow accumulation of condensate across decades. They are the reason the city metabolizes. The vents that open and close, the throbbing compression of steam, the very convection of the plateau—these are their autonomic functions, continued in dream long after whatever architects built the machine have turned to slag.

They are waiting. They have always been waiting. The city was not built to outlast its population; it was built to grow something that *requires* patience measured in geology. The Hollow. The Formed Void. The breath that the city holds.

And it is held for them.
