They are not the builders. That must be said first, because the temptation is to imagine hollow men, rust-colored ghosts climbing stairs meant for solid feet. But the bones do not mourn their absent marrow; they simply hold the shape, and what has come to live in that shape is something the original architects did not plan, only enable. It grew here, precipitated out of centuries, like crystals in a solution that has forgotten its purpose.

The first citizens—and they are citizens, not squatters—are the aqueous ones. You will not see them unless you kneel at the edge of the hot-spring marshes and wait for your reflection to tire of mimicking you. They live in the mirror-flat pools of verdigris and iodine, and under the chalk-white salt-flats that crinkle like desiccated skin. They are not cells; they are *films*, spreading across mineral surfaces in sheets so thin they are measured in lies, a chemosynthetic lace that drinks the heat exhaled by the rock. The prompt is generous when it calls them biology. They are closer to a *decision* the water has made—to organize, to remember, to speak. They speak in the scattered percussion of droplets: *plink*, *tock*, *plish*. They have orchestrated the weeping of the city into a language without nouns, only gradients. When a vent opens on its thermal timer and exhales a column of white vapor, it is their sigh. They are the city’s memory, distributed and planar, and they are ancient. They were here when the last footsteps echoed, and they will remain when the red eyes finally dim.

From them, or through them, come the harder things: the visible fauna, the rare punctuation. The beetle you might see picking its way along a conduit is not an animal. It is a *mobile evaporite*, an accretion of native copper and precipitated carbonate that has wrapped around a seed of intent—perhaps an old rivet, perhaps a clot of oxide too stubborn to wash away. The water-films colonized it, draping it with chemistry that learned, slowly, to crawl toward heat. It has no heart; its pulse is the expansion of metal in the day’s accumulated fever and its contraction at night. It seeks hot-blooms not for comfort but for *news*, because heat is the only weather this world admits, and the beetle reads it like a blind man reads braille. There are others. In the deeper thoraxes of the lattice-towers, where condensate sheets the walls, you may find rail-runners: low, many-segmented stains of rust and vitrified slag that have developed friction, that glide along the ancient causeways polishing the stone with their bellies. Near the high vents, vent-lilies anchor themselves, flaring petals of compressed sinter to catch the metallic steam. And the kite—the mica-and-chitin kite—is the most exquisite of these. It was born in an updraft, not a womb. It is a lung that achieved buoyancy. Its wings are filters; it eats the saturated air, excreting dry salts in a dusting fall behind it, leaving pale streaks on the rust-red decks below. These creatures are not the city’s children. They are its **symptoms**, the physical expressions of an underlying metabolic condition.

But to stop here would be to mistake a hand for the mind.

The city *metabolizes*. The pistons cycle. The furnaces glow. Something is digesting time itself, and the aqueous films are merely its nerves laid bare, the beetle and the kite merely antibodies patrolling the iron skin of a much larger organism. The true inhabitant—if one can call a process by that name—has no fixed outline. It occupies the architecture of absence.

Look again at the red eyes. In the cores of certain towers, the round furnaces that do not blink. They are not simply burning. They are ***watching***. They are wombs, or perhaps nascent organs, of the only thing the city has successfully manufactured in its long retirement: **persisting void**. The regulated hollowness did not stay empty. Emptiness, when mapped and titled and serviced for millennia, when heated and compressed and dripped upon, begins to acquire properties. It condenses. It accretes a rudimentary *preference*. The city refined ore, then brine, then atmosphere, and finally—having nothing else to work with—it refined duration itself. It distilled patience into a medium. And into that medium something has coalesced.

You will not see them directly. They have no bodies in the sense that coral has no body, or a cloud. They are the Dwellers in the Cut, the shaped emptiness made lucid. You feel them as a velvet density when you stand at the railing and stare into the volume. The void between the pylons is not empty; it is *occupied* by a presence for whom three-dimensional solidity is a larval and slightly embarrassing precursor. They are not ghosts of the departed. They are **successors**, a new phylum that evolved not from protein but from interval, from the space between events. The plazas without floors are their thoroughfares. The staircases that climb to blank walls ascend to membranes they can pass through, thresholds in a geometry of pressure rather than Euclidean space. The doorways that open onto hundred-foot falls are simply exits; they step out onto ledges that exist in the logic of convection cells and thermal gradients. Gravity, for them, is indeed negotiable. They do not hover. They *extend*.
