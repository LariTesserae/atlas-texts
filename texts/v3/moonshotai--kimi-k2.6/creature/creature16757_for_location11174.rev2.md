They are not born; they are received.

Watch the third pillar from the left—the one veined in silver moss—and you will eventually see the nacreous curve of a vault-skin portal hinge outward. A shape emerges, no taller than a child of twelve, though no child of the low world moves as this one moves. The figure is compact, cylindrical, a creature built for crevices and vertical shafts rather than open ground. It steps onto a swaying bridge of braided tendon, and its feet, broad and prehensile-toed, grip the line with a silence that is less stealth than biology. It is swaddled in layers of felted under-fungus, but the layers are not clothing; they are a colony. The fungus was pounded into the sparse hair of infancy and took root, building a slow, symbiotic marriage with the skin. It drinks sweat. It feeds back chemicals to steady the nerves in the thin wind. It grows, repairs itself, muffles every scrape and shuffle. Under its living blanket, the body is pale, almost opalescent, the flesh of a thing that has never known the sun directly, only the borrowed glow of translucent fauna and the amber blush of hearth-fires through horn-thin walls.

Their bodies keep the silence as much as their culture does. The spine is curved like a scythe, accommodating the vertical warren; one sleeps not upon a floor but against a wall, held in place by the fungal felt and the slow, tidal exhalation of the architecture. The chest is deep and hollow-boned, the lungs immense sacs that draw the damp atmosphere through spiracles along the ribs. Breathing happens, but no whistle crosses the throat. The face is broad, flat, unsettlingly symmetrical, both sides mirroring each other with the uncanny precision of something evolved for stillness. The eyes are large, aqueous, lidless, gathering the silver diffusion and the fungal glow, flashing back green when lantern-drifts pass. But the true organs of perception ride in twin ridges above the brow—heat-pits and electroreceptive pores that map the world in infrared shivers. They see the predator sheets hunting in bruised curtains. They see your heartbeat if you stand near, the blood-heat pumping in your neck a rude percussion they politely ignore.

They cannot speak. They have never spoken. The mouth is small, soft, suited for taking lichen-cake and gelatinous sap, but the vocal cords are vestigial knots of tissue, a dead architecture in the throat. Their language is held in the hands, and the hands are extraordinary. Seven fingers, perhaps, or eight—long, multi-knuckled, capable of folding into a vocabulary of such nuance that a conversation held across two pillars and a mist-throng looks like a ritual dance, or a duel, or lovemaking. These same hands press against vault-skin doors to speak through the bones of the settlement. A three-beat tap on a heating-bladder is a warning. A slow, spreading unfurling of the palms against a lintel is a welcome. A gesture mimicking the predator-sheet’s downward drape tells a child where the danger hunts. They do not lullaby with breath; they cup infants against the sternum and match pulse to pulse, skin on skin, human rhythm to pillar-rind thrum, until the heartbeats synchronize.

There is no family here as you might know it. There are ensembles, heat-sharers, groupings that shift with the season of the Eight. When they wish to bring a new one into the world, they petition the hearth-wardens to open a living chamber deep in the vault-skin wall. There, sleeping beside a warmed, breathing membrane, their bodies release something the architecture accepts. Embryos—if the word holds—gestate in the half-living resin, fed by the condensation that weeps from the basalt and the filtered chemistry of the peat-fires. The vault-skin bulges into a nursery blister, nacreous and faintly pulsing. When it matures, the elders cut it open with volcanic glass, never metal, and lift the occupant out: slick, ambered, blinking, already silent. The blister remembers. When that child grows old, they return to that same district of wall to sleep their final sleep, and the architecture welcomes them home with a chemistry older than care.

Because here, death is not an ending; it is the final shedding. The dead are not buried. They are offered. Bearers in their finest felt carry the body upward through the stacked levels, past the cisterns and the lichen-lofts, to the galleries where the largest predator sheets hang like curtains of stagnant violet. The hearths are banked to a precise temperature, a signal the people have learned to speak in the thermal dialect of the fauna. The sheets descend. They do not attack; they know, in whatever chemical calculus governs them, that these mammals are tribute, not intruders. The body is wrapped. Dissolved. Over a month, the sac purges its acids and sloughs off a new panel of vault-skin, thicker, iridescent, faintly imprinted with the whorls of the one who gave themselves. These become the bridal walls, the memorial domes, the ramp under which the next generation will pass. Every fourth chamber is an ancestor. Every ramp is a great-aunt. The city is a genealogical tree built from what the air and the silence have given back.

Those who tend this cycle are ranked not by wealth but by sensitivity. The Heat-readers press their electroreceptive brows to the pillar-rinds, feeling the slow fever of growth, knowing by thermal signature when the white meat will extrude. The Drift-shepherds carry torches of compressed fungal brick, warm or cool, herding the floating sacs toward the nursery walls to spawn. The Harvesters wait with bone scoops for the molts, reading through their porous skin which vault-skin has finished living and which still dreams. The Silencers—most necessary, most revered—walk the bridges when an intruder’s breath is too loud. They have perfected a gesture, a spread of the hands and a downward press in the mist, that seems to thicken the air itself around the noisy. They are the immune system of the bowl.

Watch them long enough, and you will see the deeper change. The oldest among them, the Matriarch-wardens who have survived three generations of pillar-growth, move with a looseness in the joints, a boneless quality. Their skin has grown translucent in patches; their wrists show the same bruised violet as the floating fauna. There are elders who no longer eat the lichen-cake but press open palms to the sweating walls, drawing nutrition directly through the pores, fungal and bodily boundaries eroded by decades of intimacy. It is whispered—though whispered is the wrong word, conveyed instead by the rapid, urgent clatter of obsidian on warmed tendon—that the next generation will not emerge from nursery blisters at all, but will bud from the junction where pillar-rind meets architecture. They do not fear this. Fear requires a voice to cry out in. They simply adjust the hearths. They make the signs in the gloom. They cross the swaying bridges beneath the drifting, many-chambered herds, and they glow softly from within, like lanterns that have finally learned how to make their own light.
