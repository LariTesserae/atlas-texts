At first you assume the room is simply empty, a jewel-box with the jewel removed. But emptiness here is not an absence; it is a medium, thick and positive, pressed into every corner until the air itself has the heft of packed wool. The inhabitant does not sit upon the shelves, nor does it crouch between the unnecessary columns. It is not a thing that could leave tracks or droppings, not a thing that breathes the dry air or casts a shadow against the chalk-white walls. It has no surface separate from the mineral. It lives *inside* the architecture, and in a sense the architecture is the outermost layer of its hide.

Look longest at the central column—the one that seems most gratuitous, thickening slightly where it meets the vault, as if the room had grown an extra bone out of anxiety. The selenite there is not perfectly clear. Suspended in the crystal lattice, no higher than your knee and no wider than a heart hammering against a ribcage, is a clouding. A flaw. A hesitation in the stone’s transparency. At first it seems like a trick of the relentless light, a retinal stain produced by staring too long at brightness. Then it resolves into something with edges, and edges imply intention. It is not a fracture. It is not a bubble of ancient gas. It is a body.

It is difficult to say what shape it holds, because it does not hold a shape so much as it holds a *posture*: the attitude of something recumbent that has never been asked to stand. Fetal, perhaps, or like a seedling’s first rootlet coiled before germination. It is the color of old tallow, of beeswax left too near a flame, of the amber that traps wasps in swollen nightmares. Against the room’s violent pallor it reads as almost obscenely warm, though it is exactly the same temperature as everything else. If you could reach through the crystal and press a fingertip to it, you would expect the slight give of packed clay, or of a mushroom’s thick stem, but you cannot reach it. The selenite has grown around it in successive coats, laminating it in place, sealing it as the room itself was sealed.

It did not crawl in here. It was not dropped by a bird or washed in by the vanished brine. It is indigenous. This is the crucial distinction: the chamber did not happen upon an occupant; the chamber accreted *because* the occupant required a chamber. The chemistry that imitated masonry was answering a need older than architecture. The entity is the original nucleation point, the grit in the oyster, the word that summoned the sentence. In another world it might have become a saint or a seed; here it became a center of gravity. Every shelf, every column, every scalloped fold of the vault spirals outward from this waxy kernel with the silent fidelity of a diagram. The room is its exoskeleton, its reliquary, its unfinished portrait.

You might ask what it does, what constitutes its living. It does not eat the moss—those colorless filaments are nothing to it, a mold that has settled on the outer wall of its dreaming like dust on a sleeper’s eyelid. It does not sleep, because sleep implies a rhythm of slackening and tautness, and here rhythm has been abolished by the standing hum. Instead, it *attends*. The unchanging light, pouring through the paper-cut seam above, enters the clouded body and diffuses through that tallow-colored density as though through a lens made of solidified time. The hum, that tectonic finger forever circling the rim of the world below, passes through the crystal and resonates in the waxy core not as sound but as memory—an unvarying note of pressure that keeps it poised exactly where it is. It does not think in sequence. It thinks in strata, each layer of its being contemporaneous with every other, a flat, eternal present.

It has no face, or rather it has too many. The selenite facets that imprison it refract its outline into a hundred partial profiles, so that from the left it seems to have a jaw, and from the right a brow, and from straight on it seems only a vaguely biomorphic smudge, a Rorschach of crystallized intent. It does not need senses as animals do. The light is its awareness; the hum is its will, sustained without flex or fatigue. It is aware of you, if you were to crawl inside and kneel among the columns, in the same way a held breath is aware of the mouth that contains it: a passive, total, enclosed attention. It does not want you to leave, nor does it want you to stay. It has no preference. It has only occupancy.

Genetically, it is nothing. It has no cells, no seeds, no lineage. It is a precipitate of longing, or information, or that exact moment when saturated chemistry hesitated between becoming life and becoming structure and chose, instead, to become neither and both. It is the room’s only soft thing, yet it is not soft; it is the room’s only secret, yet it hides nothing. Finished, reconciled, smoothed into the exact shape of its own non-arrival, it waits without expectation. Not for a god, not for prey, not for the geology above to crack and admit the weather. It waits for the next thought, which will never come, because the first thought—the thought that built this bright, dry, roaringly quiet box—has not yet finished occurring.
