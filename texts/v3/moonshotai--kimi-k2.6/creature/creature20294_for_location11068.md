The plateau does not lack inhabitants; it lacks animals. To search here for the scurry of fur or the beat of feather is to misread the grammar of the place, for the life that has emerged in this newborn world has obeyed a different imperative—not locomotion, not predation, but *accommodation to the unceasing note*. What lives here is tuned to the hum as a bow is tuned to its string, and every body you find will be, in some fashion, a resonator.

Begin with the most ephemeral, the ones that might be mistaken for a trick of the light if this were a place that permitted tricks. In the shallow fracture-pans where condensate beads and pools, there exist organisms that never fully own a shape. They are colloidal events: temporary crystallizations of protein and rare mineral salts swept from the raw stone. For the span of an afternoon, when thermal pressure and mineral charge align just so, they achieve a kind of animation. You will not see them; they are transparent against the lacquered water, motile only by means of shifting surface tension and osmotic hunger. They consume nothing living, lacking mouths or even membranes in any durable sense. Instead they *sort*, pushing useful ions toward the center of their aqueous bodies and excreting pure silica in microscopic tesserae. By evening, when the temperature dips and the condensate film shrinks, they dissolve back into the chemistry of the pan, leaving behind only a faint scuff of opalescence on the stone. They are the plateau’s first draft at metabolism, written in water and discarded.

Descend, then, into the vegetal felt itself. Push your hand into the saturated peat, past the surface tension, into the cool anaerobic crush of generations. Here lives something older, or at least more permanent—though “lives” may be too discrete a word. They are the **felt-laced**: pale, elongate, more resembling nerve fibers than worms. They have no segments, no eyespots, no front or back in the usual sense. What they possess is *attunement*. They thread through the compressed peat in bundles, orienting not toward light or food but along the lines of infrasonic pressure that pulse up from the deep machinery of the megaplants and spread laterally through the wet substrate. It is possible they are not separate organisms at all, but root-tissue escaped from the megaplants and gone feral, maintaining sympathetic connection to the parent trunks across distances of many leagues. When the turbines in a distant tower shift their pitch, the felt-laced shiver in galvanic response, tightening their grip on the peat, altering the capillary flow of fluids by mere microns. They are the nervous system of the ground, and the substrate breathes in rhythm with their slow, peristaltic clamp.

But the plateau is not without its travelers. Once every few decades, a megaplant rehearses a strange ontological question—*what if I were mobile?*—and produces the **prognates**. They issue not from seed or spore but from the industrial architecture of the trunks themselves. A valve-house swells, bulges, and sloughs off a mass of sap-darkened tissue, something between a fruit and a refugee. It is the size of a human torso or larger, limbless initially, though as it pulls itself across the vegetal felt it extrudes pseudopodia of dense cellulose and lignin, hardening as they touch the air into stilts, skids, crawling members. It has no eyes; the fierce light here would offer it nothing but injury. It has no ears; the sound would shatter anything so delicate as an auditory membrane. Instead its entire surface is a pressure map, sensitive to the laminar sheets of wind and the deep geological drone. It navigates by standing in the hum and feeling for the interference patterns cast by other megaplants on the horizon. It moves toward them, or rather, it moves toward the silences within the noise—the acoustic shadows of distant trunks.

The prognate’s journey is the only saga this place allows, and it is brief. It crosses the open distances for years, sometimes decades, drinking the condensate, absorbing minerals through its ventral skin. It carries in its core a concentrate of the refined elements its parent plant has manufactured: not seed, but *catalyst*, a payload of pre-processed geology meant to accelerate the next generation’s claim on uncolonized stone. When at last it reaches a fresh ridge of basalt or anorthosite—still warm from its own recent birth, still off-gassing from the mantle—it collapses. The cellulose stilts buckle. The tissue liquefies, not in death but in surrender, spreading across the rock in a film that resolidifies into the first layer of binding peat, the first stitch between stone and industry. From its dissolution, a new megaplant will eventually rise, inheriting the hum, amplifying it. The prognate is the only true migrant here, and its pilgrimage is indistinguishable from its dying.

Closer to the trunks, clinging to the ceramic valves and blackened catchment houses, you will find the **seal-forms**. They are easily mistaken for gaskets, for crusts of mineral accretion, because that is precisely how they disguise themselves. They are slow-growing, rubbery symbiotes—perhaps animal, perhaps a modification of bark—whose bodies fill the microscopic gaps between botanical tissue and industrial apparatus. They feed on vibration itself, converting the ceaseless thrum of the turbines into sluggish chemical gradients. When a joint widens with the patient expansion of the trunk, a seal-form grows to bridge it. When a pipe clogs with precipitate, a seal-form secretes a solvent enzyme. They are the immune system and the maintenance crew, and they have no more consciousness than a callus does, yet they have shaped the technology as surely as any engineer. Without them, the marriage of plant and machine would divorce in a season; with them, the refinery grows indistinguishable from the organism.

And then there are the deepest dwellers, the ones who have not yet emerged. In the fissures of the raw igneous peaks, where the rock still remembers the furnace and breathes out mineral warmth into the cool condensate, white threads are threading. They are the *first* colonizers, the inaugurator organisms, neither fungus nor root but something provisional and white as bone marrow. They do not rot the stone; they *negotiate* with it, trading acids for phosphates, etching the first microscopic topography into faces that have never known weather. In ten million years they will have made soil. Here, now, they are only a whispered promise, the embryo of erosion dreaming in the dark.

When you stand back and let your eyes close against the glare, you understand that these forms are not a collection but an *extension*. The condensate swimmers, the felt-laced, the crawling prognates, the seal-forms, the white thread in the stone—they are not a kingdom of creatures sharing a biome. They are the thoughts of a single process that has not yet learned to think of itself as separate from the ground. The plateau is one body, tuning itself, and what lives here lives as cells live in a bloodstream: briefly, specifically, and always in service to the greater hum. The sound does not host them. The sound *is* them, given temporary shape, learning what it means to be loud, bright, wet, and whole.
