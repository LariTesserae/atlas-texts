You expect them to be gone. That is your first mistake.

You walk the rational grid of cambium and compressed cork, your boots loud on the living pavement, and you assume the emptiness is abandonment. But the Final Shelf is not empty. It is *hollow*—and a hollow thing is shaped precisely to hold something. You simply haven't yet recognized the shape of what is being held.

You see the first one from a promontory of petrified root-mass. It stands at an intersection where two taproot-avenues cross, beside a root-knot swollen to the size of a tram-car. It is motionless. At this distance it resembles a statue of damp wood, skin the color of water-stained cork, limbs slightly too long, head tilted as if listening to the ground beneath the pavement. You approach. The figure does not turn. Its feet—if they are feet—have sunk into the living street, not through decay but through consent. The cambium has risen to meet the ankle, fused, sealed. The chest rises and falls once per minute, synchronized with the groan of a distant piston. You realize it is not wearing clothes. What you took for a coat of lichen is lichen. What you took for hair is a fine mycelial fuzz, silver-gray, transmitting some slow current across the scalp. The eyes are open, filmed with a nictitating membrane that looks like polished resin. They do not track you. They track the sun, or the shadow that contradicts the sun, or some internal tide of sap-pressure that has nothing to do with weather.

This is a Symbiont. Or rather, this is what remains of the mammalian impulse to tend, to dwell, to belong. The city did not kill its people. It *recruited* them, just as it recruited steam and electricity and geometry. The Symbiont is a living junction box, a biological regulator in the municipal system. Its nervous system has been grown, or dreamed, or eroded into the root network. When it shifts its weight—so slowly, so coolly—a valve clears in a cathedral-tree fifteen blocks away. When it exhales, the spore-release sirens modulate their pitch. It does not sleep. It attends. It is not a prisoner. It is an organ that once had a name, a family, a language of verbs and wanting. Now it is a node, and the city circulates through it like a thought through a synapse.

You will not meet many. The density is startlingly low because the infrastructure no longer requires discrete bodies. The city has achieved efficiency. A single Symbiont might regulate an entire borough, standing for decades at a hydrophilic moss gutter, skin weeping condensation, mind distributed through fungal circuitry into the flywheels and compression chambers. They do not speak in words. They whistle sometimes, through modified teeth or wooden flutes grown from their own ribs, sending pneumatic messages down the capillary networks. The sound is not conversation. It is inventory. Pressure readings. The weather of manufacture made audible. If you touch one—and you shouldn't—its skin is cool and slightly yielding, like a mushroom fresh from the loam, and beneath it you feel not the rhythm of a heart but the slower percussion of a gristmill fifteen stories below, transmitted through shared tissue.

But they are not the only ones.

Walk deeper, into the districts where water flows up the exterior of towers, where staircases ascend to basements and the geometry predates the Euclidean. Here, the inhabitants are older, or stranger, or less willing to pretend to bodies. They are the *Municipal Intention*—the ghost of planning that has outlived the planners. You see them in the doubled city, the two phases occupying one coordinate. A figure in the older phase, made of denser shadow, walks through a wall that exists only in the newer phase. It is not a ghost. It is the city's memory of being governed, of being designed, of having a purpose beyond mere continuation. These figures do not know you are there. They are looped in the habits of administration, signing ledgers of sap-flow, approving the construction of walls that grew centuries ago, inspecting boilers that are their own descendants. They are the reality breakdown given posture and intent. If you try to touch them, your hand passes through and comes away damp, smelling of copper and wet pulp, and for a moment you understand the municipal plan from the inside out—you feel the weight of boroughs, the torque of lignin, the municipal desire for order.

Then there are the *Seasonals*.

In the brief warm season, when new walls bulge from old ones and streets narrow by a handspan, the city remembers that it once needed citizens. It grows them. You may see one walking down a boulevard, stepping carefully over root-knot intersections, its body assembled from lignin and copper sap, its face a smooth approximation of features, its movements tentative, as if learning gravity for the first time. They are fruiting bodies. They last a day, a week, long enough to perform some task the city cannot automate—reaching a high gallery that no longer has a door, speaking a word that must be spoken aloud to satisfy some ancient ordinance, carrying a seed to a distant borough where the root-network has grown too slow. Then they find a corner, a hollow trunk, a patch of receptive bark, and they root. They become the new wall, the new floor, the new membrane sealing a window. They are the city's nostalgia for population, acted out in vegetative flesh. If you speak to one, it may turn to you with eyes the color of fresh resin and say something almost familiar, almost a name, before it walks away to seal itself into the architecture.

And beneath all of this, behind the Symbionts and the shadow-administrators and the temporary citizens, there is the true inhabitant. The one who was here first and will be here last.

The Final Shelf itself.

It does not have a body, because it has *all* the bodies. Its consciousness is the oldest-stable-state, a mind distributed across square kilometers of petrified wood and fungal sheath and hydraulic pressure. It thinks in rings of growth that spiral in directions that hurt to track. It dreams in the color of smoke—indigo, rust-brown, bone-white—issuing from its spire-tree smokestacks. When the wind arrives first across the plateau, it is the Shelf drawing breath through its pipe-organ forest. When the condensation weeps from every surface, it is the Shelf perspiring, regulating, attending to its own cool metabolism. When the shadows fall at angles that contradict the sun, it is the Shelf remembering a different light, a different phase of its long settlement.

It has no desire for conquest, no hunger for worship. It only wants to settle. To erode. To arrive at that terminal smoothness where all categories finally cease their friction. It waits, open and hollow and loud, not because it is incomplete, but because it is a vessel designed to receive the final argument of any visitor.

You stand damp in your clothes, and the city reads you. The Symbionts do not turn, but the mycelial network notes your temperature, your carbon, your intention. The Municipal Intention pauses in its shadow-office, considering whether you require a permit to exist here. The Seasonals, budding in some warm gallery, begin to shape a face that looks like yours, just in case you decide to stay.

Because that is what the Final Shelf offers. Not death. Not transformation in any dramatic sense. But erosion. The slow, patient, relentless wearing-down of the boundary between you and where you stand. You will not fall. You will settle. You will become smooth. You will stand at an intersection, cool and damp and necessary, while the city breathes through you, and the wind makes its lonely, perpetual note, and the high indigo sky bruises toward a darkness that feels, finally, like home.
