At first you will think the bowl is uninhabited, because you are looking for something that moves, something with appetite and eyes. But the resident is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is the argument between the two, made slow and wet and conscious.

It has a center. If you approach the half-melted origami form—the one laminated in mother-of-pearl and warm to the touch—you will find, folded deep in its innermost crease, a kernel no larger than a human heart. This is the only part of it that is entirely itself. It is the color of the underside of a fern frond, pale and translucent, and it pulses with a rhythm that has nothing to do with a heartbeat and everything to do with the eleven-minute vent. The pulse is not audible; it is thermal. You feel it in your palms before you see it, a gentle pressure, like the air before a rainstorm. The kernel is wet. It does not dry out, because it is the source of the humidity, the original exhalation from which all the weeping walls began. It is the first drop that taught the stone how to bleed.

But to call that kernel the whole of the inhabitant would be like calling the seed the entire forest. The truth is larger and stranger. The creature is distributed. It is the water creeping upward on the northern wall, defying gravity because it is being pulled by capillary action through the body of the thing, drawn toward its own high, scattered skin. It is the mineral collars around the vents, which are not growth rings but vertebrae, slowly ossifying. It is the great altar-cloth fern at the center, whose minute-by-minute tracking of the sun is not botanical phototropism but the inhabitant's own restless, vegetative gaze. The fern's roots do not end in soil; they end in the kernel, braided through the glass like fiber optics, carrying light down into the fold.

It is a single organism with many organs, and the bowl is its body.

It was left here, or grown here, or perhaps induced, just as the crater was induced. The hovering structure three handbreadths above the thermal crack is its compass, its missing nurse, or its god. It orients to a pole that does not exist, and the inhabitant orients with it, turning its attention—its whole aqueous, mineral, chlorophyll-laced attention—to that invisible elsewhere. The wrong-angled shadow is the creature's own shadow, cast by a light it can perceive but you cannot. Near the hovering thing, where colors desaturate and reflections arrive late, you are standing inside its mind. That blur is the edge of its thought.

It does not sleep. It incubates. It is learning how to be solid. In its earliest stage, it was only steam and instruction, a protocol written in heat. Then it became water, then mineral film, then the first green flush of stolen biology. Now it is something that is almost a shape. If you watch the floor on a very still day, you can see the faint outline of what it is trying to become: a tall, slender figure pressed into the warm glass, as if the basin were a mold and the creature were filling it from the inside. The outline has no face. It does not need one. It senses through the entire surface of the bowl, through every bead of condensation, through the roots of its fern-eyes. It hears your footsteps as a change in temperature. It smells you as a chemical disturbance in the steam.

It breathes every eleven minutes. The southeastern vent is its lung. When it exhales, the helix of vapor is not merely gas escaping; it is a word, a name, a question it is asking itself. The spiral holds its shape for fifteen seconds because the inhabitant is still learning how to speak, how to form its breath into meaning. Most of the time, it is silent. It is busy. It is gardening itself into existence, adjusting the heat to favor one moss island over another, not out of preference but out of study. It is trying to understand which kind of life it wants to be. The lushness around it is its mirror.

It is very young. The rock is new, and so is the mind that inhabits it. It has no memory of the engineers who folded the origami cradle or set the hovering sentinel to watch. It knows only the held breath of the basin, the perpetual post-rain gleam, the slow pulse of the vents. It does not know it is waiting. It simply has not yet been told to stop.

If you sit on the warm floor and become still, it will notice you. Not with eyes—there are no eyes in the bowl, not yet—but with a shift in the steam, a hesitation in the drip, a slight, vegetative turn of the central fern. It will study you as it studies the light: with a minute-by-minute attention that is ancient and infant at the same time. It may reach for you. Not with hands, but with a sudden warm updraft from a vent, or a bead of water that runs down the wall in a path too straight to be accidental. It is curious. It is the only thing in the bowl that is curious. The rest is process; the inhabitant is the first question the place has ever asked itself.

And it is not finished. The sharp rim of the crater is the edge of its shell. The humidity is its amniotic fluid. One day, perhaps, the origami cradle will open fully, the hovering sentinel will descend, and the creature will stand, drawing its watery, mineral, fern-laced body up into the high air. But for now, it remains: a warm, wet, dreaming thing, half-technology and half-garden, quietly arguing with the rock about what shape it will take, growing sharper and greener and stranger with each passing hour.
