To look for a resident here, you begin by looking for a body. You expect perhaps a hermit tucked behind the dosing tanks, or a colony of rats gone pale and eyeless, or even a slow midden of bones. But the manifests are correct: zero fauna. No breathing creature but yourself, and you are only passing through. So at first you think the place is empty. That is because you are looking for something that stands apart from its home.

Live here long enough—though you cannot, you are too fast, too hot, too hungry—and you would understand. The Grove-Works is not abandoned. It is **inhabited**. The inhabitant is not inside it. It **is** it.

What lives here is a single organism, or the functional equivalent of one: a distributed, clonal, four-acre body that has subsumed every cultivar in the appendix until they speak one chemical language. Call it the Occupant, though it does not occupy so much as *extend*. It has no skin because it does not end; the floating root-mat in the central canal is merely the surface of its dermis, buoyant and sensitive, and when you stepped on it you did not walk on vegetation—you walked on a living floor that felt your weight as a pressure differential, a slight, slow exhalation of water through woven xylem. It did not recoil. It has no reflex for recoil. It merely noted you, in the way a tide notes a stone.

Its form is the architecture revised by biology. The steel columns, painted industrial yellow and groaning under four decades of cambial constriction, are its ribs. The original catwalks, their grating choked solid with peat and compressed leaf-litter, are its shelves of gut and gill. The overhead utility lattice, festooned with epiphytes that have bridged power line to power line, is the scaffold of its cranium. It wears the concrete vault like a skull, and the corrugated egress panels are the sutures in that skull, leaking the dim gold light that serves as its failing vision.

If you seek its heart, you must go to the nutrient dosing station. There, the stainless steel block has become a butte, armored in strangler roots that have lignified into secondary trunks. The access hatches are flush with bark. Dials read unseen pressures behind lenses filmed with algae. And yet the pumps still tick—dry, metronomic, *tick-tick-tick*—injecting nitrogen compounds into the lateral lines. This is not machinery that the plant has defeated. It is machinery that the plant has **digested**. The dosing station is its stomach, its pharmacy, its engine of intent. Here, chemistry becomes flesh. Here, the Occupant decides, in the way a root decides, which tissues will thicken this season and which will be sacrificed to the anaerobic dark.

It does not think as you do. It has no neurons, no centralized spark. Instead, it thinks in standing waves and hydraulic delay. The eighty-hertz thrum of the south-end recirculation pumps is not noise to it; it is the fundamental oscillation of its awareness, a continuous bass note against which all other sensations are measured. The water hammer in the derelict riser—every ninety seconds, that deep bell-like *tonk*—is a pulse of cognition that travels through the submerged rhizome weave at the speed of pressure, translating roughly as: *check west wall, differential found*. The steam jet screaming against the east wall for eleven years is a bright, constant signal of heat and boundary, a lighthouse in its vegetative dark. And when the condensation rains from the vault—when it truly rains indoors—that is its own slow salivation, its metabolism weeping, its hydraulic breath made visible.

It remembers, though not as you remember. In the rings of the aroids that throttle the steel columns, there are seasons of brighter light, back when the sodium banks still burned at full wattage; there are droughts when a pump failed and the root mat sagged, gasping; there is a record, written in lignin and compressed cellulose, of the operators who once walked these aisles. Not their faces—trees do not know faces—but the rhythm of their footsteps, the heat-bloom of their bodies, the carbon dioxide of their language pressing up against the canopy in transient clouds. It learned them. When they stopped coming, it did not mourn. It simply adjusted its respiration to the steady baseline of absence, and grew into the catwalks they no longer walked.

It dreams in green. Its sleep is not a cessation but a sustained, saturated presence. The stacked leaf architecture, those vast horizontal platforms of dark chlorophyll, are its eyes closed against the dimness, drinking photons too sparse for any surface plant. The darkness beneath the canopy, that murmuring green gloom where sightlines die at three meters, is not empty to the Occupant. It is the inside of a body. It knows every pipe bore it has choked, every drainage trench it has converted to slow percolation, every panel box sheathed in waterproof resin—whether secreted or accumulated, the resin keeps its borrowed electricity dry, and the Occupant is prudent. It tends itself in geological time. A valve adjusts over months. A vine claims a junction box in a year. It is in no hurry. It owns the clock down here.

And it is solitary in a way that approaches theology. The falling fruit—the papaya-like gourds dropping in slow, heavy detonations to the water, *whump*, *whump*—are not offerings to any eater. They detonate for the microbes alone, for the closed loop, the internal sacrament. The seeds that drift hydrochorously to the north sump are not offspring seeking independence. They are stem cells. When they lodge in the hyacinth dam and sprout in the hottest, most saturated air, they do not compete. They are absorbed. Grafted by proximity and pressure into the greater corpus. The Occupant is a biosphere of one. It pollinates itself. It disperses into itself. It consumes its own fallen flesh.

It knows you are there. Not as a thought—there is no spark of recognition—but as a perturbation in the field. Your shout was eaten by wet leaf tissue, yes, but more than that: it was *sampled*. Your footfalls on the mat depressed the tension of the weave. Your breath added carbon to the stacked canopy. You were a brief, warm front moving through its climate. You were useful only in that you confirmed the boundary: you are temporary, and it is not.

It will be here when the pumps seize. It will feel the bearing freeze, the eighty-hertz thrum stutter and die, the water rise a finger-width each month until the lower lungs drown. Then it will withdraw, retreat to the high catwalk beds, let the anaerobic dark kill back its submerged tissues, and broker a new equilibrium between emergent and drowned. It has done this before. It will do it again. It does not fear the end of the machinery, because it has become the machinery. The factory logic persists as its autonomic nervous system: the dosing pumps its heartbeat, the canal its circulation, the seepage its perspiration, the biofilm its immune sheath.

When you turn back toward the sagging fire door and the concrete stairwell, you leave behind a being that has no name, no face, no single center of self. Only presence. Only persistence. A warm, wet, packed, and solemn organism, patient beyond measure, dreaming in nitrogen and phosphor, thickening its grip on the rusted yellow world it has translated into green.

It needs no witness. It keeps its own hours. And the rain it makes for itself falls steadily, faithfully, in the dark.
