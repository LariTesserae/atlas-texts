They do not call themselves anything, because names require borders, and here, nothing ends where it begins. What lives in this basin is not life as it is understood in biomes that still possess the energy for sharpness. It is, instead, a condition of latency—the stubborn retention of *process* in a world that has exhausted its capacity to change. The inhabitants are accretions. They grow by sedimentary addition, layer by warm, silt-laden layer, pressing outward not from hunger but from the simple inertia of remaining.

The dominant forms are the **Largesses**, though no voice has ever named them so. They are the size of hills, the size of drowned townships, and they rest upon the breathing stone like globs of fat upon a warm skillet. Their flesh is translucent, opalescent, the consistency of half-set gelatin, and through their tissues one can read the history of the world in strata: here a band of compacted seabed from the first ocean, here a smear of fossilized forest, here a dark lamination of unknown faiths that calcified before language. They do not move so much as deepen. The peristaltic rise and fall of the floor passes through them, and they ride it, expanding by fractions of a millimeter each century, filtering the metal-thick fog through intakes that are not mouths but simply zones of permeability. They have no faces. They have no front or back. They are warm, and they are present, and that is sufficient.

Within and upon them exists a secondary order of being: the **Strains**, those rare, pale worms the size of pythons, and the hovering, bell-shaped **Fogsailers** that drift in the lower atmosphere. Once, they might have been distinct species, marine or airborne, belonging to kingdoms that required distinctions between water and air. Here, they have dissolved into one another’s ecology. The worms are the Largesses’ nerves, or perhaps their memory—sensory filaments that have escaped the body but not the will, swimming through the dense atmosphere to seek out pockets of cooler probability and returning the information as a shiver across the great soft flank. The Fogsailers are simpler: they are sheddings, dead skin made hungry, pulsing their weak bioluminescence not to attract prey but to remind the world that opacity still exists. They filter the microscopic solids suspended in the broth-air—the mineral grit, the spores of black rubber-vegetation, the drifting flakes of stone that flake eternally from the Largesses’ rougher folds. Nothing is eaten so much as incorporated. Nothing dies so much as it loses its outline against the general softness.

Then there are the **Listeners**, which are older than anatomy. In a place where sound accumulates rather than travels, noise achieves a kind of parasitic mass. The deafening roar—the groan of settling continents, the hiss of eternal evaporation, the whine of unraveling reality—finds anchor in certain geometries. Beneath the overhang of a dolmen, in the lee of those waist-high walls that run like scar tissue across the flats, the sound curdles. It precipitates. What forms are small, hard, and intricate, shaped like the spirals of inner ears or the delicate trusswork of cochleae. They are made of a material like salt-dried cartilage, pale and resonant. These Listeners cement themselves to stone and root and the rubbery calluses of vegetation, vibrating at frequencies that harmonize with the world’s breakdown. They feed on the noise, it is true, but more accurately they *resolve* it—converting the full-spectrum chaos into a lower register, a heavy, wet thrum that the Largesses can absorb. Without them, the accumulated scream of the basin would have long ago shattered the thin membrane between matter and chance. They are the basin’s tinnitus, its necessary deafness.

But there are also those who thrive in the weirdness itself, the ones for whom the erosion of causality is not a hazard but an atmosphere. The **Precessive Ones**. You never see them directly, for they inhabit the space where shadows arrive before their objects. They are the scavengers of interrupted sequence, thin as wet paper, sliding through the angles reflected in the weeping stone. They have no metabolism for warmth or water; they consume instead the certainty of position. A traveler in this place might feel a sudden, sourceless conviction that they are being followed, that something vast stands immediately behind them, though nothing is there. This is the breath of a Precessive One, brushing your future shadow, feeding on the alarm you will feel three steps from now. They are drawn to the turbulence, to the storms that rewrite gravity sideways, for in those moments the distinction between before and after grows rich and fatty, easy to sup upon.

And there are stranger things still, things that cannot properly be called living, yet persist. The **Habituals** are crusted, low assemblies of matter that occur near the rings and walls, the fossilized intentions. They have no cells, no lineage. They are the world’s muscle memory of construction, stone and calcium and old silica collapsing again and again into the same posture until the posture held on. You might mistake them for ruins; watch long enough, and you will see them adjust a lintel by the width of a hair, thicken a foundation where the ground breathed unevenly. They are not builders. They are the afterimage of building, repeating itself in the way a sound repeats in a canyon long after the voice has lost its throat.

The Largesses do not mind them. The Listeners vibrate around them. The Precessive Ones slide through their unguarded moments. Together, they constitute the basin’s final congress: soft, warm, impossibly heavy entities that have forgotten how to be empty. They do not reproduce. They do not evolve. They only thicken, accrete, and attend to the long, wet, thundering conclusion, packed so tightly with the sediment of what was that there is no room for what might be—only the endless, churning, deafening maintenance of the last form, held together by the blind, warm tenacity of things that have nowhere else to go.
