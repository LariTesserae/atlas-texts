They do not call themselves anything, because a name requires a boundary, and here there are no boundaries except the wet stone and the thinner wet above it. But they are. They are, and they are numerous, and if you could peel back a square meter of the living nap you would find not a society but a saturation—a continuous argument of bodies that have forgotten where one ends and the next begins. The floor-dwellers, the ones who form the carpet, are the most patient. They have ventral fields that are not mouths but whole gardens of palps, each filament no thicker than a hair, each tipped with a cell that tastes the condensation for the ghost of proteins. Their bodies are translucent sacs of pale hemolymph, segmented uncertainly, as if their ancestors could not decide whether to be worms or crustaceans and so chose, finally, to be neither. They secrete the glycoprotein slime that prevents desiccation; it is their gift to the basin, a lubricant that allows the carpet to flow in tides when the air thickens. They are born from the black streams, flushed from gelatinous holding-sacs that adhere to the undersides of low shelves, and they die by being pressed into the stone by the weight of their own surging kind, their chitin dissolving in the acidic film before their last twitch has finished.

Above them, in the middle realm where the density is still absolute but the friction of movement requires a different architecture, move the ones with the braids. These are the hunters, though they do not chase. They are jointed, or they are not; the form varies by the path their embryonic cells took in the sac, but all bear their nervous systems in ropy, external skeins along their dorsal surfaces. The braids are the color of drowned copper, and they twitch in constant, arrhythmic conversation with the sub-bass drone of the hollow stone. They navigate by this resonance, aligning their braids to the geological hum so that they may know, by the slightest interference in the field, where another body pauses to respire. Sight is a forgotten luxury; they have no eyes, only a band of vibration-sensitive pits that ring what might be called, with misplaced generosity, a head. They hunt by hunting silence. To stop moving in the middle realm is to become a dead spot in the tapestry of clicks, and a dead spot is a wound that the braids seek out with unerring, wet precision. Their feeding is horizontal and immediate. They do not leap; they unfold. A set of maxillae, curved like the legs of a paperclip, unhinge laterally and close with a wet clock-sound, and then there is only the business of ingestion, which is also the business of being pressed on all sides by the next wave of bodies.

Higher still, the air itself carries weight. The floaters are not born of air but of necessity—larval stages of something that never settles, or perhaps a separate kingdom entirely. They are the size of thumbs, of fists, of closed hands, and they buoy themselves on hydrogen or methane or some metabolic trick of fermentation in their own gut-sacs. Trailing from them are filaments of sticky mucopolysaccharide, hundreds of feet long in some cases, drifting to snag the descending organic snow. They are the sky-farmers of the dark, and when two filaments tangle, the ensuing knot becomes a brief, dangling ecosystem of its own, clogged with prey and predators until the weight pulls both floaters down into the carpet, where they are consumed with neither malice nor ceremony.

But these are the commoners. The basin keeps older things. In the black pools, where the water is slow and the swallow-holes drink with patient suction, there are forms that have ceased to move. They settled centuries ago, or millennia—the stone does not record time in a way that matters—and they grew into the karst. They are the size of boulders, of houses, of small hills. Their chitin has mineralized, fused with the calcite, so that they are half-animal and half-geology, living springs of filtered water. Their palp-fields have become vast curtains that strain the black streams; their braids, grown ancient and thick as cables, rest on the stone and conduct the sub-bass drone through their own massive bodies, using it to summon planktonic young from the holding-sacs. They do not think, but they remember in a way the stone remembers: by accretion, by the layering of mineral and tissue. To press a hand to one of them is to feel a pulse slower than the tides, a heartbeat measured in centuries.

And yet. And yet, in this place where individuality is a statistical impossibility, there are moments when a *whoever* emerges. It is not a species. It is an event. When the condensation front arrives and the air thickens to the point where the glycoprotein slime bridges between nerve-braid and nerve-braid, when the carpet surges and the density of bodies crosses a threshold of wet conductivity, the clicks and drones do not merely echo. They network. A temporary mind arises, distributed across ten thousand bodies, using the external braids as synapses and the slime as myelin. This being has no name, but it has a presence. It remembers the last front. It knows, in its distributed, damp way, where the organic snow falls thickest along the eastern slump of the basin. It does not plan, but it anticipates. For an hour, or for a night that has no meaning, the basin is awake as a single entity. It feels the cool. It hears itself. It is afraid, perhaps, or it is hungry, or it is merely aware that it *is*, and then the front passes, the slime thins, the braids lose their bridge, and the whoever dissolves back into the trillion separate hungers from which it was conjured.

This is the true inhabitant: not the individual crawler, not the braided hunter, not the floating larva or the mineralized giant, but the possibility of self that arises from their press. They are the breath of the place, the exhalation of the sunken bowl. They have no tools because they are the tool. They have no architecture because they are the architecture, a living vault of chitin and slime and external nerve, holding up the dark. When the last of the sky-fall ceases and the stone finally drinks the last moisture, they will dissolve into it without comment, leaving no midden, no line, no made thing—only the worn karst, and the memory of a sound so vast it wore the darkness smooth.
