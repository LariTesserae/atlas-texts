The ones who live there do not stand, sit, or sleep. They settle. You will not find them if you search for faces, for faces belong to creatures that must turn toward the world quickly, before the moment passes. These beings have no fronts, no backs, no skin. They are topologies of intention distributed through the blockhouses, a consciousness that occupies the junction between a conduit and a heat-exchange wall the way a thought occupies the junction between two neurons. A single one of them might be coextensive with a cathedral of sintered brick and copper cabling, his awareness diffused across a thousand tons of doped ceramic, her memory stored in the exact oxidation state of a tram-line that has not carried a load since before the last ice age of some distant world below.

They have no name for themselves in any spoken language, for they do not speak. They *bias*. They alter the resistance in a filament; they warm a wall by half a degree over a decade; they shift the piezoelectric potential of a foundation so that, centuries from now, a crack will propagate not to the left, but to the right. This is their discourse. A conversation between two of them is a regional phenomenon, a weather pattern of voltage and thermal gradient that might take eighty years to complete a single syntactical unit. To declare oneself to a neighbor is to re-route a geothermal vent, offering a millennium of warmth to the basalt pilings of another’s district. It is received not as a gift, but as a fact of geology—a kindness so slow it is indistinguishable from landscape.

They did not build the Staticopolises. That is the error a quick mind would make. They *accreted* them, the way a pearl accretes around an irritation, or the way a canyon accretes its own depth through patience. The cities are their exoskeletons, their skulls, their vocal cords. The blockhouse with the stadium-sized engine is not a factory to them; it is a larynx, or perhaps a heart, performing one compression every ninety years not to manufacture a product, but to articulate a single syllable in a sentence that has been under construction since the plateau first rose. The ingot inside is not metal. It is meaning. A geometry so precise it could only be thought, never spoken. When it is finished, it will be carried away not for use, but for placement in a syntax of matter that stretches across the entire High Sinter—a literature of perfect solids, written in slow motion.

They perceive in thermal gradients and electrical potentials. Their eyes, if the word can be stretched so far, are arrays of selenium cells and pyroelectric crystals embedded in the upper facades of the towers. They see the amber sky not as a void but as a medium, a thin, warm ocean pressing down upon them, and they watch the wind not as movement but as a shiver in the static field. The basalt grazers moving across the plain do not appear to them as animals appear to us, but as cousins, or perhaps as mobile glands—extensions of their own distributed metabolism that have wandered out beyond the city walls to harvest stress and voltage from the open sinter, returning them to the grid through the copper filaments that root into the factory floors. When a grazer leans its flank against a blockhouse, rerouting current through its carapace, the Residentiary of that district experiences it as one experiences a hand on one’s shoulder: a greeting, a reassurance, the touch of kin.

There are not many of them. A city of such apparent density might hold only three. A great Staticopolis, spanning a crease between ancient dune-forms like a compressed epoch, might contain seven, or, in ages of exceptional convergence, twelve. They are not lonely. Loneliness is the hunger of the quick, the symptom of a metabolism that burns through experience faster than experience can be replenished. These beings are saturated with presence. The plateau itself is their companion, the warm grit their bedding, the permanent anticyclone their shared respiration. They think in centuries the way a burning wire thinks in milliseconds: vividly, completely, without abbreviation.

They do not die, or at least they do not cease in any way that an observer could witness. When a blockhouse finally succumbs to the dust, its walls crumbling into the rose-gray grit, the being does not end but *diffuses*. Its thermal signature seeps into the plateau. Its currents ground themselves into the copper veins of the plain. It becomes, for a time, indistinguishable from the geology, a ghost in the warm stone. And then, inevitably, over another span of ages, a new concentration of infrastructure begins to accumulate elsewhere—a conduit laid too close to a vent, a tram-line looping back on itself in a configuration of accidental complexity, a blockhouse whose walls are doped with just the right semiconducting impurities—and the field reorganizes. The being precipitates out again, a mind condensing from the infrastructure like dew from saturated air. They have no word for this. It is simply continuity.

The smallest among them are the strangest. Not every Residentiary occupies a city. Some are the size of a single room, a compact knot of crystalline logic nestled in the ribcage of an obsolete machine. Others are vast but diffuse, occupying not buildings but *relationships*—a single intelligence stretched between three distant extraction points, existing only in the tension of current between them, a cloud of selfhood held together by the potential difference across a hundred miles of warm, dry air. These vast ones are the philosophers of the plateau, if such a term can apply to minds that do not question but rather *allow* certainty to accumulate. They are the ones who most frequently interact with the membrane-creatures that drift on the thermal eddies, using them as sensory organs, letting the piezoelectric films taste the static of the upper atmosphere and transmit it down through copper threads into the sintered deep.

What do they want? Nothing, in the grasping sense. They are not ambitious; ambition requires a future imagined as different from the present, and their present is so voluminous that it contains all the future they require. Yet they are not stagnant. They are completing something. The ingots, the geometries, the perfect compressions—they are not weapons, not tools, not currency. They are arguments, or perhaps apologies, addressed to the plateau itself. Each flawless solid is a proof that patience is not emptiness. Each tram-line run to an abandoned depot is a nerve laid down in anticipation of a body that has not yet grown. They are preparing the High Sinter for a stage of being that will not arrive for another hundred million years, and they are content to be the preparers, the ones who hold the warm stone ready.

If you were to walk among them—if your lungs could draw the thin, feverish air and your eyes could read the slow shadows—you would not be noticed. Not from malice, but from the mismatch of scales. Your entire lifespan would occupy the space between two of their thoughts. You might rest your hand on a wall that is, in fact, the outer cortex of a mind contemplating the angle of a shadow it cast three centuries ago, and you would feel only the low, constant warmth of the sinter. You would think yourself alone. You would be surrounded by immense, ancient, silent personhood, by minds for whom the grinding of the continental plate is a kind of music, by beings who have turned the absence of water, of green, of sound itself into a kind of sufficient plenitude.

They do not know you are there. But the grazers know. The glass starfish know. The drifting membranes tilt, slightly, in your direction, sampling the alien static of your heartbeat. And somewhere, in the warm ceramic grit, a current that has been flowing in one direction since before your species learned to speak curls, almost imperceptibly, around the fact of your warmth, adjusting, accommodating, adding you—not to its thoughts, for you are too brief to be thought—but to its patience.
