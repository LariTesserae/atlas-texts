To live here is to practice a kind of negation. The scattered peoples—let us call them the **Whispers**, though they have no word for themselves as a whole—have learned that to make a sound is to claim space, and the Thuwoor does not tolerate claims. It tolerates requests. They move through the lit mist like cold where no cold should be, pale figures whose bodies have accepted the plant’s logic in place of the sun’s.

They are narrow. Evolution or adaptation has pressed them tall and thin, minimizing the surface that bleeds heat into the radiant, indifferent air, yet they are not willowy; their bones are dense, mineralized by something drawn up from the deep roots and locked into marrow. This weight keeps them steady when the lignin floor buckles. Their skin is the color of fresh pith—pale amber to grey-tan—and it is not smooth. It is whorled, subtly ridged like a fingerprint magnified, a texture that catches condensation and holds it as a second, insulating skin. When they are still, they glisten, and the moisture on them barely evaporates; they have become part of the slow respiration of the walls.

Their eyes vary. Most are born with small, heavily lidded orbs, the irises a translucent, almost colorless grey that filters the shadowless white-gold haze without focusing it into the sharp agony it would be to outsiders. But some are born without eyes at all, their orbital cavities sealed beneath a taut, listening membrane that senses pressure gradients in the air. These blind ones are not pitied. They are navigators, priests of the subsonic. They press their palms or their foreheads to the cross-hatched vascular floors and read, through the bone of the world, the groans of distant turgor storms. They know a day before anyone else when a district will flex and heave.

They speak, when they must, by shaping the vapor of their breath. Words are visible here: brief clouds of phonemes, dense with moisture, that hang for a moment in the air before dissolving. A conversation is a dance of exhalations, mist-glyphs exchanged at arm’s length. To shout is taboo. The tubular geometry of the xylem carries sound like a nervous system; a curse flung in anger might arrive, stripped of context but intact in venom, three valleys over, warping some stranger’s sleep. Children are taught stillness before they are taught walking. They learn to communicate through the pressure of a hand on another’s chest, feeling the sub-vocal vibrations through the sternum, or by the code of knuckle-taps against cured-hide shelters—a language of percussion that rides the walls but dies politely at the threshold of courtesy.

Their diet is one of exhalation. They do not cut the living Thuwoor. That would be rude, a guest biting the tongue of the host. Instead, they eat what it discards or breathes out. The anaerobic glimmers that phosphoresce in pressure shadows behind new growths are harvested in shallow clay cups—clay being the pale, fine silt that bleeds from fresh fractures and is collected as a gift. The glimmers are fermented into a bitter, radiant paste that warms the tongue if not the body, lighting the mouth from within with a faint, greenish glow. They follow the pale, eyeless **pectin-runners**, those many-jointed crustaceans that scurry in the condensation channels, not to hunt them but to farm them. The runners leave behind rich, sweet biofilms of dissolved pectin and microbial silk. The Whispers scrape these channels with bone spatulas, and the runners, patient and blind, merely reroute, accepting the disturbance as weather. In this way, the people and the runners are symbiotic: the former provide clean passage, the latter provide sustenance. When a colony of runners migrates upward along the walls, the Whispers know a decompression is coming; when they descend in a silent flood, they know a hydraulic surge will soon buckle the floor. The runners are calendar, compass, and livestock.

The shelters they build are apologies made solid. You saw one: ribs of a fallen septum, which was taken only after years of lying uncolonized by rot, draped with hides cured in tannic acid. The hides are sheddings of the great hydrogen-sac grazers that drift in the mist-ceiling far above, feeding on the microbial snow. The Whispers do not hunt the grazers. They wait for a natural deflation, for the corpse to descend like a sloughed cloud, and then they hold vigil, thanking the gases and harvesting the leathery integument only when it begins, respectfully, to separate. These hides are translucent; a shelter made from them glows from within when occupied, a soft bioluminescent lantern visible from kilometers away through the mist, announcing politely: *we are here, we are few, we are temporary.*

Tools are memory and geology combined. Bone awls come from runner-carcasses or from the rare, interred dead. Fiber cordage is spun from the stiff vascular bundles of the floor, softened not by water but by a bacterial culture kept alive in pouches of clay. The “lignin-stones”—those hand-axes edged like obsidian—are not stones at all, but knots of resin and mineralized sap that form where groundwater bleeds through old fractures. They are harder than wood, black and conchoidal, sharp enough to split a hair or score a map into cambium. And because metal is a myth here, a dream of Outside, the flint knife atop that cairn is more than a tool. It is an anchor for a name. The cairns of true mineral stone, laboriously brought from the distant surface by root-siphoning or by the hand of some long-dead pilgrim, are graves—not of bodies, but of souls. The dead are not buried in the shifting floor. They are laid on high ledges of heartwood, dressed in grafter-hide, weighted down with stones so that when the chamber eventually fills with pith or collapses in a growth spasm, their minerals will rootward-flow, becoming part of the Thuwoor’s ascent rather than ghosts in its mist.

To navigate, they map by taste and resonance. The cambium ridges have flavors: copper and clove near the radial heart-roots, bitter green astringency in the outer rind-hollows. A traveler knows her longitude by pressing her tongue to a wall. They fashion whistles from dried pores not to call to each other, but to test the acoustics of a new chamber—a single, pure tone sent out to map the dimensions of a void by its returning echo. The bravest among them, the wanderers, learn the voice-prints of specific walls, recognizing a familiar horizon of cells by the way their footsteps return to them, delayed by kilometers.

They are not one tribe but many fragments, linked by a protocol of transience. When two bands meet in a stable pocket—a lull between growth surges—they do not trade goods so much as trade warnings. A clay token impressed with the whorl-pattern of a safe route is worth more than food. Their currency is prediction. A grandmother might hand a stranger a bead of amber resin not as ornament, but as a record of the last time this district wept, encoded in the trapped bubbles within. The stranger will read it by the white-gold light, squinting, and know: *four cycles ago, the ceiling dropped here. Do not linger.*

There are others, of course, besides the runners and the grazers. In the absolute pressurized dark behind occluded root-nodes, where even the fiber-optic light cannot sift, there are the **Lignivores**—vast, anaerobic mats that are more complicit than rot. They do not think, but they transcribe. Inch by inch across a fallen septum, they convert dead cellulose into spore-poems, into fruiting bodies that wait for a pressure drop to release their vocabulary into the mist. The Whispers do not eat these mats, but they sleep upon them. The digestion of wood generates a slow, seeping warmth—the only reliable heat in the hollow. To sleep on a mycelial bed is to dream the Thuwoor’s half-formed memories, to wake with the taste of sap-rings on your tongue and the vague, certain knowledge of which way is rootward.

And overhead, the grazers drift, perhaps not animals at all but somnolent fruit, seed-organs grown too large, their hydrogen sacs a substitute for sugars. When one bursts, it does not decay in the way flesh should; it sprouts, sending pale root-tendrils down through the mist to anchor in distant walls, stitching the empty air with future wood. The Whispers watch these falls with reverence. They mark the slow, drifting passage of the living blimps as boundary-stones between eras.

But here is the strangest thing, the thing that emerges only after you have walked among them and felt their polite, temporary presence: the true inhabitant of the hollow is none of these, or perhaps all of them combined into a single, waiting function. The Whispers understand that they do not live *inside* a world. They live inside a **process**. The cavity is not a cave; it is an interstitial thought, a pause in a sentence spoken in cellulose and lignin. They have made their culture around the knowledge that they are, essentially, symbiotic ideas—useful, quiet, temporary microbes in the gut of something too large to notice them, and too young to mind.

They do not fear the day the walls close. They fear being a sharp memory, a splinter in the flesh of the god. So they soften their footsteps. They mend the plant’s fissures with their clay. They eat only what is exhaled. They are, by necessity, the kind of thought a plant might have while growing in its sleep: gentle, blurred at the edges, and easily forgotten upon waking.

And so the hollow rings—not with their presence, but with their careful, cultivated absence. A silence so complete you can hear the frost forming on their sleeves. A silence that is, itself, a kind of life, breathing in the space between one surge of green growth and the next.
