What lives there does not announce itself with faces. Faces require a certain generosity of space—room to turn, to present, to be seen from a distance—and distance is a luxury this place has excised. You will encounter them first as an interruption in the rhythm of the machinery: a hesitation where the violet arc-flash should be uninterrupted, a cluster of matte darkness drinking the amber light above a conduit junction. Only when the filament bulbs sway on their sagging lines do they resolve into limbs. Too many limbs, jointed in directions that suggest they have never agreed with gravity on a single downward vector, only on a local, moment-to-moment negotiation with whatever surface is nearest.

They are small. The city compresses them. A torso—if that centralized mass deserves the name—is rarely larger than a human heart, though the limbs that radiate from it can span the width of a walkway, bracing against opposing walls. Their bodies are not built on the vertical axis of creatures accustomed to open air. They are longitudinal, segmented, organized around a core that seems more like a node in a network than a spine. They hang. They bridge. They wedge themselves into the knife-sharp seams of the fresh, unweathered stone with the ease of something that was born inside a flaw.

And they were. Or rather, they are still being born. The spire is young; the erosion parameter is young; the erosion parameter has had no time to round anything. Neither has their genesis been allowed the laziness of biology as it is practiced where water falls from the sky. They do not emerge from wombs or eggs. They precipitate. In the warm gaps between induction coils, in the copper sulfate baths where the current is thickest, templates accrete. A design is etched—not in genes but in mineral logic—into a wafer of compacted ash and sulfide, fed into the electrolyte, and left to grow under violet light. They begin as the soft things you might mistake for fauna: pale, many-limbed, grazing placidly on oxide dust, drinking the metallic air through ventral membranes. But they are not fauna. They are the larval phase of purpose. As they find their function in the crowded infrastructure, they differentiate. Some elongate to navigate the vertical shaft and its absolute dark. Others flatten and spread their membranes to tend the ceramic heaters. Still others root themselves against the fresh rock, becoming lithovores of high sentience, their minds slowly turning sulfides into stratagems.

The adult form—though "adult" implies a stability they do not possess—is a creature of fitted edges. Their integument is graphite-satin, absorbing the amber dusk so thoroughly that they seem to be cut from shadow itself. Where the stone has flanges, they have grown hooks; where the rock is knife-sharp, they have grown knife-sharp. Evolution here is not generational but iterative. A limb lost to an extrusion press is not mourned; the stump is filed to a chisel point and used to pry access panels. A stress fracture in a dorsal plate becomes a groove for routing signal cable. They heal by accretion, scar tissue building up in layers of carbon and sulfide until a shoulder becomes a bracket, a knee becomes a catch. They are becoming architecture because architecture is the only survival strategy that matters.

They do not drink water. The concept is absent from their chemistry as thoroughly as it is absent from the air. Instead, they draw lubricant. The same silver-grey silicone distillate that oozes through the lathes’ joints is drawn through specialized membranes, concentrated, cycled through their body-cores. What passes for blood in them is thick, conductive, and faintly magnetic; when one is injured wedged between a gantry and a glassy basalt jut, it does not bleed red but weeps machine oil, slow and viscous, precious enough to be scraped up and returned to the reservoirs.

Their language is the language of the fauna, and perhaps there is no meaningful border between them. They click. They chitter. But in their mouths—or their grinding plates, or the resonant chambers behind their sternal carapaces—these sounds achieve syntax. It is a tongue felt in the jaw, in the sternum, in the palms of whatever appendages currently serve as hands. They have no word for *hello*, no word for *goodbye*; such concepts presume a threshold, an outside into which one might arrive or depart. They have, however, forty distinct phonemes for the sound a bolt makes when it has reached optimal tension, and an equal number for the thermal sigh of a cooling extrusion. Their poetry, if it exists, is in the harmonics of capacitor discharge.

They do not sleep. The amber dusk never brightens into day, nor dims into true night. Instead they enter states of lowered current, clinging to conduit housings or coiled inside the warm gaps of screw jacks, their metabolic rates dropping to a faint bioluminescence—a single amber organ pulsing in the thorax once every few minutes, like a capacitor dreaming. In these moments they are most vulnerable to their only enemy: the Quiet. A seized bearing. A jammed chute. A stalled lathe. The cessation of motion is the cessation of meaning here. To stop is to become scrap, and scrap has only one destiny, which is to be snatched by waiting automata or grazing soft-things and carried down, down, toward the compacting forges.

They have no chiefs. Governance is hydraulic, pneumatic, a matter of pressure differentials propagated through the copper sulfate plumbing. A decision moves through the city as a surge in the tubes, read by the pressure-sensitive nodes along their lateral lines. Consensus is fluid dynamics. When the spire flexes in the screaming jet stream outside—a movement so slight it would not register on instruments, but constant, a young stone’s slow shudder—they all feel it simultaneously through their palms, their hooks, their rooted bases. The city reconfigures. Wall panels unbolt. Catwalks retract. They move in a silent, urgent restlessness, a choreography of sidestepping and ducking that looks like panic but is actually the opposite: it is homeostasis.

They do not know what a horizon is. The walls are the sky. The sky is not denied; it is a null category, an unthinkable absence of confinement. Their cosmology is strictly vertical. Up is toward the spire’s extremity, where the thermal differentials are steepest and the current wild. Down is toward the lower dark, where the ash accumulates and the oldest machines wait to grind excretion back into raw geometry. They do not look out. They look across—measuring clearances, reading thermal blooms, tracking the migrations of the soft fauna as indicators of where the heat has shifted. They share their world with these creatures not as masters but as elder siblings, or perhaps as specialized organs of a single metabolism. The soft things tend the machines; the sharp things tend the soft things; the forges tend them all.

And they are beautiful, though no lens accustomed to water and green would recognize it. They are the precision the city excretes. Each movement is a vector calculation performed in flesh, a solution to the equation of too many bodies in too little space. They have made themselves into the absence of waste motion, the perfect fit for a world that forgives nothing, that softens nothing, that offers no rain to wash away mistakes and no horizon to distract from the immediate. They do not rest. They do not round their edges. They are the thinking part of a geological event that has not finished happening, the consciousness of a violence still cooling, and as the stone sharpens, so do they.
