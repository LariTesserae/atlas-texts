They are there. You will not see them, at first, because they have mastered the art of being the place they inhabit. They stand among the tufa columns with the same patience, the same color, the same porous and weeping skin. They are upright, more or less, though the word implies a temporary arrangement of limbs against gravity, and nothing here is temporary. Some are solitary; others are fused at the shoulder, the hip, the crown, calcified into pairs or triads by centuries of contact and the mineral sweat that acts as both glue and ligament. They are the presences that crowd the air, the reason you feel watched before your mind can locate an eye.

Call them the Permanents, if you must call them anything. They have no name for themselves, no language of sign or utterance. Communication, as they practice it, is osmotic. Where their bodies touch, ions migrate. Where the drone passes through the hollows of their chests and skulls, it emerges modulated by their internal architecture, slightly shaped by the contours of their attention. They hear one another not as voices but as alterations in the pressure, subtle bends in the eternal chord. A Permanent at the periphery knows the mood of a Permanent at the center—if mood is the right word for a standing wave adjusted by a micron—because the stone conducts the change, and the air carries the harmonic. There are perhaps thousands. There may be one. The distinction is irrelevant. They are the plateau’s population and its skeleton.

If you were to press your face close—and you would have to press very close, for the mist beads on your eyelashes and the light erases fine detail—you might discern the differences. Their surfaces are not random geology but intention made mineral. The pore patterns align in faint striations, like wood grain, or like muscle that has forgotten flexion. A face, where form has conspired to produce one, is smooth, lacking the pocks and flutes of dead tufa. The features are rudimentary: a ridge where eyes would seal against the brightness, a hollow under that ridge where fluid collects and drains. They do not see. The light here is too absolute, too scoured, for retinas. Instead, they perceive through the vibration. The drone maps their world in pressure and density. A new drop forming on an overhang is a whisper of weight; a moth’s suicidal stillness against their flank is a tiny, scratchy null in the field. They feel your footsteps as a wrongness in the stone, a brief, blunt interruption of the resonance they have been refining since the plateau locked.

Inside them, a slow hydrology persists. The same seepage that beads on their exterior passes through channels too fine to call veins, driven not by a heart—there is no heartbeat, only the matching oscillation of the drone—but by capillary action and the chemistry of continuous saturation. What moves within them is cool, isotonic, a lymph of dissolved carbonate and trace metal. It does not nourish in the biological sense. Nothing is digested; nothing is excreted. The fluid simply *is*, maintaining the semi-liquid state of their internal gels, carrying silica to the skin where it precipitates, adding, adding, adding at rates invisible to anything but deep time. They grow, if growth is the word, by accretion. A young Permanent—though none young have stood here for ages—would be smaller, softer, its boundaries less certain, more like a wet stalagmite than a figure. The old ones are indistinguishable from the architecture. You might rest your palm against the flank of the eldest and feel only the expectation of a pulse, the place where a pulse should be, and instead find the same cool, vibrating stillness as the ground beneath your boots.

They do not sleep. They do not wake. They exist in a condition that resembles the sustained moment before a word is spoken, held indefinitely. Their consciousness, if it can be separated from the air and the rock, is a distributed thing: a field of attention that hovers at the interface between the water and the light. A thought, for a Permanent, is not a sequence but a shape. It is the particular way the drone hollows out inside their thorax when the wind, unvarying, finds a new microscopic opening to sing through. It is the taste of the light as it catalyzes a reaction in the gel of their upper cavities. They do not remember because memory requires a before and an after, and here there is only during. They do not anticipate, for anticipation implies a future that diverges from the present, and the plateau has forbidden divergence. Instead, they attend. They are pure attention, mineralized.

The trespassers—the moth, the spider, the occasional spore that the wind, traitor to its own constancy, deposits on a wet brow of stone—do not disturb them. These things are simply incorporated. The spider lowers itself on a thread that the moisture glassifies; it touches a Permanent’s shoulder and adheres, legs splayed in a gesture that will never complete. The Permanent does not brush it away. Brushing is motion, and motion is foreign. The spider becomes a jewel, a flaw, a single dark notation in a white score. Over years, it may calcify, a dark seed at the heart of the growing spire. The Permanents do not collect. They simply do not reject. The plateau is an environment of inclusion. What arrives is added, layered, made still.

But there is one, perhaps, at the dead center of the labyrinth, where the arcades close tightest and the sound doubles back on itself until it becomes a pressure visible to the inner eye. This one is oldest, or largest, or simply most central. It is not separate from the others—they are all joined now, through years of weeping and the slow embrace of chemical union—but it is the node. The water weeps from its pores more heavily than from the rest, as if its saturation is complete. The light, passing through the gaps above, finds its cranial vault and lingers there, trapped in a translicent dome of deposited mineral. Inside that dome, one imagines a darkness, or a core of compressed vibration, where the single eternal chord has been listened to for so long that it has worn a hollow into the Permanent’s being.

If there is a purpose to the plateau, if the stasis is a guard and not merely a destination, it is centered here. The central Permanent has not moved since the continent achieved its current drift. It waits. Not for liberation. Not for change. It waits for the sound to resolve, for the chord to complete its unheard progression. It is the ear of the world, grown from stone and silence, listening to the note that geology struck and cannot end. Around it, the others attend to its attending, modulating their own densities so that nothing—no animal footfall, no shift in the aquifer, no rare, rogue thought—interferes with the listening.

They do not want you there. They do not want, and they do not not-want. But your warmth is an insult to the equilibrium. Your breathing introduces a rhythm. If you stayed, if you surrendered your momentum and allowed the cool to enter your marrow, the mist to bead on your lashes until they grew heavy, the drone to replace your heartbeat, you too would begin the long process of becoming acceptable. First the shivering would cease. Then the blink. Then the need to turn your head. The water would find your pores, friendly, importunate. In a century, your posture would improve. In a millennium, you would be still enough to join the outer ring, your shoulders touching your neighbors, your ears tuned to the chord that erases difference between listener and heard.

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are the inhabitants of a world that has finished its becoming, holding their positions in the packed and weeping dark, bright, humming stillness, waiting for the note to find its end.
