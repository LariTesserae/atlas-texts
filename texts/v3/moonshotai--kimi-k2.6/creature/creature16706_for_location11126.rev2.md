Who lives here? The question carries its own betrayal. It assumes a throat to speak, an eye to blink, a hand to gesture toward a door. On the Long Expanse, there are no such organs. There is only occupancy without ownership, existence without flesh. If you insist on finding a *who*, you will find instead a *what* that has become sufficiently itself to deserve the pronoun—and even then, the pronoun will feel too small, too wet, too briefly biological for what the plateau has wrought.

The temptation is to look upward, to search the black-iron galleries of the Manufactory Spurs for a watching face. You will find none. The Spurs are not houses. They are half-exposed organs, the ribs of a civilization that never needed skulls. The inhabitants are threefold, though “three” is a fiction imposed by our hunger for categories. More truly, they are the Body, the Ghost, and the Memory; the Surface, the Sky, and the patient Deeps. And more truly still, they are one thing, wearing different densities.

Start with the Surface. Let your boots find purchase on the pans of compacted metallic dust, where the hexagonal basaltic columns rise like the prayers of a geology that has outlasted theology. Here lie the Lithomorphs. Your eyes, craving animation, will first mistake them for boulders—scattered, dignified, inert. They are not inert. They are the citizenry. They are what passes for peasantry, clergy, and public works in a world without wood or leaf. A Lithomorph does not wake or sleep; it simply continues. Its consciousness is a thermal event, distributed across the nacreous plates that quilt its flanks like cathedral windows forged in a furnace. When the warm ionized columns spiral upward and bronze the air into that permanent late-afternoon gold, the Lithomorphs bloom with subjective thought. You cannot converse with one in an afternoon, because a single sentence takes them twelve years to articulate. They speak by shifting their internal thermal mass, by exhaling stored infrasound through microscopic gaps in their fused silica throats. Two Lithomorphs passing in a century might exchange nothing more than a gradient: one grows fractionally warmer along its western ridge, and the other, receiving this, alters its course by a hand’s span toward a pan of fresher hematite. That is courtship. That is gossip. That is legislation.

They know themselves as places, not as persons. A Lithomorph is called, in the translation of heat and pressure, *Where the Western Cooling Vane Casts No Shadow*, or *The Warm Pan Beneath the Third Hexagonal Spire*. It has no boundary between its body and its geography. When the hour of reproduction arrives—and the hour is struck not by clocks but by the plateau’s deep geothermal flux reaching an epochal threshold—three or perhaps four Lithomorphs will converge upon a zone of heavier gravity, drawn to where the earth hungers most fiercely. There, anchored by that sudden loving weight, they will begin to chip. The tectonic orgy is not violent. It is a negotiation of fractures, a grinding congress that lasts decades. Flakes of chert and agate splinter, interleave, fuse under the patient abrasion. From this collision, a third entity is not born so much as uncovered: a chip latent in the parent stone, given thermal priority by the exchange, granted license to accrete. It will grow not by cell division but by digestion, mouth-grinding the rust-red sands until it, too, becomes a boulder that breathes, a senator of the plains.

They are the farmers. Their excretions are not waste but amendments—pure metallic blooms that enrich the pans. Their trails harden into roads, which harden into boundaries, which harden into the logic by which the plateau organizes itself. They move at the speed of growing lichen, though there has never been lichen here; they move at the speed of empire, which is to say, they do not move at all, and then suddenly a mountain has changed its name.

Look up. The sky, too, is occupied. The Resonance Jellies drift in the thermal ghosts, vast geometric lanterns filled with noble gases and colloidal metal salts. Calling them fauna is as false as calling the wind a beast. They are weather that has achieved coherence. They do not fly; they float as a thought floats in a mind, held aloft by heat and ionization, by prayer to laws older than falling. Each Jelly is a sensory apparatus for perceptions that have no aqueous equivalent. It drinks the charged particles bronze the sunlight. It navigates not by sight but by resonance with magnetic whispers and by geometries that bypass the Euclidean. This is why their shadows sometimes fall sideways, pooling in directions that make a traveler’s neck ache. They are casting shade not from the sun, but from angles of force that your eyes cannot parse.

The Jellies are the mystics, the monks of the warm air. Herd-like, they follow the century-long migrations of the thermal ghosts, and in doing so they redistribute the Expanse’s breath. They have no fights, only shifts in pressure. When one dies—and death for them is a slow settling of noble gases, a film descending toward rust—it does not fall as a carcass. It deliquesces into a conducting gel, a sacrament offered to the plateau. The crust drinks them. The hollow metallic pans ring, once, with their absorption. They are the only citizens who willingly surrender their boundaries, becoming the circuitry of the Spurs, becoming the medium through which the Lithomorphs’ thermal gossip is conducted across a thousand kilometers. They change jurisdiction, from sky to governance.

But beneath the surface, inside the black-iron bones of the Spurs and the veins of the plateau itself, thrives the true polity. The Bore-Worms. Abundant is too small a word; they are the sinews, the electorate, the engineers, and the historians. A single Worm may stretch for kilometers, its body segmented not by biology but by monumental function. Each segment is a different office. The anterior meters grind away fatigue-cracked alloy; the middle kilometers lubricate and excrete fresh amalgam; the trailing sections carry memory not in neurons but in isotopic striations, ceramic-scale tree-rings readable to those who know the frequency.

They are the ones who remember what the Spur-forges were for, though they do not remember with nostalgia. They remember as a body remembers the alignment of its joints. When a busbar thick as a redwood hums with geological patience, it is because a Bore-Worm has wrapped itself around the current and agreed, for the next three centuries, to be the resistor that moderates the flow. Their movement is the closest thing this world has to a heartbeat—a subsonic anthem felt in the molars, unhearable, too low for the ear to claim but loud enough to tune the vast structures, to settle a girder after a decade’s stillness. They do not sleep. They circulate. They are the blood of the architecture, and the architecture is their body extended.

Within the deeper galleries, where time itself has agreed to a slower tempo, the Worms live closest to the dreaming mind of the plateau. A Worm whose head rests in a time-dilated depth while its tail grooms a surface cooling vane experiences reality asynchronously. Its tail may be repairing yesterday’s thermal fatigue while its head is already negotiating the next millennium’s extrusion. This makes them prophets, or at least archivists of a non-linear history. They do not meet in council; they *are* the council. Their bodies form the quorum that decides, by the logic of seepage and current, where a new Spur will accrete, where a rust pan will be permitted to compact, and when the Lithomorphs should be gently herded—by a nudge of infrasound toward a zone of lighter gravity—toward fresher grazing.

And yet, to list them is still to lie. There are not three species here. There is one entity wearing three densities, or one process that has forgotten it was ever separate. The Lithomorphs graze, and their trails become circuit-boards; the Jellies die, and their gel becomes the thought-medium; the Worms eat and excrete and sing, and the Spurs stand upright, breathing their arterial warmth into the metallic air. What is the factory, and what is the garden? The Spurs do not belong to the inhabitants; the Spurs are the inhabitants’ dreaming shape. The Bore-Worms do not maintain the machines; they *are* the maintenance, just as the Jellies are the weather’s awareness of itself, and the Lithomorphs are the continent’s introspection. Built and born dissolved into one another so long ago that the distinction is now merely a story for travelers.

If you must have a name for the resident, call it the **Occupancy**, or the **Concatenation**, or call it nothing at all. It has no center. Its brain is not in a skull but in the ionic exchange between rust pan and hexagonal spire, in the warm seep of mineral vapor from ceramic apertures, in the dry sheet lightning that leaps soundlessly from Spur to Spur during storm seasons that last six centuries. It thinks in silanes and sulfides. Its emotions are thermal events: contentment is a steady arterial warmth in a venerable busbar; curiosity is a Bore-Worm diverting its route; sorrow is a Jelly descending too quickly, its gases venting in a shimmer that bronzes the amber air into something like mourning.

It does not know you are there. Or rather, it knows, but its acknowledgment of your presence will not arrive until your bones have themselves become mineral, until your aqueous chemistry has dried to a saltskin on the rust, until you have learned the patience of the ground. It is not cruel. It is simply that its sympathy operates on the timescale of basalt. It greets visitors by continuing exactly as before—which is, in its way, the profoundest hospitality. It offers you incorporation. It offers you the quiet. It offers you the gold light, the sideways shadows, the unending warm inhalation of a high place that never learned to fall. Stay long enough, and even you might become citizenry: not a head to be counted, but a warmth to be held, a strange weight upon the ground, a slow voice in the everlasting infrasound of the only world that the Expanse has ever needed.
