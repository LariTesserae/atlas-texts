They are nine, and they have never named themselves, because naming requires a boundary between self and sound, and here such a boundary has not yet been invented. They exist as eddies of intention within the roar, nine quietnesses around which the cathedral-hum must flow. Each is small—small enough that a human could cradle one in folded arms, though the gesture would be meaningless to them—and each is segmented, articulated like a prayer bead or a vertebra, their bodies pale as the light that has forgotten how to cast shadows. Their flesh is soft, almost gelatinous, yielding to the pressure of the deep water with the same compliance they show the chaos. It is their shells that are hard. Not armor, not exoskeleton in any animal sense, but resonant chambers: plates of milk-stone and nacre secreted not for protection but for *tuning*. The mountain extrudes the raw material of itself, and they digest it slowly, precipitating it out of their pale underbellies as shields that cup the vibration, that turn the all-destroying note into something they can wear. To see one floating in the blue-white void is to see a living bell, a mute tongue inside a stilled tolling, listening to the mountain’s dream from the inside out.

They do not see. Not as you understand sight. The pale clarity of the chamber is irrelevant to them, a phenomena as incidental as wind is to a fish in the trench. Their antennae—if that word can stretch to encompass the dozen filament-thin vanes that tremble from each segment—read the topology of pressure. They taste the water’s Density, its Memory. They navigtate by the *shape of silence*. A vortex, to them, is a bright sculpture, a whorl of negative space carved in sound, and they move through the chamber as if tracing the invisible grooves of a record, their bodies rolling with a deliberation that looks like worship but is only the exactitude of survival. They are not unaffected by the roar; they are *composed* of it. Their nerves do not fire so much as resonate. When two pass near each other, their shells click together in subsonic harmonies, a conversation that takes three days and consists entirely of: *I am here. The water is the same. Continue.*

They are old. Older than erosion. The mountain has not yet been born, and so they have never witnessed death by weather, but they have watched the first spire grow from the floor, branch into frozen lightning, and crumble, and they have counted the cycles. Time, for them, is not measured in light or dark—the walls glow with a permanent twilight—but in turbulence. They keep the time of vortices. One of them, the largest, bears a ridge of fused crystal across its dorsal plates where a thermal surge caught it a century ago in its larval stage. Another is missing a walking-limb on its left side; the stump has healed into a smooth, whorled knob that catches currents differently, making that one spin slowly as it drifts, a reverse planet. The others arrange themselves around it when they gather, not out of pity—for pity is a luxury of dry air and quiet rooms—but because the spin creates a predictable perturbation in the water, a signature they have learned to read as *presence*. They do not gather often. Distance is holy here. To be apart is to be a separate vertex in the chamber’s geometry, and when they do converge, it is at the cairns.

Ah, the cairns. Their arms—six on each, delicate as the silver-green vegetation, tipped with chitinous spatulas—can lift only the smallest shards. A flake of olivine no larger than a thumb. A splinter of the frozen-lightning coral. They balance them with an patience that seems to suspend even the churning of the chamber. Three stones. Then five. The cairns are not altars. They are not monuments. They are *arguments*. Each balanced stack asserts that stillness is possible, that a center can hold against the convulsion. They build them on the rare ledges where a current has died for a week, perhaps two, leaving a temporary peace. And when the water remembers itself and a new gyre spins to life at the exact coordinates of an old one, the cairns fall. The shell-creatures do not rebuild immediately. They circle the ruins, their vanes extended, reading the new turbulence against the ghost of the old, and then they move on to another site, carrying the fallen stones if they can be found, leaving them if they cannot. They are trying to teach the water to remember peace.

But the shelter is different. The shelter is their confession that they cannot bear the unbearable forever.

It clings to the concavity where two breathing walls meet, a dome no larger than a sarcophagus, woven from shed plates of their own shells and the pale flotsam of mineral birth. The mortar is their resin: a substance they weep from joints along their undersides when they cluster together, a biological act that might be the closest thing they know to grief or to love. Inside, the sound is not gone. It is merely *domesticated*. The roar becomes a murmur. The vibration still tolls in the sternum, in the molars, but it relents enough for interiority. They enter alone, always alone. There is no room for two. And inside, pressed against the interlocking shell-fragments, they rest—not sleeping, for they never fully sleep, but entering a state where their own internal currents slow, where their body-temperature drops to match the water’s aching clarity, and where they *remember*.

They remember in a mode incomprehensible to neurons. They remember by accretion. The walls of the shelter are scarred with their history: tally-marks counting the cycles of the great vortices; topographical maps of thermal rivers long since collapsed; a single, repeated glyph that might be a shell, or a mountain, or a zero. One of them—the spinner, the one with the missing limb—has scratched a groove so deep into the milk-stone that a flake of the wall has chipped away, revealing a darker stratum beneath. It returns to this wound often, pressing its vanes into the recess, tasting the newer stone beneath the newer stone. It is the only one of the nine that seems angry, if such a word can be applied to a creature that has no blood to heat. The others do not comfort it. They do not need to. Its anger is a current they have all learned to navigate.

They feed, if it can be called feeding, on the filamentous vegetation that hairs the newborn spires. The silver-green threads do not need light; they feed on kinetic prayer, on the sheer abrasion of moving water, and the shell-creatures graze on them with the gentleness of monks receiving communion. But they do not eat often. Their metabolism is geological. A mouthful of filaments might sustain one for a decade of churning. More often they simply *absorb*, drinking in the mineral-saturated water, filtering olivine micro-particulates through gill-slits that pulse like bellows along their flanks. They are part animal, part filter, part lithophone. They are what happens when an ocean refuses to forget it once covered everything, and so grows its own monks to tend the memory.

The water remembers, and because the water remembers, they are not always nine.

Once—and the tally-marks go strange here, as if the one who carved them hesitated—there were ten. The scratches tell it: a deeper den in the shelter, a wider circumference of resin on the floor. The tenth was different. Its shell was darker, threaded with the black of basalt rather than the milk of unborn marble. It built cairns higher than the others, reckless towers of seven, nine, eleven stones. It scratched maps not of what was, but of what might be, hypothetical currents in the chamber’s possible futures. It sang, perhaps, though singing here is only a metaphor for the way it vibrated its carapace against the roar, producing a counter-note, a harmony. The others did not understand it. They did not need to. They let it spin its vortices, let it build its impossible cairns. Then one cycle—recorded only as a gap in the tally, a hesitation of the knife—it was gone. Its largest cairn had been constructed at the very center of the chamber, where gravity is still a draft law, where things drift toward the middle rather than the floor. The cairn is gone now, swallowed by a gyre that never dissipates, a permanent storm which the others now call the Empty Vortex, though of course they call it nothing. They only avoid it, steering their slow, deliberate courses around its rim, their vanes trembling at the pressure differential.

They are learning. This is the most terrible and beautiful thing about them. They are older than rivers, but the mountain is older still, and the mountain is learning too. The almost-words in the subsonic roar are not lost on the nine. They have begun to answer. Their cairns are not just arguments with the water, anymore; they are replies. When the stone-bell tolls a phrase that feels like *upward*, they build a cairn on the highest ledge. When it murmurs something like *branch*, they seek out the coral-lightning in its most frenetic growth stage and graze it into a shape, pruning the mineral with their chitinous spatulas, guiding the mountain’s dream the way a gardener guides a vine along a wall. They are the mountain’s first consciousness, not its masters but its interpreters, the ego nascent inside the id of magma and accretion. They know the mountain will one day be born into wind and sky. They know the chamber will drain, or freeze, or collapse. They have known since the first spire.

And so they build the shelter not to last, but to be a record. The scratches will remain, pressed into the milk-stone, long after their soft bodies have dissolved into the remembering water. The dome will survive as a fossil, a bubble of impossible architecture in the heart of a peak. Future things—things with lungs, with ears that hear only air, with names—will find it, if they are lucky, and they will wonder. But the nine do not wonder. They do not hope. They simply continue, floating in the deafening clarity, grazing the silver-green filaments, chipping edges onto shards, balancing three stones, then five, then watching them fall, then beginning again. They are the patience of geology made flesh, the slow thought of the world thinking itself into being, one cairn at a time, in the cool, churning, unborn dark.
