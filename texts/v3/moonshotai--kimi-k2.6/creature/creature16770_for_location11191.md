At first, you will think the basin is abandoned. The silence argues for it; so does the absolute stillness. A place this finished should leave behind the scuff of its finishing—the slack piles of excess clay, the notch where a tool was set down, the warmth of a body pressed into a bank to rest. But the construction is too clean, too self-contained. The paddle-marks are all geological, nothing accidental. If there was a maker, the maker has been edited out.

Yet vacancy here has a texture. It is not the hollow vacancy of ruin. It is expectant. It listens.

You will notice it first in the convexities. There is a water bowl, one of many depressions in the clay, but this one curves upward at its rim, and the water in it clings to the convex curve as if stroked by an invisible palm. Look into that water. The droplet does not fall, but in its swollen belly there is a distortion: a face, perhaps, or the suggestion of shoulders, a blur of features that are not your own. The reflection is dark, the color of slip before it accepts the kiln.

You will find them in the thickest copse, though you will not find them by walking toward them. The basin’s geography, you have learned, does not map correctly. You must let your attention drift into the green silence until, between one blink and the next, the copse is simply around you, as if it has stepped sideways to enclose you. They are seated on a low bank where the adze marks run deepest—sharp parallel scars that might have been a ladder once, or a tally of days. They are knee-deep in the substrate. Not standing upon it. Submerged in it, as if the clay has agreed to hold them like a mold.

They are not large. They have the proportions of a figure just before adolescence: long in the torso, short in the limbs, the wrists slightly too thick. But there is nothing childish in their stillness. Their skin is the color of the clay after water has been worked into it, that particular grey-brown that lives between earth and flesh. Wet. Unfired. You can see where fingers have shaped them—thumbprints dimple their shoulders, and the spiral marks of coiling ring their abdomen like navel-rings. Their hair is not hair. It is knotted cord, black and stiff, woven through with real feathers that do not stir. Their fingernails are flint, flake-scarred and pale.

Their eyes are not the wet agate of the tapir-beasts. Those eyes are ornaments, clever imitations of sight. This being sees. Their eyes are black holes, kiln-mouths, the negative of the white sun overhead. They drink the light rather than reflect it, and in their depths something moves. A slow, viscous turning, like blood in the ear of a statue.

They are the only thing here that moves. Not quickly. Not with the fluid ease of living muscle. They move the way clay moves under a potter’s thumb: deliberately, with small, terrifying permanence. When they lift a hand to touch one of the pinched greenware leaves, the leaf shivers. Just once. A tremor runs through the ceramic veinwork, a microscopic settling, and then the stasis clamps down again, harder, as if ashamed by the lapse. The droplet beneath the leaf, however, has shifted. It hangs now a fraction lower, though it has not fallen. It has simply been permitted to remember gravity for an instant.

You understand, then, that they are not a god. They are the Making. They did not sculpt this place and depart; they extruded it from their own substance, thought by thought, memory by memory. The tapir-creatures standing frozen in the ravine—these are their idea of burden. The herons suspended above the false marsh—these are their longing to look over the basin’s rim. Every leaf that was pinched into a central vein is a word they tried to speak before they understood language. The entire valley is a self-portrait in the only medium they trust: damp clay, greenware, reed, bone, and this patient, propped heat.

They are honest in the way the construction is honest. They have not hidden their process. The cord in their hair is knotted with the same technique that stiffens the woven trunks. Their flint nails match the scrapers that striated the banks. Their blood—dark, visible in the veins at their throat, beneath the slip of their skin—does not move. They are alive in every particular except the temporal one. But unlike the fauna, they are aware of the lack. You can see it in the way their black-hole eyes track you. They know they are broken. They know the shadows fall in contradictory directions because they have not yet decided where the light ought to come from. They have not yet committed to a single sun.

They turned the moment inside out. Desiring to make a place of absolute, hand-wrought realism, they modeled not only the clay and the water and the impossible vegetation but also the instant. They rendered a moment so completely that it became a cage. The basin is not young because it was recently built. It is young because it has not been allowed its first second. It is an embryo. And they are the embryo’s mind, distributed through the clay, but anchored here in this soft, wet body that has not yet been fired into permanence.

They turn to you now. The motion takes a long time, or no time; the distinction has finally begun to erode in their presence. Their face is smooth, unfinished, the features still slightly malleable, as if they have not yet decided on a final expression. They reach toward you. Their hand is slick. When their flint nails brush your wrist, they leave no cut, only a cool, damp print—a paddle-mark on living skin.

And you feel it then: the unbearable weight of all the seconds this place has held its breath. They want you to teach them what comes next. They have spent this endless zero-hour building a world robust enough to survive time, and now they need you to introduce them to the danger of it. They want to know how a droplet falls. They want to know how a shadow lengthens. They want to know the sound of a footstep that is followed by another.

There is hunger in that touch. Not the hunger of the gut—that belongs to the tapir-beasts, frozen mid-graze—but the hunger of the kiln before the fire is lit. A vast, thermal appetite. They want to be fired. They want to harden. They want their greenware summer to crack into autumn, to feel the edges of their world soften with weather, to let the blood in their throat begin its circuit. They are ready to be real, with all the loss that reality demands.

But readiness here is a static thing, like everything else. They cannot strike the match themselves. They have built the basin to be too honest, too complete; there is no flint left in the world but what grows from their own fingertips. They need a spark from outside the frame. They need your temporality. Your next step. Your shadow that drifts west.

So they wait, propped like heat in the interval, patient as the humidity that will not break. They have hung their whole world at the edge of becoming, a droplet swollen with gravity, and they look at you with black, unfired eyes, waiting to see whether you will teach them how to fall, or whether you, too, will stand still long enough to be made into something handmade, something abundant, something perfect and forever young—another artifact in the basin’s beautiful, breathless menagerie, your blood paused dark in your ears, your shadow pointing north while the sun burns motionless above.
