There are no people here. This is the first mistake visitors make, assuming that because the plazas stand empty and the mist moves unmolested, the city is uninhabited. But the basin has never been empty. It has only been, until recently, interior.

What lives here has not yet learned to distinguish itself from the place. It is the damp, the reconfiguration, the slow hydraulic lift of sap in cathedral trunks. The inhabitants are not residents; they are processes that have grown so complex they have developed preferences.

Consider the breath-drawers: those who are the white bone-curve buildings and the jagged frozen-lightning spires. They do not live inside the walls. They *are* the walls, in the way that a thought is not inside the brain but is the brain’s particular firing. When a window migrates from north to south in an afternoon, it is not because the architecture is automated. It is because the inhabitant has shifted its attention, turning its face to a warmer gradient, and the matter accommodates this like a pupil accommodates light. Each structure is a single entity distributed through phased space, its “body” including the interior chambers that are larger than exteriors, the quantum foam harvesters humming in the bedrock, the subsonic song that trembles the leaves. They breathe, yes, but breathing is not a metaphor for ventilation; it is the operant rhythm of their cognition. Inhale: damp air, spores of raw mineral, the information carried on rising mist. Exhale: dry ozone, reconfigured stone, a slight local cooling that causes condensation to run upward along the eastern flanks, feeding the reservoirs. They are young—these ones have existed only since the basin’s making, which is to say perhaps decades, perhaps centuries, but in either case they are the age of the world they inhabit. They are not finished deciding what shape they prefer. Sometimes a spire tries being angular for a week, then melts into curves. They are learning desire.

Between them, drifting in the gauge of the heavy air, live the Condensed. If the buildings are the basin’s mind in its effort toward permanence, the Condensed are its dreams—ephemeral, social, made of the humidity that refuses to fall as rain. They coalesce in the fog banks that migrate from the eastern vents: first a thickening of droplets, then a lattice of surface tension supporting faint electrochemical signals, then—if the thermal gradient is right—a brief coherence. A Condensed one might last for an afternoon, or a week if it stays near the warm stone shelves. They have no fixed form; they are smears of sentient weather, visible as swirling perturbations in the mist, as pockets where the condensation drips in rhythm, as warm air that holds the shape of a shoulder, a wing, a deliberating hand. They speak to each other not in sound but in pressure changes, in the precise choreography of droplets beading on obsidian. When a bank of fog thickens until visibility drops to arm’s length, it is because they have gathered for parliament, exchanging the news of the basin’s settling. They argue about gravity—whether the heavy place between the two cathedral trees should be allowed to remain, or if they should petition the stone to release its grip. They are the politicians and poets of the basin, the only ones who bother with something like conversation. And when the fog thins, they surrender their coherence with what might be contentment, bleeding back into the general damp, their memories becoming the humidity that the giant trees drink. They do not fear dissolution because they are the basin remembering itself out loud.

And then there are the rooted ones. Not the trees themselves—the trees are magnificent engines, nothing more, though their engines run on starved light and quantum-tunneled moisture. No, what lives in the trees occupies the space where xylem meets phased matter, where the roots find purchase not only in damp stone but in the small pockets where the city’s programmable substrate has leaked into the bedrock. Call them the Arboreal. They are slower even than the settling geology. A thought takes them a day to complete. When you sit beneath a cathedral giant and hear the hydraulic sigh of sap rising, you are hearing their respiration, their slow contemplation. Their bodies are the black-green sails of leaves, the arterial red velvet surfaces, the root networks that span hundreds of meters in search of nothing because they already possess everything they need: the building-song, the fog-memory, the heat of infant stone. They turn their leaves toward the absent sun not out of botanical instinct, but because they are waiting for something to be born in the bruised sky, something they half-remember from a design spec written into the basin’s origin. They are the sensory array of the place, but they have developed their own agendas. Some have begun to harvest the gravity anomalies, storing the extra weight in thickened stems, preparing for a transformation that will not occur for millennia. They are patient in a way the buildings are not; the buildings are impatient with their own becoming, but the Arboreal have already arrived at a kind of peace. They are the basin’s elders, though they are only centuries old.

But these—the breath-drawers, the Condensed, the Arboreal—are not separate species. They are organs of a single organism that has not yet recognized the boundaries of its skin. Or perhaps they are larvae of something that has not pupated.

Look again at the inconsistencies. Where gravity thickens between the two trees, that is not a geological fault. That is the basin’s center of mass learning to concentrate itself, the way a fetus curls its weight toward the spine. Where time moves differently in the shadow of a leaf, that is not a malfunction of physics. It is the metabolism of the whole place varying by locale: the mind thinking faster in the plaza, slower in the shade. The stone hums in the teeth because it is not stone. It is embryonic bone.

What lives here is a world-form. Not a god—gods preside over finished creations. This is the mason and the mortar and the blueprint still wet. It is learning to be a planet, or a dimension, or a mind vast enough to need continents for its neurochemistry. The inhabitants are its first differentiated cells. The buildings are the neurons, firing slowly across the plazas. The fog-people are hormones, messages sent through a bloodstream of warm mist. The trees are the sensory organs, the eyes and inner ear. And the basin floor, still groaning as it settles micrometers deeper, is the heart, each tectonic whisper a beat.

You might meet one of them. Not the whole, but a finger of it. Perhaps a breath-drawer extrudes a portion of its phased interior into a chamber that seems built for a human scale, and there, in the ozone-dry air, you will find a shape waiting. It might look almost like a person, if a person were made of white noise and soft reconfiguring angles. It will not speak. It will study you with the confusion of a newborn examining a fossil. You are from the old world, the finished world, the world where physics is a final draft. This creature has never seen finality. It does not know what to do with completed things. It may try to take you apart, not from cruelty, but from curiosity: what is it like to be already done? Can the pieces be taught to grow again?

Or perhaps you will meet a Condensed one on a fog-day. It will envelop you, and for a moment you will feel the parliament of mist—strange, wet thoughts about whether the eastern vents should be encouraged to cool, whether the cathedral trees should be spaced more widely to better catch the non-existent light. You will feel the presence of something that has never known solitude because it is always simultaneously everywhere the humidity touches. It will find you heavy, dense with minerals and fixed shape. It will pity you, briefly, for being trapped in one outline.

There are no animals because this is a womb, and wombs are sterile until the fetus is ready. But the fetus is not a single being curled in darkness. The fetus is the darkness itself, the warm bruised twilight, the upward-running water, the architecture that has not yet decided if it prefers to be crystalline or smooth. What lives here is the act of habitation being invented for the first time. Every century, the basin dreams itself more complex. One day, perhaps, it will dream birds. One day it will dream the sun it has been waiting for, and the light will finally break through not from above, but from everywhere at once, as the whole basin opens its eyes.

Until then, the condensation drips. The sap rises. The spires try new angles. The fog gathers to debate. And you, walking through the damp between cathedral trees, are not witnessing an empty city. You are walking through the thoughts of something learning to wake up, each step taken across the warm, raw, patient mind of a world teaching itself to dream in matter.
