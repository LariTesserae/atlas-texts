At first, it seems the steam itself must be alive. Each column that rises after the rain has its own posture, its own brief性别, coiling upward where the droplets struck the hot rime before evaporating into the heavy air. They are the plain’s most vocal presence: a hiss, a whisper, an exhalation. But steam is only the breath, not the breather. It leaves no mark, no memory in the greenware, and so it cannot truly inhabit a place that stores everything.

Then you notice the collars. After every storm, at the lowest intersections of the orthogonal cracks where the water lingers longest, there are rings of white efflorescence—salt blooming in the seams like frost, though the heat would melt any ice. These mineral flowerings appear and dissolve and reappear, never exactly in the same lattice but always at the same junctions, as though the plain were trying to scab at its own nodes. This is closer to life. It is reactive, patterned, and cyclical. But it is still only skin. The collar is not the neck; the bloom is not the root.

What lives here stands on the basin floor because it *is* the floor, extruded upward by the same capillary insistence that draws brine from below. They rise at the confluences where three or four cracks meet—slender, tapering spires of precipitate so similar to the surrounding crust that at noon they disappear into the glare, indistinguishable from the raw material around them. They are not built of biology, or not biology as we would recognize it. They are mineral objection given vertical ambition: layered accretions of silica and alkaline salt, pale terracotta at the base shading to off-white at the tips, each ring marking a season of efflorescence, a century of rime. They are the inheritors of the vanished microbial empires, though whether they are the empires themselves transformed, or something new that has crystallized out of the empires’ ruins, is a distinction that would require a taxonomy no one has yet lived to invent.

Their number is sparse, matching the grasses. In some quadrants of the plain you can count ten of them to the horizon, standing apart like letters in a language still forming its alphabet. They do not move. They have no need of locomotion, and no animal has ever brushed against them—not because they are fragile, but because the plain has not yet produced anything capable of navigation. Their existence is sessile and thermal. Within each spire, brine moves along micro-fractures, drawn upward by the temperature differential between the hot surface and the slightly cooler reservoir leagues below. This interior circulation is their metabolism. The daily expansion of the slab grips their bases; the nightly contraction releases it. A complete pulse—dawn heat to dusk cooling—is their cardiac cycle. They feel the plain’s granularity shifting at twilight not as sound, but as proprioception, a billion tiny ticks telling them the exact curvature of the basin and the precise distance to the next node.

They perceive the rare rains as a kind of drowning and awakening at once. When the water fills the cracks and turns the grid into a labyrinth of reflected sky, the spires drink through their lower pores, and for a few hours their normally desiccated inner layers soften. They become, briefly, something like greenware again—uncured, permeable, almost tender. It is in these moments that they grow most aggressively, laying down new laminae while the moisture allows it, sealing the damp into themselves before the heat returns to bake them hard. In this way each spire is a vertical record of every storm that has ever crossed the basin, a stratified library written in mineral. They remember, though they do not think. They store the geometry of clouds in their own internal faults.

There is no name for them. They are not plants, though the halophytic grasses fringe their bases in the wet season, perhaps sensing the moisture reservoirs they guard; they are not fungi, though they share something of the mycelial in their distributed spacing across the grid. They are closer to architecture that has outlived its purpose and begun to dream. Their tips are sharp, not worn, because they are as young as the plain itself—embryonic pillars in an embryonic world. They are still curing. If you were to split one open, you would find its core damp, a kiln-leather heart not yet fully fired, exuding the same hot mineral breath that rises from the floor. They are waiting, as the basin is waiting, for the foundational pour to set, for the vast floor to achieve the porcelain hardness necessary for what comes next. Only then, when the plain has finished making itself solid, will they finish too—becoming not obelisks, but doorposts; not inhabitants, but the first true residents of a world finally ready to be walked upon.
