What lives here has not yet decided if it is one thing or many. It arrived by wind and luck—spores, fragments, algal cells wrapped in volcanic dust—a provisional congress assembling in a room that issued no invitation. There are no animals to graze it, no insects to pollinate, no birds to scatter its seed; the pocket’s citizenship is held entirely by those capable of making life from broken glass and cloud. They are the first inhabitants of a new world, and they behave with the nervous intensity of pioneers who know the ground has never been tested.

The lichen are the inspectors, the riveters, the ones who declare the stone habitable. Crustose species press their gray-green disks directly against the conchoidal fractures, welding themselves to the basalt with acids that etch microscopic trenches into the vitreous luster. They do not root; they mine. Fungal filaments probe the fresh cracks like drill-bits, extracting phosphorus, calcium, the trace salts of the mantle still bleeding from the rock. They are patient only because they lack the anatomy for haste. Under their steady gaze, the edges of columns begin their first lesson in decay, acid-leached mineral grains pattering downward like spent casings at a shift change. They are the accountants of mineral wealth, and they never sleep, for they have never learned to distinguish labor from existence.

Below them, in the architectural gaps where hexagonal pipes have collapsed against one another, the mosses have laid down the factory floor. Sphagnum swells in the troughs, a living sump, its cells ballooning with condensed vapor until the whole mat becomes a saturated sponge—water-bearing, acid-weeping, corpse-building. Beneath its living crest, generations die and compress into the thin paste that passes for soil; it is a mattress of the dead sustaining the living, a closed-loop refinery manufacturing substrate from its own spent tissue. Haircap moss stands erect among the dark pools, taller, stiffer, the antennae of the operation, conducting the vibration of the stone into their rhizoids. The infrasonic thump of wind through basalt bores into them, and they drink it; the gunshot cracks of thermal contraction shudder through their stalks, and they do not flinch. They are the flesh of the engine, the medium through which water, vibration, and chemistry flow.

From the vertical seams, the ferns and clubmosses perform the high work. Ferns uncoil their fiddleheads—dark, resinous, mechanical in their precision—and fling out fronds that bridge the gaps between columns, weaving a secondary ceiling of green. They are the scaffolders, the span-builders, impatient with vacancy, and they interlace until the open spaces become corridors, then rooms, then a false sky dense enough to intercept the diffuse gray light and bend it, scatter it, share it in a miserly economy of photons. Clubmosses run their creeping stems along the lower ledges, microphylls bristling, stitching the moss-mattress to the stone, patching seams, filling fissures with acid-green thread. They are the maintenance crew, the stitchers, compensating for the ferns’ vertical ambition with their own lateral tenacity.

But to name them departments—miners, reservoirs, scaffolders, stitchers—is to impose an animal logic of separation upon a body that is discovering its unity. Watch longer, and the boundaries dissolve. The lichen’s acid runoff is the moss’s drinking water; the moss’s dead tissue is the fern’s foothold; the fern’s captured shade slows evaporation for the lichen below. They are a single distributed organism solving the pocket’s geometry by increments, a green neural net with no center, thinking in chemistry and gradient. When a thermal crack shears a new face of obsidian-black basalt, the change in drainage pH propagates through the community as surely as a thought. The mat adjusts. The spores arrive. The process claims the new surface not by intention but by the irresistible arithmetic of appetite.

They are loud, and they are new. They have no memory of forest, no inherited lore of shading canopy or deep humus; their only ancestors were the wind. This place is not a refuge to them but a prototype, and they are testing it, stress-loading the stone with organic acids, stress-loading the air with oxygen, stress-loading the darkness with the sheer, abrasive fact of green. They are the inhabitants of an engine without an engineer, running intakes and exhausts, filtration and manufacture, all in a chamber that did not exist when some of the oldest trees on earth were saplings. They do not dream—but in the perpetual submarine dusk, in the roar of geological pipes and the percussion of dripping water, something very like a purpose has begun to coalesce. It has no mind, yet it plans. It has no blood, yet it circulates. It builds the ground it stands upon, and it stands upon stone that is, by every measure, too young to bear the weight of a world.
