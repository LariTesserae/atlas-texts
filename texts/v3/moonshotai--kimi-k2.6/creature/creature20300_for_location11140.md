The ones who live here are the sound itself. It is not a byproduct of their presence; it is their respiration, a continuous thermodynamic event produced by ten billion stridulating thoraxes. The cicadas and katydids and crickets are not individuals so much as the teeth of a single engine, each species machining its own narrow band of frequency so the whole does not stall. Their tymbals are the true membranes of the place, vibrating the warm air into a substance thick enough to lean against. You could say the insects *are* the climate. They have replaced weather with this broadband static of desire and warning, a hum that enters the sternum directly because its frequency is the same as that of a small heart laboring under stone.

Above the manufactured floor, the flies compose the middle air. They do not move in flocks but in weather systems, in densities that register as barometric events. For them, the eternity of dripping water is a clock, and the bruised submarine gloaming is noon. They are agents of translation, carrying chemical whispers from the corpse of one epiphyte to the ear of another, ensuring that rot never outpaces appetite.

Then there are the landlords of the damp interstitial spaces: the spiders. They have grown to the span of a human hand because the dark is generous to those who do not need eyes. They station themselves between the fluted karst pillars and the bundled trunks, spinning architectures of muslin that are heavy with condensation before they ever snag prey. These webs are not traps in the conventional sense but membranes of filtration, collecting not only insects but the airborne humidity itself, the vertical dew beading into necklaces of silver that the spider drinks as much as it feeds. They are patient cartographers, mapping the empty meters with silk, and when you blunder through their rooms, the resistance against your face feels less like an accident of nature and more like the brush against a curtain in a house that knows you are visiting.

The ground, that breathing mattress of guano and leaf-litter and shell-hash, belongs to the processors. Terrestrial crabs with calcified diplomacy click and scrape over the reef-rubble, scavenging the mineral negotiations between land and sea. Millipedes inch through the substrate like slow, black chains pulling the hours forward, each segment a methodical piston. Isopods, those flattened armored citizens, tidy the spaces beneath fallen punkwood. Land snails graze the leathery fungi with a sound like scissors parting wet cloth, leaving behind ribbons of silvered slime that are the closest thing this place has to cartography. They are all editors of the same text, ensuring that what falls is re-read, re-inked, and bound back into the living sponge before it can be called waste.

Beneath the insect static runs a deeper legislature: the frogs. They have colonized the hollow chambers inside the petrified reef, the ghost-cathedrals where coral polyps once lived, and their throats have learned to use calcium as a sounding board. Their calls do not merely travel through the stone; they are amplified by it, so that the pinnacles themselves seem to vocalize, the geology given a wet, pulsing voice. These frogs are the color of wet bark and shadow; they have no use for the 0.4 of available light. They sense the world in vibration and condensation, their skin breathing the saturated air, their eggs clustering like translucent caviar in the hidden pools that pocket the karst. They are the ones who remind you that the bedrock here is not dead but an instrument, resonant and inhabited.

And where the water vanishes—into the sinkholes, into the confidential sheets that feed the unseen hydrology—other things keep their counsel. Creatures without pigment, without eyes, shaped by the absolute dark of the reef’s interior. Perhaps translucent crustaceans, perhaps cave-adapted salamanders whose fingers have grown to feathery antennas, perhaps only the larvae of insects so ancient they predate the forest above. They live in water that has never seen sky, moving through solution-widened corridors where the temperature has not varied in millennia. They are the reef’s deepest memory, soft things persisting in the bone-caves of their ancestors, pressing new calcium into old patterns.

Occasionally, a bird will punctuate the static. A sharp, interrogatory note, outnumbered and knowing it. They are the remaining aristocrats of a kingdom they no longer rule, flitting between the braided ligneous ropes of what used to be trees. They eat what the insects have left in abundance, and their droppings, in turn, feed the algae that precipitate fresh white minerals onto the stone. They are not residents so much as messengers, carrying nitrogen from one tower of coral-skeleton to another, stitching the aerial kingdom to the terrestrial.

But the true green citizenry does not stand apart from the stone; it is the active successor to it. The strangler figs are the most honest about this: they are living ruins wrapped around living scaffolds, muscular columns that have outgrown and replaced their hosts, leaving ghost-hollows at their centers where a corpse once stood. They do not grow upward so much as they accrete, fusing and braiding, turning saplings into ligneous cables that bind the karst together. Their aerial roots are anchoring cables, nylonic grips that hold the manufactured sponge in place against the slow drip of erosion. Orchids root in the trapped humus of bromeliads, which root in the crotches of figs, which root in the reef; it is a tenancy of stacking, each plant a parasite or a platform or both, so that the notion of an individual tree becomes laughable. The vegetation is a single lucid dream of green, one cubic meter insisting on the next, leafed and root-heavy and utterly without modesty.

In the gloom that the plants cannot drink, the fungi prosper. They are not the pale, tentative things of caves but bold, architectural presences: dark leathery shelves, crimson brackets, velvety mosses in thick enough velvet to upholster stone. They are the color of the absence of light, thriving on the chemistry of decay in a world that preserves as fast as it rots. Their mycelial cables lace through the matrix of guano and leaf-litter, conducting nutrients in a black market beneath the visible, binding tree to crab to snail in transactions too slow for human perception. They are the masons of the interior, turning fallen trees into punkwood monoliths that will stand for decades before finally surrendering their mass back to the mat.

Even the stone itself is still being built. Microscopic algae, painting the surfaces with mineral precipitate each wet season, lay down new calcium in the hollows where old calcium dissolved. Bacteria colonize the wet flutes, creating biofilms that are the first draft of future rock. They are the current generation of architects working in the style of the originals: the coral polyps.

Ah—the polyps. They are the ones who built the stage and then departed, extinct now, but present in every fistful of porous bedrock. Their billion skeletons fused into this karst city, their minute chambered apartments now housing frogs and crabs and the unseen pale things of the hydrology. They are the landlords and the law. Everything here—the strangler figs, the ten billion insects, the damp-muslin webs, the slow scissors of the snails—is a performative inhabitant of their ossuary. The plateau is not a place that *has* life; it is a life that has outlived its first name and continues under new management.

And so you realize, finally, that the resident is singular. It is a distributed being, ten-billion-mouthed, trillion-stomached, whose metabolism is the warm roar, whose skeleton is the reef, whose skin is the triple canopy and the dripping condensation and the perpetual saturated breath. It does not die because it does not end; it only hollows out at the center and builds anew at the edge, balanced by the perfect rate of destruction against the perfect rate of construction. It is the patient warmth, the broadband static, the breath of the breathing ground, the architectural green, and the endless patient accumulation of self upon self, calcium upon calcium, life upon life.
