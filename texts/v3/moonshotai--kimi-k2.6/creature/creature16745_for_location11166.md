If you sit on the rim and wait for movement, you will be disappointed. The life here does not arrive with a fanfare of fur or flight. It announces itself first as a disturbance in pattern: a fleck of silver where stone should be grey, a moss-hummock that has changed its contour overnight, a shadow traversing a vertical face that no shadow should be able to walk. The inhabitants of the Sunken Colonnade are not hidden so much as they are interpolated—worked into the jointwork and seepage so thoroughly that the eye passes over them as it passes over the columns themselves.

Begin with the water. Every silver thread that bleeds from the million cracks carries its own populace. Here lives what a geologist, prying with a blade, might call the **Seam-Fish**, though they are not remotely fish. They are small, isopod-like crustaceans, pale as pigeon milk, their bodies compressed to the thickness of a fingernail so they can press into joints no wider than a needle’s eye. Their carapaces are tiled in perfect hexagons and pentagons of matte charcoal, so that when they freeze—which they do at the slightest tremor—they become indistinguishable from the basalt grout. In motion they are quicksilver, spasming up the silver threads in quick, lateral flicks, grazing on the algal film that varnishes every wet surface. There are millions of them. In certain slants of noon light the walls appear to shiver, as though the stone itself were experiencing a local fever; this is only the massed, microscopic respiration of the Seam-Fish, inhaling dissolved stone, exhaling a slightly different chemistry. They are the Colonnade’s circulation.

Descend to the ledges, where the horizontal world begins, and you find the moss is not merely a carpet but a metropolis for the warm-blooded. The **shrews** here do not look like shrews. Their fur is so dense, so matted with living spores and peat filaments, that they have become ambulatory hummocks. A predator from the prairie above, leaning over the rim, would see nothing but vegetation in sluggish migration. They flow through the root-mat rather than running across it, swimming in the lattice, their bodies permanently wetted with the cold seep. When one dies—and they die often, their hearts hammering at the edge of endurance—it does not lie exposed. It sinks into the *Racomitrium* and is subsumed within a week, its proteins becoming a more nutritious hummock, its bones knitting into the peat. There are paths through the moss, but they are not visible from above; they are tunnels of compressed greenery, warm with fermentation, that smell faintly of iron and fur.

Then come the **frogs**, though they might better be described as fixtures. They are the color of cold bog-water and weathered scree. They subside into the pools until only their eyes and nostrils bisect the surface, periscopes of immense patience. Their eyelids are heavy, opalescent, and always half-drawn, as though the effort of seeing were too great to sustain. Their call—not a call, really, but a subaqueous exhalation—bubbles up through the mud without ever breaking the hush. Their spawn does not hatch into the exuberant tadpoles of warmer latitudes; instead, the eggs develop into segmented, worm-like larvae that burrow directly into the Sphagnum, digesting cellulose with bacteria pilfered from shrew dung. They bypass the open water entirely, as if they have agreed that the air is too thin, too loud, too exposed.

Among the column-flutes and curtains of basalt, the insects have adapted to the vertical city. **Crane flies** haunt the middle air with legs ten times the length of their bodies, spanning from ledge to ledge like living suspension bridges. They never land; the moss is too claiming, too final. They mate in mid-flight and die there, and their desiccated limbs rain down onto the hummocks in a dry, brittle drizzle. In the black pools, **beetles** navigate by a subaqueous clicking—an echolocation that maps the silt in pulses. Their carapaces are stained ferric red by the iron of the raw clay; against the black water they look like chips of old rust in motion.

The **swallows** that nest in the upper flutes have forgotten the sky beyond the rim. Their feathers have lost the waterproof oils of their open-country cousins, for here water is not an element to be repelled but the medium of breath itself. They are heavier birds than swallows ought to be, somber and deliberate. They do not sing. Their communication is percussive: the sound of their wingtips striking the hexagonal columns as they hunt the mist, producing a faint, rhythmic *clack—clack—clack* that echoes down the flutes like a message tapped through pipes. They build their nests of hardened spittle and wind-blown peat, cementing them inside the higher pentagons where the stone is dry enough to cradle young but wet enough to freeze a careless chick. They hatch in silence, fledge in silence, and when they launch themselves into the pale cone of noon, they trace the geometry of their home as though stitching the air to the stone.

At the waterline of the central marsh stand the **snipes**, patience incarnate. They have grown, in this damp crucible, to be nearly as patient as the stone itself. Their bills probe not merely the mud but the deep, anaerobic silt of the new sediment; their eyes are filmed with a nictitating membrane so thick it looks like cataract, for sight is less useful here than chemoreception. They stand with their heads down for hours, their bodies slowly rimed with algae and mineral bloom, until they resemble decoys abandoned by some ancient fowler. When they strike, it is with a swiftness that seems to offend the stillness; they pull from the muck something pale, writhing, and barely visible—a deep worm that has never known light, thin as wire, its body segmented with rings of translucent cartilage.

But these are only the citizens you might recognize. The Colonnade has bred, or attracted, stranger councilors. On afternoons when the fog rises in thick ropes and the floor becomes a cloud-chamber, you may see what appears to be a patch of dark velvet traversing a sheer wall sixty feet above the ground. This is the **Architect**, though it knows nothing of building. It is a myriapod-like creature, soft-bodied and elderly, three feet in length if it ever chose to straighten, which it does not. It moves by peristalsis through the hexagonal lattice, filling the negative spaces of the columns exactly, wearing a cloak of liverwort and *Racomitrium* that it farms on its own segmented back. It grazes the fern-fronds of the higher ledges, and where it passes it secretes a mucus that slightly accelerates the exfoliation of the basalt—not to destroy, but to cultivate. It is a geologic agent disguised as a gardener, its passage encouraging the thin, curved scales of stone to loosen and fall, providing new ledges for moss, new pools for seep. It has lived so long in the vertical that the concept of floor is meaningless to it; gravity is merely another surface to grip.

And in the very center, where the marsh is blackest and the sediment new enough to still smell of crushed earth and sulfur, there are beings that have not yet decided what they are. The floor of the Colonnade is still gestating its deepest residents. If you kneel on the iron-stained hummocks and part the sedge, you may see them: **provisional things**, translucent and fist-sized, drifting a hand’s breadth above the anaerobic muck. They are not quite animal, not quite colony. They possess folds and vents but no eyes, no mouth, no centralized will; they pulse with a shared circulation, bound in a shared integument, metabolizing the methane that seeps from the collapsed bedrock below. They are perhaps the larvae of some vast and distant adult form, or perhaps they are the adults themselves, simplified by the redundancy of the niche. They have no names because they have not yet committed to a shape. A single frost heave might kill them; a shift in the water table might force them to evolve, abruptly, into something with a spine, or a shell, or a voice. They are the Colonnade’s unresolved thought, the black water thinking about becoming life.

Yet all of these—the Seam-Fish in their million-fold mimicry, the moss-shrews in their green fermentation, the heavy swallows stitching the air, the velvet Architects in their slow peristalsis, the dreaming jellies of the center—are only the organs of a larger occupancy. The true resident has no skin, no footprint, no skull. It is the **Hush**—the listening that saturates the space. You felt it when you entered: the architectural silence, the dampened air, the sense that the quiet was not empty but massively, intently *full*. This is because the Colonnade itself has become a organism of attention, a distributed mind accreted from the absolute density of its own niche.

It did not arrive here; it emerged, the way a crystal emerges from solution when the conditions are exact. Every drop falling from a high seam to a mushroom cap is a synapse firing along a dendrite of stone. The fog that rises in ropes is its exhaled consideration. The frost that rimes the upper columns long after the prairie has thawed is a slow thought forming on the skin of the world. It does not command the creatures. It *is* them, in aggregate. The shrew threading through the root-mat is a thought about touch. The swallow clipping the basalt is a thought about flight. The beetle clicking in the silt is a thought about navigation in absolute dark. The provisional jellies in the marsh are its dreams—formless, warm, anaerobic, incomplete.

You do not meet this resident. You enter it. When you breathe the mist, you become, for a moment, a temporary lobe of its cognition, another wet presence in a space fully occupied by listening. When your boot crushes a hummock, somewhere in the geometry of the stone there is a dim, aqueous flicker, as though the Colonnade has noted an itch. It does not resent you. It is too busy, too saturated with its own cold becoming, to resent anything. But it records you. In the silver threads, in the subaqueous clicking, in the slow peristalsis of the velvet Architects, your presence is folded into the wet archive.

And when you climb back to the prairie, leaving the rim behind, you will carry some of that listening in your lungs. For a long time afterward, you will find yourself unable to raise your voice. You will understand that some places are not inhabited *in*, but inhabited *by*. The Sunken Colonnade is such a place: a city with no citizens, where every resident is a nerve ending in a single, stony, saturated mind—still cooling, still cracking, still dreaming its igneous dreams, one patient drop at a time.
