What lives here is a species of stillness.

They do not arrive in numbers. You will not find a village tucked behind a basalt extrusion, because a village would be a congregation of heat and carbon, and the swarm’s city-state would pour over it before morning, consuming the thatch and the hides and the bones. Instead, they are scattered like stray minerals—single figures, or at most a pair moving in absolute silences half a mile apart. They have made themselves granular. They have learned that to survive inside a drumbeat is to abandon the rhythm of wanting, of calling out, of running. They are low to the ground, compacted by the pressure of the noise and the proximity of stone, their joints adapted to angles that would cripple a surface-dweller. Their knees are wide and callused, their spines curved into the shape of a question that has accepted it will never be answered.

You will not see them until they allow it. Their skin—if it is skin—has become an exercise in mimicry. They coat themselves in the damp silts that gather in the cooler fissures, spitting into the paste until it adheres, then letting the mineral rain and the vent-heat bake it hard. Over days, or what passes for days beneath the shell, these layers accrete into a cracked integument of clay and mica and ash, so that a seated figure appears to be merely another basalt pillow extruding from the floor. The swarm, which treats all motion as a claim to resources, ignores them. They become topography. They become the geology’s dream of watching itself. When they must move, they do so in the pauses between the swarm’s tides, in the fractional hush after a steam explosion has deafened the east ridge and before the heat-worms have re-threaded the cracks. Their motion is not stealth but slowness—a limb lifted with the patience of crystal formation, a foot placed as if the floor were eggshell and thought were magma. The figure by the pool is typical. He or she—it is impossible to tell, and perhaps it does not matter—has knelt in that spot since the ridge rose, since the pool hissed open, since the swarm last crested. Knuckles like embedded fossils close around the hand-axe, and the motion of cracking the lithovore is older than language. It is the first technology performed in the first nursery. The shell above remembers it, and filters the light accordingly.

They are hairless, not by evolution but by necessity. Hair offers purchase; in the swarm’s dense precincts, a mane would be colonized, plucked, woven into the living storm by a thousand hooks of chitin. Their heads are smooth and low-browed, the scalp plated with a thickened dermis that has begun to resemble horn. Their eyes, which need to navigate the pale geometric panes of filtered daylight, are lidless in the way of creatures that cannot afford to blink at the wrong instant. Instead, a translucent nictitating membrane sweeps horizontally, wetting the eye with a mineral-tinted fluid that collects the dust of instant-fossils. They see less by color than by thermal gradient: the cool obsidian flows are midnight blues, the vents throbbing glands of white, and the swarm a smeared aurora of digestion and friction. They are nearly blind to the sky, which is just as well. The shell is their only heaven.

There is no speech among them. The ambient roar—the groaning of stretching stone, the clatter of armor, the hiss and percussive drip—allows no room for voice. They communicate through pressure and residue. A cairn of obsidian is not a monument but a sentence: *The vent here is young. Pass quickly.* A line of axes left beside a pool is a warning, or an offering, or a request that the next arrival sharpen the edges before the water turns to steam again. They trade in gestures so small they would look like twitches to you—a finger laid against the thigh, a chin dipped toward a cooling crack—signs passed in the instants when two of them meet at the edge of a flash-flood basin. They do not linger. A pair that remains together too long creates a disturbance in the swarm’s pattern, a pocket of absence that the insects learn to investigate. So they are profoundly solitary, each one a self-contained liturgy, repeating the gestures of survival without an audience.

They eat what the swarm provides. The lithovores, cracked open with those patient hands, reveal a meat that is gray and threaded with mineral veins, dense with the chemistry of nascent stone. It tastes of sulfur and copper. The heat-worms, when they can be plucked from a crack in the moment between the steam-burst and the retreat, are coiled and chewed for days. They do not cook; fire is redundant here, and smoke would advertise their presence like a shout. Their shelters, domed and woven from the shed carapaces of larger swarm-beasts, are not homes but sarcophagi for the living—temporary cocoons in which they sleep the shallow, vigilant sleep of prey that has forgotten how to dream. By dawn the ridge may have shifted, and they emerge into altered geometry, the shelter already half-buried by new precipitate, and they do not mourn. Attachment is a form of movement, and movement draws the swarm.

And yet they are not merely animals surviving the storm. The quality of their presence is stranger than that. They build. Not much, and not lastingly, but with an intention that separates them from the mere fauna. They understand leverage. They understand that a line of tension in dried gut can hold an obsidian blade to a shaft long enough to split a lithovore’s mantle. They understand that a cairn placed on a rise guides, and that the rise itself will not survive the week, so they must place it again, and again, a repeated futility that begins to look like devotion. They are teaching the stone, perhaps, or they are the only memory the place possesses. Everything else here is forward-driven: the swarm devouring the future, the geology erupting into the present, the flash-floods erasing the past in an hour. Only these figures look back. Only they carry the concept of *was* inside a world obsessed with *will be*.

You might ask what they are becoming. The answer is written in the instant-fossils that litter the gullies. Already their chemistry is shifting. Trace silica threads the papillar of their palms. Their bones are harder than they should be, taking up the minerals of the floor, accreting instead of merely growing. The children—there must be children, though they are kept so still, so quiet, bound tight against the adults’ torsos under mantles of clay—are born with a caul of geological paste already hardening. They enter the world already camouflaged. In a few generations, or perhaps already, the distinction between the built cairns and the bodies that build them will blur. They are being recruited into the nursery, absorbed into its project. Not as victims, but as witnesses. As the part that knows.

The swarm ignores them, until it doesn’t. And when a swarm-front shifts unexpectedly, when a flash-flood catches them between basins, when a vent opens beneath a sleeping carapace-dome, they die like everything else dies here: suddenly, and with immediate mineral consequence. Their bodies do not rot. There is nothing here to rot them. They desiccate, they silica-fill, they stiffen into postures of reaching or kneeling, and by the next cooling cycle they are already half-embedded in the floor. The geology dreams them into its strata. The cairns topple. The axes wait for hands that will not return, while the swarm chatters on, devouring the newborn world, and the diffuse light continues its ordinary passage overhead, patient and remembered, filling the vast geode with the illusion of sky.
