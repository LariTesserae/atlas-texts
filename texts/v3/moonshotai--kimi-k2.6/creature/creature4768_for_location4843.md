The fauna is overwhelming. It is the dominant fact of existence, a biological static that drowns out even the basin’s own cooling sighs. At the lowest tier, where the rubbery leaf-litter fuses into a living mattress, the substrate itself appears to move. It is not soil but a continuous, seething laminate of arthropods: isopods the size of thumbs, rolling into sulfur-yellow pills; mites like animated dust, their mouthparts forever grooming chemical wealth from the stinking air; and the larvae of things that have not yet been named, soft and bioluminescent in the infrared, writhing through the compost of shed chitin and digested pulp. They do not sleep. They have synchronized to the basin’s insomnia, their metabolisms cranked to match the geothermal tempo, generations turning over in hours, in minutes, adapting to the newness of the world with a speed that looks like panic.

Above them, the sound-makers. These are the engineers of the roar. There are the tymbal-beetles, each no larger than a knuckle, gathered in colonies that coat the cycad trunks like living bark. They contract their abdominal plates in waves, not to communicate but to vent excess heat, producing a drone that is merely thermoregulatory but collectively becomes a thunder. There are the glass-crabs—not true crabs, but arachnids with twelve legs and carapaces of translucent silica—that tap their forelimbs against the obsidian shards in endless, exploratory drumming. They are deaf; they feel the vibrations through their tarsi, mapping the sharp young terrain by sonar touch, and their percussion adds the metallic, ticking rain to the chorus. And there are the bellow-moths, if they are moths, with air sacs instead of wings, crawling through the understory on hooked legs, inflating and deflating thoracic bladders to create subsonic booms that stun the spore-flies they then lick from the air with barbed tongues.

The predators are quieter, which in this place is a greater terror. The silence of a hunter here is not stealth but a negative space carved out of noise. The largest is the lithid, a thing that might once have been a bear or a badger or something older, now pale and eyeless, its fur evolved into a dense mat of vibration-dampening bristles. It moves through the lung-thicket by freezing between the pulses of the tymbal-beetles, syncing its breath to the gaps in the drone. It has no eyes; it has a skull broad and flat as a shovel, lined with pressure sensors that can read a heartbeat through three feet of compressed vegetation. It eats the sound-makers by the hundreds, its jaws unhinging to scoop up colonies whole, its gut a furnace that can digest chitin and silica alike.

But the lithids are not the ones who have noticed the stones.

That honor belongs to something smaller, newer, more provisional. Call them the knappers. They are the size of large rats, though they share no lineage with anything mammalian. Their bodies are segmented, plated with flexible keratin, and they move in loose, squabbling troops through the lower canopy and across the razor-floor. They have too many limbs—six for locomotion, two smaller pairs folded beneath the thorax that end in delicate, three-jointed digits. They are nearly blind, navigating by a combination of scent-trails and the acoustic shadows cast by the basin’s roar. And they have discovered that the world has provided them with edges.

It began, perhaps, with scent-marking. A knapper, scraping its thorax against the conchoidal flank of a fresh basalt flake, found that the stone held the musk longer than bark. Then, a territorial dispute: a knapper struck another with an obsidian shard gripped in its minor hands, and the other bled, and the troop went silent in the way that attention manifests in deafening places. Now, the behavior has spread like a meme through the basin’s scattered troops. They collect the accidental blades. They do not make them—the geology has already done that—but they select for the sharpest, the most balanced, the ones that fit the grip of their minor hands. They carry them. They groom them with tongue and gland, coating the glass in a biofilm that prevents the brittle edges from shattering. They have begun to cut.

Not well. Not artfully. But they sever the tough thermotropic vines to clear paths through the lung-forest. They split open the waxy bulbs of the chemotropic plants to get at the fermenting sugars inside. They have learned to drive off the smaller glass-crabs by striking the obsidian against basalt, producing a shriek of frequency that disorients the arachnids’ sensitive feet. They are not intelligent, not yet, not in the way of fire and story. But they are on the cusp. They have entered the argument with the world, and the basin, which does not sleep, watches them with something like interest.

And then there are the depths. Below even the knappers, below the lithids, in the places where the fresh crust is thinnest and the magma still breathes through cracks like wounded lungs, there is the melt. It is not water. It is too hot for water. It is a soup of dissolved minerals and extremophile archaea, a primordial broth that should not exist in a world that already has complex multicellular life. Yet here it is, a competing genesis, bubbling in the dark. Things live in it. Things that are not yet cells but not quite chemistry. They are the basin’s own immune response, or its dream, or its next experiment. They climb the walls of the cracks in slow, accretive waves, building themselves into towers of living sediment that collapse and reform, that remember the shapes they have taken and try them again. They are learning to be solid. They are learning to be slow. They are the deepest inhabitants, and they have the most time.

The basin does not sleep. It is a nursery and a battlefield and a laboratory all fused into one, a place where the urgency of biology has been turned up until the dial breaks. Everything here is provisional. The plants are making up for the lack of light with greed for heat. The animals are making up for the lack of quiet with a roar that has become a medium. The stones are making up for the lack of hands by growing into the shapes of tools. And the knappers, warm and chittering in the dark, are making up for the lack of history by beginning one. They have not yet learned how to sleep. None of them have. There will be time for that later, when the basin cools, when the sound settles into rhythm, when the darkness learns to dim rather than obliterate. But for now, there is only the permanent present, the white-noise storm, the endless, urgent now of a place that has just been born and cannot bear to close its eyes.
