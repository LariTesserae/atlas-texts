What lives here is tenure. Not tenure as possession, but tenure as the long, grinding accommodation of a thing that has decided to stay. The great ones—they have no name in any language, though a traveler might call them lords or engines or sins—are old not in the way a mountain is old, but in the way a scream is old when it has been echoing so long it becomes the texture of the air. Their age is measured in the polish of their ball-joints, in the mirror-slickness of heartwood ground against heartwood for centuries until the fit is perfect and the motion is eternal. They stand kilometers apart not from scarcity but from dignity; each one is a continent of intent, a vertical refinery whose digestion of bedrock and ore is the only heartbeat in a world without pulse. They do not think. They do not rest. They settle.

To settle, for them, is not to stop moving. It is to find the posture from which all motion becomes inevitable. Their trunks articulate on hydraulic woody muscles, cambium cracking like ordnance as they swell and track the hard white sun with reflector arrays that tick and whir like the escapements of a god-sized clock. Where a wound opens in the petrified bark, it does not bleed water. It weeps a sap that runs thick and copper-green, heavy as mercury, freezing slowly in the permanent cool, and the cambium knits itself back together with the sound of a forge cooling. Deep in their cores, where oxygen fears to go, anoxic furnaces smelt the future from metallic slurry. They are pregnant with violence. They give birth to seeds forged in chemical fire and launched on cataracts of vented gas, and they do this not out of desire but because production is their nature. A thing that forges before it flowers does not know want. It knows only the next exhalation, the next compression, the next crucible.

Consider, then, the hosted life that clambers through this industrial anatomy. Consider the beetles first: enamel-shelled and fist-sized, they are not intruders but itinerant metallurgists, tending to the ribbed fluting of the trunks with the certainty of maintenance priests. They drink heat in their blood; they clean vents of slag with mandibular precision, and they lap at the copper-green sap until their own shells take on the galvanic blues and rust oranges of their diet. In the higher exhaust orifices, tube-worms of articulated chitin anchor themselves, filtering precipitated minerals from the vented gases, building their bodies directly from the tree’s exhalations. They are the filters and the scabs of the system, turning waste into wall, plugging the gaps where the organism meets the thin, hungry air.

Between the trunks, the mobile digestion wanders. Herds of ash-grey ungulates drift like low-pressure systems across the compacted fallout, their many stomachs fermenting shed bark-plates and compressed nodules into volatile grace. Along their spines, flash-pods ignite with a sound like tearing canvas, a defense against the pack-hunters that wheel in the thermal columns overhead. These hunters are thin as cracks in the sky, all pinion and talon and appetite, their vocal cords shaped into industrial sirens that announce the descent. Even the grazing is violent. Even the predation is productive. The ungulate that falls does not merely die; it compresses, its chemistry mingling with the metallic ash-fall, becoming the mineral substrate from which the taproots will draw tomorrow’s ore.

For here is the secret that emerges only after you have watched long enough: the separation between host and hosted, between furnace and flesh, between the rooted giant and the creature that laps its sap, is a fiction of scale. The beetle is the tree’s manicure. The tube-worm is its kidney. The ungulate, drifting and grazing and igniting its flash-pods, is merely the organism’s digestion made mobile, wandering out to process the raw and returning itself, in time, to the ash. The hunter is its culling hand. The roaring, grinding, stuttering weight that arrives before any particular form resolves from the white glare is not the environment. It is the respiration of a single distributed body that has learned to wear the shapes of many creatures. It does not think. It does not need to. It produces, it grinds, it outlasts. It is the tenancy of chaos, settled so thoroughly into its own violence that the violence has become its kind of sleep. And in that sleep, the grinding never stops; the ash never ceases to fall; the forge at the center of every trunk never cools. It only waits, with infinite patience, for the next minute to arrive.
