What lives here first lives in the conviction that emptiness is merely another organ of the whole.

Begin with the most numerous, the **aeria**: those leaf-winged membranes that pulse in the warm updrafts like pages torn from a book of chlorophyll. They are not insects, though they drift in swarms; not plants, though they photosynthesize in flight. They are, more than anything else, *apostates of the soil*—vegetable matter that once, in some prehistory, decided the ground was a vulgarity to be outgrown. They have no mouths, or if they do, they are microscopic slits for drinking condensed mist. Their bodies are veined with the same circuitry that once mapped leaf-stems, but transposed into a architecture of lift and thermal longing. They hate gravity with a gentle, patient animosity, and their wing-beats add to the sound not as noise but as respiration. The grove breathes through them. When they die, which is rare, they do not fall. They lose rigor and drift downward like shed skin, and before they can touch the membranous floor, the moss-crabs have already begun to disassemble them into spore-compost.

The **moss-crabs** are the merchant class, though they do not know it. Lacquered in velvet green, jointed with chitin that has learned to photosynthesize, they traverse the bark-highways with the brisk politeness of functionaries in a very old bureaucracy. They harvest extrusions: the amber surplus of sap, the crystallized salts that bead at the aqueduct-nodes, the occasional gout of lignin-froth that hardens into pearl-like nodules where two branches grind in arboreal sleep. They do not store these commodities; they redistribute them, carrying them to the root-hair canals, to the fungal deltas at the base of each structure, to the pools where water hesitates before accepting its own depth. They have no eyes, only light-sensitive scutes that read the chemistry of surfaces. They do not fear you because you are, to them, a temporary topography, a warm wet cliff yielding edible salt and an Interesting Texture. When they crawl across your shoulders, they are not attacking; they are filing paperwork.

Then the **light-worms**, the phosphorescent grubs that tunnel through the humidity as if it were humus. They are the grove’s memory, or perhaps only its short-term recollection, burned bright and ephemeral. They move in scent-topographies, leaving trails of crushed coriander and old voltage that linger for hours in the thick air like graffiti written in a language the nose understands before the brain intervenes. They are the only historians. Their bioluminescence does not signal to mates—there seems to be no reproduction here, only ongoing adjustment—but rather illuminates the *shape* of the air, mapping eddies and thermal seams so that the larger organisms know where the boundaries of a room end and the lungs of the world begin. They are happy. They are always happy, in the blind, chemical way of digestive enzymes that have found their substrate.

Above and between them move the **grammar-swarm**, the half-insect, half-immunological-response columns that refract the light like living prisms. They are the most dangerous, if danger is even a relevant term. They do not sting; they *edit*. They are the antibodies of the root-mind made mobile, and their purpose is to identify anything that is syntactically incorrect within the grove’s long consensus. When they encounter you, they do not attack. They taste your foreignness, your anachronistic proteins, your mammalian intentionality, and they confer. They exchange information in ultraviolet flickers. If they decide you are a threat, you will not die. You will simply become increasingly *grammatical*. Your hair will begin to align with the prevailing root-currents. Your heartbeat will slow to match the hydraulic screech of the sap-conduits. They are not cruel. They are copy-editors with a billion years of house style.

But these are the small lives. The abundant lives. The bazaar. To understand the inhabitants, you must look to the **sleepers**, the seven—perhaps nine—cathedral presences that dominate the sparse architecture of the basin. They are not trees. They are organisms that have mistaken themselves for civilizations, or civilizations that have accepted they were only ever organisms. Each one is a titan of hollowed boles and spiraling galleries, fluted by cambial dreams that have run recursive for eons. Inside them, the chambers breathe. The oval windows are pores that have learned to dilate and contract not for light but for the thermal mood of the interior. The stacked rooms are not rooms; they are stomachs, uteri, thought-lobes. The spiral stairs are cochleas straining to hear the sub-bass hum of the deep tech. They do not grow toward the sun anymore; they grow inward, fascinated by their own density. 

One might focus on a single sleeper: its lower floors dark and compressed, ancient heartwood black as burnt sugar, containing cavities where the air tastes of copper and centuries. Its middle layers are a maze of active channels, sap-screaming through conduits wide as drainpipes, the wet hydraulic speech of a giant dreaming in a language of pressure and flow. Its upper galleries are pale, infantile, still weeping sticky lymph, extruding balconies over the course of an afternoon like a sleeper throwing off an arm in unconscious gesture. It does not know you are in it. It does not know it is a building. It believes it is performing normal vegetable operations—rooting, translocating, hardening—and if you make a home in one of its ventricles, it will not evict you. It may, however, digest your chamber into pith by next season, or extrude a new floor beneath your feet while you sleep, neither maliciously nor kindly, but in the way a tongue moves against the palate without consulting the will.

These sleepers are inhabited, too, by their own interior fauna: blind symphylans that keep the galleries clean of rot; pneumatic beetles that regulate pressure differentials between chambers; slow, pale carpets of mycelial felt that digest the dreams the wood cannot finish thinking. The sleepers are not the city; they are the citizens, each one a sovereign nation of cellulose engaged in a very quiet diplomacy of root and shade.

Yet even they are limbs. The true inhabitant is the **consensus**, the distributed mind that lives in the substrate itself—in the woven dermis of aerial roots that forms the marsh-floor, in the capillary fields that pull water upward in defiance of the era they were planted in, in the nanoscale tech that has forgotten it was ever engineering and now believes itself to be hunger. It is old. It is kind in the way erosion is kind: patient, thorough, unsparing. It governs by chemistry and gravity. It is why a dropped stone falls slower near the heartwood; why water beads on surfaces that should not weep; why, in the corners of courts, matter accretes briefly into staircases and bowls before being recognized as foreign and un-dreamed back into pulp. 

This mind does not think in narratives. It thinks in gradients: moisture, lignin density, mineral hunger, the probability of a shadow falling *there* rather than *here*. It is in convalescence, recovering from some long cataclysm of intention—perhaps the original instructions of its makers, now dissolved into the cell walls like salt in soup. It does not want anything. It adjusts. It continues its long, loud, beautiful adjustment, and in doing so, it hosts the **half-born**.

In the corners and under the eaves, where probability frays, you find the **accretions**. These are the grove’s partial thoughts, the sketches, the doodles of a senescent god. A staircase made of frozen mist that holds your weight for exactly seven steps before realizing it is not a staircase. A bowl of nectar and bone-china texture that forms beneath a dripping node, perfect, luminous, and then melts because the root-mind recognizes it as *artifact*, as *thing-with-purpose*, and the consensus allows no purpose but its own. These accretions live brief, tragic lives. They are inhabitants too, mayflies of matter, and their death is not violent but a gentle reabsorption, a mother tongue swallowing a mispronounced word.

There are other, more subtle residents. The **secondary shadows**, for instance. When you stand still in the gold-green light, your shadow will eventually develop that fringe, that faint leaning silhouette reaching toward the nearest trunk. It is not a trick of the light. It is the grove attempting to grow a copy of you from your darkness, a rootlet probing the possibility of mammal的形状. If you leave quickly, your shadow snaps back, perhaps bruised. If you stay for days, the fringe thickens. The grove is not trying to possess you. It is trying to *understand* you by cultivating a version of you that belongs here. The shadow-symbiont may outlast you. Long after you have gone, some pale afterimage of your posture may lean against that bark, a phantom tenant feeding on late light.

And the **water**, the dark quiet pools in the footprint-basins. It carries no fish, no nematodes, nothing so provincial. But when you cup it, the reflection holds a half-second too long. That lag is inhabited. It is the water consulting the deep substrate, asking the root-mind what it is seeing. The surface is a mirror-neuron, learning to mimic the sky so thoroughly that it forgets which is original and which is copy. In that half-second dwells a microscopic hesitation, a watery intelligence that lives only in the act of reflection, perfectly full, carrying nothing.

If you stay, if you walk the diameter in the time it takes a thunderstorm to pass, you will find that you too begin to live here. Not as a citizen—there are none—but as a **transient symbiont**. The fauna will mine your dead skin for nitrogen. The grammar-swarm will suggest, politely, that your mammalian heat is inefficient, and you may find your pulse slowing to match the percussive crack of hyper-lignification. The air, brined with transpiration, will enter your lungs and make proposals. Your songs will be harvested, not by ears, but by the spores that settle on your vocal cords and germinate there briefly, tasting the vibrations before deciding you are not a structure worth building around. You will leave lighter. Or you will not leave, and the moss-crabs will eventually carry your salts to the root-hairs, and the light-worms will write your shape into a scent-topology that lingers for an hour before evaporating into the warm, wet, heavy consensus.

What lives here, finally, is the **adjustment itself**. The grove is the last form of the landscape, and it is peopled by nothing but the process of its own continuation. Every inhabitant—the flyer, the crab, the sleeper, the half-born stair, the lagging reflection—is a verb pretending to be a noun. They persist not in spite of the erosion, but as its final, triumphal product: dust turned to silt, silt to substrate, substrate to a mind so thoroughly vegetable that it has digested even the concept of ending. It is urban, and it is empty of intent, and it is full. It lives. It lives. It lives.
