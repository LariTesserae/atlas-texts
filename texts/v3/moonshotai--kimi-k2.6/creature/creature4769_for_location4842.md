The first thing to understand is that no one lives *in* the Infant Spine. They live in the hesitation. They inhabit the moment between the mountain’s inhalation and exhalation, in the decision the stone has not yet finalized about whether it will suffer occupation. You will not find crowds here. You will find *presences*, sparse as the lights, each one having negotiated a separate truce with the rawness of the place.

At the railhead, you meet the **Commuters**. Not travelers—there is nowhere to travel to—but transit-dwellers for whom the aerial tramways are permanent address. They are thin in a way that has nothing to do with hunger. Their bodies have been winnowed by altitude and implication, stripped of everything superfluous until they are little more than intention wrapped in oilcloth. Their lungs are wide and patient, bellows adapted to sipping oxygen from the thin, mineral air. They wear condensation rather than letting it bead and roll; their coats, black or oxidized green, seem permanently wet, as though they have learned to hold the damp against their skin for comfort. They do not blink often. The tram operators, especially—the ones who tend the sagging cables between nodes—have eyes that reflect the violet of the reality-dampeners, not with any mechanical augmentation, but because they have stared so long into the fog that their retinas have begun to store the color. They speak in the pauses between the cable-song and the groan of the crust, pitching their voices to frequencies that compete with the industrial drone. They know the exact tensile strength of silence. When a tram arrives at a platform, they do not step out so much as *resolve* onto the decking, having spent so long in the mist between points that their location has become a matter of consensus rather than fact.

Then there are the **Refiners**, the forge-folk, and they are something else entirely. If the tram-dwellers have learned to live with uncertainty, the Refiners have armored themselves against it. They work the foundry-cathedrals where the pre-ore is convinced into certainty, and the work has made them heavy. Not fat—*dense*. They move as if gravity has taken a special interest in them. Their shoulders are bowed not by labor alone but by the accumulated weight of extracted inevitability. They wear leather aprons over leather aprons, riveted with copper discs that bear the serial numbers of the batches they have certified. Their skin is the color of forge-red held too long against flesh; their palms are hairline-cracked maps of every certainty they have hammered into being. They speak slowly, in complete declarative sentences, because half-measures have been burned out of them. A Refiner does not say, “I think it might rain.” A Refiner says, “The moisture will fall,” and the moisture, hearing the authority in the voice, often complies. They are the anchors of the Infant Spine, the human counterweight to the mountains’ indecision. They eat food that has been cooked twice—once by fire, once by declaration—to ensure it knows what it is. They are not unkind, but they are *finished*, and they look upon the unfinished world around them with the exhausted patience of midwives attending a birth that has lasted generations.

Between the fixed nodes of transit and forge, you find the **Dampkeepers**. These are the ones who tend the hanging gardens, who harvest the ferns that photosynthesize violet light, who coax lichens out of the expansion joints. They are soft where the Refiners are hard, loose where the tram operators are taut. They wear moss rather than clothing; not as a fashion, but because the moss colonizes everything they own and they have learned to treat it as textile. Their hands are stained chlorophyll-black, and their fingernails grow in spirals because the geometry of local space has gently persuaded them that straight lines are merely a suggestion. They collect the upward-running water in blown-glass bulbs and use it to brew teas that taste of elsewhere—of lowland meadows and tidal flats, places the drinker may never have seen but remembers with the specificity of a dream inherited from a parent. The Dampkeepers are the most numerous, if such a word applies here, and the most provisional. They do not live in the habitation blocks stacked like ingots; they live in the *between* of those blocks, in the cavities where condensation pools, in the maintenance ducts that serve as aqueducts. They are the ones who have accepted that the Infant Spine is not a place to be conquered but a humidity to be inhabited. They speak to the null-bears—not in words, but in the language of thermal radiation and peripheral attention. They leave kettles steaming on exterior platforms not as offerings, but as waypoints, so the great adjacent beasts can navigate the complexes without stepping too fully into human-committed space.

For the **null-bears** are inhabitants too. Do not mistake them for wildlife. They are not predators, not prey, not accidents of evolution. They are the original commuters, native to the reality breakdowns long before the first rivet was heated. They exist in the superposition of warm and not-warm, here and nearly-here. A null-bear does not enter a room; it notices that it has always been in the room, and reality adjusts. The inhabitants have learned not to fear them but to *route around* them, the way water routes around a stone. Children—those rare, improbable births that occur only in the deepest damp, where biology has agreed to tentatively commit to its own continuation—are taught from infancy that a null-bear in your peripheral vision is merely the mountain dreaming with its eyes open. The bears leave pawprints that age backwards, yes, but they also leave *warmth*. In a place of endless twilight and industrial chill, that warmth is currency. The oldest Dampkeepers know how to read the bears’ passage not as threat, but as weather: a warm front moving through causality, bringing with it a season of softened edges.

There are others. The **Breach-Wardens**, who tend the electrified fences around the reality breakdowns. They are scarred in topologies that do not map to skin. One might have a wound that exists only when viewed from the left, or a tattoo that migrates across the body depending on the observer’s mood. They carry tuning forks instead of weapons, striking them against the railheads to sound out the local stability. They wear null-bear fur, not as trophy but as camouflage, cloaks that render them adjacent to danger without being part of it. They are the loneliest, for they stand at the edges of the possible and listen to the stone argue with itself about what it will become.

And deeper still, in the chasms where the light is *unfinished*, there are the **Uncommitted**. These are the ones who stepped into a reality-breach and did not step back, or who did step back but at an angle. They live in the pre-geology. You might see them in the fracture planes, standing upright on vertical walls, not defying gravity but existing in a local consensus where “down” is still under review. They are not ghosts. They are colonists of the possible, citizens of the mountain’s unmade future. Sometimes they send messages upward—crystals that grow in the foundry vents bearing faces that almost belong to people, sounds in the steam pipes that almost resolve into voices. They do not die, exactly, because death requires a solid commitment to a single biological outcome. They simply become less probable, fading into the superposition until they are merely a likelihood, a warmth in the stone, a sense that someone is standing just behind the mountain’s shoulder.

What binds them all is a relationship to time measured not in hours but in *solidification*. A romance in the Infant Spine unfolds across years, because a touch must first negotiate the certainty of skin. A meal is an act of persuasion, convincing the food to nourish. A building is not constructed but *cultivated*, scaffolded against the possibility of its own collapse until the collapse agrees to wait. The inhabitants do not age so much as they *accrete*, layer by layer, decision by decision, becoming more themselves with the same patient inevitability that the mountains use to become stone.

If you stay—and the staying is difficult, for your lungs must learn to breathe implication and your bones must agree to carry you even when local gravity is indecisive—you will change. You will begin to leave two sets of footprints. You will find yourself carrying an umbrella for rain that fell yesterday. You will develop a preference for the forge-red spectrum, finding daylight garish and overcommitted. You will learn to stand very still when a null-bear occupies the space you were about to step into, and you will apologize not to the bear, but to the space, for the inconvenience.

You will become, in other words, a local expression of the Spine itself: provisional, patient, and dense with unrealized form. You will not belong to the city. The city, inasmuch as it is a city, will belong to the same uncertainty you do, and together you will wait for the world to finish compiling itself, breathing in the ozone, wrapped in damp oilcloth, holding your shape by choice rather than by law.
