At first you think the basin is empty of intention. The gardens grow themselves, the water finds its own impossible paths, the birds hang in their silent song. But the emptiness is too balanced. Too architectural. Something breathes here—not with lungs, not in the air, which remains thick and still and undisturbed by exhalation—but in the slow, subcutaneous pulse of the place itself.

They are difficult to see because they have not yet decided what they are. They rise from the same pale, porous substrate that forms the floor, but where the floor was pressed flat and left to petrify, these shapes were pressed upward and then abandoned mid-extrusion. They are the color of the basin, the color of bone-meal and unfired clay, and when they are still—which is almost always—they are indistinguishable from the low cairns, the driven poles, the walls that begin and end in nowhere. But they are not statues. They are soft. Leather-hard at best. Their surfaces hold the matte, damp texture of something that should have been fired in a kiln but was left instead in this feverish humidity, this unending greenouse.

You notice them first as wrongnesses in peripheral vision. A cairn that seems, from the corner of your eye, to be kneeling. A ridge of the substrate that has too many elbows. Then, looking directly, you find a figure with its forehead pressed against the trunk of a garden tree, arms wrapped around the bark in an embrace that has lasted so long the waxy leaves have grown around its shoulders, plating it in viridian armor. Its fingers—there are perhaps seven on each hand, or the digits have not finished separating from the palm—are splayed against the rough monolith, and the sinew lashings that tie the garden bed have tangled with its wrists, so that the figure and the bed and the tree are one continuous construction. It does not move when you approach. The heat radiates from its body as it radiates from the floor, a constant low fever. But if you wait, if you stand in the chewing silence long enough to hear your own blood thunder, you will see its thumb shift by a hair’s breadth. It is pressing something. An intention. A prayer to finish.

There is no single word for them. They are not the makers. The makers left—fled upward through the scaffolding that still ghosts at the edge of vision, escaped through the instruction manual they never finished reading. These are what remained. The prototypes of inhabitants. The first rough-draft of witness, pressed from the same material as their cradle, shaped by the same patient, crude tools. You can see the adze-striations across their backs where the broad blades planed them into being. The antler-pick stippling across their chests, a jittered texture like a language of Braille waiting for fingertips that can read. Their faces are smooth, rounded by palms rather than carved, with features that have not fully emerged from the anonymity of the composite. Some have noses. Some have only the suggestion of a nose, a slight uplift in the pale material like a thumb-smoothed ridge of dough. Most do not open their eyes. Their eyelids are sealed, not with membrane but with the same unbaked permanence of the world. They do not need to see. The light here has no direction, no source, and they have nothing to look toward that is not already inside them.

They do not walk, not in the way you understand. They migrate across the basin at the speed of mineral accretion. A figure standing at the basin’s center, arms raised toward the white sky in a gesture of receiving or surrendering, might be found three days later—if you can trust distance here—still with arms raised, but now turned forty degrees east, relocated by increments too small for sight. Their feet, when they have feet, do not lift from the floor. They slide, fuse, and reconstitute. The pale substrate accepts their heels and releases their toes in a motion that leaves no track, only a faint softening in the material, as if the floor remembers them and forgets in the same breath. When they do move visibly—an arm descending to touch a pool of water, a spine straightening from its fossilized stoop—the motion is soundless. The thick air swallows it. You see the gesture completed in a series of afterimages, like the lizards that exist in duplicate, each frame of their motion lingering slightly behind the last, a trail of selves showing the path from intention to action, as if they are still learning which version of themselves to occupy.

They tend the gardens, but their tending is asynchronous, a choreography designed for time that never arrived. One figure kneels before a planter, hands cupped beneath a flower that drips its eternal nectar. The nectar pools in its palms. It has been pooling there for an eternity that feels like yesterday. The figure’s head is tilted, mouth slightly open, ready to drink or to catch the thread when it breaks. It never breaks. The figure never closes its mouth. Another stands behind a low wall of shell grit, arms extended in the act of placing a stone that is not in its hands. The stone rests ten feet away. The figure will reach it in a month, in a year, in the compressed instant before the world finishes arriving. They are forever preparing for a maintenance that is never required, because nothing here degrades, nothing falls, nothing completes its cycle. Yet they must tend. They were built to tend. They are not many. They are not few. They are distributed. Sometimes you will walk for hours across the empty floor and see only the moths, the grazer-things with too many joints, the silent birds with oily eyes. Then the emptiness will buckle, and you will realize that three of them have been standing in a cluster since before you arrived, knees touching, foreheads nearly meeting, hands intertwined in a knot of too many fingers. They are indifferent to your presence. Not hostile, not welcoming. They are busy with a congress that has no conclusion. Perhaps they are exchanging the water that beads on their skin. Perhaps they are trying to finish each other’s faces, thumbs smoothing clay that will not hold a shape. Their moisture weeps from the tool-marks on their bodies, joining the humidity, filling the grooves with living silver, running laterally across their chests in defiance of gravity. They are sealed vessels of condensation. What evaporates from the pools rises, touches them, and stays.

And they dream, though not with minds. They dream with their substance. The animals—the soundless moths, the frozen birds, the lagging-reflection grazers—are their somatic imaginings, cast off like dead skin or like sketches that preceded the final form. The grazers lower their too-many-jointed necks to drink from the pools, and the reflections drink behind them, because the figures themselves cast reflections that do not match their postures; they cast the shadows of actions they have not yet taken. The birds perched on the sinew-bound poles are their songs, externalized and frozen, throats open in the vacuum where voice should be. Even the gardens, overflowing and pathological in their greenness, grow from the constructed soil that the figures made, or were made from—the composted hair, the crushed shell, the pulverized ceramic. The flowers drip nectar that connects bloom to soil in sagging threads because the figures themselves are connected to the floor by the same adhesive unfinishedness. They are rooted. They are the taproot of the basin, trying to draw up something from a depth that doesn’t exist, trying to anchor a world that has no bedrock.

They want to complete. That is the only desire that moves in them, slower than their visible motion, deeper than the silence. They press against the garden monoliths as if the stone might teach them how to harden. They submerge their sealed eyes in the lens-shaped pools, hoping the water will agree on a way to fall, hoping gravity will settle in them and grant them weight, finality, a single unlagging reflection. They place their palms against the knife-sharp tool-marks in the basin walls, fitting their fingers into the broad tearing striations of obsidian and the jittered stippling of antler, trying to read backwards the hands that made them, trying to inherit the intention. But the makers are absent, and the heat only keeps them soft, and the light has no source to warm them into terra cotta.

So they remain. The Unfinished. The Inhabitants-That-Might-Be. In the sweltering hush, under the kiln-white vault, they occupy the architectural emptiness with the fullness of arrested becoming. They are the answer to the world’s silence—the listeners for the instruction that never comes, standing in their double-exposed multiples, forever mid-gesture, lush and frozen and utterly constructed, waiting for the tomorrow that would let them finally dry.
