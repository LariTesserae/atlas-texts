At first, you mistake the inhabitants for background. The stridulators, for instance—the grasshopper-kin—seem like ambience, a living texture of the air. But they are not background any more than breath is background to a lung. Each is small enough to rest in a curled palm, pale and cave-waxed, wingless in the way of things that have never needed the sky. They have no ears; the architecture would be redundant. Their entire bodies are tympanums. Every flex of thorax against abdomen scrapes a microscopic file, and from ten billion of these frictions the pressure emerges that you move through, that pushes against your eardrums, that becomes the medium itself. They do not sing to one another. They sing because to sing is to breathe, and to breathe is to exist. They are born mid-chirp, and when they die they fall into the porphyry still vibrating, their minute husk instantly replaced, the note held without a gap across a thousand million mouths. In their anatomy, silence is a cavity that has no name.

But they are only the voice. Beneath their roar moves the jaw.

The millipedes are the teeth of this place. Wrist-thick, warm to the touch, their segments banded with ochre, rust, and the pale mineral dust of their meals, they do not travel across the world so much as digest it from within. They are the reason the bio-porphyry is smooth, maternal, worn into flowing hills and bell-shaped valleys. Their thousands of legs do not walk; they abrade. They ingest the compacted chitin of ancestral hives, the ossified droppings of extinct swarms, the calcified secretions of structures built long before their own kind emerged. They are covered not in bristles but in a fine, fur-like down that traps spores, so that wherever they grind, they plant even as they consume. They communicate by grit, exchanging particles of stone and bone through antennal contact, tasting the history of the route the other has eaten. They are the slow rivers that carved the gullies—not with water, but with the acid of their passage and the endless, whispering erosion of their locomotion. They are the digestion of history, and the world yields beneath them because they have eaten the sharpness away.

Where the millipedes move, the moths cling. They are the stillness, the counterweight. Their bodies are heavy, furry, swollen to the size of a fist, colored in the dusty white and grave-gray of organisms that have never known the sun. They do not fly often; flight is a profligate expenditure in a world where nutrition drips from every surface and the air itself is thick with broth. They hang in velvety curtains from the bio-porphyry, wings folded like the sodden pages of a drowned book, antennae twitching in a tongue-like tasting of the chemical language. They have no use for eyes. They read the world by scent: the nitrogen spike of a collapsing larva, the pheromone bloom of an approaching swarm, the sour warning of a pool turning acidic. They are the librarians of the dark, cataloguing the chronicle of the damp in their motionless, quivering way. When they must move, they drop with a sound like wet fabric tearing, heavy, graceless, startling, only to find a new fold and cling again, translating the dark into readable chemistry. They are the place’s way of paying attention.

In the blood-warm pools live the masons. They are soft, pale, unarmored—larval things, shrimp-blind, that secrete from their abdomens a silk finer than any thread. They gather the debris of death around them: the hollowed heads of mites, flakes of calcified droppings, slivers of black leaf-matter, even the dried husks of their own prior moltings. They bind this detritus into cases that are at once coffin, fortress, and involuntary art. They drag these cylindrical homes across the sedimented floors, and when they pupate, they do not emerge. They die in their cases, as all their kind have died for ages uncounted, and the cases remain. Generation upon generation, century upon century, these silk-bound corpses accumulate into dams, into weirs, into the ledges that cup the warm water and alter the vertical geography of condensation. They are the only inhabitants who shape the world with something resembling intent, yet they possess no minds. Their architecture is a secretion of the gut, as automatic as sweat, as inevitable as the hardening of arteries. They are the bridge between water and stone, and in death they achieve the permanence their soft, blind bodies could never claim in life.

The air between the Roof and the floor is not empty. In the thick soup of the upper gloom move the darting ones. You never see them fully. They are negative space, shadows that eclipse the swarms of gnats, bodies heavier than birds but without the architecture of feathers or fur. They are not bats, though they navigate by screaming. They are not insects, though they are jointed in ways that hurt the eye. They emit a cry—a sharp click, a needle of anti-silence punched through the roar—that lets them see by the rebound. They are fast in a world that worships slowness. They are the only ones who seem to remember urgency. Perhaps they eat the stridulators by the mouthful; perhaps they harvest the moth-curtains; perhaps they do neither, and sustain themselves on the spore-thick air itself. When one passes near, the air pressure dips, and for a half-second the deafening chorus falters, and in that gap you hear the true depth of the darkness underneath. They are the holes in the sound, and the sound rushes to fill them.

Then there are the surgeons. The parasitic wasps. In a world so bloated, so drowning in excess, so overfull of soft bodies and unguarded eggs, they are the agents of precision. They are small—some no larger than a grain of rice, others long as a finger—but they are the only things here with edges. Their bodies are amber and black, glassy, exact as obsidian scalpels. They do not fight; they operate. They find the larvae of others by following vibrations through the bio-porphyry, tracking the heat-signature of a pupa, tasting the chemical signature of distress in the damp. They inject. They lay. They alter. They are the immune system of the whole, keeping any one species from overrunning the rest, enforcing a brutal arithmetic upon the abundance. Without them, the pools would be entirely mite. Without them, the millipedes would cover every inch in a living carpet. They are the sting that reminds the fullness it is mortal.

But these—all of these—are merely the organs. The skin. The microbiome of something larger.

You begin to understand this only when you stand still long enough to feel the floor breathe. The warmth that rises is not merely geothermal. It is the exhalation of a body so vast it has replaced the mountain range beneath you. It fills the voids under the porphyry, a mycelial or vascular enormity—neither plant nor animal nor fungus in any taxonomy you possess—a network of warm tissue digesting the last vestiges of bedrock far below, turning stone into sugar, exhaling nitrogen and heat. It does not think. It metabolizes. And in metabolizing, it dreams architecture. The pillars that brace the Roof are its fingers, extruded and abandoned to mineralize. The gullies are its wrinkles. The subsonic hum you feel in your sternum is not the earth; it is the peristalsis of this sleeper, the slow churn of its billion-year gut. The stridulators feed upon its excretions. The millipedes graze upon its hardened memories. The moths taste its chemical moods. It is not the setting. It is the first inhabitant, and all others are its guests, its symbionts, its unconscious thoughts made mobile.

And above it all, the eldest. The Roof. It began as uncountable colonial organisms—roots, leaves, fiber-forming invertebrates—climbing toward a sun that proved insufficient. When the light failed, they did not wither. They turned inward. They wove themselves into a ceiling, a continent of fused cellulose and petrified sap, a lattice of matter that forgot its origin. They are still alive. They drip. They sweat. They catch the wandering photons that stray through some distant crack, and they convert them not into growth upward but into growth inward, adding layer upon layer to the underside of their own sky. They do not know they are a roof. They believe—if belief is possible here—that they are still reaching. They are the final inhabitant, the ceiling that dreams it is a floor, the sky that became a bed, sleeping with its eyes open, dripping its condensed patience onto the world below.

So. Who lives here?

A chorus that has never stopped singing. A set of teeth that eats history. A curtain of stillness reading the dark. A soft builder that mortars its own bones into walls. A hole torn through the noise. A needle that enforces death upon life. A warm, dreaming gut that replaced the stone. And a roof that forgot the sun.

They do not live beside one another. They live inside one another. The distinction between creature and place was swallowed long ago, and what remains is a single, patient, roaring organism—old, full, loud, dark, and forever.
