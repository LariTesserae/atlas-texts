The ones who live here do not know they are tenants. They arrived long after the blueprint had calcified, settling into walls that were already older than the mountains that ring the basin. The *Sedimenters*—or the thing the *Sedimenters* became—are not gone so much as translated. If you could press your palm flat against the warm, nacre-smooth flank of a tower and hold it there for a century, you might feel what remains: a seepage, a mineral exhalation indistinguishable from old metabolism. The calcium still sweats. The hexagons still accrete at a rate below the threshold of biology. The colony is dead in the way a forest floor is dead beneath a living canopy; the organism has spread itself so thin through the infrastructure that its distributed body is now the neighborhood itself. It does not grow anymore, but it approves. The current tenants feel this in their lateral lines and antennae, a faint calcium discipline, an architectural bias toward the communal. They house themselves in tubes not their own, and in doing so, they continue the project without intention.

Begin, as everything here begins, with the fall. The snow descends forever from a surface none of them will ever see, a rain of particulate grease and broken organics, so thoroughly scavenged in its downward passage that by the time it reaches the lower wards it is a currency nearly spent. The microbes own this layer. They are the bankers of the basin, numbering in the decillions, holding the chemical contract that makes the warm dark profitable. In their concourse, no molecule passes untaxed. They colonize every flake, every dying cell, every dissolved whisper of rot, turning it through their guts and exhaling it as a new precipitate to fall again. The water does not nourish; it is *negotiated*, a broth kept at a slow simmer by the same deep seams that warm the towers from below. They are the foundation. Without them, the stone would starve.

Then the *Clickers*. They are the noise made flesh, the percussion section of the boroughs. Armored in manganese-dark scutes that drink the last photons and give nothing back, they colonize internal nooks in clattering aggregations thousands strong. Their society is one of vibration. They do not speak so much as rattle, joint against joint, carapace against hexagonal sill, a syncopation that fills the auditory void with the sound of sleet on heated slate. In absolute dark, each individual is a tiny acoustic beacon, and together they form a rattling topography, a map you could read with your skin if you shared their patience. They are scavengers, opportunists, the unemployed labor pool of the city, always between gigs. They are also the only ones who seem to remember that the upper world exists; they orient themselves to the faint pressure differential above the highest spires, clustering where the warm currents buffet, waiting for the next dead thing to descend into their clattering maw.

Against this noise, the isopods move in sheets. Thumb-sized, matte and rust-colored, they are the masonry restoration society, the wallpaper and the maintenance crew. You hear them before you feel them: a dry, mandibular whisper as ten thousand mouths plane the facing walls, each bite removing a micrometer of nacreous history. They leave the calcium smoother than they found it. Their antennae map every hexagonal pixel, every softened balcony edge, every drift of pearl-grey sand that has filled a lower avenue to the depth of a grave. They do not hunt. They graze. They are the city's slow appetite turned inward, digesting its own architecture at the same rate the Sedimenters once built it. In mass, they are a rustling carpet, a living textile deployed across vertical surfaces. When a shadow too large and too silent drifts up the corridor, they do not scatter; they pause, a held breath in chitin, and resume only when the displaced water stills again.

From the portholes and the windows, the filter-worms extend their fans. They are the curtains, the gills, the wide-eyed retirees of the metropolis. Each plume is a delicate inversion of the darkness, a negative space scored with ciliated threads that strain the microbial snow. They are sessile citizens, rooted in the wall-flesh, and their rhythm is the basin’s own: feed, retract, feed again. When the globes pass, they vanish like startled thoughts, snapping back into their calcified sleeves, leaving only the trace of a current-kink where their feathery inquiry had been. They own the middle spaces, the interior rooms, the pneumatic gaps between the living and the stone. In their way, they are the most architectural of the survivors, because they cannot leave. The city must continue to pass water for them to continue to be.

The ribbon-eels own the heights and the depths, the vertical transit. They are the memory of routes, winding through hexagonal windows with the languid entitlement of long habitation. Their bodies phosphoresce in a spectrum no resident eye can read—an infrared love-language, a courtship of warmth spoken in gradients only thermoreceptors might parse. They move without haste because haste is a luxury of the illuminated; here, time is measured in strata of occupation. Each eel seems to hold a homestead in its sinuous length, returning to the same spire-apartment, the same cul-de-sac, the same blind corner where two currents marry. They never hunt in haste. They are not cruel; they are simply certain. Their prey feels a gradual encirclement, a warming as the broad flank passes, and then the event, soft and compressive, ending in a corridor gone momentarily empty.

Above them, in the open avenues of the water-column, drift the globular predators. To call them lung-like is too innocent; they are living bellows, pulsing through the streets on silent jets, following a schedule older than the fauna that dreads them. They are the public transit of death, arriving with the regularity of a tide made flesh. Their bodies are pale, translucent things—perhaps faintly opalescent in the absolute dark, though none could say—and they hunt by displacement, by the subtle rearrangement of pressure in a space that has no other vacancy. Predation, when it comes, is an inhalation. There is no tearing, no chase. A clicker simply ceases clicking. An isopod sheet grows thin in one corner. The water closes over the absence the way a dark broth seals over a spoon’s withdrawal. The globes are not hated. They are obeyed with the unquestioning deference given to gravity or weather. The fauna have learned them like a calendar.

Deep beneath their drifting courses, in the silt-choked ground floors and the foundations half-buried in pearl-grey drift, move the burrowers. Faceless, soft-bodied, or perhaps merely patient beyond the limits of exoskeleton, they are the plumbers of the metropolis. They open new routes through compacted lime and ancient sediment, redirecting the slow chemical tides that warm the lowest chambers. Their work means that no current ever follows exactly the same path twice; the city’s breath is bronchial, a branching system of ever-shifting passages. They do not emerge. They are felt only by their effects: a warm jet where there was once stillness, a clearing of isopod rustle as a wall is undermined, a new corridor opening in the drifts. They are the only ones still building, though what they build is void rather than stone.

And at the very crown, where the warm currents buffet the uppermost spires with the violence of a slow-motion gale, cling the pale filaments of chemosynthetic algae. Sparse as whiskers on a drowned cat, they are the city's vanity, its silver hair. They need no sun; they feed on the earth’s exhalation, on the mineral breath of thermal vents translated into the water column. Green is a forgotten rumor here, but these threads hold a pallid luminescence, a ghost of chlorophyll in a world that has replaced photosynthesis with geology. They are the only thing that reaches upward, not toward light but toward the chemical rim where warmth meets the black lid of the surface.

Together, they do not form an ecosystem so much as a tenement. The compression is social as much as physical. The eels know the isopod sheets will be thickest where the walls are oldest; the Clickers colonize the same nooks that filter-worms have already declared profitable; the globes follow the Clicker acoustics as surely as if they were reading a transit map. There is no frenzy because there is no room for it. Every cubic arm-span is claimed in time-share, folded ten-deep, a favela of warm flesh and carbonate where occupancy is inherited like instinct. The fauna navigate by a geometry without mind. They evacuate certain streets in synchronized migration as if obeying traffic laws, but the laws are only current and appetite, instructed by the calcium grammar the Sedimenters secreted into the stone long before the first eel coiled through a window.

The quiet holds them all. Sound is 0.8 here, a monastery of near-silence broken only by the wet crunch of time, the hiss of ten thousand microscopic mouths, the low tectonic groan of a tower settling deeper into its own patient weight. They do not sing. They do not cry. Their hearts beat against the warm water, layered like tenants in rooms without doors, and the city—having never known light, having no need of it—continues its ancient thriving dark, not as a ruin, but as a habitation perfected by the absence of its makers.
