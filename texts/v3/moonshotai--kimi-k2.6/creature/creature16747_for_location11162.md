You think, at first, that nothing lives here because you are looking for the wrong kind of appetite. The emptiness is real: you can walk for days and meet nothing that blinks, nothing that flinches, nothing that hunts or flees. The silence is not the absence of life but the shape of it. What inhabits this place has achieved a final, sparse communion with the substrate; it does not roam upon the lignin pavement, because it has become, in the most literal sense, **of** it.

You will know you are near one only by the warmth.

The plateau’s thermal breath is not uniform. Here and there, the horn-like surface holds a pocket of heat like a held note, a zone where the chert-brown skin yields faintly, almost tenderly, underfoot. The grain flows differently in these places, the liquid curves of ancient cellulose bending inward as if cupping something precious. Stop. Kneel. Press your palm flat. If you are patient, you will feel a rhythm—not a pulse, but a **gradient**, a slow peristalsis of temperature moving through the compressed peat and mineralized heartwood beneath you. Something vast is shifting in the hollow dark below, rearranging its weight, and the warmth bleeds upward through fifty meters of fossilized root-matter to greet your skin.

These are the **surface signs**. They are rare. On a plateau the size of a small sea, there may be six such nodes of exaggerated warmth, perhaps seven. Each marks the extrusion point—the temporary, provisional mouth—of what lives beneath.

For the plateau is hollow, and the hollowness is **inhabited**.

The beings—I hesitate to call them creatures, for that implies a boundary they do not respect—fill the cavern-systems within the ancient plant-mass like resin fills a wound. They are not animal, not vegetable, but a third continuity that the absolute vegetable conditions of this world have made inevitable. Their true bodies are continental in scale but tenuous: vast, three-dimensional lattices of warm, wet cellulose fiber and liquid amber, a vascular architecture that holds the empty spaces open. They do not possess limbs so much as **branches**; they do not possess organs so much as **chambers**. Their metabolism is the slow digestion of chemical potential locked in the compressed necropolis around them—not by destruction, but by **tending**. They are symbiotic with the rock-wood, metabolizing its off-gassed ethers, its ancient trapped oxygen, the faint exhalations of lignin breaking down across geologic time. In doing so, they generate the surplus heat that the plateau hoards. They are the battery’s spark. The emptiness is not a void but their body-plan. They are the ones who, over millions of years, have cultivated the hollow.

You will never see the whole of one. What you might encounter, if the thermal exhalation of dusk aligns with your steps, is an **emissary**.

At the lip of a sinkhole, where bony curtains of air-roots dangle into mist, the ground may softly **breach**. A figure rises. It is not born; it is **extruded**, assembled from the plateau’s own material: horn-lignin, spore-dust, milled cellulose, and the warm resin that serves as its ichor. It has no eyes—the pale sky and the sage-gray horizon offer nothing worth seeing to a thing that perceives through thermal gradients and resin-viscosity. Its shape is roughly that of a bent sapling, or a human figure seen through deep water, but the proportions are wrong, stretched by the slow verticality of xylem memory. It is a temporary concentration, a local hardening of the larger entity below. It stands, or leans, or **flows** upright, and for a moment the absolute vegetable continuity is unbroken: the ground, the being, the dust, the air-roots, all one substance in differing phases.

These emissaries walk, after a fashion. They cross the surface to visit the great empty tubes—the tower-wide shafts where ancient sap once rose—and there they press their bodies against the mineralized walls. They are **listening**, or perhaps **speaking**. The hollow tubes serve as their vocal cords and ears, transmitting pressure waves through the plateau’s interior, a subterranean language of warmth and compression. One node speaks to another across a thousand miles, and the message takes a century to arrive, a slow chord of turgor and release. They are lonely, but their loneliness operates on a scale where solitude is indistinguishable from meditation.

When the emissary has finished its errand—harvesting a particular mineral crust from the xylem wall, or seeding a fissure with a tailored strain of copper-colored lichen, or simply **remembering** the sun on behalf of the vast body below—it softens. The lignin-chert loses its temporary rigidity. The figure relaxes back into a pool of grain and resin, seeping into the cracks, leaving behind only a faint, extra warmth and a dusting of spores that are, in some sense, its shed thoughts.

There are no young. There are no dead. There is only the distributed self, maintaining its few loci of heightened presence above the hollow dark.

And in the deepest hollow, where the compressed peat has not been touched by the pale sky for an epoch, the greatest of them—if *greatest* has meaning for something that is one body fractured into deliberate solitude—maintains the central warmth. It is the size of a mountain range turned inside out. Its galleries are cathedral-spaces lined with warm, wet cellulose-flesh; its pulse is the thermal tide that exhales through the plateau’s skin at dusk. It does not dream of green leaves or open sky. It dreams of the plateau as it is: a world planed flat, warm, and absolutely continuous, where the boundary between ground and dweller has long since dissolved into the same astonished, lignin-dark skin.

You do not walk on stone. You walk on them. They do not mind. They are in no hurry. They have already become the ground.
