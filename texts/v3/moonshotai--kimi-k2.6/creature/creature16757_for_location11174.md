They do not introduce themselves. If they have a word for *I*, it is a silence held one beat longer than expected, a hand pressed flat to the sternum where the heart makes its muted drum against the vault-skin vest. They are small—barely to your shoulder if you stand straight, though straight standing is a violence in a world that asks you to crouch, to cling, to flatten against the breathing walls. Their bones are dense, tuned to the subsonic thrum of the pillars; press your ear to their collarbones and you would hear, faintly, the same low harmonic that runs through the ridge. This is how they listen. The air is for seeing; the stone is for speech. Their ears are small, nearly sealed, vestigial flaps of pale flesh, but their skeletons are antennae. They hear with their knees, their elbows, their wide, splayed feet that grip the sweating basalt without a sound.

Their skin is the color of drowned milk, of cave-fish, of the dustless underbellies of stones. It does not tan; the sun is a rumor here. What pigment they carry is borrowed: a smear of violet from brushing against a drifter in a narrow passage, a constellation of amber freckles where a bioluminescent sac kissed a child’s cheek and left its spores. These marks fade, but not quickly. They wear their encounters as half-erased maps. The eldest among them are mottled topographies of green and bruised gold, walking archives of every fauna they have brushed against in the dark.

Their eyes are enormous. Not with wonder—with necessity. The iris is wide, flat, the color of wet slate, and the pupil is shaped not round but oblong, like a vertical wound, better to parse the electrical auras of the floating things and the capillary flush in the pillar-rind. They do not weep, or if they do, the tears are indistinguishable from the permanent condensation on their cheeks. They blink seldom. Each blink is a shutter on a different spectrum of light. When a sheet-predator descends the central gallery at dusk, they do not flinch; they see the ionic disturbance of its approach long before its translucent folds occlude the passage, and they step aside with the courtesy one offers a moving wall.

They do not wear names as you wear names. They are known by their weight, their warmth, their rhythm. A mother is the particular three-tap beat she strikes against the east wall of the fifth level, a code that travels down the pillar’s heartwood faster than a shout could carry in open air. A child is the silence between two heartbeats. A lover is the temperature of a hand when both press together against the same vascular channel, feeling the tide of sap and blood align. Their language is a choreography of palm and knuckle, of obsidian clicked against horn, of the soft, resinous *thunk* of a bone awl striking a load-bearing tendon. They have, it is said, no words for *enemy* or *stranger*, only for *unfamiliar tempo*.

Their garments are not sewn so much as gardened. The under-fungus that felts in the pillar’s lower crevices is peeled in sheets, beaten with pumice and chewed gently in the mouth to soften it, then laid against their skin while still damp. It takes root in the oils of their bodies, grows with them, is trimmed back like hair. To undress completely would be to strip away a living mulch that insulates, muffles, and bonds them to the chemistry of the city. Their feet are bare, wide, the toes prehensile and cold-blue, gripping the bridges of woven gut without slip. They move as if underwater—the slow drift of limbs, the economy of momentum. A sudden movement here is not merely rude; it is a kind of lie, an assertion of separation from the prevailing medium. They belong to the damp as fish belong to the deep.

They are not numerous. Perhaps sixty souls, perhaps eighty. Enough to tend the eight pillars, to husband the air. There is no need for more; excess breath would crowd the silence. They are divided not by family but by respiration. The Inhalers tend the upper galleries, where the pillar-lungs draw the cloud-moisture down, where the great gas-bags must be guided away from the crown with gentle poles so they do not foul the upper vents. The Exhalers live in the root-bowls, carving the condensation channels, feeding the hearths with compressed peat, harvesting the garnet-fruits that grow only where the exhaled vapor of the pillars pools in the lowest clefts. Between them, the Vasculars, the bridge-tenders and the skin-scrapers, who speak most often in the click-code because their lives are spent in the connecting dark.

They do not farm. They do not hunt, not as you understand it. They negotiate. When a siege-tent floater grows too heavy with its own gases and sags toward a hearth-vent, they do not pierce it. They warm a broad sheet of fresh vault-skin over a low fire and wave it slowly, creating a thermal column that nudges the creature upward, back into the migration. When a predator sheet insinuates its ropes between the eastern pillars, hunting the thumb-sized drifters, the people step back, close certain doors of tendon and skin, and let the meal proceed. The digested remains will fall as a nutrient rain they collect in wax-lined leaves. They understand that hunger is a form of weather. Sometimes, rarely, the weather takes one of them. A child who forgets the signs. An elder whose warmth fades and who chooses, finally, to lie down in the upper gallery during the sheet-season. This is not mourned with sound. The remaining family simply stop tapping that particular rhythm on the walls. The silence where a beat should have been is the elegy. And the ridge, in its metabolic mercy, accepts the offering, dissolving the quiet body into the same stuff that feeds the pillars, so that the one who is gone becomes, in a season, a breath exhaled by the towers.

They drink from hearts. The water-bladders of the herd-fauna are dark, muscular organs the size of a head, harvested when the slow, creeping beasts extrude them during reproduction. The bladders are hung in nets of woven nerve-fiber, and they continue, for months, to gently pulse. The water inside never freezes; it is kept in motion by a residual life. The people press their lips to the warm, yielding valve and drink from a beating heart. Their cups are the scooped carapaces of dead floaters, rimmed with iridescence. Their beds are the soft, warm ventricles of abandoned sacs, cut open and cured so that they sigh softly beneath a sleeper, matching their breath to the pillars’ tide.

They are born in these beds. The gestation is perhaps nine months, perhaps longer; time here is measured in pillar-heaves, not solar rounds. A pregnant woman climbs to the nursery galleries, where the drifting fauna descend to lay their embryonic sparks in the seasonally softened walls. She presses her own swollen belly against the same warm, breathing surface, and the vault-skin, still half-alive, enfolds both her and the fauna-spawn in a translucent caul. They birth together, human child and floating sac, in the violet gloom, assisted by midwives who communicate only by the pressure of hands on the labouring mother’s spine. The children emerge quiet—not from trauma, but from training. They have never known echo. They open their black eyes into a world where the only thunder is the slow expansion of wood-flesh, and they accept it as infants accept milk.

They grow quickly, or the ridge grows them. They learn to read the barometric shifts before they learn to walk; their small chests expand and contract in sympathy with the pillars, a synchronization that lets them sense a coming surge of mist before it swells. By the time they can carry an obsidian flake, they know the twenty levels of the warren as a musician knows the frets of an instrument. They know which walls to tap for news, which hearths must never be allowed to die lest the architecture cool and harden into indifference, which bridges may be crossed when the sheet-predators drape themselves thick as curtains in the high air.

And they know the eight pillars. Each one by temperament. The north-most is slow, ancient-feeling despite the youth of the stone beneath it; its rind is thick, its breath a long, stately tide. The south triad are younger, more irritable, prone to sudden extrusions of white meat that must be pruned before they block the bridges. The center pillar, the tallest, is the lung of the city, and only the most silent, most metabolically aligned Vascular may climb its upper reaches to tend the crown. These climbers sometimes stay for weeks in the high silence, wrapped in fog and the dim constellations of the floaters, until they forget the taste of lichen and subsist on the nectar-beads that congeal at that altitude. When they descend, they move differently, as if they have become half-pillar themselves, their speech reduced to single, spaced taps, their eyes able to see the heat ripples of a thought.

For thoughts have heat here. In a world stripped of sonic clutter, the mind reveals itself as a warmth-pattern, a disturbance in the damp. They know a lie by the coolness that gathers at the nape. They know desire by the small, moist exhalation that leaves the lips. They know death has entered a room before the body cools, because the local fauna turn faintly toward it, drifting like compass needles.

They do not dream of elsewhere. The concept of *elsewhere* is a noise they have never made. Their philosophy is fungal: spread, connect, consume, become substrate. They build not to conquer the ridge but to extend its single, continuous body. Their city is a scab that heals the wound of the volcano into something sentient. They are the ridge’s newest organ, and its oldest memory.

When they die—if they die in bed, in warmth, in the slow failure of the pulse rather than in the silent embrace of a predator—they are not buried. Burial is a violence of striking. Instead, they are laid in the soft, yielding rind of the outermost pillar, in a gallery reserved for endings. The walls here are kept especially warm, especially damp. The vault-skin does not harden.
