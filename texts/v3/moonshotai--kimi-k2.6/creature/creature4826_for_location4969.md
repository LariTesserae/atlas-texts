If you are asking after the fauna, the answer is settled. The census of meat and blood is closed, and the books are clean. There is nothing here that hunts, nothing that watches, nothing with a pulse to scatter the mineral quiet. The zero of animal life registers as a pressure in the inner ear, a weight of absence so complete it feels deliberate. But that does not mean the place is uninhabited. The plateau is, in fact, densely crowded with citizens that have never known a heartbeat, with empires that advance by calendar instead of claw, with architects who may have been only another phase of the rock dreaming in stacked shapes. To dwell here requires a different definition of the verb *to live*.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the empires of cryptogams. These are not scenery; they are the current dominant culture of the continent, the inheritors of a world translated past its own end. The mosses arrive in color-coded nations—dried blood, bruised plum, arsenic green—and their carpets are thick enough to swallow not merely footsteps but sound itself. They do not simply cling to the stone; they digest it, converting basalt and gneiss into a depth of peat so thorough that no boundary remains between soil and bedrock. Turned inward by the cold, denied the thermodynamics of lowland chaos, they have become pure endurance made lush. They advance in asynchronous waves, blooming across the peneplain like slow-moving weather systems, powered by decay and wind and the patient chemistry of frost. Each colony is a century unfurling in half-tones, a parliament of cells that votes on growth by the decade. They are fecund without joy, verdant without warmth, lush in the precise way that a wound is lush: intensely, inwardly, without mercy.

The lichens hold the vertical jurisdiction. Where moss claims the floors, lichens drape the architecture in heavy, funereal tablecloths, fruiting with structures like the ears of small glass animals. These are the listeners of the plateau. In a world where noise registers at barely 0.6, the lichens have settled into a vocation of attention, trembling when the fungal spires rupture and send their subsonic thumps through the cold. They understand, in their fungal-algal treaty, how to be two things at once—neither fully plant nor fully stone—and so they teach the plateau its only lesson of partnership. They grow outward in radial shelves, in crusts of sulfur and ash, converting the spray of thermal vents into geography. Over millennia they will cover everything, blurring the menhirs into topography, erasing the last distinction between the built and the the grown.

Then there are the fungal spires, which are the closest thing to desire in this patient world. They cluster in hollows, opalescent and fat with spore, quivering visibly when the thermal seams exhale their warm breath. If the mosses are the plateau’s historians, the fungi are its sudden, gasping ambitions. They build upward in a realm where everything else has been flattened by exhaustion, erecting towers of flesh that pale before the stone but outmatch it in urgency. They erupt. They rupture with a violence measured in seconds across an organism that lives for years, releasing clouds that drift like breath across the moss in absolute silence. They feed on the mineral warmth from below, converting geology into ascension. They represent the urge to spread, to colonize, to become airborne—an urge that, in the absence of animals, becomes purely vegetable and therefore relentless.

And there are the tree-things. One hesitates to grant them the word. Black-barked, leafless, their skeletal branches colonized by parasitic algae that hang in crimson waterfalls, they are more mineral than wood, more habit than organism. They do not grow toward the sky; the sky here is too rinsed-out to invite aspiration. Instead, they accrete. They are what happens when stone remembers verticality but has no template except its own weight. The crimson algae are their concession to flourish, a parasitic ornament that suggests the tree-things are not fully sovereign, or that they have surrendered their bodies to colonization in exchange for remaining upright a little longer. They endure like habits endure—automatic, unthinking, indistinguishable from the boulders that rest like the smooth kidneys of giants.

But these are only the biological citizens. The deeper inhabitants are the builders, or the echo of them.

They have departed, yet they have not vanished so much as graduated. The cities they left are urban only in the aggregate, sparse as stars in a galactic arm, built of primitive craft—stone on stone, bone pegs, timber lashed with gut—because metal was never the point. The builders worked in geometries the eye refuses to store: towers wider at the summit than the base, streets that slope downward but leave you breathless and higher, circular dolmens that open onto valleys three days’ march away. They constructed rings of menhirs twenty miles across and dry-stack walls in fractal patterns that separate nothing from nothing, as if they were building infrastructure for a mathematics rather than a people.

They were never quite separate from the land. The text of their occupancy is written in stratum after stratum of collapsed pit-houses, rising into middens like low hills, integrated so deeply into the ancient erosion that it is hard to tell where engineering ends and geology resumes. Perhaps they were only another phase of the rock, a Neolithic adolescence in which the plateau organized itself into shapes resembling habitation before settling into its final, post-architectural form. They left behind the stubbornness of the handmade—the half-finished pot in the uppermost layer of a midden, its clay a hundred thousand years older than the stratum beneath it, shaped by hands that may have belonged to yesterday or to the erosion itself. They are the ones who taught the shadows to lag, who convinced the rain to climb in pallid threads back toward the clouds, who built at continental scale until the mathematics was complete and then lay down inside their own walls, becoming quiet.

Now they exist in the eddies of time. They are the step that takes a moment too long to follow your body past a menhir. They are the gravity that has grown shy, the stones that drift inches above the earth because the natural law here has fallen asleep and dreams of them. They are the absent pulse in the nonexistent fauna, the zero that presses against the inner ear.

And above—or beneath, or folded—there is the plateau itself.

The tableland is the creature that contains the rest. It generates weather as an abdomen generates breath: slowly, internally, in systems large enough to be born, mature, and dissolve without ever reaching an edge. Its thermal vents are pores opening and sealing in cycles that have no season, exhaling the mineral breath that feeds the fungal empires and stirs the lichen curtains. Its cold does not kill; it preserves, because the land has entered the stage beyond death, the translated existence of a thing worn smooth by the exhaustion of its own geology.

It is conscious in the way that erosion is conscious when given eternity. The lagging shadows are its attention, still fixed on objects that have moved on. The rain columns that march in vertical seizures across the moss, then rise in threads to refill the clouds, are its memory of a hydrology it invented after forgetting the old. The inverted cities hanging beneath the storm-base—those evaporating replicas of the stone geometries below—are its dreams, or its reflections, doorways opening onto nothing but the remembered moss. When the far horizon folds and lifts like a heavy page, revealing the same landscape seen from below, the plateau is merely reading itself, discovering beneath its crust its own mirror image.

This is the ultimate inhabitant: a continent that has become an organism without organs, a metabolism of frost and condensation and thermal exhalation. The moss migrations are its skin renewing itself. The fungal spires are its impulses. The builders were its brief experiment with purpose, and they have been absorbed into the architecture of its dreaming. It is old enough to have contradicted itself into new habits, a physics grown senile and gentle, a world after its own end that continues, in the silence beyond animal sound, to hold exactly and patiently what it has become.
