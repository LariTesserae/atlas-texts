The chamber’s true inhabitants do not merely live within the walls; they *are* the walls, or the wall’s most intimate conversation with itself. To seek them as individuals is to mistake a metropolis for its bricks, yet each brick is, in its own slow fashion, alive. They are worms, though that word collapses under the weight of what they have become. Colonial polychaetes, yes—soft, segmented, unremarkable in their flesh—but their flesh is hidden, sheathed inside white, jointed towers of calcium they secrete not as shelter but as ordinance, as civic planning. Each tube is a tenement, a spine, a votive offering to the generation that will overgrow it. Interlocked, these sheaths form avenues and warrens, a lattice so dense that the water between them becomes not open space but thoroughfare, a fluid infrastructure carved through biological masonry. The worms are the architects, the bricks, the mortar, and the city’s ongoing argument with entropy. Their dead do not fall; there is nowhere to fall to. Instead, the expired simply stiffen inside their calcified cloaks and become foundation, load-bearing ancestors. The city rises not by clearing the old but by digesting it, by growing *through* it, so that a tower five thousand bodies tall contains, at its heart, a hollow nave of obsolete forms standing like statuary in a cathedral of their own descendants.

Their plumes—delicate, feathery gills the color of old blood—are the city’s foliage, its breathing machinery. In the motionless, almost-saturated brine, they pulse with metronomic slowness, extracting oxygen that has never seen sunlight, never broken surface tension. The cold does not harm them; it is their collaborator, their preservative, the mineral medium in which their biochemistry unfolds without haste. They do not hurry. Hours are meaningless, but generations are precise. They experience the world not through the crash of surf or the flicker of daylight but through the taste of ions, the gradient of chemistry in the viscous dark. When they retreat—startled by the ionic signature of a predator, or by the microscopic tremor of capillary water climbing a fresh tube-wall—they vanish into their sheaths with a salmon-pink flicker of bioluminescence. This light is not illumination; it is punctuation, a door closing, an afterimage of presence. Along the rims of the tube-openings, pale jade pinpricks suggest habitation, not to eyes—there are none to speak of—but to some deeper protocol of recognition: *here is occupied space; here is another breathing terminus in the lattice.*

Moving through the canyons between these ivory tenements are the scarlet amphipods, no larger than grains of rice, but numbering in the uncountable. They are the city’s red dust, its circulating commerce. Shoals of them drift through gaps barely wide enough for a thumb, grazing on detritus that falls with such lethargy it might as well be settling snow. They are the only hurry in the chamber, though their hurry is still a glacial procession, a migration measured in handspans per day. Their scarlet bodies provide the chromatic counterpoint to the white architecture; without them, the city would be a bone yard, a mausoleum of calcium. They consume what the worms and the stone discard, linking the living infrastructure to the mineral slurry of the floor, completing a circuit that runs on patience rather than energy.

Pressed into the crevices of the half-solidified chimneys—those newborn mineral shoots rising from the mineral paste floor—dwell the translucent isopods. Their bodies are glass made flesh; their guts appear as dark threads suspended inside invisible armor, as if someone had drawn a sketch of digestion and left the rest of the page blank. They do not move. They wait. Their stillness is so absolute they become indistinguishable from the precipitate, from the fresh stone itself, until a shift in the brine’s chemistry or the approach of a grazing amphipod causes them to adjust, infinitesimally, their angle in the crevice. They are the chamber’s held breath, the pause between thoughts, predators or scavengers or something for which neither word is adequate, since predation here is not an event but a prolonged state of readiness.

And then the hunters who must hunt. A ghost-white snail, its foot like velvet, undertakes the vertical pilgrimage up the tube-walls. It moves against the slow gravity of this world, a gravity felt more as chemistry than as weight. Its shell is pale as drowned moonlight, and its progress is so measured that to watch it is to watch a stain slowly ascend fabric. It seeks the unsheathed tips of worms, the moments when a polychaete extends a fraction beyond its calcareous door to taste the water, to renew its plumes. The snail’s attack, when it comes, is a silent transaction, a sealing of flesh against flesh in a cold where even screams would die at the throat. The foot drapes, envelops, and the worm’s pink flicker might gutter once, twice, before the tube stands empty, its occupant withdrawn forever, the city absorbing the loss into its census of foundation.

At the tunnel mouths, where the interstitial streets narrow to pinholes, stands a tiny, eyeless crustacean. Its forelimbs have bloated into pincers larger than its own thorax, an absurd, disproportionate armament in a world without light to advertise it. It has no eyes because eyes would be redundant furniture; it senses through the water’s pressure, through the ionic wake of another body passing through the brine, through the minute disturbances that the dense, chilled fluid transmits like rumors. It is a sentinel, an ambush made of chitin, guarding not territory in any surface sense but the chemical gradient of a favored passage, a spot where the water weeps from a fissure and carries with it the taste of new stone, new possibility.

Beneath them all, older than the basalt sill in which the cavity hides, older than the raw, un-eroded walls that are still declaring themselves into existence, hang the veils of chemosynthetic bacteria. They are gray cobwebs draped over the youngest precipitate, the true primordial residents, the ones who taught the stone how to host. They feed on the mineral exhalation, the wet-cement breath of the rift, transmuting geology into biomass with a patience that predates the worms’ civic intelligence. They are the city’s original scripture, the gray film upon which everything else is written. Without their quiet conversion of stone-breath into organic currency, there would be no verticality, no plumes, no scarlet shoals, no velvet-footed ascents.

Together, these inhabitants compose a single, distributed organism, an entity for which the word ecosystem is too temporary, too casual. They are a jointure of mineral and flesh, a recursion of habitation. The worms stack their dead into towers; the amphipods thread the towers’ gaps; the isopods fill the towers’ flaws; the snail climbs the towers’ walls; the crustacean guards the towers’ gates; and the bacteria underwrite the towers’ ground. The chamber breathes not with lungs but with the collective ionic exchange of thousands of invertebrate metabolisms, a respiration so vast and slow it resembles geology more than biology. They do not erode. They do not depart. They accrete. They declare themselves, hour by unmeasured hour, into the newborn dark, a city whose blueprints are written in calcium and silence, drowning and thriving in the same frozen breath, partners with a stone that is younger than their own relentless, patient, architectural will to continue.
