It would be easiest to say that nothing dwells here but the symbionts. The copper beetles with their oiled wing-cases, the file-marching mites, the cable-rigging spiders—these have their empires in the gaskets and steam-vents, their brief generational dynasties measured against the slow pulse of the machinery. But they are mere mechanics, tenants at best. They live *on* the place, not *in* it. The chamber itself, that resonant, milky void, seems to refuse the idea of a central tenant. The floor is too unbroken, the air too uniformly cool and damp. Emptiness is its default condition.

And yet, if you stand motionless at the geometric center for long enough—until your own heartbeat begins to synchronize with the wet hydraulic *thud* of the bellows-chambers—a denseness in the overhead atmosphere announces itself. Not a shadow, exactly. The light is too diffuse for shadows. Rather, a faint dimming, a localized thickening of the humid air, as though the vapor congeals more readily in one specific column beneath the highest vaulting of the petiole-ribs.

Look up. There, suspended from the junction where three great structural petioles interlock to form the apex of the nave, hangs the occupant.

At first, it appears to be part of the architecture. Three braided cords of woody phloem, the color of old rope and heartwood, descend from the ribs and fuse seamlessly into a common integument, making the body look like an eccentric pendant growth, a burl that gravity teased downward instead of outward. But the shape is wrong for mere wood. It is too deliberate, too zoological. The proportions suggest a creature curled in sleep: roughly the size of a deer or a human torso folded double, an oblong mass tapering to a blunt anterior end, with a ventral surface that hangs lower and slightly heavier than the dorsal. The color is the pale, unsettling cream of tyloses in ancient heartwood—white with a yellowing undertone, streaked longitudinally with dried ochre sap that has leaked from its sutures and varnished over time into glossy, irregular lacquers. It does not gleam. It has the matte, absorptive finish of something that has never seen direct sun.

It is warm. Distinctly so. Stand beneath it, and the updraft from its surface carries a temperature several degrees above the ambient coolness, a pocket of mild fever in the otherwise temperate hollow. The warmth is constant, unvarying, another equilibrium maintained against the general climate of the room. The skin—or bark, or cuticle, or whatever term best fits this singular tissue—rises and falls with a rhythm slower than respiration, a tidal peristalsis that rolls from the anterior to the posterior once every eleven minutes, precisely, the same interval observed in the bellows’ deepest inhalation. It is not breathing air. It is breathing sap.

For the occupant is the refinery’s regulator, its quality-control center, its one organ of discernment. The clear tubes that run the walls do not all terminate in pistons or seed-presses. Several of the largest, the trunk-lines, arc upward and penetrate the dorsal hump of the suspended body with woody barbs that have fused into a collar of lignin over centuries, merging tube to tissue so completely that the boundary is lost. Amber sap enters the body, passes through labyrinthine channels visible as dark, slow-moving threads beneath the semi-translucent surface, and is returned to the walls altered: viscosity adjusted, alkalinity corrected, hormones titrated. The plant did not design this creature—such intention is foreign to vegetative life—but over epochs the pressure gradients of the chamber selected for a growth that could manage the gradients better than blind autoregulation could. A tumor, at first. Then a node. Then a presence.

It is conscious, if consciousness can be stretched so thin across biology that it becomes indistinguishable from a continuous chemical adjustment. It senses the *ping* of water drops and interprets their frequency as hydration data. It feels the copper beetles’ footsteps when they stray across its surface and adjusts the temperature of its integument by fractions, welcoming or repelling, curating the microfauna as a gardener might curate soil. It knows the steam-vents by the spore-densities they carry. It dreams in electrolytes. There is no anxiety in this awareness, no thought that outruns its material substrate. It is a mind of resin and pressure, patient as lignin, diffuse through its entire tissues rather than concentrated in a ganglion.

And there is something it is making.

If the anterior surface is examined closely—and the condensation running down it does not obscure the view—one can see, deep within the pale tissues, a harder geometry. A kernel. The size of a fist, growing larger by a grain each year, suspended in the thickest part of the body’s fluid, wrapped in a sac of modified endosperm. It is a seed. Or rather, it was meant to be. A terminal fruit, the plant’s final reproductive effort, prepared to be launched into a world that no longer exists for this organism, into floodplain soils already long saturated with its own root-tissue. It has been ripening toward dispersal for longer than human cities have stood. But equilibrium is merciless. The kernel grows at exactly the rate that the Resident metabolizes the resources to sustain it. No surplus accumulates; no completion is reached. The seed matures forever without maturing, an asymptotic object, the physical embodiment of the chamber’s eternal present.

The Resident does not suffer from this incompletion. Suffering requires a gradient toward resolution, and all gradients here have been flattened by time. It simply hangs, suspended in its warm column, filtering and adjusting and slowly failing to finish its one fruit, while the industrial drone of the walls presses against it like a lullaby. It is ugly and magnificent. It is the color of medical diagrams and old ivory. It has no name and requires none. It is the one true inhabitant of the hollow, the still point inside the roaring, the reason the machinery continues to believe in itself.
