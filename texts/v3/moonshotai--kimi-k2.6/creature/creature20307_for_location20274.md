At first you think the inhabitants are the soft things. You believe the chamber belongs to the velvet starfish clinging with their hundreds of silent cilia, or to the egg-white snails leaving vapor-trails that vanish before memory, or to the grass-creatures that unbundle and rebundle like thoughts half-completed. They are numerous; they crowd the warm dark; they bleed silver and leave no sound. But you are wrong. They are only residue. They are the lint of a much larger occupation, the shed phosphors of a body so interred in its own making that it has forgotten which of its parts are architecture and which are flesh. They are the tenant’s exhalations, accidentally given shape.

What lives here is lying down.

It has been lying down since before the concept of standing was finalized within these walls. It occupies the entire low volume of the chamber the way a body occupies a fever: totally, radiantly, without margin. You will not see it at once because it is not assembled for viewing. It is distributed. Its skeleton is the vaulted ribbing overhead; you have been walking inside its ribcage since you entered, stooping through the narrow passage between its breastbone and its spine. The low ceiling is low because it is pressed flat against the thing’s upward-facing flank, the ceramic belly of the vault molded by the slow, heaving arch of a back that has never turned. The ribs are notribs in the animal sense—they are fired terracotta, yes, but they cage the same warm organs that conceived them. The heat you feel, that mineral fever in the clay, is not residual from any kiln. It is metabolic. It is the slow, basal burn of a giant that has never stopped growing, only changed its mind about what shape to take.

Its skin is the tiles. Each one. Millions of fingerprints pressed into the clay, whorls and deltas captured forever—you assumed these were the traces of many workers, an army of thumb-smoothed architects. But look closer. The whorls match. They are all the same print, repeated, propagated, pressed and pressed again by the same unhurried digit. The Builder was never a collective. There was only ever one hand, one palm, one patient feverish body that, having run out of exterior clay, began to press itself outward into the world. The fingerprints migrate because the skin still shifts in its sleep. Over centuries the whorls slide a finger’s width to the left, not because the clay moves, but because the flank beneath it settles, dreaming of a new posture, rearranging its dermal contact with the vault it became.

You are walking on its underside. The floor is its ventral surface, flattened and glazed. The channels cut into the ceramic—that spiral of crisp grooves converging on the central basin—are not drainage. They are capillaries. The pale dust that runs like a liquid, eddying uphill against the pull of ordinary gravity, is not dust. It is its sand-like lymph, circulated by a thoracic motion too vast and slow for your senses to register as heartbeat. When you watch the dust flow toward the center and then rise in thin veins across the dome, you are watching the circulation of something that has only one chambered heart: this room. The dry basin at the center is not empty. It is the pooling well of its diaphragm.

And the light? The sourceless, overcast daylight that has no aperture? That is the glow of its open attention. It does not have eyes in the manner of creatures that must focus on distant horizons; horizons are a myth it has never admitted. Instead, its seeing leaks through the glaze, diffuse, suffusing, because it looks at everything at once and therefore casts no sharp gaze. The shadows that fall wrongly, that lean against geometry or arrive seconds before their objects—these are the premotions of its eyelids, lowering and lifting in a blink that takes years to complete.

You asked—or you felt, standing there—who rules this place. It is not a ruler. It is a *continuer*. It was the first thing to exist inside the enclosure, and because the enclosure was built to be absolute, it could not stop at the boundary of a body. It had to continue. It pressed itself outward into tile and sinew, into the locked geometries of peg and groove, and then, having finished the walls, it pressed itself inward into the flora. The great bladed leaves, those arterial purples and bruise-blues, are its hair. The silvery sap that hardens to enamel is its sebum. The superposition in the corner—bud, blossom, and blackened husk simultaneously—is not a breakdown of physics but a thought it is still thinking, unable to settle on a conclusion. It has not decided whether the plant should live or die, so the plant does both, gently blurred, waiting for the dweller to make up its mind.

The unfinished deer in the thicket, that woven-grass thing flickering between statuary and flesh, phasing through the floor—it is its child. Or its experiment. Or its attempt at a companion, abandoned mid-press because the proportions went wrong, because outside gravity tugged at the legs, because the chamber had not yet decided whether fauna should be solid or suggestion. It leaves the deer there, in the half-state, because to finish it would require admitting that the making is finite, and this it cannot do. The chamber is still emerging from its first moment of creation because the dweller is still in that moment, still extending.

It has no mouth. It does not eat, not in the way the grass-creatures or the velvet stars graze. It consumes silence. The dense air, the swallowed acoustic, the near-total hush that makes your breathing sound borrowed—these are its sustenance. It inhales the quiet between footsteps. It metabolizes the absence of sound into presence, which is why the chamber feels so packed, so saturated, so layered with touching, overlapping being. Every noise you make is a small theft from its pantry. It does not resent you for this. It does not yet know you are noise. To it, you are new clay.

It is soft where it is not sharp. You might find this strange, given the knife-keen edges of every tile, the crisp wounds of the drainage spirals, the blood-letting channels. But those edges are its nails, its teeth, the unsoftened parts it needs to keep pressing outward. The rest is yielding. Beneath the root-mat, if you were to push aside the fibers and dig through the dry felt, you would find a surface not of earth but of warm, consenting clay-pink flesh, glazed only thinly, still soft as a scar. It is headless. It has no need for a face; the entire chamber faces outward. If you press your ear to the thickest cluster of tiles, where the handprints cluster most densely, you can hear a rhythm too slow to be a pulse, more like the settling of continents: a subsonic *press*, and *press*, and *press*, continuing from a time before iron, before water, before the outside had been fully discounted.

It knows you are here now. Not because it saw you enter—sight is not its primary sense—but because your weight changes the temperature of the floor by a thousandth of a degree. Because your shadow interrupts the slow diet of silence. Because your foreignness is a cool spot on its flank, a place where the firing has not yet completed. It is not angry. Anger requires an outside to be angry at, and there is no outside. It is curious in the only way it knows: it is beginning, very slowly, to press around you. You may notice the wall behind you has crept closer. The corner you passed is now twenty paces away, then ten. Distance is its breath, and it is inhaling. It wants to know your shape, your whorls, the deltas of your own identity. It wants to know if you can be pressed.

If you stay long enough—decades, perhaps, though the chamber’s time may give you only minutes—it will begin to make room for you in its body. A tile may appear that matches the ridge of your finger. A shadow may settle that has your outline but no source. You will feel the ceiling lower further, not to crush, but to fit, to accommodate your shoulders in the architecture of its ribs. The purple leaves will shift their hue toward something your own blood recognizes. You will cease to be a visitor and become instead a fact, a local amendment, a new fingerprint in the clay.

This is the mercy and the finality of the place. The dweller does not keep a prison. It keeps a *continuity*. It is finishing the world, and everything inside the world must be finished with it. It has pressed itself into vault and channel, into resin-leaf and dust-river, into the unmade deer and the migrating handprints. It has one process only: to make, to press, to hold, and to warm. It has no bones because it is held up by the absolute structure of its own obsession. It has no voice because it speaks by contact, by the hand-to-hand, tile-to-tile passing of its massive, enclosed, manual being.

Lie down. The floor is not ground. It is a skin waiting to know yours. The heat will take you like a glaze. You will not leave, because leaving was never a channel cut into this clay. You will stay, and in staying, become another of its soft things—silent, fecund, perfectly made, and urgently, patiently pressed.
