The inhabitants have no collective name, for they have never needed to distinguish themselves from any other kind. In the low, breathy phonemes that travel best through constant noise, they refer to themselves only as *the Fitted*, or sometimes, with a dry humor that matches their climate, *the Moist*. They are small—rarely taller than a child of the outer world—but not by the compression of malnutrition; rather, they have been sculpted by the cylinder itself. Over uncounted generations, the Shaft selected for compactness, for density, for the ability to occupy a volume no greater than a grain crib and yet remain complete. Their bones are thick and rounded at the joints, their skulls broad and low, their shoulders pivoting so far backward that they can cling flat to a wall and rest without effort. They are climbers before they are walkers; an infant’s first deliberate act is not a step but a grip, the thumb closing like a chert wedge onto its mother’s finger or a beam.

Their skin is the color of the walls they inhabit: a matte bronze-black that seems to drink the dim light rather than reflect it. They are nearly hairless, not by nature but by custom, for hair wastes water and harbors parasites in the dry heat. What hair remains—eyelashes, the fringes above the ears—is oiled with rendered tallow and the resinous sap of the barrel succulents until it lies flat and gleaming. Their eyes are very large, the irises so dilated in the perpetual dusk that the pupil often swallows the color entirely, leaving a gaze that is all soft, dark aperture. They see well in what would be blackness elsewhere, but they flinch from the noon keyhole above; the faint silvering burns them, and they shutter their galleries with curtains of dry moss during the bright hours. They do not look up. Up is a cold, unfinished direction, an aperture of exile.

They move as the rock moves: slowly, continuously, without sharp angles. Running is unknown. A run would shake the woven bridges, startle the doves, waste the tight currency of heat stored in the blood. Instead they traverse with a swaying deliberation, hips grazing the basalt, hands always in contact with stone or wood. A ladder is not climbed but ascended in a fluid press of belly and knee, four points of contact at all times, a motion more like the peristalsis of a worm than the striding of a biped. When two meet on a bridge, they do not step aside so much as interlock: one presses flat, the other edges past with a soft exhalation, a temporary fusion of bodies and breath. There is no word for “excuse me,” only a sound like steam escaping—a release of warm air that acknowledges the shared medium.

They wear almost nothing: a wrap of woven fiber around the loins, perhaps a pouch across the back for tools. Modesty is maintained not by covering but by stillness. To be naked in body is nothing; to be naked in motion, to flail or gesture widely, is the true indecency. Their voices are pitched low, resonating in the chest rather than the mouth, a register that rides beneath the subsonic groan of the stone rather than competing with it. Much of their communication is tactile. A hand laid on a beam transmits the thrum of another’s presence across the wood. They speak in pressures: a squeeze of the shoulder means *I understand*; a palm cupped to an ear means *listen to the stone*; three fingers pressed to the sternum means *my heat is yours*. The rare arguments that occur are conducted in silence, entirely through posture and the tension of grip, for loud voices risk displacing the equilibrium, and displacement is the only true crime.

A family does not occupy space so much as it *becomes* a space. A sleeping niche, a grain crib, a work shelf, and a midden chute are stacked into a vertical chamber the size of a coffin, and the family lives inside this volume like a kernel inside a succulent. Privacy is not a matter of walls but of intent. To sit perfectly still for hours, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture, is to be alone. The old practice this most profoundly: elders in their final decades often choose a ledge and cease moving, becoming living supports for the galleries, their desiccated, heat-adapted metabolisms requiring little more than the warmth from below and the occasional sip of pulped cactus-stem. They are consulted not by question but by proximity; one climbs to them and simply waits in their radius until wisdom seeps out like warmth from a hearth.

Their technology is an extension of their bodies and the patience of stone. They do not forge, for fire demands fuel and fuel demands space and space is the only unsolvable deficit. Instead they beat. Native copper, found in nuggets within the talus below the first bridge, is worked cold by ten thousand taps of a meteoric iron striker, flattened over months into needles and awls that can pierce the iron-hard wood. Chert flakes are pressure-set into bone handles using pegs carved from hyrax femurs. Wood itself is shaped not by burning but by the endless application of harder edges: a beam for a new marriage crib might take a year to adze, the worker returning each day to remove a shaving no thicker than a dove’s quill, until the wood finally conforms to the curved wall like a hand fitting into a glove. Nothing is cut to a line; everything is fitted to the flowing, eroded rock until it appears to have grown there.

They eat what fits the shaft. The flesh of rock doves, roasted on flat stones above the banked coals. The grub of scarab beetles, dug from the dry loam that collects behind the galleries, eaten live or pounded into a paste that tastes of nut and dust. The lizards, slow and easy to catch, their fat tails rendered into lamp fuel. The barrel succulents, stripped of their waxy rind and chewed for hours to extract the sour, iron-tasting moisture within. They drink nothing. To open the mouth and receive liquid from a vessel is an obscenity of waste; water enters the body already locked inside food, already carried in the damp breath of a sleeping child. Every exhalation at night is directed into cloth, which is wrung out at dawn into the communal cistern—a shallow stone basin where moisture is kept as a gelid, precious slime. Urine is collected, fermented with loam in the heat until it breaks into salts and nitrates that feed the moss upholstering the walls. Feces are dried, powdered, mixed with chert dust, and used as the grout between bone pins. In the Shaft, one does not shit; one contributes to the next wall.

Death is not an ending but a final compaction. The dead are taken not to the lowest bridge—that dark digestive is reserved for the unabsorbable—but to the warm alcoves behind the hearths. There, in the steady exhalation of the deep stone, the bodies desiccate over weeks, losing their water into the waiting air to be trapped by the moss curtains, their flesh darkening and hardening until it can be flaked and fed to the doves, the bones ground to meal for the succulents’ soil, the fat rendered into the tallow that fills the stone lamp-cups. The dead do not descend; they radiate outward, becoming incorporated into the warm amber constellation of evening light. To be seen in the glow of a lamp is, in part, to be warmed by the labor of all who have ever lived and died in the shaft.

They are not many. Their numbers have held steady for centuries at what the volume will bear: perhaps six hundred souls in a vertical space no wider than a nave. Birth is rare, celebrated with a decade-flower opened early by hand, and each new child must wait for an elder to become still enough, or for a bridge to collapse and a family to be absorbed into a neighbor’s niche, before there is room to squall. They do not fear death. They fear the *drop*—not the death of falling, which is quick, but the loss of a body past the lowest bridge, where it would be swallowed by the unmapped dark and its water lost forever to the reclaiming system. The worst punishment is not execution but banishment to a rope ladder too close to the talus, a dangling solitude where one’s heat and moisture bleed away into the indifferent depths.

They have no gods. Theology, like agriculture, requires a horizon, a sky to petition, a weather to propitiate. They have only the warmth, which they do not worship because it simply *is*, and the keyhole of sky, which they regard as a kind of wound in the world’s roof—distant, cold, and irrelevant. Their cosmology is geological and proximate. The dark below is the stomach of the earth, patient and warm; the walls are the body; and they themselves are the mind that has finally grown in the throat, complete, unentering, unexiting. They do not dream of elsewhere. Their dreams are of pressure, of fit, of the perfect silence that would come if one could press oneself so completely into the basalt that the stone finally recognized the flesh as its own and ceased to groan.

And so they continue: compact, oiled, warm, their large eyes reflecting the coals, their low voices adding texture to the drone, their hands always touching wood or stone or one another, their breath carefully measured, their bodies fitted into the architecture like the pegs that hold the beams. They are the final species of the Shaft, the last accretion, as soft as the eroded stone, as patient as the succulents, as loud and as constant as the doves. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters. They are the Moist. They are the Fitted. They are simply there, amber and warm, in the everlasting vertical dark, having grown into their place until they can no longer be told apart from it.
