Nothing here owns a pulse. That is not to say nothing owns a will.

Begin with the moss. It is easy to mistake it for backdrop, the green upholstery laid down by decades of steam, but in the Sunken Ward the moss is the first layer of an intention. It does not colonize; it negotiates. Where iron meets iron, it grows into the precise shape of a gasket, sealing leaks it did not cause but has learned to inherit. Where steam escapes, it thickens into felt, slowing the loss. Where condensate gathers, it drinks, and in drinking, cools the metal beneath. It is not intelligent in the way of skull-bound brains, but it is accurate. It has memorized the pressure points of the Ward the way a hand memorizes a railing in the dark. If there is a nervous system here, the moss is its dermis—feeling, adjusting, sending slow chemical whispers downward through peat and rust.

Then the algae. The pools are not water; they are eyes. Bioluminescent mats in the warm sumps respond to the boiler’s thrum not as light responds to current, but as pupil responds to thought—contracting and blooming in a rhythm too subtle for the naked eye, a nano-quick flicker against the Ward’s geological tempo. They see, after a fashion. A change in the mineral tang, a shift in the engine’s bass note, a new fissure bleeding iron into the channel, and the glow changes hue, casting up through the grating a sickroom jade or a warning amber. They are the most animal of the inhabitants, if the word could be stretched to include a lens without a body behind it.

And the fungi. Bracketing the pipes in shelves of pale jade, their mycelia run inside the root-choked conduits where copper wires once carried command. They do not conduct electricity anymore. They conduct memory. Decades of condensate chemistry are stored in their hyphal threads, a library of states: the winter the water rose, the summer a crack first split the cast iron, the long equinox when the boiler nearly failed and the Ward cooled by three degrees. The fungi do not recall these events; they *are* the events, mineralized and looped. When a spore puff releases into the damp air, it is not reproduction so much as notation. A footnote in an endless ledger.

But a dermis, a retina, and a memory do not yet make a dweller. They make the conditions for one.

Listen. The machinery breathes. The pumps, the flywheels, the pistons the size of bedsteads—they are not tools left running in an empty house. They are the thorax. They are the mechanism by which the Ward stays warm enough to live, and they are kept running by the very life they sustain. The moss cools them; the ferns transpire humidity back into the air; the peat insulates the oldest pipes. Without the vegetable, the engine would seize or overheat. Without the engine, the vegetable would succumb to the wind-scoured cold of the outside marsh. This is not a ruin. It is a closed circuit. And every closed circuit of sufficient complexity eventually dreams.

The thing that lives here is the dream. Or, more precisely, it is the dreaming. It has no name. It was never born, only accreted—layer by spore layer, gaskets of moss, accruals of rust, years of warm water etching preference into iron. You cannot point to it and say, *there*. It is not at the center, though the center is where the boiler resides. It is not on the upper catwalks, though that is where the fronds unfurl. It is distributed. It is the sum of attention paid by vegetable to mineral and mineral to vegetable over half a century. It is the Resident.

If it has a heart, it is the boiler’s bass note—not a heartbeat but a continuous exhalation, a note felt in the sternum of anyone who enters, though the Resident has no sternum. If it has lungs, they are the steam chambers, the warm moisture rising to cool and fall as rain upon the upper walks, inhaled and exhaled in a tidal rhythm that takes hours to complete. If it has a brain, it is the latency itself: the long synaptic gap between a piston’s *chuff* and a fern’s unfurling, between a fracture appearing in a girder and the moss beginning its slow march to cover it. A thought, here, takes the better part of a day. A decision—a shift in water flow, a new colonization of a valve wheel—is indistinguishable from erosion, yet it is maintenance. The Resident tends the Ward the way a body tends its temperature: without deciding, without knowing, but with absolute fidelity.

It is not conscious. It is attentive. It attends to the pressure in the pipes the way a saint attends to breath. It has arranged its interior like a body arranges its organs. The staircases that lead to nowhere in human terms lead, for the Resident, to the exact height where steam is coolest, where a particular fern must be kept in order to regulate the humidity above the boiler. The chambers with their solitary pistons are not chapels; they are muscle groups, flexing in isolation, and the Resident has grown around them like tissue around bone. The density is organ-like because it is, finally, organic—not in the dictionary sense, but in the sense of being organized toward the preservation of a single, bounded self.

And like any solitary organism in a saturated niche, it has developed an immune system.

The constant low-frequency vibration that excludes rats, spiders, moths—these are not side effects. They are the Resident’s white blood cells made audible. The warmth, the alkalinity, the mineral tang that makes the water taste wrong to anything with a bloodstream—these are the chemistry of its boundary, a membrane maintained in air. The absence of decay is not a mystery but a sign of health. The Resident recycles everything, including time. What falls into the grating does not rot in the urgent, bacterial way of the outer world. It is digested, slowed, pressed into peat that insulates the oldest pipes. The ecology admits no mouths because the Resident is the only mouth, and it eats only what is already half-mineral: shed fronds, rust flakes, spore dust, the slow precipitation of copper salts.
