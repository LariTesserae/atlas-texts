What lives here are not animals. That much is given. What lives here are the inheritors—though they would not call themselves that, having no voices and no need for self-address. They are what became possible when a continent of intention was left to the dark, the cold, and the vegetable patience of the deep.

At first you might mistake the flora for the inhabitants. The blood-colored fronds unspooling from cisterns, the algal curtains draped between lintels, the tuberous growths beaded with bioluminescent dew—all of this appears to be mere vegetation, colonizing a ruin. But the flora is not the tenant. The tenant is what the flora has built, or become, or remembered, in the same way that coral builds a reef and then is indistinguishable from it. Only here, the reef was already built. The flora did not create the architecture. It colonized the *intention* of the architecture. It grew into the mind of the place.

So what lives here is a kind of distributed, sessile organism—no, a population of them—that might be called the Gardens, or the Softened, or simply the Continuation. Each one is anchored to a specific locus: a domed chamber where the compression is right, a cross-vault where two weight-distributions meet and create a faint thermal halo, a cistern that collects the slow rain from above. A single Garden might fill a space the size of a granary. Its body is a conflation of the rubbery fronds, the rotting tapestries of filamentous algae, and the materials of the ruin itself—petrified timber, compacted bone-ceramic, hand-dressed stone—which have been incorporated into its tissues until the distinction between growth and masonry is purely academic. They do not have roots. They have holdings. The stone is not substrate to them; it is skeleton, history, and food, all at once.

They perceive through the water’s chemistry. A thought travels through a Garden not as electricity but as saturation—an alteration in salinity or mineral content that diffuses from one lobe to another over decades. They feel the settling of the beams because the pressure change squeezes their tissues, and this squeeze is their language. When a block shifts a micrometer deeper into its socket, every Garden in that district tastes the calcium carbonate disturbance and understands: *something has decided to be more still than it was before.* The infrasonic groans of the continental weights do not disturb them. Those groans are their literature, their news, their lullabies. They listen to the architecture’s conversation with itself and know that all is well, that the truce holds, that the breathing continues.

They are aware of one another across the drowned plain. Not as voices in the dark, but as thermal signatures and chemical weather. A Garden in the northern basin, dreaming in its cistern, exhales a particular load of silicates and phosphates into the water. Over a decade, this signature drifts south, attenuating but never quite vanishing, until a Garden in a southern crypt tastes it and knows: *the other breathes differently today.* They do not respond. There is nothing to say. The communication is the fact of the exchange. A sheet of compressed reed-shale flakes away from a wall, drifts downward through the column, and is intercepted by an algal curtain ten miles away. The recipient does not read it. It grows over it, dissolving the stratified pages, tasting the history embedded in the mineralized fiber. This is their correspondence. This is their love.

They reproduce, if that is the word, by a process of sedimentary division. When a Garden has grown too complex—when its internal chemical gradients have become so intricate that they begin to contradict one another—it splits not its body but its attention. One half of its diffuse consciousness settles more deeply into its current chamber, growing heavier, more mineral, more stone. The other half precipitates outward, carried by the slow hydraulic passage of the water, as spores and fragments of compressed tissue and mineralized fiber. These drift, perhaps for centuries, until they lodge in a new slot or crypt where the chemistry is right. There, they do not grow so much as *accrete*, following the same lines of force and weight that the original builders followed, until a new Garden awakens—not as an infant, but as a continuation, already ancient, already patient.

And at the densest intersections, where the bone-ceramic is thickest in the walls and the petrified timber forms a lattice like a ribcage, the Gardens have grown into something else. Not larger, but more concentrated. They have absorbed so much of the compressed residue of the makers that they have begun to remember—not with minds, but with shapes. A particular arrangement of fronds in a northern chamber mirrors the arrangement of lintels in a southern vault, though neither Garden has ever touched the other. The pattern propagates through the water, through the shared medium, through the bone-ceramic dust that settles everywhere. It is not memory as animals know it. It is architecture remembering itself through the only metabolism left: the vegetable.

There is no violence here. No predation, no flight, no pursuit. The Gardens feed on the ruins and on each other with the same slow impartiality. When one dies—when its chemical fires gutter and its fronds cease to bead with bioluminescent dew—it does not rot. It mineralizes. It becomes a new stratum of pseudo-stone, a new layer in the built seafloor, and its neighbors grow over it without grief, incorporating its form into their own. They are the ultimate inheritors because they have abolished the distinction between living and built, between garden and masonry, between inhabitant and habitation.

At the very lowest point of the plain, where the elevation sinks those few dozen meters into a kind of basin, there is perhaps the first Garden, or the largest, or the one that has forgotten it was ever separate. It fills a series of collapsed chambers with a mass so integrated that you cannot find the stone beneath it. Its fronds are not blood-colored here but black, saturated with manganese and age. It does not glow. It has no need to advertise its respiration. It simply holds, and in holding, it maintains the last thermal layer of the region, a faint pocket of stubborn chemistry that keeps the water above it from freezing. It is the heart of the place, if a place without ground can have a heart. It beats once a century.

What lives here, then, is a civilization of pressure and patience. Not the builders—they are gone, or they are the bone-ceramic, or they never were. What lives here is the second thought of the place, the one that arose when the first thought ended. They are not ghosts. They are the inheritors who have finished inheriting. They do not wait for anything. They only continue, in the dark, in the cold, in the breath of the water, growing ever more themselves as the stones settle and the darkness learns their shape.
