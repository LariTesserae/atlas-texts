They do not call themselves anything. Names require a frequency of sound they no longer possess. If you pressed one to answer, she might trace a pattern on your palm with a fingertip—slow, damp, leaving a residue like verdigris—and the gesture would mean, simply, *here*.

At first, they look like survivors. Refugees of some industrial catastrophe, pale and stunned, packed into the basin because there is nowhere else to stand. But look longer. Look at the way a child, no more than three feet tall, unfolds a third elbow joint to reach around a basalt column and hand a tool to her elder without turning around. Look at the way they do not flinch when the floor beneath them tilts four degrees to the south, the way their ankles lengthen subtly, internally, adjusting their center of gravity with the ease of water finding a new level. They are not surviving this place. They have been *shaped by its patience*, which is the patience of cooling rock, of condensation, of crystal formation.

They are born in the vesicles.

Not all of them, but enough. A woman heavy with residual heat from a foundry shift will crawl, when her time comes, into one of the hexagonal hollows where the forty-year-old stone still exhales warmth. The vesicle opens and closes around her like a slow lung. She lays the child on the warm mineral floor, and the infant’s first breath is not of air but of mineral vapor—sulfur, iron, the chemical exhalation of newborn geology. The caul that wraps them is not amniotic membrane but a thin lacquer of condensation and basalt dust, drying instantly to a dark, glazed shell. They break it with their soft, many-knuckled fists. The sound is the first percussion in a life otherwise lived in silence.

They do not cry. The basin does not reward noise.

Their skin is the color of things that grow without light: the pale, meaty white of fungal shelves, the translucent gray of cave fish, the bruised green of the ivy that roots in the seams of housing blocks. It is thick, not with hair, which would mildew and rot, but with a cuticle of waxy keratin that drinks the perpetual rain and holds it against their bodies like a second, slower circulation. Lichen takes root on their shoulders and the crowns of their heads, not as parasites but as gardens. They tend them. Certain species of moss are prized, combed and cultivated into padded ridges along the spine to cushion them against the raw stone when they sleep in the hollows. Other growths—dark, blistered algae—line their palms, allowing them to grip the slick iron railings and the condensation-slick columns without slipping.

They have eyes, but the eyes are small, recessed, almost vestigial. The light here is flat, gray, ordinary, offering little shadow and less contrast. They do not need to see across the basin; they need to read the humidity, to feel the subsonic thrum of the magnetic bearings in their teeth, to navigate by the temperature gradients that rise like invisible architecture through the packed streets. Their hearing is tactile. They press their foreheads together to speak, transferring vibrations through the frontal bone, sharing the low hum of their thoughts directly. A conversation is a quiet leaning-together, like two damp stones settling against one another in a stream.

They do not sing. Nothing here sings. But they feel. Oh, they feel.

The foundries are their churches, their kitchens, their wombs. They do not work shifts in the human sense. There is no wage, no product that leaves the basin. The conveyor belts carry material to and from the holes in the ground because the city is digesting itself, and they are its enzymes. They tend the slag heaps, urinating on the cones to fix the mineral matrix, shaping them with their hands until the crystallization takes hold and the heap becomes harder than the virgin basalt. They are not manufacturing goods. They are cultivating geology. They midwife the stone.

An adult in her prime might spend a decade at a single extrusion point, lying prone over a crack in the floor that pulses with residual heat, her body draped with damp rags to protect her from the worst of the steam, feeding the rift with powdered aggregate. Her sweat runs into the crack and mineralizes. Her hair, if she lets it grow, falls out strand by strand and is incorporated into the new rock as organic impurity, a fossil before she is even dead. She is becoming the ground. They all are.

The density is absolute. There is not a hand’s breadth of emptiness, and so there is not a hand’s breadth of solitude. They sleep in stacked cells, in hammock-webbing slung between shifting building faces, in the expansion joints that are the width of canals. When a neighborhood tilts five degrees in a month, they do not evacuate. They rehang their doors. They reweight their feet—lead soles, magnetic strips, articulated stilts. Their furniture is built on gimbals and counterweights. Their tables hang from the ceiling; their beds are slung nets that seek the level regardless of the floor’s ambition. They live in a three-dimensional braid, bridges and ladders and conduits threading between the raw columns, and they navigate it with the unconscious intimacy of blood moving through capillaries.

There are castes, though they would not use that word. There are the Deep Ones, smaller, denser, who rarely climb above the lowest thermal layers, their skin nearly black with mineral deposits, their lungs adapted to the heaviest, coldest air. They tend the deepest cracks, the warm hearts of the young basalt. There are the Bridgewalkers, light-boned, with elongated fingers that can span two hexagonal columns, who move through the upper tangles where the silent exhaust baffles vent steam. They keep the heavy birds—those silent, brooding things—as symbiotes, not pets. The birds nest in the machines, and the Bridgewalkers harvest the eggs, which are rubbery and mineral-rich, and in return they clean the baffles of fungal overgrowth that might clog the vents. And there are the Foundry Tenders, largest and hottest of all, with broad backs radiating heat through thickened, callused skin, who stand in the damp and the cold and never shiver because their metabolisms run on the geothermal memory of the rock itself.

But these distinctions are fluid. A Deep One might climb to bridge level after a seismic shift opens a new vent. A Bridgewalker might descend to give birth in the warmth, and stay. Their society is less a hierarchy than a convection current, individuals rising and falling through the thermal and physical strata as their bodies change.

They have no old age, not exactly. As they accumulate years, they accumulate mineral weight. Their joints calcify. Their skin thickens into something approaching the layered obsidian that bands the half-built walls. The elders do not die so much as slow, their metabolisms matching the cooling of the stone around them. Eventually, one will choose—or perhaps be chosen by the rift—to lie down in a warm vesicle and not rise. The stone breathes around them. The condensation drips. The moss and the ivy colonize the crevices of their immobilized bodies. Over decades, they become the architecture. A wall. A buttress. A living, warm core around which a new building is poured. They do not decay. The damp preserves them. The cold keeps them. They are the ancestors, structural and present, holding up the city with their fossilized hands.

And they are not lonely, because nothing here is lonely. The eyeless beetles trace humidity paths across their thighs. The pale moths lay eggs in the folds of their collars. The rat-shaped things—those skittering, multi-jointed creatures—share the conduits with their own young, and the distinction between the human and the post-human and the merely animal has dissolved here, too, into a single continuous topology of settlement.

The children play games in the hexagonal hollows, hiding in the gas bubbles, holding their breath when the vesicles close. They race across the tilting streets without falling, their bodies pre-adjusting to shifts they feel through the soles of their feet before the level changes. They eat the fungal shelves and the ferns that uncoil from the vesicles. They drink the condensation, the mineral rain, the weeping of the trapped air. They are soaked through, always, with the wet of the place, and they are warm at the bone where the rock remembers its birth.

Above them, the ordinary sky remains flat and gray and ignorant. But down here, in the packed, humid, crystalline silence, the inhabitants pulse with the subsonic thrum of the engines. They are the city’s nervous system, its digestive tract, its skin. They are what lives here: quiet, damp, extra-jointed, mineral-tongued, patient beyond measure. They have never known finished ground. They have never known a silence without weight. They are waiting for the stone to decide whether the rift is a wound or a mouth, and while they wait, they have become the answer.
