The ones who live here do not draw breath so much as allow breath to settle in their lungs, where it remains, damp and mineral-rich, for long spans before being slowly exhaled as a vapor that does not rise. You will not see them at first. They have colonized the stillness so thoroughly that they resemble the things of their making.

There is a figure, for instance, seated on the lip of a soapstone hearth on the third tier. At first glance it is a bundle of pale furs heaped against the stone. But the furs are their hair, grown long and fine and dusted with the same white lichen that coats the outer rim. The face is smooth, unlined not by youth but by the cessation of animation. The eyes are open, wet, catching the sourceless light with a silvery, fish-belly gleam. They are looking at something—perhaps a moth that lighted on their knee. The moth has been there for eleven summers. The figure does not blink. The figure does not need to blink.

But they are not dead. Watch the chest. It rises, imperceptibly, over the course of an afternoon, with the sound of wet wool shifting against stone. Watch the fingers, curled around a spindle of whalebone. The fingers are ivory-colored, the nails thickened and translucent as river ice. Over the course of a season, the spindle may turn a fraction of a degree, drawn by sinew cord twisted around its shaft. She—if it is a she; secondary characteristics have softened into the architecture of the bowl, becoming androgynous as dolomite—is spinning something. Not thread. Time, perhaps. She is feeding duration into the spindle, and the spindle takes it and keeps the equilibrium taut.

You will find more as your eyes adjust. The bowl is crowded with them. They occupy the city with the same density the city occupies the bowl. A broad-shouldered form bridges two walkways, gripping the limestone rail with toes that have grown long and prehensile, the better to navigate rope bridges that must never sway. He has been mid-stride for a century, his weight distributed so perfectly that the hemp has not stretched. On the lowest tier, where the mist pools thickest, a cluster of smaller figures—children, you would call them, though they are centuries deep into their youth—kneel around a basin carved from a single scapula. They are drinking, or they have just finished drinking; the water in the bone cup holds the dent of their lips but does not ripple. They are not frozen in fear or enchantment. They are simply residing in a different temporal register.

They built this place to hold a moment. Not a treasure, not a secret. They built it to hold the moment just before change—before rot, before thaw, before the outside world's insistence on becoming. They were a people of the tundra, who watched mammoths stride into bogs and not decompose, who skinned foxes that kept their snarl years past the season. They learned that cold was not absence but presence, a medium dense enough to hold form forever if respected. So they came here, where the permafrost pressed up against the sky's underbelly, and they built a vessel to hold their civilization at its apex.

Now they are neither ghosts nor statues but something more tenacious: a continuous, communal act of preservation. Their thoughts move through the root systems of the dwarf willow, which is why the willow weaves itself so faithfully across the thoroughfares—it is following neural pathways, old roads of intent. Their memories condense on the spiderwebs as moisture, and the spiders drink them, spinning new geometries from ancestral cartographies. The white moths are their exhaled dreams, powdering the gypsum with the soft stuff of sleep that was never fully released.

And there are caretakers of a kind, though the word implies too much motion. There are Those Who Tend. One moves along the upper chute where the water sheets down. You will miss her if you glance away. She is adjusting—has been adjusting—a quartz pebble in the gutter, shifting it a hair's breadth to redirect a seep that would otherwise, in ten thousand years, erode a threshold. Her hand is webbed with frost at the knuckles, not painfully, but as if her body were slowly translating into the language of ice and peat. She has been at this task since before your grandparents were born. She will be at it when your language is dust.

They do not speak. Sound is change; vibration is disturbance. But they communicate in the groans of settling wood, in the slow pressure of their bodies against sod walls, in the chemical whisper of root to root. When one of them—they would not count themselves as individuals, but as the City’s continuity—when one requires something, the moss thickens. When another dreams, a moth hatches from the dream and rests upon a beam, breathing its white wings open and shut with a movement so gradual it is finished only when the seasons turn full cycle.

The fox on the mid-tier walkway is not a fox, or not just one. It is their attention made manifest. They pour a portion of their collective regard into the shape of a canid, and it stands there, breathing, blinking, utterly silent, ready to leap should anything threaten the equilibrium. It has been ready for six hundred years. Its readiness is a fortress.

And there are deeper ones. Below the visible tiers, in the undercut root-cellars and peat-lined cavities where the light filters down through layers of translucent sod, there are the Old. They have almost finished becoming architecture. One lies against the bowl’s inner wall, limbs splayed, peat packed around the torso until the boundary between skin and wall is only a gradient of texture. The Old do not breathe at all, or rather, the entire bowl breathes for them, a slow osmotic exchange of oxygen through moss and lichen. They are the foundation now, the ballast that keeps the pocket in the earth from collapsing back into mere geography. Their thoughts are the sourceless light itself, pale, diffuse, filling every corner without origin.

If you sit quietly enough, if you match your heart to the bowl’s rhythm—one beat per hour, then one per day—you may feel what they feel. Not peace, exactly. Peace is too dynamic, implying a contrast with strife. What they feel is saturation. Completeness. The profound, cellular satisfaction of a form that has reached its exact shape and been permitted to keep it. They have no chiefs because nothing needs deciding. They have no children because nothing needs continuing. They have only maintenance at the speed of geology, and the endless, glowing present.

You may be tempted to wake them. Do not. Not because they would harm you—they would not react quickly enough to harm—but because the harm would be to the bowl, to the compromise. A shout would take years to fade here, bouncing off the gypsum, disturbing the spiders, shaking the droplets from their webs. A touch would leave oils on a sleeve that would not evaporate for generations. You are a storm to them, a sudden weather front of heat and haste and mortality. They have adjusted around you already, imperceptibly, tensing the sinews of their suspended city by microns to absorb your disturbance. By the time you climb back out through the notch in the earth, they will still be settling back into equilibrium, a process that will last until your great-grandchildren’s bones are themselves white and driftwood-silvered.

They are the residents. The keepers. The compromise made warm and breathing. And they will be here when the overcast coin of sky above finally cracks, when the permafrost thaws, and when it does not—here, holding this single, infinite note of bright and humid silence, in the pocket pressed into the earth like a thumbprint in wet clay.
