You come to know them first by the sound of their hands. Not voices—voices are flattened things here, smothered by wet air before they can travel a body-length—but the rasp and *tick* of bone on wet stone, the daily, inevitable scraping. Before you see a face, you see the knuckles: splayed, preposterously wide, the metacarpals like flippers evolved for bracing against slick travertine. They use these hands to negotiate every alley, testing the film of water on a lintel before passing beneath, spreading their weight across the ropy mineral crust as they descend. Their bodies follow the logic of the architecture: compact, low to the ground, spines curved by generations of corkscrew passages and ceilings that weep. They do not stand at full height; even in the rare open pockets they hold themselves coiled, ready to duck, as if the sky itself were a low-hanging ledge that might strike them.

They are pale—not the pallor of sunlessness alone, but the chalky half-color of the rock itself. Their skin drinks the water and holds it, trapping lime and silica in the creases of palms and the folds of elbows. Over years, these deposits tattoo them. A Scarp-dweller carries their history in raised white cartography across the forearms: here, a decade of scraping a particular doorstone; there, the ghost of sleeping against a favored, drier wall. The oldest among them are almost illegible, covered in mineral palimpsests, their skin shed so slowly that they appear to be turning into the geology they tend. Children are born slick, coated in a caul of birth-fluid that their mothers do not rush to clean; within days it cures into a fine white dust, the infant’s first interface with the place. The young are strangest: their eyes are wide, opalescent, unfocused in the grey light because they do not see by it. They see in heat. To them, the walls breathe in infrared, exhaling warmth through micro-fissures, and they point to seeps before the water arrives, reading the stone’s intentions. This sight fades as they age, calcified out of them by custom and the narrowing of need, though a few retain it—these become the Sump-Readers, those who squat by the central pool and watch its barometric pulse with pupils that dilate not toward darkness but toward thermal memory.

They call themselves, when they call themselves anything, *Those Who Scrape*. The name is literal. Each dawn—marked not by sunrise but by the retreat of the central sump—the population stirs as one organism and attacks the night’s growth. Bone blades, antler tines, knapped scrapers of greenstone: every doorway is defended. A lintel left for a day becomes a lintel half-sealed; left for a week, it is a tomb. The threshold is the boundary of the self. The sound of this labor is the city’s heartbeat, a thousand small abrading motions echoing off the young scarp, a chorus of refusal. They will not let the stone forget them. They will not allow the geology to heal over the space they occupy.

Their dead they call the Completed, but more often they are simply the Folded.

When breath stops in the bowl, the body is handled with the same practical intimacy that governs every other function. A corpse is washed not with water but with the mineral slurry of the walls, then folded—knees pressed to chest, chin down, bound in the reed mat upon which the person died. Kin carry the compact parcel up through the dripping tiers to a fault in the raw flint, a crevice still sharp-edged and unweathered by time. There, wedged into the planet’s new wound, the body is plastered with fresh lime and left. The travertine does the rest. Within a season the form is armored; within a year it is a flowstone effigy, organic darkness wrapped in crystal sleeve. The Folded do not rot in the open air; they are absorbed into the negative space, teaching the infant earth how to hold life by offering it the dead to practice on. A grandchild scraping a wall might strike a femur-shaped void inside a stalagmite, recognize the ancestor, and whisper the morning’s news. The architecture is a ledger of bodies. The city is built not only of mud and grief but of this willingness to be incorporated.

To live here is to have no skin, no perimeter of self. Privacy is a word from a drier, stupider world. A cough travels through three families; the sound of a couple coupling is as unremarked as the drip of water; a nightmare vibrates through the reed-mat ceiling to the niche above. Families are fluid, defined by sleeping proximity rather than blood. Children belong to the alley they first drew breath in, passed from shoulder to shoulder, learning the topography of shoulder-width passages as a second skin. Wet-nurses are whoever occupies the niche upslope. Warmth is not a private resource but a communal exhalation; they press together in the driest ledges at night, mammalian heat pooled in dense clusters, their breath rising in a single soft cloud that the fog swallows.

And there are the ledges. Dryness is the only currency that matters. The driest spots—where the condensate settles last, where the mineral breath of the wall exhales outward instead of weeping down—are inherited, traded, sometimes surrendered in old age as one descends inevitably toward the bogs. To hold a dry ledge is to be noble; to lose one to a debt or a dispute is to begin a slow, damp dying. The lowest dwellers, those in the standing water, tend the fermenting reed-beds that generate the slow biological heat of the lower chambers. They are the warm-makers, the bog-kin, and they trade their labor upward for algae paste and a night’s sleep on a slope where the fog lies thinner.

Their sustenance is the grey itself. They farm the glassy algal sheets on south-facing stone with the care of vintners, wiping away competing molds, scraping the green paste into troughs of carved breccia. When a weep shifts and salts the crop, they subsist on rime—the mineral crust ground and slaked into a chalky meal that fills the stomach but leaves the body craving. The sump yields pale, eyeless crustaceans, translucent ghosts that offer a fleeting sweetness on the tongue. Meat from outside is a myth, a flavor remembered only by the oldest wood-draggers who have touched the rim and returned shuddering from the assault of moving air.

Because sound dies here, their language is amphibious, half-vocal and half-tactile. They speak in low chest-tones, vowels rounded to carry through mist, consonants softened. But more reliable are the finger-glyphs traced on the wet walls—a literature of temporary instruction that blooms for an hour or a day before the condensate reclaims it: *The east channel blocks. The ledge above is warm. I am sorry.* They also click, sharp tongue-clicks against the palate that mimic the *tick* of forming crystal, audible farther than whispered words. Names are short, Thermal descriptors or scraping-sounds: *Thrum*, *Silvershoulder*, *Wetfoot*. Anger is expressed hydrostatically: a ledge withheld, a scraping deliberately undone so that a neighbor’s door begins to close.
