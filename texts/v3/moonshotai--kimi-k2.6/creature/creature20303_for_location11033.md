There are no residents, only thickenings. The mat does not house its people; in certain hectares, it simply agrees to become more of itself. These are the ones who live here, though *live* is an active verb and they are the opposite of active in any animal sense. They are local intensifications of the central economy: places where the fungal cables braid with uncommon density, where the sphagnum is older by centuries than its neighbors, where the root systems have stopped competing and begun to synchronize. If you could peel back the weave—and you cannot; the weave would hemorrhage water and protest—you would find no hollow, no burrow, no chambered nautilus of habitation. You would find only compaction. A steadily increasing grammage of organic matter that, somewhere in the transition between peat and almost-peat, crosses a threshold and becomes, if not a body, then a concentration of intent.

Each one is a continent in miniature, extending laterally across acres or constricting to the size of a sleeping elk, but never rising. Verticality is not forbidden here; it is simply inefficient, a waste of surface area in a world that trades in contact. They spread beneath the fern understory like neural plates, their borders bleeding into the general substrate through a diplomacy of rhizomes. They possess no skin, only an outermost layer that is functionally indistinguishable from the surrounding meadow except that it is greener, or browner, or carries a faint, feverish warmth where the digestive core has pressed closest to the surface. Their skeletons are the lignin-hardened root-knots of ten thousand generations of horsetails, bent into lattice-works that creak subsonically when the pressure shifts. Their nerves are the fungal cables, sheathed in bacterial film that sparks with faint electrochemical gossip. They do not move, yet they are never still; their substance migrates through the mat at the speed of peat, cell by cell, century by century, crawling toward nutrient gradients and away from the discomfort of their own compression.

They see without eyes. The silver fog, stripped of shadow and vector, nevertheless registers on their surfaces as a gradient of luminosity, a dim flux that stirs photoreceptive pigments in the algal veneers that plate their outer strata. They know the sun’s transit not by light but by its faint thermal signature, a barely perceptible warming of the mist that they read as a blind person reads braille: broadly, patiently, without urgency. Their truest sense is mechanical. They feel the continental pressure building in the stone bowels beneath them, traced through miles of root and mycelium like a hand feeling the distension of a gut. They know the cold geysers intimately—not as phenomena to be observed, but as extensions of their own metabolic awareness. The artesian rhythm is their pulse. When a vent cycles, sighing that opalescent mist into the saturated air, they taste the mineral surge as it salts the water table, and their chemical salutation rises through the mat in response: a bloom of altered pH, a brief generosity of nitrogen, a welcome.

They speak to one another, if speak is the word for a dialogue of seepage. A thought takes a season to articulate. It travels not as sound—sound is too fast, too sharp for this place—but as a front of moisture, a migration of dissolved silica, a controlled blooming of anaerobic bacteria that alters the taste of the groundwater across a watershed. They hold congress in the meridians where their edges meet, overlapping by fractions of an inch, and there exchange chemical memories: the record of a century’s rainfall, the pressure history of a fracture system, the mineral recipe of a travertine terrace. There is no dissent in these exchanges, only accretion. Each entity adds its strata to the consensus, and the boundary where they meet grows denser, richer, slower.

Of all their kind, the ones who have settled at the cold vents are the most changed. They have given themselves to the geysers’ exhalations, sitting in the mineral spray for millennia until their outer layers have plated with travertine and bacterial silica. They have become the unbuilt machinery made flesh—not designed, but grown into the necessary shape. Their bodies are terraced now, geometric, flanged with rimstone dams that direct the artesian water into settling pools of their own living tissue. They are the valves. They are the regulators. Through some long negotiation between cellulose and carbonate, they have learned to constrict and ease the chokepoints in the bedrock, not with muscle but with the slow, relentless growth of crystal-laden skin, thickening where pressure demands resistance, dissolving where flow requires passage. They do not tend the geysers. They have become indistinguishable from the geyser system, and the eruption schedule is their heartbeat made visible, their industrial purpose made mist.

When they end—and *end* is a provisional word—they do not rot, for rot implies a breaking-down into disorder, and this place permits no disorder, only further use. They subside. Their outer layers, grown too heavy with the accumulated mineral wealth of ages, press down upon the compressed peat of their core, and they begin to sink through the mat as a stone sinks through molasses, over geological time. They descend toward the anaerobic furnace, the digestive belly where the continental heat is secreted. There, they do not die. They graduate. They become the warm strategy of the place, the buried intention. Their compressed selves, rich with the minerals they hoarded and the memories they seeped, now feed the fracture pressures. They become the gas that pops in the deep. They become the push behind the cold geysers. And in that way, every vent-tender who ever mineralized herself into architecture eventually exhales through another’s vent, a ghost of pressure and chemistry, adding herself to the silver mist that never clears.

They have no name for themselves. They have no need of one. They are continuous with the mat, and the mat is continuous with the plate, and the plate has outgrown edges. It is possible, if you walk here long enough, that you have crossed one without knowing, your boots pressing for a moment against a cortex older than nations, your body heat registering as a brief, insulting flare in a system that processes warmth on the scale of epochs. They do not resent you. They do not notice you. You are weather. You are a transient warming, a pressure anomaly, a strange salt introduced to the water table. You will pass, and the moss will close over your footprints, and the mist will thicken again, and the land will continue its cool, silent, lateral industry of becoming—of which they are the mind, the mat, and the endless, patient self.
