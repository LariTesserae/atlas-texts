They do not possess a word for solitude. The concept would require a vacuum in the air, a silence between heartbeats, an arm’s length of unoccupied space, and these things do not occur. What they call themselves translates only roughly—not as a name, but as a continuous verb: *Those Who Are Currently Passing.* They are the interstitial tissue of the pocket, as necessary and as unnoticed as the air that shares their mouths.

To picture them, begin with compression. Generations of lateral existence have made them small—rarely taller than a child of the outer world—but dense, as if the body has decided that footprint matters more than reach. Their bones are slightly thickened, their joints hyper articular, capable of folding a torso into the wedge-shaped gaps between dwellings. A sternum pressed to cedar lathing, a hip turned sideways through a doorway meant for egress, not entry: this is their ordinary gait. Their spines are bowed in the permanent suggestion of a crouch, not from age but from the architecture of their lives, where ceilings are ledges and corridors are ladders. They climb more than they walk, and their feet have grown prehensile in all but name, toes splayed and muscular, the pads leathery and broad for gripping rungs worn smooth by a thousand previous descents.

Skin is the color of the rose-gray gneiss, though not by genetics alone. It is a mineral patina, decades of exfoliated stone and moth-scale dust worked into the epidermis until the people resemble the walls they cling to. Hair is sparse; scalps are instead crested with cultivated lichen, tufts of lantern-lichen transplanted and tended in a living crown that marks one’s lineage by phosphor-hue. The old woman who tends the western threshold has a scalp of such virulent chartreuse that she herself becomes a beacon, a walking doorway. Children are born with only a faint mustache of spore down, which darkens only when they are old enough to press their own waxy seed-pod vessels to the bladder-moss for their first independent drink.

Their eyes are large, black, and wet, lidless in the way of certain deep fishes, protected instead by a slow nictitating membrane that slides laterally from the inner canthus. In the statistical light, ordinary blinking would lose too many photons; their vision is a cumulative practice, a patience of rods and cones that pools gold dust into coherence. They do not see sharply. They see thermally and texturally, reading the warmth of basalt, the cooler shimmer of a dew-slick obsidian blade, the fevered silhouette of a bird against the pale butter shaft. By contrast, their hearing is almost vestigial in the conventional sense—there is too much roar for nuanced audition. Instead, they have developed a sensitivity to pressure. They feel sound through the soles of their feet, through the hardwood pegs of their bridges. A bat’s shriek in the upper dark registers as a needle of vibration in the mastoid bone. They communicate by compound means: a glottal click that cuts through the cricket drone, a hand pressed flat against another’s sternum to speak in subsonic thrum, or simply by the rearrangement of bodies in space. A shoulder turned away is a negation. A belly offered is a question.

The day begins in theft. Before the dawn reversal, when the mountain still exhales upward and the air is at its most desiccated, the dew-scrapers ascend. They are not a caste, exactly, but a temporary condition, like being submerged. Anyone might become a scraper if they wake in the dark with a dry mouth. They climb the inner face with bone picks and obsidian blades—their most hoarded objects, flakes of volcanic glass knapped to a concavity that fits the thumb—and they seek the high, unplanted surfaces where nightly thermal contraction has drawn the last vapor into micro-droplets on the stone. It is a race against the embargo. The moment a bead forms, the plants know. The glassine hairs of the mattress-floor begin to stir. The scrapers slice the dew from the rock with gestures so practiced they seem like grooming, gathering the moisture into waxy seed-pod cups. To fail is to feel the stone go matte under the blade as the roots below inhale the prize. There is no punishment for failing except thirst, and thirst is the constant gravity of the pocket.

They farm, but not in the outer sense of dominion. Their agriculture is an act of negotiation. They stuff crevices with cuttings, not planting *at* the rock but *into* it, begging purchase from the stone. They trim the moss-fur that carpets the bridges, harvesting the trimmings into fermented wads stored in gut-bladders. They crawl among the aerial roots of the dominant vine, massaging its nodes to encourage the waxy pods that become their only vessels. They tend the heavy thicket-beasts not as livestock but as landscape features, coaxing the creatures into corridors where eventually, inevitably, the beasts die and their corpses are instantly claimed—roots punching through lattice floors, insects boiling up from the mattress, the people themselves gathering to flense the meat and crack the broad thigh bones for tools before the green can fully swallow the offering. Nothing is slaughtered. Everything is timed.

Their waste falls through. It is the law of the floors. Lattice and slat and interwoven root lets the dry, compacted pellets of their bodies pass immediately downward, into the churn of detritivores, into the root-hairs, into the closed loop. There is no ritual around excretion because there is no distance from it. A body that holds its waste is a body hoarding illegally, stealing from the commons. Even grief is processed this way. When one dies, the body is not lowered but laid directly onto the floor mattress, and the floor accepts it with the alacrity of thirst. The survivors do not weep; tears are moisture, and moisture belongs to the embargo. They press their foreheads to the departing sternum and hum a note low enough to vibrate the sternum itself, a last communication through the shared medium of bone. Then they step back, and the plants close over, and the lizards blink, and the person becomes another layer in the coral.

They are not unaware of the slit, but they do not think of it as an aperture to an *elsewhere*. Nothing in their language corresponds to *outside.* The slit is merely the Mouth, an organ of the mountain’s slow respiration, sometimes inhaling, sometimes exhaling. It brings the moth columns and the scentless air, and these are not visitors but weather. They have myths of the outer slopes, but only as a kind of inverted cosmology: they speak of the Great Smoothing, an infernal realm without surfaces, where nothing can cling, where water runs uselessly in streams (a concept they find grotesque, like bleeding to death on the ground), and where life is too dispersed to touch itself. They believe the mountain swallowed the world to save it, folding the lush into this pocket to keep it from the entropy of distance.

Their time is measured by the accretion of dwellings. A generation is called a *Squeeze*—the interval between the laying of one floor level and the moment the rising mattress of root and compost forces the construction of another. They count age not in years but in how many times the mountain’s thermal breath has reversed during one’s memory. A child is new-vent. An elder is pre-whistle, someone who remembers when the upper vents were fewer and the bats fewer still.

At dusk, when the walls exhale and the updraft whistles, the people pause. They hang in their swallows’-nest rooms, they press their carved bone flutes to their lips—not to play melodies, but to add their own frequencies to the roar, completing the chord. It is an act of acoustic sufficiency, a declaration that they are part of the sound-wall. In those moments, individual bodies blur. A mother and her offspring do not end at skin; they are the same warm volume, sharing the cup of pressed bladder-moss, breathing the other’s exhalation immediately back into the lung. The warmth makes them drowsy. The moth scales settle on their cheeks. They do not dream of open spaces. They dream of density: the comfort of a bridge that holds, the wet crunch of a succulent leaf, the absolute safety of being held on all sides by breathing stone.

They will not leave. They cannot leave. They have become the final organ of the mountain—a culture not of expansion but of packing, of agreeing to be the last possible life in a place that has forgotten how to die. They are small, gray, moist, many, and loud in their silence. They are the inheritors of embargoed moisture, the citizens of the squeeze, the ones who passed, are passing, and will pass, pressed together in the warm, dark, thriving throat of the ruin.
