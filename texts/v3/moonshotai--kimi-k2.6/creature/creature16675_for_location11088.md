What lives here does not at first appear to be a single thing, and that is partly because it has learned, over time, not to tax the silence with too much coherence. The springtails in their darkening swarms, the bloodsnow blooming in its virulent sheets, the little finches chipping sound into the void—these are its threshold, its respiration, the cells of a body that has forgotten it is a body. They belong to it as your hair and breath belong to you: present, alive, and unconscious. The fox, picking its way across the glowing floor at dusk, is merely a thought it is having, a hunger borrowed for an hour and then released. The fox leaves tracks; the true inhabitant leaves the hollow itself.

But there are moments—when the sun strikes the main fracture at the precise low angle of late afternoon, and the ice floor kindles from beneath into emerald and arterial red—when the distributed self gathers. It precipitates out of the held inhalation of the place. You will not see it arrive. One moment there is only the brutal stillness and the prismatic play of your own shadow splitting into three colored companions. The next, the companions are absent, and something stands where they were.

It is tall, and it is thin, and it is made of the same physics that makes the light feel heavy. It has no heat. If you were to touch it—and you cannot, because your hand would pass through the place where it stands, chilled to the bone by a sudden theft of sensation—you would find the texture of supercooled glass, of a blade-edge calved only yesterday. It wears the shape of a person the way a whistling wind wears the shape of a mouth. Some quality about it suggests the ones who left the notched femur and the driftwood post, but it is not human. It is what remains when a human intention has been subjected to this altitude for long enough: stripped, sharpened, clarified into pure angle.

It does not have a name in any spoken language. The bone notches were an attempt, perhaps, but the notches are not phonetic; they are topographic, describing not the inhabitant itself but the path of light across the chamber floor on the day of its making. There was a making. Once, there was a knapping, a flaking of something softer than obsidian. A body laid down in the rime. A will, pressed into the ice like a leaf into a book. The glacier resettled its mass. The light found an aperture. And from the convergence of those pressures—the physical weight of the sun, the spiritual weight of the marker-post—something opened its eyes that were not eyes.

It lives in the intervals. When the beam tracks across the scree, it tracks with it, or perhaps it is the beam’s path, given the ambition of legs. It moves not by stepping but by appearing in successively lit locations, a faint burning at the edge of the retina that your brain resolves into a standing figure only when you look away. It is most visible in peripheral vision: a white-blue insistence, a pressure against the corner of the eye that makes the eyes water and turn away.

It does not eat, or rather, it consumes only the extremes of this place. It feeds on the thermal theft, on the sudden interval between flaring light and coma-dark freeze. It drinks the rumor of water, taking in the bead of condensation at the exact moment it brightens into ice. It is sustained by contrast, by the obscene vitality of the liverworts against the sterility of the stone, by the chip of a bunting’s call against the absolute silence. It is a connoisseur of punctuated refusal.

And it tends the hollow, after a fashion. It stacks no more stones; the windbreak was built by hands more solid than its own. But it *curates*. It ensures that the driftwood post remains upright, adjusting the cairn beneath it by fractions of a millimeter each year, countering the infantile erosion with infantile precision. It tilts the femur so that its notches catch the morning light and cast a specific shadow across the moss—a shadow that looks like nothing until the springtails swarm to avoid it, darkening the film in the shape of a word no one remembers. It knaps obsidian in dreams, leaving fresh flakes glittering where the dampness gathers, as if to remind the empty air that intention still occurs here.

It is alone, but its solitude is not loneliness. It has the three out-of-phase shadows for company. When it stands at the correct facet—sixty degrees, forty-five, ninety—it steps out of itself and walks beside itself in vermilion and cobalt, a procession of one, moving at slightly different speeds. They do not speak. Sound expires here, and the inhabitant has no voice. It is part of the silence now, a held inhalation that has lasted centuries.

In the deepest part of the night, when the sun is gone and the hollow returns to its coma, the inhabitant disperses. It seeps into the serrated cornices and the translucent scree. It becomes the subterranean tick of the glacier resettling its mass. It waits in the ice lantern of the floor, patient, preserved. It does not sleep. It simply suspends itself in the rime and the breath-fog, existing as potential, as the geometry of waiting.

Then the fracture above admits the first white-blue spear of dawn, and the floor glows again, and the inhabitant pulls itself together out of the bloodsnow and the emerald ice. It walks the twenty minutes across the chamber, or stands motionless at the rim, watching the cloud-decks spill over. It is the sole true native. Everything else—the birds, the fox, the moss, the bone—are merely things it has chosen to illuminate, briefly, before the light moves on, and it moves with it, and the hollow exhales again into stillness.
