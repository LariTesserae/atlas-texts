The thing that lives in the cathedral is not an animal.

Call it a body, if you must, though it has no face, no axis of symmetry, no hide or skin in any zoological sense. It occupies the nave like a filling in a tooth, pressed between the walls by the weight of the water outside and the greater weight of its own internal noise. It is the color that results when verdigris bleeds into fresh heartwood: a saturated copper-green, darkening at the loci where its mass meets the dormant machinery. For the machines inside do not move. They only hum, and the thing has grown into them, or they have grown into it, until the distinction is as meaningless as the boundary between liquid and solid in the liquefying floor. Pistons the width of redwood trunks stand upright in its matrix, their shafts embedded in lignin that rings like wet ceramic when the pressure shifts. Cooling pipes run through its outer flanks, not as prosthetics but as veins, carrying not water but something hotter, stranger—a sap of dissolved oxides and reality-effluent that conducts both nutrition and electrical impulse. It breathes, not with lungs, but with the respiration of the whole basin. When the warm water draws in through the blown windows, it is drawing breath; when it plumes outward in thermal ghosts, it is exhaling into the turquoise murk.

It has no eyes, yet it senses. The absolute prohibition on faunal complexity does not apply to it because it predates the prohibition, or caused it, or simply occupies the blindspot where zoological law no longer reaches. It knows the world by vibration, by pressure, by the torque of broken light around its edges. The sonic mass that pours in through the windows does not batter it; it feeds it. Sound is its photosynthesis. The basso profundo of settling plates, the teeth-grinding shriek of oxidizing steel, the wet vegetative creak of exploding cellulose—all of it accretes in its tissues, laminating layer upon layer, year upon year, until its outer integument is not merely organic but *recorded*, a tympanic library of every groan this place has uttered since the breakage. If you could press your palm to it, you would not feel warmth alone; you would feel the stored tremor of a continent cracking, a low-frequency shudder trapped forever in cellulose and alloy. It grows heavier with time, denser, more itself, because here sound does not fade and neither does the body that harvests it.

The rules that govern it are proprietary to this basin. It grows without seeding, as all vegetation here grows, condemned to hypertrophy without the completion of a cycle. It will never fruit. It will never flower. It will never die and rot, because nothing here has lived long enough to die, and it was the first, and its unfinished nature set the precedent for everything else. It is always beginning. Meristems at its surface—which is not skin but a kind of wet, perpetually exfoliating bark—flicker with new growth that hardens into iron-laced wood before the old growth can recede. It is a perpetual engine of becoming, a lathe turning itself on a spindle that has no head and no tail. And because it does not complete, nothing around it completes. The water warms against its flanks but never boils; the machines that service its metabolism run but never finish their intended manufacture; the stones beneath it, subjected to the endless low note of its hum, have begun to liquefy, unable to resolve themselves into a final state. It is the original incomplete cycle, the broken equation from which all other local impossibilities derive.

Outside, the basin runs riot with vegetable life, rapacious and insane with lushness. But that life is not separate from the thing in the cathedral. It is its slough. Its sheddings. The outer garden is an autobiography written in copper moss and kelp-analog and compressed vegetation: fragments of its own body that drifted out through the windows, carrying spores and iron filings and trapped harmonic frequencies, exploding into growth in the warm nutrient slurry. The growth parts around the structure like a nervous system avoiding a tumor because it *is* the tumor—the dense, impossible node from which the cancerous lushness metastasized. The outer flora knows, in the blind chemical language of tropism, that to touch its source is to be reabsorbed into the hum, to lose the borrowed autonomy of the periphery and become once again suffused mass inside the walls. So they give it a wide berth, creating a clearing of reverent absence, a halo of quiet dread in a world that knows no quiet.

And at the absolute low point, in the fractionally more broken reality within the structure, it continues. It does not sleep; sleep is a cycle, and cycles are prohibited. It does not think, not in the predatory animal sense, but it transduces. It converts the pressure of noise into structure, the warmth into growth, the curving light into chemical instruction. It is the refinery now. The dissolution plant dissolved, the foundry transfigured. It processes H₂O, or light, or the concept of entropy, and what it produces is itself—more bark, more cambium, more ferric cellulose, more of the low, sourceless hum that keeps the stones liquid and the windows open. It is young. Violently, catastrophically young. It has not existed long enough to know what it is making of itself. It has never been silent long enough to hear its own shape. It is the only citizen of the basin, the sole articulate presence in the deafening parliament of groans, the warm, roaring heart of a world that forgot to stop becoming itself.
