They do not stand so much as occupy vertical space, filling the interstices between stone and fog with a presence that is humid, mammalian, and deliberate. The Scarp-dwellers—Those Who Scrape, the Folded—are compact by necessity and by evolution, their bodies low-slung and dense, weighted beneath the collarbone by lungs that have learned to draw sustenance from air thick enough to drown a lighter creature. Their stature is not stooped but *folded*: joints thick and closely articulated, as if their skeletons have already begun the slow compression that the geology demands. Heads sit low on necks widened for the carriage of water-skins and stone-milk troughs. Feet splay into spatulate platforms, the toes prehensile and strong, gripping travertine that has never known dryness. Their gait is a shuffling adhesion, a controlled slip.

Skin is the boundary where the body negotiates with the hole, and the negotiation has left its treaty marks across every surface. The white deposit-scars are not blemishes; they are cartographies. Across shoulders, down the hollow of throats, webbing the knuckles, the lime-rich water has etched permanent river-deltas, pale and slightly raised, a topography of old weeps and forgotten postures. Every inhabitant wears the same map, though each is unique in its tributaries. The youngest are still pink-fleshed, still soft, but by the age of first scraping—a ritual measured not by years but by the ability to hold an antler curl steadily against a drip-line—the mineral glaze has begun to set. Skin does not tan here; it accretes. Hair, where it grows, mats into felted cords heavy with travertine dust, wearing slowly into rope-like topknots that clack softly against stone when they bend to their work. Breath plumes from every mouth, adding to the communal cloud, and when they press close for sleep in the stacked niches, their exhalations condense on one another, so that by morning a family of five may be lightly fused at the shoulders by a rime of their own making.

The hands are the primary instruments of identity. Wide-handed, yes, but it is more than span: the palms are broadened into shovels by decades of spreading slurry, the fingers thickened at the pads until the prints have been worn smooth and replaced by a horny, translucent integument. Nails do not flake here; they laminate, layer upon layer of keratinized response to alkaline water, until they resemble natural trowels or the cuspids of some digging beast. A child of the Folded learns to scrape before it learns to speak. The gesture is the first language: the diagonal slash that says *this threshold is mine*, the horizontal sweep that says *water, pass through*, the tight orbital curl around a weep-hole that means *grow here, brace this wall*. Every family covets not merely a dry ledge but a distinctive stroke, a signature in negative space carved each dawn into the fresh stone-flesh, a mark that will be obliterated by noon’s fog and remade again before the next darkening.

To see them at work is to watch a single organism tend to its own shell. There is no privacy because there are no individuals in the wet-economy sense—only *positions*, only *functions*. A person is the space they currently occupy multiplied by the heat they exude. The young, those born inside the bowl without memory of the outer marsh, possess the strangeness of adaptation: their eyes do not focus as yours or mine do. In the diffuse silver-grey where shadows are theoretical, they have learned to read the infrared exhalations of the weeping walls. They lead their elders through the warren not by torchlight but by the thermal signature of a fresh seep, the crimson bloom of a fermenting reed-bed below, the cool blue blank of a clogged sluice. Elders navigate by the arterial music of drips and the taste of barometric pressure on the tongue, but the children are heat-seers, their irises adjusting to spectra where stone breathes and mammalian bodies glow like coals in a fog. They point, and the tribe follows, occupying the warm veins in the rock as blood occupies a limb.

Among the Folded, the self is continuous. A mood is a communal weather system; a fever is a village concern. They know the lung-condition of their neighbour because the air they share is barely sufficient for two, let alone twenty. Their voices are flattened instruments, attenuated by the mist to a conversational range narrower than an outstretched arm, so much of their discourse is tactile—hand-pressure on a shoulder blade in a passageway, the sharing of a scraping rhythm, the transfer of warmth in a sod niche. They do not name themselves with the florid individualism of ridgelands peoples. A person is called by their relation to the sump’s breath: *Rising-He-Who-Sluices*. *She-of-the-South-Face-Algae*. *Third-Cusp-Above-the-Brine*. Identity is topoanatomical, a function of where one sits in the breathing geometry of the hole.

They eat the colour green, and they eat stone. The algae films scraped from south-facing faces are reduced to paste—mineral-sweet, chlorophyll-bitter, the taste of sustained life. It smears across reed-bread, ferments in intestine and reed-bed alike, generating the slow biologic heat that keeps the lower chambers from freezing. The white rime, the mineral bloom, is harvested as famine-food, chewed until the salts spark on the tongue. The pale crustaceans drawn from the sump are too rare to be mere nourishment; they are omens, their translucent flesh shared in thimblefuls among the nearest dozen, a sacrament of the outside that has drifted down into the new. Meat is a myth told by the oldest, those few who remember the drag of timber from the marsh flats, who speak of chewing muscle that once moved under its own power. The young do not crave it. Their bodies have forgotten the chemistry of predation; they are lithovores now, symbionts of the infant earth.

Water is their clock and their scripture. The central sump rises toward evening, retreats by dawn, and in its pulse the Folded read not only time but intention. There are those who tend it—no priests, only attendants—who watch the lagged luminosity on heavy-fog days when the pool holds sunlight from an hour not yet arrived, re-emitting a silver-green depthlessness that answers questions put to it by reed stalks dropped from a measured height. The barometric inhalation is consulted before any new channel is cut, before a niche is excavated, before a body is laid into the wall (for the dead are not carried out, but pressed into the softest mud between stone plates, there to mineralize into the architecture within a season). To poison the sump is the only unforgivable sin. Waste, instead, is borne up the sharp rim in a procession that resembles penance more than sanitation—litters of compressed reed and human night-soil hauled in shifts, a pilgrimage to the old world above, where openness yawns vertiginously and the wind moves without obstruction. Those who make this climb return shaken, wrapped in borrowed blankets, unable for hours to speak of the terrible unbounded grey.

They understand, in their bones and in their scrapers, that they dwell in a rejection—a place the planet has not yet agreed to keep. The young scarp still pings with micro-fractures, still settles in its skin. The Folded answer this uncertainty with the scrape. Every morning the thresholds are cleared, the lintels planed, the weeps encouraged into their proper channels. It is not maintenance; it is conversation. With wide hands they grip the infant stone and say, *We are here. We fit. Include us*. They are not tenants but midwives, easing the geology into its own existence, insisting that a wound can also be a mouth, a hollow can also be a home. In the active silence of the bowl they persist—another species of accretion, soft and warm and stubborn, growing alongside the travertine, keeping pace with the rot, matching the world’s newest point with their own relentless, tender pressure.
