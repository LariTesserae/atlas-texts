The ones who live here are the sound itself, at first—the continuous structural roar of their own making. They are the lignin titans, the cambium courts, though those names suggest animals, and these are nothing of the kind. Each spire is a being the way a cathedral is a being: not because it thinks, but because its entire structure is an argument about force and water, maintained without rest. They have no faces, yet they have faces; the channels grooved into their bark are an expression constantly rewriting itself. You can read a tower’s recent disposition in the width of its aqueducts: widened channels mean a surge of upward thirst, lignin aligning to brace for ascent; narrowed, pinched grooves mean it is consolidating, pouring its substance inward before some anticipated internal division. They speak in hydraulic pressure. They greet each other across the kilometer-wide silences by shifting their buttresses, altering the tension in their upper foliage so that the foghorn drones between them become a slow, ground-bass conversation. It is not language. It is load-bearing mathematics made audible.

If you could scale one—though there are no animals here to do the climbing—you would discover that the architecture is not hollow but crowded. The capillary tunnels, wide as subway tubes, are boulevards of lightless industry. Here lives the second order of inhabitants: the thermophilic civilizations. Biofilms of archaea and bacteria coat the tunnel walls in luminous strata, the source of that faint phosphorescence pulsing in the pavement below. They are not passengers. They are the legislators. Their metabolic heat dictates where the wood will soften and where it will harden; their collective chemical vote determines where a new branch will cantilever and where a platform will deliquesce back into sap. The tower does not think, but it obeys the thermal democracy of its own guts. In the hottest root-marrow, near the geothermal interface, extremophile bacteria process the planet’s deep heat and translate it into chemical instructions that ride the ascending water, upward, upward, a capillary telegraph older than cognition.

And then there is the ground again, the great Third. The pavement beneath your feet is not a floor but a skin—the calcified, petrified dermis of the continent’s deep rhizomatic cortex, the mycelial body whose true mass lies miles below and miles wide. The towers are not separate from it; they are its sudden, eruptive fingers, exploratory and nervous. The black resin-ceramic is extruded sealant, an immune response of the deep body against the wound of uplift. It drinks the water because the cortex drinks the water. It patches its own cracks because the cortex cannot tolerate breach. When the pavement shudders beneath your feet, that is not geology; that is the substrate flexing, testing whether the plateau is ready to be wholly enveloped again, or whether these new hot spires are worth maintaining. The cortex remembers the root-sea below. It is patient. Its timescale is not growth but sedimentation.

All across the shoulders of the titans, where the upward-veering water loses its battle with gravity and weeps into the baking mist, lives a fourth order: the epiphytic symbiotes. These are not towers; they are opportunists, air-rooted filter-gardens that cling to the high flares in clusters like green ceramic barnacles. They serve as cooling vanes and mist-traps, colonizing architectural stress-points where a tower’s own channels have not yet reached. They are distinct, smaller, sharp-leaved and heat-tolerant. In exchange for real estate they stabilize the young bark with their root-mats and exude resins that seal micro-fissures. They are the closest thing to skin-flora here, ornamental and vital at once, the only organisms that choose their homes rather than being extruded by the deep plan.

And the seeds—oh, the seeds. The ballistic ovules. They are the only things that ever travel in straight lines. Each one is a self-pressurized embryo, an oil-drum of compressed potential that sleeps for months in high cradle-cupolas, drinking hydraulic force until its seams are drums. Then: the cannon report, the crack of departure. They are the emigrants, the colonialists, the desperate arguments fired across the steaming void. Most will miss, smashing to paste against the obsidian skin where the seal-lichens will consume them and patch the scar with phosphorescent tissue. But those that strike true—those that embed in the flank of a distant tower or in the rare nutrient-rich gash of new uplift—will not sprout a tree. They will extrude a city. They will grow the next right-angle, the next aqueduct, the next foghorn drone. Every tower was first a wound in another tower’s side, or in the cortex itself.

Between them, in the silences, drift the last inhabitants: the fungal hyphae that thread the humidity like smoke. They do not build; they negotiate. They are the mortar between the bricks of the substrate, the translators between the deep cortex and the young spires. They unfurl microscopic fruiting bodies after every ballistic impact, sampling the genetic news, updating the mycelial network with the chemistry of success or failure. They are the librarians of this place, their records written in nitrogen and phosphorus, unreadable to anything but root and time.

There is no predator, no prey. There is only addition and subtraction, the shearing and the bridging. A tower splits, steams, rejoins itself higher—a mitosis that is also a redesign. The cortex exhales a new shoot that punches through the obsidian skin, already knowing architecture, already knowing water. The archaea shift their quorum and a branch turns ninety degrees. The epiphytes drink the mist and cool the shoulder. The seed sleeps, pressurizes, waits for its thrust.

They are all one body distributed across scales, or they are ten thousand bodies coincidentally aligned toward structure. It hardly matters. They live as architecture lives: by the relentless conversion of heat and height into form, by an ancient agreement among bacteria and cellulose and deep steam to build something that needs no mind to want, desperately, to stand.
