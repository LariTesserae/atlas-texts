There is no creature here, in the beginning. There is only occupation: a body taken up by the necessity of converting one mineral into another. If you look closely at the shingle-work cladding the raw basalt walls, you will not find a colony of shells. You will find **caliper-beasts**—though “beast” is a kindness their anatomy does not deserve. Each is a latch. Each is a hinge. Two valves of oxidized iron, gray and flaking with volcanic black, joined by a ridge of translucent silica that serves as knuckle and seal. They affix themselves to the vertical faces in tessellated ranks, not growing downward like barnacles but outward like rivet heads on the hull of a ship. Their forty-second cycle is the nearest thing the chamber has to a heartbeat: ratchet open, filter the metal-thick water, grind the suspended precipitate between iron plates, ratchet shut. The sound is not a snap but a slow exhalation of scissors, a hisssssss that runs wall to wall, millions of overlapping apertures breathing mineral breath. A young one, no larger than a thumbnail, may spend a week traversing a meter of virgin rock, creeping on a film of its own secretion, its valves opening and closing like a man pacing a cell. The elders no longer move. They have fused to the wall, become the wall, their edges indistinguishable from the basalt they clasp. They are the chamber’s way of keeping its own wounds shut.

Drifting in the dense, gelid water above the floor, the **torque-salps** deny gravity not through buoyancy but through sheer mechanical persistence. They hang in curtains of thousands, each individual a link in a living ammunition belt, translucent segments threaded one to the next by tissue as thin and tough as oiled silk. From each segment protrudes the helical shell: a drill-bit, a screw-thread, a turbine carved by accretion rather than lathe. In any other sea they would swim, but here the water is too crowded, too thick with incumbent bodies and suspended salts; instead, they rotate. Each helix turns as if screwing upward through a medium just shy of solid, harvesting dissolved sulfate and native copper from the alkaline brine. The motion is minute—perhaps a full turn per minute—but taken in aggregate, twenty thousand helices churning in unison generate the bass thrum that lives in the jawbone and the inner ear. They do not think; they transmit. They are the drive belts of an engine room suspended in its own coolant, passing nutrients hand over hand along the chain, their bodies little more than translucent housings for the turning.

Between the chimney-stacks, the **loom-striders** have become architecture. They span the gaps with legs like polished drill-rods, four or six or eight depending on the distance to cross, each leg terminating in a secretion that welds them to igneous glass or to the flanges of mineral boilers. They do not move from their stations. They have gone beyond mobility; they are the rigging. From their abdomens extrude threads of pure pyrite, drawn fine as wire, which they weave into nets that do not entrap but channel. A chemical gradient is a kind of wind here, and the nets are sails set to catch it, funneling hydrogen-rich brine toward waiting mouths, distributing redox potential across the foundry floor. Their legs jitter in spasms—tick, tick, tick—setting up standing waves in the brittle webs, a sound like a wrench tapping a pipe to test its integrity. They feel the mountain’s digestion through vibration alone. They are the chamber’s nervous system, its telephone wires, trembling at the news of a fresh vent opening a new fracture three meters away.

And in the open corridors between these fixed points float the **gauge-jellies**, the only true drifters, though they are no more soft than the rest. Their bells are a transparent caul of collagen-like tissue, within which turn vanes of native copper, precise and balanced, slowly sailing on the chamber’s sluggish convection. They are the closest this place comes to ornament, yet their beauty is strictly instrumental: the vanes are shaped like regulator springs, like dial faces, like the anemometers of a weather station. They do not feed by filtering alone; they live off the redox differential between the alkaline vent-water and the cold, oxygenated flow from above, their copper wings turning in a bath of dissolved electron. When two bells graze in the current, the vanes touch and ring—a clear, dropped-coin note, infrequent and exact. They are not creatures but readouts, evolved instruments that register the pressure, the pH, the slow geological temper of the room. They chime not for joy but because the data of their collision demands an acoustic signature.

Beneath the caliper-beasts, creeping over the knife-rubble of the floor, move the **film-processors**: sheets of matte tissue the color of tarnished pewter, sliding between shards that would shred anything with blood. They are the digesters, the lowest clerks of the operation, secreting acids that dissolve fresh glass, precipitating phosphates into the interstices, paving the sharpness with a slow, creeping polish. Where they pass, the basalt’s new edges begin, imperceptibly, to blunt. They have no distinct shape; they flow like mercury, dividing around obstructions and reuniting, leaving behind a trail of solidified sulfur compounds that the caliper-beasts will later grind.

Higher up, near the ceiling of needles, dwell simpler **bell-forms**, smaller cousins to the gauge-jellies perhaps, lacking copper mechanisms but pulsing slowly in the dark, contracting their bells once every several minutes to drive themselves in tiny arcs through the dense water. They are the chamber’s idle thoughts, directionless and pale.

And there are others still—nameless accretions, sessile colonies budding from fractures like weld beads holding the rock together, tube-forms and flange-worms that have given up locomotion entirely to become part of the chimney machinery, adding their own thin exhalations to the subsonic drone.

None of these beings hunger as warm creatures hunger. Their urgency is mineral. A caliper-beast does not seek prey; it seeks charge. A torque-salp does not flee a predator; there is nothing here that kills in the sudden, wet manner of jaws and dissolution. Death, when it comes, is precipitation, is silting-up, is the slow choking of one’s own exhaust as the chimney grows over you. The loom-strider dies when its web pulls loose from a fracturing stack and it hangs slack, a broken circuit. The gauge-jelly dies when its copper vanes, after centuries of turning, finally deplete their gradient and seize, sinking to become a strange coin on the rubble floor, eventually engulfed by a film-processor and recycled.

What lives here, then? Not a menagerie. An assemblage. A distributed engine whose components happen to eat, happen to reproduce, happen to crowd the chamber beyond what its small volume should allow. They are process made flesh—no, made harder than flesh. They are the living answer to a question the mountain asked when it split open twenty years ago: what is the most efficient shape for survival in a cold, sharp, mineral-rich wound? The answer was the piston. The filter. The chimney. The sieve.

They are forty paces of patient, mechanical fertility, humming at the wrong speed, loud with the industry of a biology that never learned to be soft. They do not know one another. They do not need to. They fit together, edge to edge, tick to tick, helix to helix, chiming and hissing and thrumming in the overcast light, turning the cathedral of stone into a single, breathing, unplanned machine.
