If there are presences here that could be called inhabitants—rather than mere occupants—they do not reveal themselves through movement. To look for them as one looks for deer in a forest, or for heads in windows, would be to misunderstand the terms of their existence. They are not hidden; they are confluent. The plateau has dissolved its rock over epochs, and in the same manner, gently, it has dissolved the boundary between organism and stone.

You will know them first by the absence of algae.

In a world where every seep and drip-line runs green with microscopic hunger, there are places—ovals of stone at shoulder height, patches no broader than a splayed hand—where the biofilm has been erased with methodical patience. The stone there is paler, cleaner, licked to the smoothness of old porcelain. If you pressed your palm to such a place, it would feel fractionally warmer than the surrounding wall, as if something had rested there recently and had taken the chill into itself. These are the grazing marks. The feeders move through the avenues not as herds but as solitary punctuation, harvesting the thin living rind from the towers with tongues that are muscular, mineral-adapted, and older in their lineage than the corridors themselves.

They are tall. The architecture of the karst forced this. A creature that would navigate the lower avenues must be narrow enough to pass between the opposing walls, yet able to reach upward to the higher seeps where the crustose lichens fruit most abundantly. Natural selection here has favored the vertical. They stand between two and three meters in height, but their width—shoulder to shoulder—is rarely more than the breadth of a human skull. Their chests are laterally compressed, their ribs overlapping like the ribs of a fan, their lungs long and set high to accommodate the shallow profile. Their spines are articulated with extra vertebrae, giving their posture a continuous, questing curve, as if they were always leaning slightly forward to hear the stone.

They have almost no color. Their skin is the precise matte gray-green of damp dolomite, not through pigment alone but through symbiosis. Their epidermis hosts a colony of calcium-fixing bacteria identical to those that stain the towers, so that a resting individual becomes visually indistinguishable from a fluted column. They do not grow hair; instead, their skin weeps a faint saline mucus that holds the mist and encourages the growth of the very lichens they eat. They are mobile gardens. A starving individual simply stops moving, stands in a place of good drip, and feeds on the crop that accumulates across its own shoulders until the season turns. They carry their pastures with them.

Because sound here is smothered, and light is merely a brighter or dimmer gray, their senses have migrated into other registers. Their eyes, when they open, are small and protected behind thick, cloudy lenses; they see thermal gradients the way others see color. A warm seep is a beacon. A cool sinkhole is a pit. The rest of the time, the eyes are sealed. Their primary sense is a broad, flattened nerve pad that runs from the palms of their hands, along the inner wrists, and up the inner arms to the armpits. When they walk—and they walk always with their arms spread, fingertips brushing both walls—they are reading the corridor. The temperature of the stone, its exact degree of saturation, the taste of minerals dissolved in the film of water, the vibration of distant drips: this is how they map the continent. They know the plateau as a continuous textural narrative. They can feel, through the stone, the weight of a cloudbank moving in from the western escarpment hours before it arrives. They can detect, by the subtle cooling of a wall, whether a swallow hole has opened a new drainage channel somewhere below.

They do not speak. They have no voice, no percussive language, no music. In the rare event that two meet in a passage too narrow to pass, they engage in something closer to chemical negotiation. They press their foreheads together, and the film of water between their skins acts as a conductor. Salts, hormones, and the mineral signature of their recent paths exchange across this bridge. It is not a conversation in sentences but a transference of itinerary. One offers the taste of an iron-rich seep three days north; the other responds with the location of a recent hatch of the pale limestone moths. Then one retreats into a side fracture, and the encounter is concluded without friction.

They are not numerous. The plateau cannot support density. A single individual may patrol a territory of two hundred square kilometers, following a route that takes eleven years to complete before it repeats. They remember the route not in memory but in their feet. The particular abrasions of a certain corridor, the exact width of a plaza, the angle of a leaning pinnacle: these are their mnemonics. Their brains are small but distributed; clusters of neural tissue are lodged in their limbs, particularly in the long bones of the forearms, allowing them to process sensory input locally, with a slowness that matches the reactiveness of stone.

They are oviparous. The female finds a chamber where the redeposition of calcite is active, where the atmosphere is heavy with aerosolized calcium. She deposits a single, leathery egg in a drip pool. The egg is dense, white, and slightly soluble. Over seven years, it accretes a shell not of calcium carbonate but of the same bacteria-silicate film that coats the towers, so that by the time it hatches, it is already a pebble, already camouflaged. The young emerge fully formed but miniature, no larger than a human forearm, with skin so thin it is translucent. They immediately begin grazing. They grow for forty years, reaching their full height with a slowness that makes their development almost indistinguishable from erosion.

Death, when it arrives, is not an end but a change of pace. They do not collapse. Their metabolism simply slows below the threshold of movement, and they choose a niche—a recess in a corridor wall, a sheltered lee behind a pinnacle—and lock into place. Their bodies, dense with the calcium they have consumed and concentrated, resist decay. The bacteria on their skin continue their work. Over centuries, the body does not fossilize in the catastrophic manner of sudden burial; instead, it calcifies in situ, layer by layer, as the dripping water redeposits the minerals they ate in life. They become stalagmites. They become the small, anomalous ridges on the floor of plazas. They become the strange, organic swellings on corridor walls that later travelers—if there were travelers—might mistake for intentional sculpture. And in this way, they finally join the architecture. The standing dead alter airflow in minute ways, diverting mist into new channels, seeding the next generation of drip-lines. Their contribution to the karst is not culture but topography.

There is, perhaps, one other thing. In the highest caves, where the soft-padded creature moves, something else is said to occur. The males, when their eleven-year circuit brings them near the summits, sometimes climb to those lightless heights not to breed but to listen. The caves, being closer to the surface tension of the aquifer, transmit the seismic speech of the entire plateau—the settling of a sinkhole a thousand kilometers away, the microscopic yielding of a tower under its own weight, the slow crystallization in the deepest chambers. The males press their broad nerve-pads to the cave floor and remain motionless for weeks, receiving this subterranean news.

Whether they understand it, or whether they merely taste it as a particularly rich mineral gradient, no observer could say. But when they descend again, they move differently. Their paths shift. They avoid certain plazas that they formerly frequented; they begin to haunt others. They behave as if they have heard something—a rumor of collapse, or of renewal, traveling through the stone at the speed of groundwater. They behave as if the plateau has spoken to them, and as if, in their silence, they have at last found a voice to answer with.

So the city is not empty. It is occupied by a solitude so complete that it resembles vacancy. Walk through the corridors and you will not see them, unless you see the cleaned stone. You will not hear them, for their footsteps make no sound on the wet floors. But if you stand very still in one of the broader plazas, and if the mist shifts just enough to carry the chemistry of your presence into the deeper avenues, you might feel the temperature of the walls around you change—fractionally, almost imperceptibly—as something tall and narrow draws its fingers along the stone in the next corridor over, reading you as you stand there, wondering whether the architecture itself has finally, at this hour, begun to move.
