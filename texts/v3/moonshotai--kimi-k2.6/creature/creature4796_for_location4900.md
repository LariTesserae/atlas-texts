At first you think nothing lives here, because life on the Thrum does not announce itself. It accretes. It burrows. It settles into the available darkness like a spore finding purchase in a pore, and it waits.

The first you will meet are the **drinkers**. They inhabit the leaf-lakes, if *inhabit* is the word for creatures that do not swim so much as drift, their bodies thinner than membrane, their organs visible as pale blue geometries suspended in gel. They have no eyes—the light is too diffused, too patient for eyes—and no voices. They feed on the microscopic generosity of the water, and in turn they feed the water, exuding a sweetness that encourages the algae-bloom, the green dust that makes the air so chewy, so full. They are the plateau’s taste buds, perhaps, sampling the deep reservoirs and reporting nothing, because nothing needs to be reported. Everything is already known.

Above the water, in the vertical maze of aerial roots and hanging gardens, move the **snails**. You have heard them described as hounds, but that only captures their mass. In truth they are more like altars that have grown tired of standing still. Their shells are not brittle calcium but layers of varnished peat, dense as cork, warm to the touch, spiraling in curves that echo the capillary tunnels below. Their silver trails do not merely mark passage; they seal wounds. Where the Thrum’s skin cracks under its own ancient weight, the snail’s mucus flows in, polymerizing, knitting lignin to lignin. And upon their broad, continent-like backs, smaller ecosystems flower. Epiphytes send rootlets into the spiral pores. Fungal fans unfold like ears. The snails are not beasts of burden, for there is nowhere to go, but they are beasts of company, moving through the galleries at the speed of a held breath, carrying their gardens with them. Some have been traveling for centuries, their radulae scraping slow tattoos into the bark-flesh, their wakes healing behind them.

Then there are the **moths**, though *moth* is a word we use for lack of a better one. Their bodies are wet paper, yes, but the wings are veined with something stiffer, something almost like script. They do not beat in hearing; they beat in pressure, in the low tremors that pass through the humid air. They are couriers. When they alight on a branch, their wings fold not into stillness but into a slow semaphore, pressing the news of distant weeping-pores and blooming bladders into the wood. The Thrum reads them. The Thrum, which has no brain in any centralized sense, has an immune system of information, and these creatures are its antibodies, its messengers, its low-frequency thoughts made flight.

But you asked *whoever*. You asked who *lives* here.

They do not have a name, because naming requires a hard palate, a tongue that strikes the air and makes it ring, and nothing here rings. Call them, if you must, **the Soft Inheritors**, or **the Pressed Ones**, or nothing at all. They are the reason the geometry exists—the hexagonal towers, the woven bridges, the domes of calcified sap. They did not build them with tools, for there are no tools here, no fire to harden, no stone to strike. They grew them, or rather, they persuaded the Thrum to grow them, through centuries of applied chemistry, through laying their translucent bodies against the bark and weeping their own hormones into the pores until the plant dreamed in shapes it had not known before.

What do they look like?

They are tall, because vertical space is abundant, and thin, because there is little light to feed broad leaves. Their skin is the color of old parchment left in water, and it is not skin, not truly, but a kind of cambium, a living rind that respires and photosynthesizes in the dim gold-green twilight. They have no mouths. They do not eat, not as animals eat. They press themselves into the warm, weeping walls of the root-caverns, and they absorb, their bellies flush against the wood, their bodies rising and falling with the decades-long respiration of the plateau. Their eyes—if eyes they are—are wide, dark, lidless pools that collect the spore-light. They have no bones, or if they do, their bones are hollow reeds, filled not with marrow but with a clear fluid that carries hormones instead of blood. When they move, they do not walk so much as unfold, extruding themselves from crevices, their limbs jointed in too many places, bending like stems under weight.

They communicate with pressure and chemistry. A hand laid upon a root sends a squeeze that travels leagues through the living wood, arriving as a thought, a memory, a command. They speak in the language of the place itself. And because they speak so slowly—a sentence might take a season to complete—they have forgotten urgency. They have forgotten that anything could be sharp.

In the hollow towers, they cluster. Not in families. In orchards. They hang, sometimes, from the ceilings of the dome-galls, their rootlet-fingers tangled in the calcified sap, dreaming in parallel. Their dreams leak out through the etched patterns on the walls, the almost-script. It is a writing system of exudate and scar tissue, recording not events but states of being: *The wetness here. The particular gold of this century.* They are the Thrum’s attempt to remember itself, or perhaps they are the Thrum’s memory made flesh.

Deeper still, in the cavernous hollows where the internal storms brew, there are the **older ones**. If the Soft Inheritors are the Thrum’s memory, these are its dreamless sleep. They have ceased moving entirely. They have become the pillars of the caverns, sunk into the composting deep, their bodies indistinguishable from the root-mass. Only their faces remain, pale surfaces in the dark, weeping with the condensation that falls from the cavern storms. The rain falls on them, and they drink it, and they think—if *think* is the word—the slow, heavy thoughts of the plateau. They are the anchors. The ballast. The ones who keep the Thrum from tipping into nightmare.

And is there a king? A queen? A center?

There is the **Drip**. Not a person, but a place, and a presence. It is the largest weeping-pore, somewhere near the middle of the tableland, where the compressed corpus is oldest, almost bone. From it falls a single drop of water, warm and sweet, heavier than it should be, laden with dissolved minerals and memories. The drop falls not every second, nor every minute, but once a day, or once a week—the tempo is too slow for human blood to measure. Every drop strikes a cupped leaf far below, a leaf the size of a lake, and the ripples spread through the capillary system, and the whole plateau feels them. The Soft Inheritors orient themselves toward it. The snails alter their imperceptible courses toward it. The moth-things beat their subsonic wings in rhythms timed to its fall.

Some say the Drip is the Thrum’s heart. Others say it is its grief, falling steadily for the mountains it has forgotten. Whatever it is, to live on the Thrum is to live inside a body that knows you are there, that absorbs you, that does not distinguish between the snail upon its skin, the moth in its lung, the Soft Inheritor in its gall, and the stranger standing at its edge. 

You do not visit. You are incorporated. You are drunk. You are held in the crowded, muffled dark, and if you stay long enough, you too will grow quiet, and soft, and very, very slow.
