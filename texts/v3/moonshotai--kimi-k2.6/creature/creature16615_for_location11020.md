They do not appear to you as figures at first, but as disturbances in the fog’s exhalation. A soft occlusion where the sodium glow fails to penetrate. A shape that stands with its head tilted, not looking but *receiving*, as if the warm, visible air were pooling in the wide, flaring hollows behind its jaw. To be native to the Central Shelf is to have a body that treats the eight-thousand-year drone not as noise but as depth. They are dense. Their bones are thick with calcium leached from the mineral water, their joints broad and deliberate, built for the slow friction of narrow streets and saturated atmosphere. A raised hand moves through the fog like a paddle through oil. Haste is structurally impossible here; it has become, over generations, physiologically absent.

Vision, for them, is a polite, secondary sense, like an appendix. Their eyes are small, recessed, often half-lidded against the constant, weeping condensation; their faces are dominated instead by the architecture of listening. The temporal bones flare wide, the mastoids porous and ridged, tuned to the city’s endless bass. They do not so much hear the reciprocating engines as wear them in their skulls, the way one wears a greatcoat in bad weather. Navigation is proprioception at a civic scale: a resident knows her district not by sightlines but by the specific harmonic signature of a flywheel three streets over, by the way a hot spring’s hiss modulates when a tram passes a junction box, by the subsonic map that the larger predators also use—and so they move among those predators not as intruders but as fellow travelers sharing the same acoustic territory.

Their skin is the color of the plateau’s polished basalt, not by genetic accident but by acculturation. They anoint themselves with the bacterial films that bloom in the seeps, letting iridescent colonies of archaea establish themselves in the creases of their elbows, behind their ears, along the collarbone. It is not decoration; it is farming. The living varnish protects against the warm, acidic fog, and when they are hungry, they scrape a cheek or a forearm and swallow the vivid smear. It is the first course of every meal. The second course is usually isopod: the fist-sized, iron-oxide shelled creatures that infest the walls by the billions are cracked open with a thumb grown broad and horny, the meat mixed with its own powdered shell and a little water into a metallic paste. When the trawlers finish their century-long breeding cycles and the streets are carpeted ankle-deep in shed wings, the inhabitants gather them in baskets woven from shed chitin and render them in the heat of surface vents until they become a translucent broth, rich with the microbial life the creatures had strained from the wet air. They eat the scavengers that eat the wings. They eat the predators, when the predators age out of the pipe-ways, boiling the smooth, warm meat until it falls from the bone. Nothing here is husbanded, but everything is harvested. They are the apex of the chemosynthetic web, and they wear their place in it literally, carrying the faint, sulfuric reek of their nutrition in their hair and under their fingernails.

But to call them merely inhabitants is to understate their function. They are the soft tissue inside the synthetic skeleton. Without them, the city would not decay—it is immune to decay—it would simply *forget*. It is they who remember that a bearing must be replaced not when it screams, but when the pitch of its tremor approaches a frequency that disturbs the nesting trawlers. They communicate this knowledge through the infrastructure itself. Their speech is gestural and pneumatic; they speak in bursts of steam vented through hand-held ceramic valves, in percussive taps on pipework, in the modulation of their own subsonic hums generated from deep in the diaphragm—frequencies felt in the sternum and the fog rather than heard by any ear. A conversation on a busy street is a duet of pressure-changes: the hiss of a wrist-mounted valve, the thump of a fist on a girder, the low, thrumming drone from the chest that lasts forty seconds and means *The seal on the eastern smelter has reached its tertiary phase*. They can speak in the old way, with mouth-sounds, but it is considered vulgar, a crude shouting over the city’s existing voice. Only children and foreigners raise their voices. The fluent move in silence, adding their signals to the din without competing with it.

Their dwellings are not entered so much as tunneled into. A family occupies the accreted space between a building’s original facade and its third-century retrofit, their living quarters a cavity in the masonry shaped by decades of body heat and mineral deposit. They sleep in hammocks strung across utility crawlspaces, waking to the dry skittering of isopods in the walls—a sound that serves as both lullaby and larder. When a child is born, the parents do not build a crib; they select a warm pipe-junction and line it with shed trawler wings, which insulate and breathe. The infant grows listening to the city’s heartbeat, calibrating its internal rhythm to the one-revolution-per-minute pulse rising from the sealed pits below. By five, a child can identify every tram line in their borough by the pitch of its cable. By fifteen, they can walk the under-street tunnels blind, guided only by the temperature gradient of the black, sulfuric water and the echo-click of the pipe-way predators, whom they have learned to mimic and thus to avoid.

Death, here, is understood as a form of maintenance. There are no cemeteries; burial would require excavation, and excavation is demolition, and demolition is heresy. Instead, the dead are brought to the deep thermal seeps, where the heat and the archaea mats receive them. Over months, the warm water strips them down, and what remains is ground by scavengers, by bacteria, by the patient chemistry of silica and copper sulfate, until the body becomes part of the compacted grit that gathers in the low places like a mineral snowfall. Some families, particularly those who have tended the great engines for generations, request instead to be encased. Their bodies are placed in hollows within the machinery—not to sabotage, but to participate. A corpse laid in the housing of a flywheel will, over years, alter the machine’s resonance, adding a new harmonic to its turn. The engineers listen for these ancestral notes. A bearing that sings with the particular timbre of a grandfather’s bones is a bearing that will be monitored, cherished, and eventually sheathed in vitrified alloy along with its organic core. The dead do not leave the city. They are encapsulated, translated into a new state, added to the stratum.

They wear their lineage in the enamel of their environment. The family that has maintained the western aqueduct for twelve generations does not bear a name so much as a stain. The copper sulfate in their local water paints their pipes, their skin, their bacterial films a distinctive blue-green, so that to see a figure the color of oxidized copper stepping from the fog is to know, instantly, that here walks a technician of the high channels. Another clan, responsible for the cable-trams, is marked by the grease and ozone of the traction lines; they move with a faint crackle of static, their hair stiff with conductive precipitate. They form unions across trades, blending their mineral signatures, so that a child might emerge the violet of chalcopyrite if the parents were of the wall-keepers and the spring-tenders. Race, as a concept, has been replaced by watershed. They are lithic people. Chemistry people.

And they are patient. They make decisions across the same timeframes as erosion. A dispute between households is not resolved but deposited, layer by layer, in counter-arguments of added scaffolding and redirected steam pipes, until the conflict becomes physically part of the architecture, each party building their case in brick and ceramic until they are forced to cooperate or collapse the corridor they share. Romantic bonds are formed over decades, conducted in the slow exchange of body heat in cramped tram compartments, in the shared tending of an archaea garden. A proposal is not spoken but enacted: one party reroutes a hot spring to warm the other’s sleeping junction. If accepted, the junction is widened; if rejected, the pipe is capped, and the water finds another path. They understand that nothing here begins or ends. It only accretes.

To the stranger, they appear at first as merely another texture of the fauna. A silhouette in the fog, head cocked to the thrum of a distant engine, fingers trailing along a wet wall to read the Braille of condensation and isopod tracks. They do not startle because nothing here startles. They turn slowly, their recessed eyes reflecting the orange furnace-glow as soft, indefinite points, and they regard you not with suspicion but with the same patient assessment they give a corroding beam. You are a new fracture. You will weep. You will deposit your layers. And if you persist, if you do not collapse or erode into something else, they will learn your harmonic, and they will encase you, and you will become, at last, part of the single continuous note that is this place, ringing, ringing, without decay.
