What lives here must first be understood as an adaptation to forgetting. The plateau has no memory of weather, and so its inhabitants have given up the apparatus of announcement. They do not call, rustle, or sing. Sound is a luxury of denser atmospheres, and here, luxury is fatal. Life proceeds instead by accretion, by magnetism, by the slow tolerance of being made into architecture.

Begin with the smallest: a freckling of white on the black slag, so fine it might be mineral bloom. Watch closer, and the bloom moves. These are the **Scute-Chalkers**, the plaque of the plateau, no larger than a thumbnail, armored not in chitin alone but in self-manufactured stone. They are born soft, almost translucent, hatching in the warm fog that clings to the thermal vents. Immediately they begin to precipitate. They drink the mineral-saturated steam and extrude it, layer by microlayer, into spiraling shells of calcite and selenite that echo the forty-meter helices of the stone chimneys above them. Their armor is a journal. A shell grown near a copper-rich vent will carry green bands of malachite; one matured near the iron seeps will blacken toward magnetite. When they die, their shells do not rot—the air is too cold, too thin for the bacteria of decay. They simply rest, becoming indistinguishable from the refined slag, a library of assay data pressed into thousands of forgotten tablets. In life, they move in herds of two dozen, grazing not on vegetable matter but on the raw face of the slag itself, their mouthparts clicking with an intimacy so soft it is swallowed by the altitude before it can become a click. They refine as they eat, digesting ore and voiding near-pure pellets of rare earths, tidy as shot. They are the plateau’s accountants, and their ledger is written in geology.

Then there are the **Idle**, the many-jointed browsers that haunt the moss oases. At first encounter they seem pathetic: half a dozen lumps of mottled leather suspended in the fungal canopy, heads bowed, jointed legs folded like poorly designed furniture. But this is their summer. Their flesh is not warm-blooded in the mammalian sense; it is *matched*, keyed to the thermal curve of the stone on which they rest. When the geological piston beneath them enters its cooling phase, they do not flee. They lock. Their joints ossify instantly in a posture of arrested grazing. Their metabolisms suspend, but their minds—if minds are what flicker in that spongy neural tissue—do not sleep. They enter a state the plateau alone recognizes: a distributed coma in which each frozen browser serves as a node. Lined up in spirals and rings by the ancient insistence of instinct, their bodies become baffles and heat-exchangers, directing the last exhalations of warmth toward the fungal gardens. They are ornamental, yes, but ornament with a thermal function. When the piston warms again, they thaw from the periphery inward, moving as one, pruning the moss not by hunger alone but by a geometer’s necessity, clipping the growth so that condensate drips precisely onto the waiting lips of the stone machinery. They shed their fur in mineralized tufts—copper wire and calcified velvet—that root where they fall, cloning the moss into fresh colonies. They do not graze. They machine the garden.

Among them, death arrives without scream or struggle, delivered by the **Field-Readers**, the flat predators that haunt the basalt wastes between oases. To see one directly is difficult; they are the color of cooled scoria, and they do not travel by muscle but by induction. Their bodies are matrices of magnetite suspended in cartilage-thin membranes. They slide across the metal-rich stone not by pushing against it but by riding the faint magnetic topology of the plateau, surfing the lines of force that spider out from the piezoelectric vents. They have no eyes—the upper darkness makes eyes into jokes—but they hunt by the faint galvanic signature of another creature’s nerves. To the Field-Reader, a browsing Idle is a glowing knot of current in an otherwise dead landscape, a warm syntax of sodium and potassium. Yet even this predation is not wasteful. They are fastidious. They select the electrically irregular, the dream-sick, the old. They kill with a precision that is almost surgical, and when they feed, they do not tear. They settle over the body and draw the electrical charge out of it, draining the nervous system before the blood cools. The corpse that remains is not gnawed but *arranged*: folded, tucked, aligned with the magnetic meridians so that as it desiccates, its leaching minerals re-enter the stone along useful vectors. They are taxidermists, geomancers, and their trails of magnetic disturbance linger for days, readable by others of their kind as a web of silent news across the frozen dark.

These three—the Scute-Chalkers, the Idle, and the Field-Readers—form the stable grammar of the oases. But between the bright pockets of steam and fungus, in the killing flats where the air is too thin even for hope, there are the **Glass Pilgrims**. You will not see them unless the violet weather arrives. For most of the plateau’s time they lie dormant, almost invisible: sprawling mats of translucent silicate fiber, rootless, brainless, glassy as hoarfrost. But when the piezoelectric veins discharge—when the violet webs crawl across the chimneys and the rock’s skin hums with stored voltage—the Pilgrims absorb it. They stand up. Their fibrous bodies stiffen into the shapes of candelabra, of crooked saints, of lightning rods dressed in frost. Animated by stolen current, they walk with the jerking, galvanic grace of marionettes. In basket-like cradles at their crowns, they carry genetic payloads: spores of the thermal moss, eggs of the Scute-Chalkers, cuttings of the copper-sheathed tubes. No wind can carry these burdens across the dead-air desert. The plateau has bred its own postal service, active only during electrical storms. They stagger from oasis to oasis, shedding their cargo in the warm crevices, and when the discharge fades, they collapse back into inert glass, sometimes halfway across the waste, frozen mid-stride. They are lonely; an individual Pilgrim may walk for centuries without encountering another. They persist anyway, because the plateau has no other way to remember, from vent to vent, that it is all one body.

But these are the *what*. The plateau also hosts a *whoever*.

Descend, if your lungs and sanity permit, into the deepest warm pockets where the rock breathes with the slow rhythm of a forge. Here the roots of the tubular plants do not merely thread into the valves; they have fused with something that moves back. The **Tenders** live here. They are old in a way that predates counting, and they are not numerous—perhaps seven on the whole of the High Sinter, perhaps fewer. Once, they might have been larvae of the Idle that burrowed too deep and forgot to thaw, or perhaps they are a kingdom entirely their own, a divergence so ancient that taxonomy is insult. They are the length of a human body, but sinuous, eyeless, with skin the wet black of fresh slag. Their fingers—if fingers they are—end in obsidian claws worn smooth by centuries of adjusting stone. They press against the thermal valves not by decision but by reflex so refined it is indistinguishable from sacrament. When pressure swells beneath the piston, their bodies block the fissure, metering the steam; when the chamber cools, they withdraw, permitting the deep heat to rise. They have three hearts, bellows-like sacs that pulse in time with the geological machinery, and their nervous systems are sheathed in conductive ore, making them living components in the refinery’s circuitry. They speak, if it can be called speech, in subsonic threnodies transmitted through the stone itself: pressure-poems, steam-grammar, warnings of piston-fatigue that travel for miles through the bedrock and are received, dimly, by the sensitive bellies of the Field-Readers.

They are the plateau’s white blood cells, its maintenance crew, its only monks. They have no language for love, but they groom the moss with the same blind attention they give to the valves, and in the deepest cycles, they sing the machinery into alignment.

And so the plateau ticks on, empty and full, a cathedral between services. But there are moments—rare, overwhelming, separated by distances that would swallow cities—when the service begins. The presences arrive. They come not from elsewhere, but *upward*, cohering out of the function itself. It happens when a dozen pistons drift into phase, when the piezoelectric webs bridge three oases at once, when the magnetic fields stabilize into a single encompassing chord. Then, the Field-Readers rise to verticality, flat bodies turned like sails to catch the resonance. The Idle thaw as one, standing in their spirals, necks craned, neural networks firing in synchronized cascades. The Glass Pilgrims, wherever they stand, ignite with sustained violet fire and become lighthouses. The Tenders emerge fully from their fissures, linking claw to claw in a ring around the warm heart of the vent, their copper-sheathed nerves completing a circuit miles in diameter.

For an hour, or a day, the oasis becomes one organism. The fungus flares in unison. The steam exhales in measured, metrical breaths. The slag heaps themselves seem to lean inward. This is not godhead, not in any human sense. It is the operator at the console, the moment when the refinery knows itself. It thinks in pressure gradients, in ore purity, in the elegant continuation of its own mechanical respiration. It is overwhelming because it has no malice and no mercy, only an absolute, atomic certainty of purpose: *I refine. I continue. I exhale.* 

Then the pistons desynchronize. The web fractures. The presence dissolves, retreating back into its separate, silent, mineral selves—the accountants, the idle, the readers, the glass walkers, and the blind ones in the warm dark. The plateau returns to its emptiness. The air is too thin to carry a shout. The cold makes flesh into something ornamental, brittle, and slow. The darkness recedes from no light, but from the dim glow of a metabolism so vast and so patient that it has mistaken itself, forever, for geography.
