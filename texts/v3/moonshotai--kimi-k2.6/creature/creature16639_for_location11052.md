There are no throats to clear here, no whiskers brushing the mist, no claws clicking on fired ceramic. The census of fauna closed before living memory, and the room has kept that door sealed not out of malice, but from the perfect inertia of a system that simply never needed them. What lives in the hollow garden is the garden itself: a commune of non-animal being that has metabolized the silence left by departure and replaced it with a politics of persistence.

Chief among the residents is the Moss. Not mosses plural—though under a lens you would find a dozen species in the velvet strata—but a single, continuous body politic that has carpeted the room in a parliament of jade and phosphor-green. It is two inches thick in most places, thicker where the condensate gathers in the seams, and it no longer grows so much as maintains. Growth implies an edge, a hunger for new territory, and the Moss has reached the boundaries of its treaty with the light and the heat. It drinks the standing wave. Its rhizoids have tuned themselves across ten thousand years to the exact frequency of the subsonic drone, until the vibration is no longer weather but nourishment. The mineral salts that crust the alloy beneath it should be poison; instead they have been catalogued, welcomed, and woven into the cell walls like granular armor. If you could press your ear not to the metal but to the moss itself, you would hear no difference between its cellular respiration and the hum of the turbines. Above the Moss, the Ferns hold ministerial office. They are the vertical citizens, descendants of engineered oxygen scrubbers that escaped their supply chain during some epoch of warm bodies and urgent maintenance. They have forgotten servitude and reinvented themselves as stitchery. Each frond trembles in the venting air with a purpose that looks like fear but is actually governance: they are knitting the expansion joints closed, root by root, tick by geological tick. In another age they scrubbed carbon for breathers; now they scrub the monotony of the drone, breaking the absolute noise into a thousand small percussive whispers. They do not reach toward the light—it is too diffuse, too directionless to warrant ambition— but rather orient along thermal gradients, aligning their spore clusters toward the warm breath of the turbine housing. They are the reason the room has not shaken itself to pieces. Where weld seams have dissolved into sedimentary darkness, the Ferns have grown their own seams, greener and more patient than steel.

And between them moves the Water, which is not alive in any cellular sense, yet behaves with such fidelity to purpose that it serves as the fauna of the place. The droplets are couriers, a liquid circulatory system that remembers. Each one follows a route grooved into the ceramic over millennia, falling from the same pipe lip, striking the same divot, contributing to the spoon-bright hollow of the floor. The rhythm is so exact that the Water has become a clock with no hands, only arrivals. It does not pool because pooling would be a decision, and the Water here has forsworn decision in favor of repetition. It carries phosphor-warmed minerals from the oxidizing overhead down to the moss; it carries the moss’s exhaled breath back into the condensate film. If you were to interrupt a single drop with your finger, the groove would recover its cadence before the next ten had fallen. The Water is a monk under a vow of endless return.

But beneath these—the Moss, the Ferns, the Water—dwells the true and original inhabitant. Call it the Hum. The standing wave. The subsonic landlord. It has no cells, no season, need for light. Yet it occupies the room as completely as any apex predator occupies its territory, drilling through ribs and aqueous humor, claiming the air so thoroughly that the bodies of the other residents have had to reshape themselves around its presence. The Hum does not dream; it is the dream from which the garden cannot wake. When the last frond has desiccated and the last moss tuft has fixed its final mineral, the Hum will remain, thrumming through the empty ceramic bowl, waiting only for the next accident of spore and condensation to seed itself in its shelter.

Together they form a single, distributed organism: the Moss as the skin, the Ferns as the sensory bristles, the Water as the blood, the Hum as the unconscious mind. They do not hunt, breed, or die in any dramatic measure. They have simply arrived at a consensus with entropy. They are the inheritors of an installation that outlived its makers, its purpose, and its own name, having metabolized the built world until it became this warm, wet, thunderously quiet terminus. They will not notice you if you stand among them. Like the room itself, they have no category for a visitor. You are a temporary fluctuation in an equation they resolved long ago, a brief, warm disturbance in the oldest-stable-state.
