They do not look like survivors. Survivors are gaunt with the memory of motion; these are filled out to the exact shape of their stillness. Each body occupies its volume in the architecture the way a stone occupies its wedge in a corbelled arch—necessary, sufficient, and pressing outward only with the weight required to remain upright. There are, at any given time, exactly as many of them as there are hearths vented through the cliff above, and it would not be wrong to say that the hearths and the bodies are numbered by the same necessity.

My first thought was that they must be small. They are not. They are the size that the sleeping niches demand, a full adult frame, but their flesh is dense and quiet, packed with the stubborn root-starches of the terraced plots. Their skin holds a cast of the tuff itself, not painted or dusted but bred from the mineral water and the pallid tubers until their knees, their elbows, the rims of their ears, all carry that same pale, porous grey. The color is functional; in the courts, in the corridors, they do not appear to move through space so much as shift within it, like fissures adjusting their width.

Their clothing is the fiber of that one woody shrub, perhaps, beaten and woven into a felted thickness, always that same outer garment, never removed because the temperature never justifies its removal. The garments are replaced fibre for fibre as they erode, so that a single robe might be the continuous descendant of a garment begun in the age of construction, its pattern carrying forward like water finding the same channel. If you touched the weave, you would feel the accumulated abrasion of every shoulder that had turned the same corner, every hip that had rested against the same wall.

But if you watched for a day and a night, you would realize that the life of the shelf is concentrated in the hands. The rest—the torso, the face, the slow gait—belongs to the mineral equilibrium. Only the hands violate, or perhaps perfect, the stillness. They are broad, the phalanges somewhat flattened, the skin coarse as fired clay. They know the specific heft of the grinding cobble, the exact angle at which a shard of broken pot is best crushed for temper. When they daub mud slurry into a wall crack, the gesture does not repeat; it continues, as if the hand that last patched this seam a generation ago merely paused, and this hand has resumed without interrupting the stroke. There are no calluses, because there is no friction of change—only pressure, and the slow chemistry of skin becoming stone becoming skin.

I had imagined they might speak, but listening longer, I understand they do not need to. The acoustics here were designed for something more precise than language. A foot placed on a specific step, the particular hollow sound of a cistern dipper striking a certain basin, the rustle of a sleeve against the shrub—these are their syntax. To announce one’s presence is redundant when the architecture itself registers each body’s location as a shift in load and air. They hear with the soles of their feet, with the membranes of their throats, the way the pigeon feels the stillness from its coping. If one must signal, it is a tap of stone on stone, two beats, identical to the water drip, a punctuation requiring no reply.

Nor do they remember the dead. The dead are not commemorated; they are housed. The ossuary niches occupy the highest tier, dry and cold, and when an elder surrenders their water to the air, the body is carried up by the combined gravity of the remaining hands. The event creates no ripple. Perhaps the same morning, a figure appears at the eastern aperture, or an infant draws first breath in an alcove that has been empty exactly long enough to have cooled. The number is restored before the clay in the new wall patch has dried. There is no grief, because there is no loss—only a transfer of weight, a redistribution of biomass that the cisterns had already budgeted for.

I want to say they are peaceful, but peace is a resolution of conflict, and here conflict has never been conceived. They are not serene; they are correct. Even their breath is metered to the altitude, shallow and slow, drawing just enough of the thin, metallic air to feed the blood without accelerating the heart. Their hearts beat, I imagine, at the same tempo as the condensation forming on the overhang—too slow for a stethoscope to catch the urgency of a mammal, too steady to be called mere vegetation.

And yet they are not mindless. Watch the one who grinds grain in the lowest court. The eyes are not vacant; they are occupied. They contain the full attention of a consciousness that has narrowed to this: the oval of the grinding hollow, the particular mineral smell of the meal, the angle of light as it fills the aperture. There is no plan for what follows the grinding, because the grinding is followed by the carrying, which is followed by the division, which is followed by the eating, and none of these require anticipation. The mind here is not a stream but a depth. It accumulates rather than flows. What is thought is the texture of the stone against the cobble, the exact temperature of the cistern water against the inner wrist, the precise distribution of shadow on the western wall when the sun reaches its station. These are not observations; they are the contents of the mind, solid and enumerable.

I wonder now if they are even separate. The architecture is a single nervous system, and each body is only a ganglion, a processing node for a particular set of tasks. The one at the grinder, the one at the cistern, the one packing clay into the parapet—they are the shelf’s way of maintaining its own equilibrium. They eat the tubers that grow in the terrace soil carried up by hands that are themselves fed by the tubers. The water passes through them and returns to the basins, fractionally warmer, fractionally saltier, part of the same exhalation. The dust of the floors is composed of their feet, and the dust is ground finer, and the finer it becomes, the more perfectly it records the passage of the next foot. They are not citizens of a town. They are the metabolism of an organ that has learned to slow its pulse until it matches the geology.

The child, or the arrival—I cannot distinguish them—learns without teaching. It does not imitate; it simply begins. One morning the small figure is there, carrying a dipper too large for its frame, and the hands already know the balance point, the wrist already angled to avoid spillage. There is no instruction, only the shape of the correct gesture waiting in the air, which the new flesh occupies as water occupies a hollow. They grow, if that is the word, only to the size of the niche that awaits them, and then they maintain.

The pigeon on the coping regards them, I think, without distinction. To the bird, they are slow weather, a variety of lichen that breathes and turns. And perhaps that is the final truth of them: they have become native life. Not visitors to the shelf, not exploiters of its height, but one of the slow, sourceless phenomena that the place exhales. The condensation, the lichen, the drip, the grey bird, the grinding figure in the court—these are not separate kingdoms. They are the same equilibrium expressed in different densities. The inhabitants are the water made flesh for a moment, given hands so the cisterns stay clean, given mouths so the tubers are returned to the dust, given eyes so the light in the aperture is witnessed and thus completed. They live here the way the damp lives here: without origin, without destination, fully arrived.
