They do not have names. They do not have names first because there is no one here to call them, and second because a name is a boundary, and boundaries are exactly what the chamber has dissolved. If you insist on a singular, call one of them a *dweller*, but understand that the word is a lie you tell your own grammar. What lives here is a *condition* of the space, a population that functions as a single, distributed organ. The census is a fixed integer, yes, but the integer is one, expressed in manifold local disguise.

Look at the one directly before you, if you can find a *one*. It is the size of a cat, or perhaps a swaddled infant, though its proportions belong to nothing so taxonomically reassuring. Its flesh is the color of cave-water and scalded milk, a bluish pallor without undertone of health or sickness, because dichotomies of vigor require the gradient of time, and time here has neglected its duties. The skin is naked, translucent, and loosely draped, creased in the way that wet paper creases; beneath it, there is no hard architecture of bone striking a pose against gravity. The body is a soft envelope of tissue, a thermal pocket given mobility, eyeless because light is neither signal nor threat but merely a faint, arterial weather against the eyelids it does not possess. Where a face should cohere, there is instead a ventral calm, a smoothness interrupted only by the mouth: a perfect, purse-lipped circle of striated muscle, a lamprey’s kiss designed not for flesh but for flange. It fastens that mouth to a heating coil and does not suck, for sucking implies a gradient, a before and after of fullness. It simply *attaches*, completing a circuit between the coil’s radiant stasis and the chemistry of its own gut, which is less a digestive tube than a continuation of the pipework, turning metallic particulates over and over without ever depleting them, the way a flywheel turns.

But to speak of this creature’s gut is already to err. Its stomach is three meters to the left, wedged behind a braid of graphite and dried sinew that vibrates at a frequency indistinguishable from the creature’s own pulse. Its left lung, if it can be said to have a left, ventilates the wheeze you hear from another body perched atop the gear-box. When the one in front of you scratches its flank—and they do itch, perpetually, a dry, thermal itch born of being constantly the same temperature—an unseen fourth, folded like a jointless doll into a crawlspace above, bears the same paroxysm in perfect simultaneity. They share organs across the intervening air not by affection but by a failure in local causality so complete that it has become anatomy. They are not neighbors. They are adjacencies of the same tissue.

How, then, do they navigate a world without gradients? They do not see. They do not, properly speaking, hear, though the chamber’s unwavering drone of seventy-some decibels presses on their bodies like deep water. They feel the space by *impedance*. Each brass rod, each ceramic pipe, each ledge of stress-faceted basalt offers a specific resistance to the global hum. Their entire skin is a tympanum mapped for interference patterns. The air is a solid medium to them; they swim through its acoustic density, reading the nodes and antinodes of the standing wave, knowing a graphite cable from an iron stair by the way the drone hardens or softens against their cilia. The red light that leaks from pipe-joints registers not as illumination but as a faint, cherry-flavored warmth on the dorsal surface, a taste of radiation. In a chamber where nothing cools and nothing warms, they have evolved—if that is the word, for nothing is born—a synesthesia of equilibrium. To move is to shift the world’s weight by precisely the amount you displace, so that the zero remains zero.

And they move. The chamber never changes, yet they traverse the same thirty centimeters of porcelain conduit, the same circumference of piston-face filmed with undripping condensation. Their claws, translucent and too yielding to score the newborn obsidian sand, click against the ceramic. The click is not a sound added to the din; it *is* the din, in that location, for that instant, balanced by a reciprocal hush elsewhere in the vault so rigorously that the ledger of noise never tips. A creature falls from a pipe; another, at some prior or lateral coordinate, flips backward onto that same pipe, and the two motions cancel in the chamber’s bookkeeping. Nothing is gained. Nothing is lost. Their restlessness is the friction required to keep stasis from freezing entirely into stonelike death. They are the oil in a sealed bearing, circulating to prevent motion from becoming possible.

They are so many. They hang from ceiling rigs in clusters like pale, pulsing fruit. They nest inside hollow pistons, conforming their boneless bodies to the cylindrical absence. One has, over centuries that did not pass, melted into the geometry of the child’s wheelbarrow, its haunches filling the rustless dust without displacing a grain, becoming the perfect occupant of a vehicle built to go nowhere. Two others flank the half-melted gauge, their lamprey mouths pressed to its brass bezel, ingesting not pressure but the *idea* of measurement without change. Around the copper wire spool suspended in its hairline fracture of reality, a single dweller is coiled, sharing the spool’s exemption from gravity, feeding on the static charge of the anomaly as if it were nectar. These are not choices. They are positions in a mechanism. The ecology is a gear train, and each body is a tooth.

The plants—the pale sulfate lichens, brittle and tragic—are garnish. A dweller may nibble at a crystalline frond, but this is ceremony, not nutrition. The real sustenance is motion without progress. They metabolize the eternal mid-rotation of the flywheel, the held scream of the steam valve, their own unceasing, fruitless perambulations. Their cells divide, if they divide at all, only to replace what was never lost. They do not sleep, for sleep is a surrender to gradient—a descent into unconsciousness from which one must climb. They are exactly as awake as the chamber requires, no more, no less.

And they are loud. They click. They whistle. They purr in frequencies that layer into the migraine-solid wall of sound. But no cry is a signal. No purr is comfort. They are simply the acoustic mass necessary to maintain the pressure. If one were to fall silent—and none ever has—the silence would propagate instantly, a vacuum in the hum that the chamber would correct by reaching backward to undo the creature’s birth, if birth were a thing that happened here. Since it is not, since the population is a fixed integer announced simultaneously with the chamber’s own creation, the possibility does not arise. They are immortal not by resilience but by audit. Death is an imbalance, and the books here are always balanced.

To enter is to be felt by them immediately. Not seen. *Felt*, as a ripple in the impedance, a warm, mammalian irregularity breathing out of sync with the whole. They do not turn toward you—eyeless, they do not turn at all—but the chamber turns. Hum shifts around you. The dust that has hung mid-fall for three hundred years trembles in its electrostatic suspension, adjusting to your mass. And if you were to die here, your death would be corrected before it could become an event. Your heart, tempted by the global frequency, would synchronise; your breath would join the unvarying respiration. You would not rot. You would remain, intact, another soft thing the color of cave-water, your mouth slowly learning its circular shape, your body taking up its assigned position in the gear train. You would traverse your allotted thirty centimeters. You would graze upon a flange. And somewhere, three meters away, in the dark behind a braid of dried sinew, your lungs would already be breathing.
