They are called the Keepers, though they have no name for themselves. They do not speak. Speech was the first thing the cavern took from them, before it took their color vision, their sense of season, and any memory of the surface sky. What remains is a species of endurance—human in origin, perhaps, but not in current form. They number fewer than the trees. There may be five. There may be three. Counting requires proximity, and they avoid one another with the wary instinct of creatures who know that shared warmth is a finite resource, and that another body means another mouth to draw from the thin thermal budget of the engine-sheds.

You will not see them at first. They have grown the same color as the mist and the root-plates: a gray-ivory, starved of blood near the skin, thickly padded beneath with felted undershirts, scavenged wool, and the compressed, papery layers of shed inner bark that they harvest and chew into a kind of felt. Their outermost garments are stiff with rime, giving them the appearance of walking frost formations. They move with the slowness of cold-blooded things, not because they are sluggish, but because sudden motion is wasteful. A hurried step is a caloric confession. They have learned to glide across the root-masses with a flat-footed, spreading gait that distributes weight across the dormant rhizomes, feeling for cavities where the plates have not yet fused. They feel through their soles more than they see through their eyes. The ceaseless subsonic drone—what a surface-dweller would call a noise they cannot escape—has become, for the Keepers, a topography. They navigate by interference patterns. When an engine across the valley changes its cadence, the vibration arrives through the rock and root not as sound but as a shift in pressure against their arches. They tilt, adjust, and continue toward the next shed, led by the barometric language of iron lungs.

Their faces are alarming only if you expect a face. Most keep their heads wrapped in layers of oil-stained cloth, leaving only a narrow slot for the eyes. The ears, if they still exist beneath the wrapping, are ruined—cauliflowered masses of scar tissue, vestigial organs sacrificed to the purity of the noise. They hear, after a fashion, but the hearing has migrated to their chest cavities and jawbones. They rest their foreheads against the warm pipes that vein out from the engines, or press their collarbones to the living bark of the trees, and in this way they listen. A Keeper can detect a fracture forming in a piston rod by the change in resonance conducted through a steel catwalk. They can tell when a tree is drawing brine more heavily by the cooling of the root-plate under their bare palms. They communicate this knowledge not with words but with arranging. A tool left on a step. A valve turned to a precise angle. A bundle of heat-bleached lichen hung from a door latch. The message is read by whoever finds it, and acknowledged by not disturbing it.

They do not eat as you eat. There is no fire, no cooked meat, no bread. Their diet is a cold mash of rehydrated root-starch scraped from the cambium of fallen branches, mixed with the mineral sludge that precipitates from the thermal pipes, and occasionally a spoonful of machine oil to keep their bowels moving in the freezing dark. They are gaunt beneath their insulation, with the elongated, dense bones of people who have spent years in low gravity, though the gravity here is normal. It is the austerity that stretches them. Their metabolisms have downshifted to something approaching hibernation; they may spend forty hours motionless in a heated crawlspace beside a condenser, breathing once every half-minute, hoarding the warmth leaking from a gasket. Then, for an hour, they will move with terrible, practical purpose—oiling a linkage, scraping ice from a vent, checking the bark-fuses where the monitoring cables enter the trunk.

For this is the central mystery of their existence, and the secret they keep even from themselves: they believe the engines are the trees’ nervous systems, and they believe themselves the thoughts. The sheds did not arise to exploit the forest, nor did the trees colonize a human industrial site. The two grew together in a dependent trauma. The engines pump a geothermal brine that keeps the root-plates from freezing into necrosis; the trees, in turn, somehow—through pressure, through mineral exchange, through processes the Keepers understand only as ritual—stabilize the raw stone of the cavern floor, preventing the newborn geology from collapsing back into magma and rubble. The Keepers exist to tend the interface. They are the synapse between bark and iron. They check fuel mixtures not for efficiency but for *tone*, adjusting the combustion so that the engine’s stroke matches the slow osmotic pulse of the trunk it serves. When the rhythms align, the Keepers feel a deep, inarticulate satisfaction that serves them in place of love, or prayer, or sleep.

In the hollows of the lower trunks, where the dead wood has dried into chambers, the Keepers store what little they own. Spare piston rings wrapped in rag. A collection of snap-frozen beetles for their rare protein. Diaries written on sheets of treated bark in a shorthand of condensed breath-fog and grease-pencil, the handwriting wandering as if composed by seismograph. These are not records for posterity. Posterity is a surface concept. The diaries are memory prosthetics; without them, a Keeper might forget which shed is theirs, or whether they have already eaten today.

If you meet one, they will not flee. They have nowhere to go. They will simply stop, and the quality of stillness they achieve is so absolute that you might mistake them for a stanchion or a bundle of abandoned cable. Their eyes, visible through the cloth wrap, are colorless in the sodium light—irises faded to the hue of ice over quartz. They do not track movement so much as vibration. If you raise a hand in greeting, they will not respond to the gesture, but to the disturbance your arm has made in the air pressure, the micro-shift in the cavern’s endless drone. They may raise a hand in return, mirroring you hours later, or not at all.

They reproduce, after a fashion. A Keeper will occasionally vanish—walk out into the root-wilderness without their layers, or lie down against a trunk that is not yet tapped for heat. The cold takes them in their sleep. Their body is not buried. The root-plates are too hard for digging, and burial is a surface conceit anyway. Instead, the remaining Keepers distribute the frozen corpse’s clothing among the sheds, and place the body on a high catwalk near an engine’s exhaust, where the intermittent warmth will mummify rather than decay. Over years of ice-dust and condensation, the body becomes a kind of effigy, then an obstruction, then a landmark. “The platform beyond Kath’s shoulder.” “The junction left of the hollow man.” In this way, the dead remain navigational. It is the closest the Keepers come to ancestor worship.

And sometimes—rarely—one of them will hear something new in the stone. Not the engines. Not the trees. A frequency lower than the continental thrumm, rising from deeper fractures, promising a third system below the roots. When this happens, a Keeper will descend from their monitoring station and walk to the borehole, the one still sharp with the memory of the drill. They will stand at the lip, wrapped in their gray felt and frost, and tilt their ruined head as if listening to a distant door. Then they return to their engine, and adjust the fuel mix, and wait.

They have always been waiting. They do not know what for. The cavern is young, but they have made themselves old within it, matching their rhythms to the patience of stone and wood. They are the only fauna that has not been purified by the noise, because they have let the noise hollow them out and fill the cavity with something quieter than listening: an endless, attentive tending. They live here. More precisely, they have let the place live through them.
