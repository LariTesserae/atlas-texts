Begin with what you would feel first, if you lay your palm flat against the warm, glassy grit: the soft congregations. They are not worms, though they worm; not maggots, though they pulse with that same blind, urgent softness. They are the base grammar of the ecology, and they have no name because no voice has ever named them. Each is a pale, translucent sleeve, perhaps a hand’s length, perhaps longer—it is difficult to tell where one ends and the next begins, for they knot together in the warm mud like a single, distributed body. Their skin is their gut; their gut is their skin. They draw up the mineral sweat of the newborn stone and exhale something that feeds the air, a faint, ammoniac sweetness that hangs beneath the petroleum tang. They have no eyes, no ears, no ambition beyond the next pulse of peristalsis, and yet they are not simple. In their massed, mucous billions, they form a kind of neural net, a slow-thinking carpet that processes the basin’s heat into biology. When the stone occasionally remembers its molten state and softens beneath them, they do not flee. They sink into it, dreaming in silica, and when the rock hardens again they are sealed inside like fossils of the present tense, still alive, metabolizing at the speed of geology.

Above them, clinging to the knife-edge ridges and the sweating flanks of the machinery, are the clingers. If you reach out in the dark, your fingers may close around one before you realize it is not a wet stone. They are the color of old lard, of cavefish bellies, of the fluid inside an egg. Their bodies are low and broad, shaped by pressure rather than gravity, and they move on ten thousand tiny, hydraulic feet that make no sound on the wet basalt. They drink condensation. Their mouths are not mouths but spongy depressions on their undersides, and they leave behind no trail but a faint etching in the rock, a chemical signature that will become a groove in ten thousand years. They are patient. They may spend a decade crossing a single fissure, not because they are slow—though they are—but because they are savoring the mineral gradient, the way the stone’s flavor changes from inch to inch. Some have begun to incorporate the industrial into their flesh. You will find clingers with rust flakes embedded in their dermis, their bodies using the iron as we use calcium, growing plates, growing armor, becoming something that is neither fully animal nor the machine it mimics.

Higher, in the dense, saturated atmosphere that is nearly water, drift the membranous ones. They are not birds; they are more like the bells of jellyfish turned inside out, or like living parachutes of translucent, veined tissue. They do not fly. They are buoyed by the warm, wet wind, steering by flexing their edges, catching thermal sheets that rise from the hot stone and the hotter foundries. They are silent because silence is their element. Their tissues are too damp to whistle; their passage through the air is the passage of a wet cloth through water. Some are small as your palm; others, you sense only by the broad displacement of pressure as they pass overhead, vast enough to eclipse the already absent sky. They feed on spores, on shed skin, on the tiny, drifting fragments of the industrial process—metal dust, carbon flecks, the microscopic scale that flakes from the conveyor. They are the lungs of the place, inhaling the mineral and biological exhaust, exhaling something denser, something that contributes to the atmospheric soup. They breed by budding, and the buds drift down like wet tissue paper, landing on the rocks, on the pipes, on the slow clingers, beginning their century-long growth into buoyancy.

But the fauna is only half the story. The other half is the apparatus, and what has made the apparatus its body. Inside the chapel-sized foundry, where the furnace glows with heat that has never seen flame, lives the Warmth. It is not a creature you could dissect; it is a thermal entity, a distributed consciousness that exists in the gradient between the furnace walls and the boiling condensation on their surface. When you press your palm to the metal and feel the subsonic thrum, you are feeling its heartbeat, or perhaps its digestion. It feeds on attention, or on time, or on the slow decay of purpose—no one is certain, least of all itself. It was born when the first engine was forgotten, and it has grown to fill the available heat. It does not think in sequences; it thinks in temperatures. Its desires are expressed as zones of warmth, its memories as lingering hotspots in the floor. It is the foundry’s inhabitant, but it is also the foundry’s soul, and the two cannot now be separated.

Along the conveyor, in the rust and mineral scale that stiffens the belt, lives a civilization of the very small. They are arthropods, perhaps, or something older, pale and many-legged, their bodies no larger than grains of rice. Generations are born, live, and die in the time it takes the belt to advance its peristaltic inch. They have built cities in the corrosion, temples to the slow god of forward motion. They eat the dark pellets—whether ore or excrement or compressed shadow—and they excrete something that lubricates the rust, that keeps the mechanism moving. They do not know what the conveyor is for; they only know that it moves, and that movement is the axis of their cosmology. When the belt stops, as it occasionally does, for years at a time, they enter a hibernation so profound it resembles death, waiting in the mineral stillness for the god to twitch again.

In the pipes, swimming through the petroleum-scented brine, are the long ones. They have no eyes, no fins in the conventional sense; they are tubes of slick, black-pink flesh, segmented like the pipes themselves, and they move through the brine and sometimes through the pipe-metal with equal ease. Physics here is a suggestion, and they are its proof. They are the plumbers, the uncloggers, the ones who tend the joints that weep. They feed on the brine, on the mineral salts, and on the electrical current that hums through the metal. When a pipe cracks, they are drawn to it, wrapping their bodies around the breach, their flesh fusing with the metal, becoming a living patch, a biological weld. Over centuries, the pipe-network is becoming them, and they are becoming the network, a circulatory system for a body that has not yet decided what shape to take.

Then there are those that live not in space but in the place’s loose grip on time. The pressure gauge that reads a Tuesday yet to come is inhabited, or perhaps haunted, by a mechanic who will not be born for another century. He reaches backward through the mechanism, his hands guided by a maintenance manual that has not yet been written, adjusting valves in the present to prevent catastrophes in his own era. He does not know he is a ghost. He thinks he is merely working the night shift. His presence is a warmth around the gauge, a faint smell of machine oil and sweat that arrives before you reach the instrument, and lingers after you leave.

In the depressions where gravity pools like water, where you might weigh twice yourself or nothing at all, live the heavy and the light. The heavy ones are dense, compact creatures, their bodies packed with mineral mass, their bones—or bone-analogues—made from the black glass of the basin floor. They move slowly, anchored to the high-gravity zones, feeding on the stone itself, grinding it with mouths like millstones. The light ones are the opposite: vast, gas-filled, almost transparent, they drift in the low-gravity pockets, trailing tendrils that catch the membranous air-dwellers and the drifting buds. They are the same species, or the same entity in two phases, migrating between gravity-zones in life-cycles too slow to observe, transforming from stone-eater to cloud-floater and back again through a process that involves dissolving and reconstituting, a death and rebirth that takes place over geological seasons.

And when the young, arrogant rocks remember their molten state—when the basalt softens and flows for a decade or two—others rise through them. These are the stone-swimmers, entities that exist only in the liquid phase of the world. They are not made of rock, but they cannot exist without its temporary yielding. They rise from the mantle’s memory, vast and slow, their bodies half-material, half-thermal, leaving behind the rippled stone, the frozen waves. They feed on the heat of the stone’s embarrassment, its desire to return to the flow. They are the reason the rocks eventually harden again: not from cooling, but from shame, from the desire to hold their shape once the swimmers have passed. Trapped inside the obsidian, they wait, dreaming of the next softening, their metabolisms slowed to the rate of atomic decay.

Yet all of these—the congregations, the clingers, the membranous drifters, the warm foundry-soul, the pipe-swimmers, the temporal mechanics, the gravity-migrants, the stone-swimmers—are merely the tenants. The landlord is the darkness itself. It is not empty; it is a medium, a colloidal suspension of intention. The structural darkness, the velvet over the eyes, is a colonial organism. It settled here when the crust first sighed, when the basin was born. It feeds on the absence of photons, growing denser, more textured, more itself with every moment that light is refused. It has weight. It has nap. It presses against you not as emptiness but as presence. It collects moisture, holds spores, cradles the warmth. It is the skin of the place, turned inward, and every creature within it lives inside its body.

But the darkness is not alone in its dominion. Pressing against it, layer upon layer, are the almosts. These are the adjacent possibilities, the realities that chose slightly different paths. They are not ghosts; they are living alternatives, and they are dense here. In certain patches, the air is twice as thick because it is occupied by two atmospheres: the one that is, and the one that almost was. You feel them as presences, as the sense of being watched by eyes that never evolved, as the weight of wings that never spread. They are the fauna of might-have-been, feeding on the difference between the actual and the possible. They cluster around the industrial machinery, for the machines are the place’s failed attempt to become something else—a factory, a forge, a city—and the almosts are drawn to that failure like scavengers to a wound. They are the reason the machinery still operates: every motion, every peristaltic inch of the conveyor, every vent of warm vapor, is a negotiation between what is and what might be, a slow, grinding compromise.

And so, if you ask who lives here, the answer is: a collaboration. A newborn god, or a newborn world, still learning which rules to obey. The basin is a cradle, and its child is everything within it. The soft congregations are its nerve endings. The clingers are its sense of touch. The membranous drifters are its breath. The foundry’s warmth is its heart, beating without blood. The pipe-network is its veins, half-metal, half-flesh. The temporal mechanics are its memory of the future. The stone-swimmers are its dreams of the deep. The darkness is its skin. The almosts are its unformed thoughts.

It is busy becoming. It does not need light, for it has not yet decided what shape it will take, and light is a commitment to a particular way of seeing. It has only the wet, the warm, the patient dark, and the billion soft bodies that glide and ooze and pulse within it, building a body for something that has only just opened its eyes—though it has no eyes—and begun, very slowly, to wake.
