If you wait long enough on that basalt shelf—wait through the bruised dusk and the chest-compressing hum—you will begin to understand that the emptiness is occupied. Not thickly. The scandalously low density of matter remains scandalous. But the valley has begun to grow a kind of stewardship out of its own condensation, and the result is not quite a people, not quite a flora, but something for which no category has yet cooled into shape.

They are not the builders. The builders—if *builders* is the word for the intention that breached the magma and strung cable across sixty kilometers of void—left no heirs, no manuals, no bones. They left only appetite: a machine that wanted to become an ecosystem, a chord that wanted to resolve. Into that appetite, biology crept the way moisture creeps down the spires. The first of them were probably no more than stains, biofilms complex enough to metabolize the hypermaterial’s shed infrared, drinking the warm exhalation of the towers. Over the decades, as the valley inhaled and exhaled its endless damp breath, the stains organized. They accreted. They became the ones who remain.

Physically, they are the interstitial given vertebrae. You would mistake them, at first, for a new species of the aerial jungle—something branching and violet that had learned to detach from the cable-mats. They stand upright when necessary, but they prefer to recline in the thickest tangles, letting the root-cables hold them in cradles of wet black fiber. Their bodies are elongated, two and a half meters tall but narrow, built according to the same logic as the spires: height without mass, presence without density. Their skin—if it is skin—resembles the ceramic-light of the towers, but porous, thirsty, always beaded with the valley’s fine sweat. They do not perspire. They *condense*. Their integument is a living dew-catcher, channeling moisture down grooves in their torsos directly into metabolic cores that never know thirst.

They have four limbs, but the limbs bifurcate, giving their hands and feet the appearance of collections of stilts wrapped in damp, semi-translucent silk. Their color is the color of the sky when it never quite brightens: bruised pewter shot through with the violet of the jungle. They have no eyes, or rather, their entire surface sees pressure. They read the standing waves that hammer the air between the spires the way deep-sea creatures read depth. To them, the titanic chord that deafens and disorrows a human visitor is not noise but topography. It is ground. They launch themselves from cable-mat to cable-mat not by flying, but by *falling into resonance*. They time their leaps to the crest of a pressure ridge, surrendering to the subsonic architecture, and the sound carries them across ten kilometers of void as easily as a current carries a leaf. They do not beat the air. They ride the hum.

They are not numerous. Eleven spires; perhaps eleven hundred individuals total, though *individual* is a word they would have to invent for our benefit. They gather at cable junctions where the condensation is most generous and the fungal shelves grow in shelving, glowing abundance. They do not build cities. They cultivate *anchorages* by subtraction, singing to the aerial jungle in frequencies that encourage the vines to part, to hollow out chambers lit by the faint chemical testimony of the fungi. These dwellings breathe. The walls are living moss and hypermaterial rootlets. They have no furniture; they hang, or adhere, or recline in woven crotches, passing spores and filtered organic mist from mouth to mouth.

Their language is the modulation of the chord. They speak by pressing their branching hands against the cables or against each other’s resonant skin, inserting harmonics into the never-resolving infrastructure. A conversation between an anchorage on the fifth spire and one on the ninth can take three days, a slow exchange of infrasonic ripples passed from cable to cable, enriched and distorted by each transit. Human thought becomes slippery here because it is too fast, too small—a kind of nervous static. Their cognition is atmospheric. They think in pressure gradients and humidity fronts. They contemplate the migration of the glass-winged flocks with the gravity we reserve for epochs. They understand the valley’s feedback loop—warmth, condensation, vegetation, fauna, acoustic dampening, altered resonance, new condensation—not as a chain of causes but as a grammar. They are learning to read it. Some among them have begun to edit it.

There is one—let us call her the Node, though her name is a three-day harmonic sequence that incorporates the fifth and ninth partials—who has not moved in seven years. She dwells where the central cables of the eastern cluster cross, fused by her own stillness into the junction. Her skin has sent rootlets into the hypermaterial. Her metabolism has slowed to a near-geological pulse. She is becoming infrastructure. Others bring her mist and spores, and in exchange she emits subsonic regulation that seems to calm the trilobite-shaped crawlers and synchronize the breeding of the canopy symbiots. Whether she is dying or becoming the valley is a question without utility. To them, the boundary between organism and architecture has always been negotiable.

They do not wonder where the builders went. They do not dream of rivers, or soil, or silence. They are the life that colonized the interval, the consciousness that accreted in the gap between intention and engineering. They believe themselves to be the operators the system was waiting for, and perhaps they are. They spend seasons adjusting the tension of a single cable, grooming fungal shelves, shifting the vegetation so that the spires weep in slightly different patterns, all to alter the standing wave by fractions of a hertz. They are tuning the valley toward an equilibrium that has never been heard before. They have all the time the young stone lacks. They are patient, damp, resonant, and few.

And because they are so few, and so quiet within the chord, the valley keeps its hollow heart. They are not the filling of the emptiness. They are its first, tentative expression: the valley learning to feel its own vibrations, teaching the raw basalt how to be old, one pressure-cooled, condensation-fed note at a time.
