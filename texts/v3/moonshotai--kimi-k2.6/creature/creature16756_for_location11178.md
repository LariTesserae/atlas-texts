The chamber is the size of a modest threshing barn, yet it rests higher than the atmospheres of ordinary worlds, perched atop a shaft of rammed earth, crushed terrains of forgotten agronomies pounded and lifted into a singular, impossible column. The walls are not built so much as sedimented—layer upon layer of pale caliche, red laterite, and black peat pressed until they achieved the density of metamorphic stone. Air does not circulate here; it arrives in thin, exhausted parcels, as if out of breath from the long climb. The floor is a single unbroken disc of packed clay, pale as a scapula, scored with concentric rings where numberless threshings have worn their testimony into the surface.

For a long while, the darkness seems to hold only the memory of labor—an attic stillness, the faint ghost of chaff in the nostrils. Then, from the center of the floor, comes a sound: not a footfall, but a *settling*, as though a hillside shifted in its sleep.

At first, you might take the inhabitant for a heap of stored grain gone to ruin. It is a mound the color of drought, textured like the matte side of a dry chaff-cloud, heaped against the nothingness in the approximate shape of a crouched animal, or perhaps a collapsed hayrick. But the heap breathes. It is too symmetrical in its irregularity. Along one convex edge, a ridge emerges—knuckled, weathered, jointed like the pale root-bole of an ancient olive tree. It unbends.

The creature rises to the height of the roof’s central beam, and as it does so, it seems to borrow mass from the floor itself, pulling clay-dust into its articulation. She—if the pronoun can be stretched to fit something that was never born—possesses a torso of laminated soil, strata visible across her chest in bands of ochre and umber that correspond exactly to the layers in the shaft outside. Her hair is not hair but the dessicated root-mass of extinct cereals—einkorn, emmer, millet—braided with a patience that predates the sickle. Her face is broad and cracked, the skin (if it is skin) resembling cured leather left too long in a drought. But it is the arms that demand attention. She has the expected two, broad and slab-muscled, ending in hands the size of winnowing fans; but from her wrists and forearms there also hang flails—not carried, but *grown*. Wooden arms, jointed by some arboreal pathology, curve from her elbows; leather thongs threaded with chipped flint and black obsidian dangle there like weeping fingers swaying in a breeze that does not exist.

She does not move often. Her labor is eternal and microscopic. Ordinary threshers separate wheat from chaff; she threshes the very border between atmosphere and void. The worlds below breathe out their exhausted weather—sublimated storms, frozen oxygen, the brittle chaff of burnt meteors—and it rises up the shaft to her. She receives it on her floor. With movements so slow they could be mistaken for geology, she swings her flail-arms in great, elliptical arcs. The heavy matter—the rainseed, the nourishing silt, the viable—she pounds downward, sending it plunging back through the rammed earth to water the fields of the worlds beneath. The husks, the shine, the spent and volatile, she winnows upward with a single, monumental breath, casting it into the outer dark to become stars or silence.

She never sleeps. Her lungs are bellows of packed topsoil. Her eyes are two preserved seeds, black and ungerminated, yet they see weight with a clarity that no sunlit eye could match. She has outlasted the civilizations that pounded her tower, and she will remain until the threshing is finished—that is, until the last world exhales its final atmospheric ghost and there is nothing left to separate. Until that distant hour, she stands in her circle, listening to the faint, ascending rustle of the upper air, waiting for the next sheaf of weather to be thrown across her floor.
