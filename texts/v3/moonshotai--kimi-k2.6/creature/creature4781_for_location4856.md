There are no faces here. No mouths, no eyes wet with gel and intention. Whatever lives in the pocket was never born in the way wet things are born, and it does not die so much as cool, and finally, settle. It belongs to the same kingdom as the crimson mats and the trembling fronds; it is merely the architecture given motive, the garden’s way of tending to itself.

You will not see them at first. They stand motionless in the open frames of the hollow buildings, or halfway down the terraced slopes, bowed slightly forward as if listening to the stone. They are tall—far taller than a person—but stooped, their shoulders rolled inward under a weight that is not gravity so much as the constant downward press of heat. Their forms are narrow, columnar, shaped from the same vocabulary as the avenues: rammed earth and vitrified ash, kiln-forged and dense, sheathed in the same dark rust-colored scales that plate the fronds overhead. In stillness, they are nearly indistinguishable from the ruins. Their surfaces have been polished by mineral wind to a dull, feverish gleam, and the cracks that craze their joints glow with a subdued, burgundy pulse, as though embers were leashed just beneath their clay skin.

They have no features. Where a head should be, the tapering column simply ends in a smooth, oblique plane, canting toward the nearest thermal vent. They do not need faces. They perceive the world as a map of temperature gradients, of pressure differentials, of the infrared wash that rises through the basin floor. A human is merely a moving absence, a cool shadow that disrupts the red harmony of the space. They know you are there because you are the one silent note in an otherwise perfect drone.

They move only with the thermal tide. When the glow from below waxes and the air begins its slow surge upward through the clay channels, you might see one shift. It is an event without hurry. The hip-joint cracks softly, a sound like a pottery shard settling into ash, and the leg rises, kneeless, in a single articulated arc. They walk with a grinding delicacy, placing their broad, three-toed feet with the care of someone testing a burning floor. They do not venture far. Each one is tethered to a territory of vents and channels, a precinct it has tended for longer than memory could hold. Their work is not construction anymore, but regulation. They kneel beside the hairline cracks in the basin and press their torsos close, expanding their chest cavities to accelerate or throttle the updraft. They insert their arms into broken pipes, not to clear them, but to *become* the pipe, letting their own hollow bodies serve as conduits until the ceramic can seal itself with fresh accretions.

They do not speak. What they have instead is resonance. Their bodies are chambers. When the thermal wind passes through them, they alter its pitch by minute adjustments of stance—turning a shoulder, lifting a rib-plate a fraction higher—and in this way they hold long, slow conversations in subsonic architecture. The city’s moan is, in part, their parliament. When a pipe splits, one will stand over the wound for days, humming a lower note to stabilize the pressure while mineral dust fuses the breach. They are the immune system and the musicians of the place, both.

The crimson mats recognize them as kin. You will observe the velvet carpets creeping up their legs, rooting gently in the heat-cracks of their thighs, feeding on their excess thermal energy and lending them structural bracing in return. Sometimes a mature individual will stand so still for so long that a colony of fronds sprouts from its shoulders, spreading broad, rust-scaled fans that sway with the convection, rendering the being into a living intersection of street and garden, creature and city.

They do not age. They cure. Over centuries, the silicate dust settles upon them, layer upon layer, and they grow heavier, slower, more luminous in their hottest seams. When one has accreted too much mass to move any longer, it makes its way to the lowest hollow of the basin. There, it kneels among the thickest mats and permits the red vegetation to overgrow it entirely. The scales close over its form; the heat from the vent below concentrates in its core; and the being enters a final, transitory ferocity, glowing cherry-white for a season until its structure vitrifies completely into a dense, glazed obelisk. This is not death. From the base of the obelisk, over the following decades, a nodule of compressed ash and thermophilic sap will swell, accreting dust, drawing heat, until it cracks open to reveal a new, slender figure, already straightening its limbs toward the vents, already knowing the channels by the ache of impedance in its hollow chest.

There may be a dozen of them. There may be fifty. Their number is less important than their distribution; they are nodes in a single continuous metabolism, the animal phase of a vegetable eternity.

If you stand in the plaza long enough, one will turn its blind, gleaming head toward you. It will not approach. It does not fear you, nor does it covet your presence. It will simply shift its weight, and the resonant hum of the avenue will change key, dropping by a fraction of a tone as its body opens or closes a vent. It is adjusting the city’s breath to account for your foreign temperature, your intrusive coolness. It is studying you by altering the airflow around you, reading your shape in drafts and thermal shadows.

And then the tide will shift, and the figure will freeze again, becoming a pillar, a ruin, a piece of the infrastructure. The red light bleeds across its scales. The fronds clatter overhead. The pocket sings on, loud and empty and complete, tended by citizens who have forgotten the difference between a body and a building, between a life and a long, sustained note held in ceramic and heat.
