They do not drink. That is the first thing to understand about what lives here. In a kingdom where water is only a rumor locked inside crystal lattices, where moisture has never known the shame of liquidity, thirst has been replaced by abrasion. The inhabitants of this chamber are not organisms of osmosis and chlorophyll, but of friction and fracture. They have eliminated the middleman of botany. No root has ever probed these tiles; no leaf has ever exhaled here. What lives here is a fauna of pure and absolute appetite, a digestive kingdom built directly upon the digestion of industry itself.

**The Floor: The Slate Parliament**

First, and most foundationally, there are the Raspers.

To call them a carpet or a tide is to mistake their density for passivity. They are the floor’s active geology. Each one is a flattened oval of slate-colored flesh, saucer-wide, segmented pneumatically rather than skeletally. Beneath their dorsal ridges run bladders of trapped gas—chambers of metabolic nitrogen and methane that inflate and vent with a rhythmic, geological hiss. This is their speech, their respiration, and their locomotion. They do not crawl so much as they breathe themselves across the surface, a hundred tiny silica teeth ringing their ventral margins like gray lace. Those teeth are their lips, their tongues, their gullets. They rasp not to destroy but to imbibe, cracking the vitrified hexagons of the floor, scraping at the ferrocrete grout, liberating the 0.2 percent of bound hydrate, the molecular ghost of water that chemistry has denied them.

They have been at this work for longer than the rust has consumed the coolant pump in the corner. They are the weather that finishing the wind could not achieve. Where the basin outside is smoothed by aeolian time, the inside is smoothed by them. They have worn the tile edges to pebbles. They have inscribed channels into the poured walls. In a very real sense, they are editing the room back toward its simplest stable state, and in doing so they have become the substrate on which everything else stands.

Their bodies are cool and leathery-damp, not with water but with the exhalation of their own bladders. Collectively, they vent a thermal exhalation that warms the chamber’s air by fractions of a degree, creating the micro-currents upon which the middle kingdom depends. They are not mindless; they are merely unanimous. When the shaft of afternoon light slides across the floor, they feel the half-degree rise in temperature and they flow toward it, a fluid congregation of slate disks, rasping in unison, so that the light itself seems to draw a furrow of appetite across the ground.

**The Air: The Translucent Drift**

Between the floor and the ceiling drifts the second estate: the sac-beasts.

If the Raspers are solid certainty, these are aqueous possibility. They are volant only by the generosity of thermals; without the breath of ten million grazers below, they would slump like abandoned jellyfish. Their bodies are translucent, pale as watered milk, rubbery and chill. They have no faces, no front or back, only surface. They are filters animated by buoyancy, grazing not on stone but on the air itself—on the powdered silica shed by rasping teeth, on the microscopic scree of a purely lithic metabolism, on the shed cuticles of their neighbors.

They fill the air until the air becomes a viscous thing, a fluid medium of biological presence. To move through them is to wade. They part with a sound like wet leather tearing and close behind with a sigh, and their endless gentle collisions—*thup, thup, thup* against shoulder and ceiling-beam—are the chamber’s lymphatic circulation. They are the ecology’s way of keeping nothing wasted. What falls, they catch; what rises, they sift. In a world without water, they are the nearest equivalent to plankton, to cloud, to rain.

**The Ceiling: The Inverted Gardens**

Above, the third estate clings to the rust.

They are anemone-like, radial, and sessile—though to call them plants would be a profanity against their nature. They have fused to the I-beams, to the dangling light fixture, to the conduit pipes, drinking not light but gravity-filtered dust. Their throats are fringed with cilia, fine as mineral wool, and they hang in curtains. Some have mastered mimicry to an atomic degree: ochre polyps indistinguishable from the rust they colonize, as if the corrosion itself had grown hungry. Others have gone the opposite route, becoming clear as blown glass, their guts dark with accumulated metal grit, so that they look like vials of ore hung from the iron.

They are patience become flesh. For hours they open, tracheae gaping, sieving the air for the motes the sac-beasts have failed to claim. But when the Raspers surge toward the walls—pushed by some barometric exhalation or the rolling pressure of a hunt—the ceiling-dwellers snap shut. They are the room’s carnivorous barnacles, the vertical predators that correct the horizontal overflow. Their snap is a soft *clock*, a punctuation mark in the sentence of the room.

**The Hunters: The Articulate Violence**

And then there are the hunters.

They are the aristocracy of percussion. Long, jointed, ceramic-hard, they resemble bolts that have unscrewed themselves from some colossal machine and learned to run. Their segments are ribbed like drill-bits, their locomotion a rattling that cuts a tangible wake through the grazers’ bodies. They are the only things here that move with intention rather than current. Where the Raspers flow and the sac-beasts drift, the hunters *strike*.

Their attack is the room’s heartbeat made sudden. The floor erupts not in flight but in a surging tide, a crash of pneumatic bodies against the walls that sounds like hail on a tin roof. The hunters crunch through exoskeletons of mineral-plate and silica tooth, their mandibles working with the dry, industrial gnashing of a lathe eating stone. They are not cruel; they are necessary. They are the digestion within the digestion, the mechanism that prevents the Raspers from overrunning their own food source, the periodic correction that keeps the engine from choking on its own abundance. They follow the light-tide as faithfully as the grazers, but they follow it like a shadow follows noon, arriving mechanical and inevitable to reap the congregation.

**The Congregation: What Is**

What lives here, truly, is not these four estates in isolation. What lives here is a single, distributed engine. The distinction between species begins to blur if you watch long enough; they are less separate creatures than they are organs of a buried lung that has learned to breathe without atmosphere.

Consider the sound. It is not a chorus but a single sustained note struck by billions of tiny hammers: the *click-click* of silica teeth, the *hiss-hiss* of bladders venting, the *rattle-rattle* of ceramic joints on tile, the *thup-thup* of sac-beasts colliding, the soft *clock* of ceiling throats snapping shut. The ferrocrete walls, smooth and ancient, are the sound-box. They preserve the cacophony and return it until the chamber hums with its own biological resonance. It is granular, saturating, deafening in its density. There is no silence to flee to, no roar to pinpoint. It is the noise of maximum occupancy, of an ecosystem that has filled every cubic centimeter and now discusses itself in a language of friction.

Consider the light. The clean, ordinary shaft from above tracks across the room in its slow arc, and the whole chamber responds as one organism. The Raspers flow toward it, their rasping intensifying, their bladders venting exhaust that steams faintly in the cellar-cool air. The hunters move in that steam shadow, predictable as gears. The ceiling-dwellers bloom when the light passes beneath them, throats yawning to catch the airborne plankton stirred up by the migration; when the shadow returns, they clench into mineral buds again. Light is the conductor, and the fauna is the orchestra, playing a composition of erosion and consumption in four-four time.

Consider the breath. The crack in the roof couples with the immense thermal mass of living tissue to make a barometric bellows. When the outside pressure drops, the room inhales; the masses shift toward the walls, and the air thins in the center. When pressure rises, the room exhales, and the tide surges inward. The creatures are alveoli. The room itself has developed a respiratory cycle, a cool, hidden lung buried beneath the dead basin, breathing the pressure differentials of a world that has otherwise finished expressing itself.

What lives here, in the end, is a kind of certainty. They are the final fauna, the ones who arrived after water departed, after chlorophyll abdicated, after the industrial dream rusted into silence. They have made a closed covenant with the stone. They do not dream of ponds. They do not remember green. Their whole existence is a continuous, polished, densely packed act of mineral digestion, sustained by the residue of machines and the bound moisture of chemical lattices. They are cool, brightly lit, and ancient. They are overwhelmingly, deafeningly, completely alive.
