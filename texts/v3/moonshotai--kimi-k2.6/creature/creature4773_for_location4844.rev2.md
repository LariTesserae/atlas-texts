What lives here has learned to treat the artificial as native.

Begin with the filter-feeders, the silver school, because they are the sky. Each individual is the length of a human hand, but they have not operated as individuals for sixty years. They move as a single respiratory event, millions of ventral surfaces pulsing in metronomic unison, gill-rakers combing the water for the metalloid snow that sifts down from the smelting cadence above. Their silvering is not merely coloration. It is freight. Generations of stripping copper salts, antimony, and rare earth precipitates from the effluent have burdened their tissues with accumulated ore. They are heavier than they should be, slower to rise, denser in death. When one dies, it does not float; it sinks like a dropped coin, a brief, bright note in the green murk, contributing not to the soft ooze of a natural seabed but to the angular geometry of the poured slag below. The ventral sides of their bodies have darkened to a soot-grey from decades of staring up at the overcast lamps, while their backs remain pale, fed by the false starlight of perforations in the ceiling. They are the only creatures here who know weather, because the false twilight is the only weather they have ever needed. When the shift-lamps gutter in sequence, a signal travels through the school faster than the light itself—electrostatic, chemical, ancient even in its engineering—and the entire mass wheels toward the ceiling, a single thought of hunger rising to meet the dark.

They are prey, technically, though predation here is a slow and hydraulics-driven affair. The bottom-crawlers, the armored things the size of terriers, do not chase. They are foundry-floor detritus given appetite and patience. Their carapaces are not chitin in any classical sense but sclerite laced with precipitated ferric oxides, dark red and flaking in sheets that mirror the rust blooms on the lowest reinforcement tiers. They gather in the thermal oases where outflows bleed waste heat into the surrounding four-degree vastness, raising the local temperature to a luxurious ten degrees Celsius. Here, the engineered kelps and hairline algae manage their sparse photosynthesis, and the crawlers graze with piston-driven mouths—hydraulic feeder jaws that thump against the ceramic substrate to dislodge filaments. They are territorial in the way that valves are territorial: they claim a radius of warm current and defend it with the clanging of armor against armor, a dull industrial percussion that adds to the Sea’s constant voice. Over generations, they have become not merely consumers but curators. Their droppings fertilize the oases; their grazing prevents the kelp from overreaching the outflow grilles and starving. They tend the only gardens in the Compensation Sea, gardens that exist solely because machinery bleeds.

Between the warm oases and the silver high-roads of the midwater, the girder-clickersClaim the architecture itself. These crustacean analogues were meant to be janitors, a mobile crew designed to scour moldlines and weld beads of colonizing biofilm before it could clog inspection ports. They escaped containment and became inhabitants. They traverse the ferroceramic trusswork, the intake grilles, the knife-edge rims of settling basins, moving in a ceaseless peristaltic procession. Their claws do not grasp so much as test; they tap, they gauge, they click. Workers on the dry side have learned—not formally, but in the folklore of maintenance crews—that a change in the rhythm of the clickers means a change in the Sea. When the thermal contraction in the ceiling pipes becomes too sharp, too rapid, the clickers fall silent, retreating into the interstices of pylons. When the circulation turbines drift off-harmonic, the clickers gather at the intake mouths in dense aggregations, sensing the pressure differential before human instruments register it. They have become a diagnostic, a living telemetry system that the dry side never requested and does not officially acknowledge. They eat the oxidation. They eat the algae. They keep the angry architecture from softening into decay, and in exchange, the structure provides them with infinite, labyrinthine territory.

And then there are the anode angels. No one introduced them. They are an emergent form, a biological event that occurred when the silver schools, the crawlers, and the metallic snow collaborated by accident. Where a spent sacrificial anode falls from a worker’s gloved hand and drifts to the basal plate, where a dead filter-feeder sinks and lodges against a drainage flange, where a stunted kelp anchors to a weld bead—these three things conspire. The anode, rich in zinc and designed to corrode so the pylon does not, becomes a furnace of electrolysis in miniature. The metalloid-laden flesh of the dead feeder feeds a bloom of chemophagic bacteria. The kelp, if it survives, grows not toward the absent sun but toward the warmth of oxidation. Over a decade, an anode angel accretes: a lump the size of a fuel drum, half mineral and half tissue, sessile, warm to the touch, humming faintly with galvanic potential. Small clickers nest in its crevices. Larval filter-feeders, too young to school, drift in the eddy of its chemical outgassing. It is the closest thing the Compensation Sea has to a reef. It has no precedent in any design document. It simply lives, a negotiation between utility and time.

The workers traverse all of this. They descend in lock-step shifts, their heated suits glowing faintly at the joints, carrying waterproofed clipboards and replacement anodes that may, in twenty years, become angels. They do not think of themselves as inhabitants. They are commuters, dry-siders, temporary aliens in an environment built to be endured rather than inhabited. And yet they are the most thoroughly synchronized organisms in the entire Sea. They exhale in the pauses between drop-forge thunder. Their hearts, over years of descent, attune to the basso drone of the circulation turbines. They feel the false twilight in their sternums before their helmet lamps register the dimming. Some veteran technicians—those who have spent decades on the same pylon rotation—have begun to recognize individual crawlers by the oxide-patterns on their carapaces. They know which anode angels are old enough to harvest for scrap (they don’t; it is forbidden, though no one remembers why) and which silver schools will part cleanly around a diver’s frame versus which will swirl in confused eddies, heavy with metal, brushing against faceplates in a dry, scales-on-glass whisper.

They speak of the Sea as a coolant bath, a pressure mass, a necessary buffer. But the ones who have been down longest know the truth: the Compensation Sea has started to compensate for them, too. The animals do not fear them. The clickers do not halt their diagnostics when a suited figure passes. The filter-feeders, in their millions, have begun to adjust their vertical migrations by a few seconds to avoid the lock-step exhalation bubbles of the descending shifts. The crawlers graze on, undisturbed, as workers inspect outflow temperatures inches from their hydraulic jaws. The boundary between the maintained and the wild here is not a sharp flange but a smear, a mutual dulling of edges. The workers poured this ocean, braced its walls, calculated its currents. Then they introduced life to clean up after the machinery. Now the life has settled in, has grown heavy with the byproducts of industry, has begun to inform the machinery in turn, and the workers—cold, suited, refusing to marvel—descend into it every shift, breathing carefully, moving with the current, at home in a world that rings with the sound of something too large to stop.
