What lives in Condensate is not a populace in the usual sense. It is a consortium of accretions, a slow agreement between animal desire and mineral process. The humans—if that word still fits—are perhaps the least numerous and the most recent species to have adapted to the caldera’s logic. They did not conquer this place. They *precipitated* out of it.

Begin with the bodies. The light, which arrives as an assault, has sculpted them. Over cycles too long for a visitor to witness, their biology has entered the same negotiations with brightness and silence that governed the striders and the scab-pigeons. Their eyes are not merely shielded; they are altered. A nictitating membrane, pale and crystalline, films each orb—not flesh, but a lens grown from the same photonic tissue that laces the floatsacs. Behind it, the iris has atrophied into a narrow fracture, dark as obsidian, filtering the white weight of the sun into manageable geometries. A newcomer learns to navigate by dark interruptions; a native is born knowing that shadow is the true terrain, and that to see is to read the negative spaces the light cannot hold.

Their skin tells another story. It is not tanned but glazed. A lifetime of exposure to the mineral-laden vapor has left them with a subcutaneous deposit of silica, a fine, ceramic patina that turns their complexions the color of old porcelain, of sinter, of the cream-colored terraces themselves. This is not armor, though it protects; it is a record. Each inhabitant wears their history in calcified streaks where sweat once ran. They do not bathe in the conventional sense—they undergo *sloughing*, standing in the hottest steam vents until the old layer cracks and peels in translucent sheets, revealing the newer, pinker mineral film beneath. Beauty here is measured in refractive index, in the smoothness of one’s thermal glaze, in the mirror-quality of a cheek that has accreted decades of condensed brine.

They breathe, but the apparatus is no longer external. The rebreathers of the first settlers have long since been internalized, obsolete hardware replaced by biology. Their lungs are lined with a chemosynthetic symbiont, a microscopic cousin to the striders, filtering scalding vapor and extracting not just oxygen but trace nutritive salts. To open one’s mouth in Condensate is not to speak but to drink the air. Their voices, when they must make them, emerge as subvocal steam—hums so low they travel not through the thin, hungry atmosphere but through the ground itself, through feet planted on basalt, transmitted bone-to-bone in brief, seismic exchanges. A conversation in Condensate looks like a dance: two figures facing each other, touching fingertips to ceramic pressure-pads, or simply standing still, letting their joined heartbeats speak through the earth. Sound, here, is a private tremor.

But the humans are only one constituency. To live in Condensate is to accept that the town belongs equally to the striders, the scab-pigeons, the mantids, and the floatsacs. Indeed, the categories blur. A citizen is anyone who waits, who accretes, who participates in the silence.

The **striders** are perhaps the truest citizens. They have colonized the industrial infrastructure as naturally as they have the hot pools. In the lower levels of the extraction plants, where flash-evaporators leave drifts of pure silica, striders congregate in patient congregations, their translucent bodies backlit by the amber pulse of their own sieving organs. The inhabitants do not shoo them; they are living gauges of thermal health. A change in the striders’ gait signals a shift in the caldera’s breath, a pressure drop or a new fissure opening in the deep. Children—those rare, slow-growing offspring of the Condensed—learn arithmetic by counting strider legs, learn patience by watching one pause at a meniscus for an hour to drink. When a strider dies and fuses into the floor within a week, its body is not cleared but incorporated. Doorways are adjusted around these fossils. A hallway that narrows because a strider died in it is considered improved, not obstructed; the architecture *wants* these contributions.

The **scab-pigeons**, those sessile lumps of appetite, are the town’s memory. They grow on the walls in generational layers, each one sipping condensation and excreting a pure grade of ceramic binder. The oldest buildings are not maintained by human hands but by the accumulated excretions of centuries of scab-pigeons. The inhabitants harvest the excess—not with picks, but with gentle, coaxing strokes that persuade the organisms to release a bead of binding material. It is a domestication so complete that the architecture is a collaboration. A home is considered mature when its walls are entirely obscured by living, drinking stone. To touch a building is to touch a thousand hungry mouths. The oldest district, up on the gentler inner slope, has walls three feet thick with generations of scab-pigeon colonies; the inhabitants speak of those walls by reciting lineages, knowing which layer was laid in a heavy steam year, which in a dry cycle.

The **floatsacs** are the weather and the calendar. Their color and altitude predict pressure changes. When they drift low, clustering around the cooling racks in silent, glistening herds, the inhabitants know the steam cycle is entering a quiescent phase. At these times, the Condensed emerge from their subterranean chambers in greater numbers, moving between buildings to conduct the trade that cannot be automated: the exchange of bodily sloughings, which are rich in rare proteins; the inspection of the ionic separators; the silent, tactile councils where decisions are made. The floatsacs, in their diaphanous drifting, are witnesses. And their young, when they fission and rain down in gelatinous beads onto the hot rocks, are gathered in dark glass bowls and fermented into a briny, translucent wine—the only intoxicant that can survive the heat. To drink it is to dream in convection currents, to feel oneself expand and drift toward the cooling racks.

Most crucial, most unsettling, are the **terrace mantids**. The inhabitants do not worship them, for worship implies a hierarchy of voice and response that does not exist here. Instead, they *emulate* them. The mantids are the aristocracy of stillness. They stand on the sinter ledges like philosophers parked at the edge of an endless computation. The Condensed believe—or rather, behave as if—the mantids are processing the landscape in thoughts too vast and slow for mammalian minds to intercept. A human in Condensate learns early that motion is not the default; stillness is. To stand motionless for hours, absorbing heat, letting the mineral air deposit its microscopic gifts upon the skin, is considered a form of work, a state of gathering. The mantids, when they strike, demonstrate the only kind of violence permitted here: absolute, silent, and brief. The inhabitants have adopted this economy of action. Disputes are not argued. They are settled by standing in opposition until one party moves away, acknowledging by their retreat that the other’s stillness was more deeply rooted. A murder, if one occurred, would be a swift, noiseless extension of a limb, over before the steam could drift between them.

What, then, of their society? It is a society without noise, without hurry, without accumulation of speech. There is no government building, no square. Authority is held by the *Keepers of the Meniscus*—those individuals who have achieved a skin patina so thick and an inner silence so profound that they can lie prone at the edge of the boiling mirror-pools for a full day without blinking, monitoring the color shift from aquamarine to blood orange. They read the caldera’s mood in mineral saturation. Their rulings are not announced; they are inscribed on thin silica plates that are left on the main transit paths. A law here is a physical object you must step around or pick up. A tax is a measured contribution of one’s own calcified sloughings to the construction of new walls. A festival—rare, almost geologically spaced—occurs when an obsidian vein surfaces anew, polished by subterranean currents until it shines like spilled ink. The inhabitants gather, not in a crowd, but in a scattering, each standing at the correct distance to see the black glass shine, and they remain there, sometimes for days, watching the light return to itself.

They do not marry so much as *co-precipitate*. Two individuals who have spent years in silent proximity, whose thermal rhythms have synchronized until they rise and slough and drink at the same moments, will eventually move into the same low, ceramic dwelling. Their union is marked not by a ceremony but by the installation of a shared condensation trough. Over time, their sloughed skins, mixed in the household scab-pigeon colonies, create a unique ceramic signature for their home. Children, when they come, are born in the coolest hours, deep in the subterranean chambers where the earth’s breath is merely warm, not scalding. Newborns are wet with a caul of mineral film, and their first act is not to cry—for what would be the use?—but to open their dark, narrow eyes to the assault of light and close their mouths against the steam. They are raised communally in the sense that every adult is a model of stillness for every child. They learn to walk by imitation of the strider: patient, deliberate, the thermal gel on their own thickened footsoles allowing them to tread the scalding basalt with the same patient deliberation. They learn to read the upward-flowing water not as strangeness but as plumbing, as hydrology’s quiet agreement to run against gravity.

And death. The most telling adaptation. The dead are not buried, for the ground is too hot, too hard, too much itself. They are carried to the sinter terraces and placed where the steam pressure forces water uphill. The body is left until the mineral-laden vapor, the very breath of Condensate, deposits layer upon layer of silica and salt upon it. Within a month, a human corpse becomes indistinguishable from the landscape—not as a haunting, but as a contribution. The dead become balustrades. They become threshold stones. They become the dark interruptions by which the living navigate. In this way, the emptiness between the buildings is not empty. It is populated by the fused, patient bodies of those who stood still long enough to become architecture. Walk from the extraction plant toward the housing district on the far rim, and you are not stepping across dead space. You are crossing the accumulated residue of generations, human and otherwise, all of it now mineral, all of it now home.

So if you ask who lives in Condensate, the answer is: a silence that has taken form. The inhabitants are a kind of moving mineral, a biological ceramic, a temporary arrangement of the same salts and steam that built the caldera. They have no ambition to leave. They have no literature of sound. Their history is written in the thickness of walls, in the fossil record of striders and people indistinguishable from one another. They live in the mandatory spaces between things, the intervals of steam and light, and they have learned that to survive here, one must not merely endure the assault of brightness and the discipline of quiet. One must consent to become part of the deposit. To live in Condensate is to agree, at last, to be added to the layers.
