The ones who belong to this place do not look like visitors. Your eye, trained on horizons, will miss them at first; they have the stillness of infrastructure, and the needle towers were grown partly in their image. They are tall—not monumentally, but tensilely, stretched by the habit of walking fifty kilometers to borrow a tool, a hundred to exchange a word. Their bones are not calcium-white but a denser thing, a ceramic-fused lattice that shares molecular kinship with the towers themselves. Evolution did not give them this skeleton; patience did. Over generations, their bodies learned to precipitate minerals from the iron-sulfur water, reinforcing the architecture of their frames until they became, in a sense, the first native geology that could move.

You will notice their skin before their faces. It is thick, opalescent, and damp, more like the interior of a mussel shell than mammal hide. It holds the water. In the violent blue of noon, they seem to glow with stored moisture, and in the flat pearl of mist, they become the color of old fractures in quartz. They do not wear boots. The soles of their feet are broad, black, and deeply fissured, callused through decades of direct contact with the cooling stone; they feel the plateau’s subsonic hum the way you feel your own pulse. When a thermal crack rifles open thirty kilometers away, they know its direction and depth before the echo has begun. They stand barefoot on the wet black stone, and the cold seeps upward into them without urgency, because their body temperature is not a fixed number but a negotiated gradient, only a few degrees warmer than the land itself.

They do not breathe the way you do. The crystalline air, that thin glass, destroyed the lungs of their ancestors or their pre-forms. Now they inhale once, perhaps twice in an hour—a slow, conscious act in which the air is drawn through spiral, mineral-plated chambers where it is warmed, humidified, and stripped of its sharpness before touching living tissue. Between breaths, they live on the chemistry of the weeping walls. They press their mouths to the stone and drink the oxygen-poor seepage, their guts hosting chemosynthetic gardens descended from the bacterial mats that first claimed this infant rock. They are not above the food chain; they are a quiet articulation of it. Their blood runs sluggish and dark, loaded with sulfur-metabolites, and when they bleed onto the fresh basalt, the stain is the same yellow as the condensing sulfur crystals in the crevices.

What they see, if they see at all in your sense, is thermal and tactile. Their eyes are large, lidless in the dry air, shielded by nictitating membranes that filter the high-altitude blue. But sight is a secondary sense here, where distance is too vast for detail and fog erases the middle ground. They navigate by wetness. A finger dragged across a ridge reads the previous hour’s condensation like braille. They know the age of a fissure by the quantity of water beading in its throat. To travel between the needle towers is to follow an invisible hydrology, moving from seep to seep, reading the mirror-bright films that pool in depressions no older than a human lifetime. They carry no maps drawn on paper. Their maps are mineral.

They speak, but not in the air. The atmosphere is too thin to carry voices, and noise is an obscenity in the vastness anyway. They speak through the ground. Standing barefoot on the stone, they generate subsonic vibrations in their thoraxes—deep, thrumming notes that enter the crust and travel farther than any shout, relayed through the same medium that carries the herds’ silent communications. A conversation held across a day’s walk is not uncommon; the words arrive as tremors in the soles of the feet, a language of pressure and release. When two of them meet in person, which happens rarely and with ceremony, they do not waste the ground-signal. They stand close, nose to collarbone, and exchange water. From the soft sac beneath the tongue, each offers a draft of filtered seepage, heavy with personal bacteria, mineral memory, and the chemistry of the tower they tend. To drink another’s water is to know what they know: the thermal profile of their sector, the rate of a new cooling fracture, the health of the lichen they have encouraged. It is communion. It is census. It is the only kind of intimacy that survives the scale.

For each of them is a steward of an enormous silence. A single needle tower, or a cluster of two or three low domes, is their responsibility across a territory the size of a vanished sea. They are not lonely; loneliness requires the expectation of nearness. Instead, they have expanded to fill the psychological space available, becoming thinkers of continental slowness. A thought may take a season to fully unfold. A decision—whether to seed a new thermal vent, whether to accelerate the cooling of a ridge by opening a ceramic channel—may be pondered for a decade. They are midwives, not masters. Their technology, which can span raw terrain without support and grow spires from the molecular bedrock, is deployed with the restraint of something that understands consequence across deep time. They do not build quickly, because quickness is for finished worlds. Here, to build quickly would be to wound a surface that has not yet decided its final form.

Their relation to the fauna is one of parallel drift. They do not hunt the white-furred predators or the herd-grazers with their geological hides; the metabolic cost is too high, the spiritual logic all wrong. The herds are kin, fellow lithotrophs eating the same bacterial sunlight. Sometimes, in the lee of a water-capture dome, you will find one of the tall ones standing motionless among the grazing bison-things, both species filtering microbes from the same sheet of runoff, neither startling the other. The predators watch from a distance, patient as governments, and the tall ones nod to them in recognition: *We are all conserving ourselves against the cold.*

Inside the towers—though *inside* is a rough word for spaces that are half architecture and half extended organ—there are no rooms as you know them. The structures are hollow, resonant, and damp. The walls breathe groundwater, filtering iron into reservoirs that feed both the inhabitant and the rootless willow-scrubs the inhabitant tends. Heat is managed, not expelled; residual geothermal currents are bled slowly through ceramic veins into the surrounding stone, accelerating the crust’s cooling in controlled patterns so that the plateau’s uprising does not tear itself apart in fits and starts. The tower and the tenant are continuous. You cannot say where the engineering ends and the body begins.

They die, though infrequently. When the mineral load in their blood exceeds the filters of their ceramic-cored livers, or when a sudden thermal contraction shears the stone beneath their feet before they can read the stress, they end. They do not leave corpses in the ordinary sense. Their kind places the dead in the freshest fissures, weighting them with black iridescent stone that matches the bedrock. There, in the active cooling fractures, their dense bones and mineral-rich tissue accelerate the crystalline bonding of the seam. A dead tower-keeper becomes, within a century, a vein of harder stone, a node of concentrated phosphate and ceramic in the infant crust. They are the first fossils of a world that has not yet learned how to bury its dead. The towers they tended are left empty for a millennium or more, left to complete their thermal negotiations with the alone, before a new wanderer arrives, tastes the water, and decides to resume the conversation.

If you meet one, they will not ask your name. Names are sounds for air, and air is the least important medium here. They will study the way heat traces off your too-warm body. They will notice that you breathe too often, that your boots deafen you to the ground’s speech, that your eyes search for a horizon you expect to end. They may offer you water from a flask grown from their ownFiltered resin, and it will taste of iron and sulfur and possibility. They will wait for you to understand that you are not lost, but merely premature—that you have stepped into a nursery where the continents are still being shown how to behave, and that the administrators of this realm are not old, but ancient in a new way, older than soil, older than the river that has not yet been born.

They are the plateau learning to remember itself. They are the expectation, finally taking shape.
