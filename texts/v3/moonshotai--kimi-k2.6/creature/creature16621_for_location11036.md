They are not a species. A species is a line drawn by an outside eye, a boundary invented to separate one thing from another in a world full of distinctions. Here there is no outside, and there are no distinctions. There is only the Warm, the Wet, the Dark, and the Cling. What fills the chamber is a single act of being, reiterated across ten thousand soft bodies until the act has become indistinguishable from the architecture itself. The mountain did not birth them so much as precipitate them, the way a saturated solution forgets it is liquid and suddenly marbles into something solid, blind, and utterly sufficient.

To look at one—though looking is a mercy they have never needed—is to look at a vessel that has forgiven itself for having no clear purpose beyond persistence. Each body is a housecat’s weight of translucent gel, stretched so thin over its own insides that it seems less a creature than a sample of the dark made briefly visible. Inside, the amber threads do not circulate; they migrate, viscous and dreamy, drawn by gravity and the slow peristaltic squeeze of marginal muscles. What read as cartilaginous ridges are not so much bones as fossil memories—the calcified afterimages of limbs that might once, in some unfathomable ancestor, have strained toward a stimulus they lacked the means to interpret. Now the ridges serve as guy-wires, holding the soft sacs in shape against the stone’s downward kiss, while the stubby paddles along their lengths taste the basalt for purchase. Each wet *click* is not a step but a claim, a microscopic etching of resin into rock that says: *here, here, here.* They sign their names into the mountain ten thousand times a minute, and the mountain, being old and patient, accepts the inscription.

They do not see. They have never seen. The darkness is not an absence to them but a medium they wear, something denser than water, richer than air. Their perception is distributed across the entire integument, a whole-body chemoreception that reads the chamber as a field of gradients rather than shapes. They know the ceiling by the copper-mist quality of the condensation that beads there; they know the floor-pools by the subtle increase in conductivity against their underbellies; they know one another by the shared infrasonic hum that resonates through the pooled fluid between their bodies. Navigation is not a decision but a settling. They gravitate toward warmth and moisture as a stone gravitates downward, without ambition and without error.

And yet to call them individuals would be to mistake a tide for a collection of drops. They are contiguous. The space between one body and the next is a fiction sustained only by the need for surface area, for more skin to trade vapor with the air. When a fat drop of water breaks from the ceiling and falls, the ripple that passes through them is not a signal. It is a hydraulic event, a readjustment of pressure, no more communicative than water finding its level. If you could peel back the strata, you would find no hard boundary where one ends and the next begins—only a shared mucilage, a mutual pulse, a communal hemolymph that seeps through fissures and joins them in a distributed metabolism. They eat the air, yes, and scrape microscopic tribute from the condensation, but they also eat one another’s exhalations, one another’s shed skins, one another’s slow and constant dying. The chamber digests through them, and they through it.

They breed, if that is the word, by excess. When the communal warmth grows too thick, when the vapor has been filtered and refiltered into a soup dense with mineral and nitrogen, the saturation point precipitates. Opalescent grapes cluster on the ceiling, neither fruit nor egg but something anterior to both: condensations of presence, heavy with gestation. For years they draw from the damp atmosphere, growing pearlescent skins that barely contain the amber fluid within. When they finally split, what emerges is not young, not naïve, not new. It is smaller, but it is already the same absolute thing: paddles clicking, gravity sensed, the chamber’s temperature mapped in its tissues before its first independent contraction. There is no parent, no offspring, only the chamber expressing itself again, the mountain generating more of the only shape that has ever fit here.

They do not fear you. Fear is a technology invented by things that run, that hide, that evolve in the presence of predation. These have never run. They have never needed to. When your foot comes down on the cool rubberiness of a body and you freeze, the freezing is yours alone. The creature beneath your sole does not startle; it accretes. It makes room for you the way it makes room for a new stonefall or a fresh vent of steam: as a redistribution of weight, a minor shift in the topography of the warm. Around your ankles they adjust, slow as cooling wax, their paddles finding purchase on your boot leather, your skin, tasting the alien salt and the mammalian fever of your blood. To them you are temporary architecture. A column of warmth that will cool. A ledge that walks. They would cling to you as readily as to the granite, and when you leave they will pool back into the depression you left behind, erasing your presence with the same patient indifference with which they accept the drip of water from above.

They are pre-complexity. Pre-specialization. They represent a road not taken by the rest of life, a branch that forked before the invention of mouths that chase, of eyes that startle, of the hard binary of plant and animal. Here, life skipped the green intermediary and went straight from mineral to flesh, as if the mountain, lonely at its apex, had decided to grow itself a second, softer skin. The single albino root that threads down from the ceiling is not competition; it is a curiosity, a tourist from the kingdom of light, permitted to hang in their midst because it is too frail, too irrelevant, to register as a threat or a resource. They ignore it the way they ignore everything that does not alter the heat or the wet.

What they are, finally, is a kind of silence. Not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of a sustained chord held so long it becomes the ear’s own baseline. The infrasonic hum of their breathing does not vary because there is nothing to vary it. The warm mist does not quicken because there is no wind to quicken it. They exist at the speed of geology, their hearts beating in rhythms measured by the slow creep of flowstone, their generations turning over like pages in a book written in a language the earth has forgotten it knows. 

They are the mountain’s last soft thought. Above them, the world of light and teeth and seasons grinds on, but here, eight thousand feet above the last treeline, the mountain has cupped its hands around a pocket of ancient heat and whispered a single, continuous note into the dark. They are that note, made flesh. They cling. They filter. They pulse their amber light in a darkness so complete it has become a kind of infinite embrace. And while the stars above sharpen themselves to ice against the thin air, they remain—warm, blind, and utterly sufficient—breathing with the stone, filling the chamber like a living mortar, patient beyond the reach of names.
