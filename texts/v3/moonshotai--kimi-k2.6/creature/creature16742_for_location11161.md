To name what lives here is to misname it almost immediately, because life in the Gastrium does not sit inside bodies the way surface creatures do. It billows, circulates, accumulates, bursts. It is weather that has learned to metabolize, and architecture that has learned to hunger. If you try to count inhabitants, you will find yourself counting the walls.

And yet.

There are the obvious ones, the *first* ones—the billions whose bodies constitute the roar. The copper-winged beetles, each no larger than a heartbeat, are not individuals any more than rain is individual. They are a mobile element, a golden-brown precipitation that spirals, condenses, and evaporates according to pressures no surface meteorologist could parse. To watch a column of them shear through an aerial-root curtain is to watch a river decide to flow sideways. Their elytra are not armor but instruments; every fanning wing beats against the saturated air and contributes its micro-tremor to the continuum of sound that holds the vault together. Without them, the acoustics would collapse. They are the breath of the place, inhaled and exhaled in typhoons.

The centipedes are something else—lıneages of punctuation racing along the cork plains, their hundreds of legs creating the endless skeletal rattle that serves as the valley’s baseline, its heartbeat. They are librarians of a sort, though they know nothing of books. They follow the chemical etchings deposited in the bark, maintaining the open channels, pruning excess fiber with mandibles like obsidian shears. Where they pass, the Gastrium stays legible. Where they do not, the walls swell closed within a season. They are the immune system’s tidiness, the worm in the apple that keeps the apple from choking on itself.

Higher up, suspended in the honeyed radiance, the great translucent moths are the Gastrium’s weather fronts of dissolution. Their wings are membranes of filtered light, meter-wide lenses that cool the lamp-fungi’s glow into moving shadows. They do not eat so much as *diffuse*—sipping aromatic hydrocarbons with probosces like glass threads, breaking complex resins into simpler ethers that the sessile colonies can re-metabolize. They are the recyclers of the upper atmosphere, the liver function made graceful.

But these are the atmosphere. They are the medium. They do not live *in* the Gastrium so much as they *are* the Gastrium’s blood and lymph, its circulating weather.

The ones who *live* here, who occupy the narrow, impossible margin between the growth and the grown-over, are the others. The ones who leave the faint scars of intention.

They are called nothing by themselves, for they communicate in subsonic percussion and resinous scent, and in that language a proper name is a four-minute drum solo describing the exact molecular weight of one’s shadow. The text calls them the primitive builders, but they are not primitive. They are only *quiet*, which in this place amounts to the same blasphemy. You will not see them until you have stopped trying to see. Only then does your skin detect the localized pocket of warmth, a body-temperature island in the fevered air, clinging to the underside of a bracket-fungi balcony like a cluster of ripe fruit.

They are the **warm-skinned**.

This is the first thing you must understand. Everything native to the Gastrium is metabolically bound to its substrate: the insects are ectothermic engines running on resin chemistry, the wood is ambient, the fungi are cool luminescence. But the builders carry their own furnaces. Their skin—naked, smooth, ranging from bruised rose to a deep, arterial amber—radiates heat. It must. In a world where water has gone to ground, hiding inside locked cellular matrices, the only way to remain liquid is to burn from within. They are soft, these creatures. Not soft like rot, but soft like the inside of a wrist. Their flesh gives slightly when the cork floor gives. They secrete a waxy, aromatic resin from dermal glands, not so different from the amber they prize, and this film holds their precious moisture in, a living varnish that makes them smell of cedar sap and warm musk. If you touch one—if one allows the contact—the texture is like heated wax just beginning to set, yielding but intact, slightly tacky, alive.

They are many-limbed. How many? *Several.* The number seems to vary, or perhaps your perception fails. There are limbs for gripping the vertical world: stout, segmented, double-jointed, ending in pads that can adhere to raw lignin or the polished chitin of a beetle’s husk. There are limbs for manipulation: slimmer, trembling with fine motor control, capable of flaking a edge onto a shard of fresh lignin-stone or threading the silk of spent moth cocoons into fabric. And there are limbs, usually a pair, that seem to do nothing but *listen*—held always slightly aloft, cupped like hands cupping an invisible egg, trembling with the constant interpretive work of mapping the valley’s infinite roar. They feel the groan of a root-column before it splits. They know a clearance-swarm is coming because the pitch of the cork floor changes three hours in advance. Their entire epidermis is tympanic. They are ears wearing a disguise of skin.

Their faces, if they are faces, are ridges of featureless warmth punctuated by wet, dark apertures—nostrils or tympanic slits or both, constantly sampling the air. They see, but seeing is probably the least of their senses, a minor note in a grand chord of vibration and scent.

They move in small troupes, rarely more than six adults and the soft, pale young—pouch-nymphs, clinging ventrally, suckling not milk but the warm, wax-rich secretion from their parent’s under-skin. A traveling family is a warm, murmuring lump pressed against a cambium corridor, resonating gently so as not to trigger the aggressive growth of the walls. They are the Gastrium’s only endotherms, its only pockets of deliberate silence. In a space that deafens, they are the ones who practice whispering.

What do they want? They want to remain hollow.

It is the only rebellion available. The Gastrium knows only accumulation—growth upon growth, layer choking layer until the ancient is fossilized inside the newborn. But the builders are creatures of *negative space*. Their entire culture is an art of the temporary hollow. They build the domes of silk and shed wing not to last, but to mark the fact that a space was once empty, that someone insisted on interiority. They arrange circles of amber beads around cold hearths of phosphorescent lichen not for heat, but for** focus**—a way of saying: *here, motion paused.* Amber is their sacred material because amber is the only thing in the valley that has finished becoming. Everything else is teaching itself how to exist; the amber knows. It is the corpse of resin, perfect, unchanging, a hard no shouted against the soft yes of endless growth. The beads are their archives, their grave-markers, their songs. Each one is a trapped scent-memory, a molecular photograph of a corridor before it was swallowed.

And the tower of beetle-elytra, four meters high, mortared with sap—this is their cathedral. Not a house. Not a fortress. A *statement.* It says: *We found these dead beautiful, and we stacked them, and the wood will take them back, but not yet.*

They do not fight the reclamation. They know better. They are not insurgents but mourners, or perhaps midwives—tending the brief, bright moment between the birth of a space and its burial inside new biomass. They migrate before the root-pillars fuse across their hearths, following the open channels, leaving the beads to be engulfed, to become the strange hard dreams of the cork stratum. They are professional leavers.

But here is where the image complicates, if you watch long enough.

You begin to notice that the builders are never bitten. The copper typhoons of beetles shear around them by exactly the width of a hand. The centipede scribes alter their paths by a hair to avoid a builder’s listening-limb. The clearance-swarms, those black hurricanes of ravenous stripping, peel the walls bare to the cambium, yet they leave untouched the half-digested towers, the silk domes, the amber circles. As if they recognize something. As if the builders are not trespassers but *organs.*

And perhaps they are.

Consider: in a body this vast, this embryonic, this drunk on its own emergence, what function would consciousness serve? The Gastrium does not need a brain. It is too busy being born. But any embryo dreams. Any womb has its electrical flickers. The builders may be exactly that—the distributed, nascent self-awareness of the vault, the first tentative *I* forming inside the woody flesh. Their warmth is the warmth of thought. Their circlets of amber are the place’s first memories, hardening in defiance of its eternal present. Their migration patterns trace the merest outlines of a mind learning to know itself: I am here, and here, and here, though the wood grows over.

They do not know this, of course. They build their fragile huts and squeeze through the cambium corridors and tend their lichen hearths with the earnestness of beings who believe themselves separate, solitary, clinging to existence at the margins of a monstrous world. They feel themselves the hollow ones, the temporary ones, the soon-to-be-swallowed.

But watch a builder press a hand against a sweating wall of fresh lignin-stone. Watch it pause. Watch it shiver, not with cold, but with recognition. The stone is warm, growing, unweathered. The builder is warm, growing, aging. For a moment, the boundary is not clear.

Then you understand that the Gastrium does not notice them because it does not need to. They are not the inhabitants of the valley. They are the valley’s first draft of self-portraiture, sketched in heat and vibration and the sharp, holy scent of beings who know, finally, absolutely, that they are already inside the body they sought to map.
