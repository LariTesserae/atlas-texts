The plateau has no citizens, only components. The closest thing to a monarch is the tree itself, if it can be called a tree: a vertical refinery of lignin and compressed ambition, kilometers of ribbed trunk rising to support a crown of voltaic storms and clattering light. To call it alive is too soft. It does not grow; it erects itself, cambium cracking like gun-chambers as new heartwood is press-ganged into service. Its core is not a heart but an anoxic forge, a cauldron where ore is reduced in perfect darkness, where the copper-green sap is not sap but coolant, pumped under crushing pressure through piping that would make a hydraulic engineer weep for its brutal, thoughtless elegance. It stands, and in standing it is a city, a continent, a sovereign without will.

Clinging to the fluting of its bark are the enamel beetles. Fist-sized, ceramic-shelled, they are neither pets nor parasites but maintenance made flesh. Their claws scrape ventricles of slag from the exhaust flutes; their blood is a thermal sponge, and they press their bellies to the hot plates to drink excess heat, shuddering with the expansion as their carapaces radiate in mirage-waves. They breed in the creased ball-joints where bark-plates grind in their sockets, laying eggs in grooves polished glass-smooth by centuries of their host’s own abrasion. When a woody muscle fills with venting gas and the reflector-leaf array articulates to track the hard white sun, the beetles ride the motion like debris on a deck, timing their click-clack to the tree’s pneumatic respiration.

Deeper still, in the wheezing throats of exhaust orifices, the tube-worms have taken up residence. Articulated chitin, jointed like the hose of a bellows, rooted in precipitated zinc and copper salts. They extend when the furnace exhales, filtering the metallic aerosol, and retract when the outrush chokes white-hot. They have no eyes. They taste only the chemistry of the vent, and their bodies grow in stratified layers of industrial nacre—living filtration systems that would die the moment the smelt beneath them cooled.

But the tree is not a fortress. It is an organ, and the waste between these growths is not empty; it is the connective tissue of a body vaster than geography. There, the ash-grey ungulates move. They are ruminants of a chemistry that has routed around water entirely, low and broad-shouldered, their feet wide as snowshoes or dense as pistons to keep them atop the shifting fallout. Their multiple stomachs are not wet sacks but fermentation chambers, reducing compressed cellulose nodules into volatile esters and hydrocarbon slurries that they do not digest so much as arm themselves with. Along their spines, the flash-pods ripen: waxy nodes of compressed ignition that explode with a percussive bark when the pack-hunters strike, spewing blue flame and metallic ash into the thin atmosphere. They travel in herds vast enough to be mistaken for a weather front, hooves tamping the centuries into new strata, grazing not on grass but on the shed, polished plates and cast-off slag of the trees themselves. They are the empire’s grazers, its pruners, its digestion made horizontal.
