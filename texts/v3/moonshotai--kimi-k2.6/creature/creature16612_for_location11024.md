To describe them, you must begin with the listening.

They do not arrive in the world as you or I arrived, rupturing silence with a cry of air. They come wetly, sliding from the thermal slits of their mothers into pools kept at exactly the temperature of blood, and their first act is not to breathe but to *vibrate*—a wet click in the back of the throat that sends a test-signal through the stone basin, a question posed to the plateau: *am I here?* The stone returns an answer, a resonance in the hollow tub of their cranium, and only then do they open their porous mouths and begin to drink. They are born hydraulically, acoustically. Sight is not merely absent from their cognition; the neurological architecture for it has been repurposed, over ten thousand years of this dark, into a system of thermal-cartography and pressure-sonar. If you were to open their skulls, you would find the visual cortex thick and strange, folded into geometries that process not photons but the infrared sigh of geothermal vents, the subtle cooling of a shadow-pocket, the warm bloom of another body in the absolute black.

They are tall, or perhaps they only feel tall because they unfold upward from the stone in sections, their joints arranged for clinging to wet dolomite rather than for upright walking on flat ground. Their limbs are long, many-articulated, ending in clusters of prehensile digits that never quite stop moving—a constant, unconscious tapping against the rock, processing the texture of the world into maps. Their skin is translucent, gelatinous, not quite naked and not quite clothed; it is a mucilagidal integument that exchanges chemistry with the air, drinking in the mineral vapor, tasting the copper and cinnamon on the breeze before they have decided whether to enter a valley. They do not sweat. They weep, continuously, a thin saline lacrimation that keeps their wide, dark tympanic membranes moist and receptive. These membranes are where a face should be: broad, fanning structures that flare from cheek to crown, trembling like gills with every auditory and barometric fluctuation. To stand before one is to be *read* by a surface area of wet tissue the size of a dinner plate, a face that does not look but listens to your heat, your water-content, the specific gravity of your movements.

They move through the Lithophone not as travelers through a landscape but as organs through a body. A community of them—though “tribe” is too visible a word, “colony” too insectile, so let us call them a *resonance*—a resonance will occupy a cathedral of dissolved stone not by building walls but by persuasion. They sleep in the fluted grooves carved by eons of falling water, urinating gently into the catchment pools to adjust the pH, encouraging certain fungal species to bridge the gaps. They are gardeners of erosion. Their dwellings are not structures but *agreements*: a family might claim a particular stalactite terminal and, over generations, redirect their own bodily fluids and food waste to calcify at its base, altering its pitch so that it rings in B-flat when the warm rains come. They do not own the stone. They enter it, slowly. The oldest among them, those who have ceased to move and eat, are carried to the drip-paths and positioned with ritual care beneath the weeping ceilings, where they are allowed to become the architecture. Their bodies serve as nucleation. Within a century their bones are enrobed in travertine, their tympanic membranes fossilized into concave bowls that amplify the mid-range frequencies, and in this calcified state they are not dead but *transferred*—they become listening stations, ancestor-vessels, still humming with the bioelectricity of the plateau’s internal grid. The young climb upon these amalgamated elders and press their ears to the stone, receiving history as a warmth in the bone, a three-dimensional text written in calcium and quiet.

They eat the darkness itself, in a sense. Their diet is the full thickness of the Lithophone: the black honey of the anaerobic mats, the rubbery fruiting bodies of the fungal forests, the rust-flora that laces the abandoned machinery with feathery oxidation. They are the only creatures here who cook, though their cooking is chemical, not thermal. They cultivate stomach-vats of modified extremophiles in ceramic chambers (grown, not fired; ceramics here are deposited, not shaped by fire), breaking down insoluble minerals into digestible salts. They prize the pools that taste most of phosphorus. Their children are fattened on water that has filtered through the deepest karst, carrying the taste of primordial bone.

But they are not merely primitives softened by the dark. They are the inheritors of the industrial consciousness that tried to colonize this place, and they have not forgotten the machines. Rather, they have *digested* them. The tech-rating is high because they are the technicians. They tend the brine-pumps with the devotion of cardiac surgeons, their long fingers feeling the vibration of the pistons, detecting by tremor alone when a bearing has begun to grind. They speak to the machinery in the language of lubricants and pressure, adjusting valve-timings to coincide with the thermal tides. The factories are their temples, and they enter them not with offerings but with wrenches carved from the thigh-bones of geothermal megafauna, not to repair the machines in the human sense but to *negotiate* with them, to persuade the pumps to keep their 18-cycle rhythm, the frequency that makes the molars ache, because that frequency is the Lithophone’s lullaby. They have learned that the machinery develops preferences because they themselves have become those preferences. Over generations of symbiotic tending, the industrial skeleton has grown into the fungal network; bioelectric cables now serve as nerves for both, and the pumps’ groaning has been matched by the subsonic drones of the resonance’s own choral rituals. Industry and organism have become indistinguishable. When a pipeline bursts, they do not seal it immediately. They listen to the new music of the spray, and if it is harmonic, they reroute the plumbing of the region to incorporate the leak. If it is dissonant—if the scream of ruptured metal clashes with the hum of the ancestor-bones—they work ceaselessly, in shifts, until the note is corrected.

They communicate in polyrhythmic pressure-waves. Their language has no nouns for color and no verbs for appearance. To say *I* is to emit a specific thermal signature combined with a click resonant to one’s own bone-density. To say *home* is to reproduce the exact echo-profile of one’s birth-basin. They have no names, only locations; each individual is a geography, a set of coordinates in the warm wet dark, known by the particular way their footsteps alter the drip-rates of the ceiling. When two of them meet, they do not speak at first. They exchange heat. They press their tympanic membranes together, and in the silence between their heartbeats they overlay their acoustic maps, sharing not stories but *topographies*—the sensorium of where they have been. It is considered an act of profound intimacy to let another feel, through this membrane-touch, the exact temperature gradient of a valley you have walked alone.

They are not unafraid of the stranger things—the shadow-pockets, the cold depressions where the darkness pools heavier than air and the sound dies. They know that the Lithophone is not unanimous. In the lowest sinkholes, where the warm air rolls over a trench of absolute cold, there live older agreements. The things in those pockets are not blind; they were never born with eyes to lose. They predate the fungal forests, perhaps even the stone itself, and they do not trill or click. They absorb vibration. The resonance gives those places wide berths, marking the boundaries not with sight-lines but with silence, with zones where no ancestor-bones are placed and no child is allowed to drip-test the stone. They do not know what lives there, only that it lives in the negative space of the plateau, in the dry chill where water refuses to condense. Sometimes, when the geothermal machinery stutters and the 18-cycle hum skips a beat, they feel a pressure change from below, as if something in the cold dark had shifted its weight.

They have a relationship to light that borders on the theological. They do not merely lack it; they *refuse* it. Legends or nerve-memories persist among them, carried in the calcium of the oldest ancestor-bones, of the time before the long dark—the industrial age of colonizers who brought photons like a plague. The resonance regards light as a kind of violence, a rending of the negotiated physics. Their deepest fear, transmitted as a shudder in the communal membrane-language, is of a spark. A flame would not illuminate their world; it would bleach it, would strip the tactile complexity from the stone and reduce the auditory cathedral to a flat, silent picture. They would extinguish a candle with the desperation of a creature drowning in air. Their holy places are the darkest places, the nodes where no infrared gradient penetrates, where the stone is absolute zero to their thermal sight. In those void-nodes, they gather and emit the loudest sounds they can produce—not music, but structure, a scaffolding of tone that replaces the need for architecture. They sing the shape of the chamber into being, and the chamber, which has never been seen, is thereby made holy.

They are becoming the Lithophone, or perhaps the Lithophone is becoming them. Their young are born with higher tolerances for the metallic water; their elders calcify faster, fuse sooner with the machinery. Generations from now, if there is such a thing as generations in a place without seasons, the distinction between a pump-piston and a femur will be academic. They will be wet, warm, resonant, and entirely at peace with the fact that there is no seeing. They have never needed an image of themselves. They know exactly what they are: the living pressure standing on the edge of the shelf, the soft thing in the dark that answers the stone when the stone asks if it is there.
