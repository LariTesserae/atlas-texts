At first you think the city is empty. Then, standing long enough in the plaza where the red water and the blue water meet, you notice that some of the formations have preferences about where the drips fall.

They are there, nine of them, or eleven—figures not carved but accumulated, standing to their waists in the shallow basins, wedged into the channels, draped against the walls like the moss itself. They are what became of the caretakers, or perhaps what the caretakers became *for*. Each started as flesh, bone, intention, but the mountain does not distinguish between organism and architecture. Over decades of exposure, their bodies learned the same lesson as the ferns and the fallen leaves: that to persist here is to be absorbed, laminated, made useful. They did not resist. They stepped into the mineral-rich channels and stood still, letting the water do its slow, patient work.

Now they are sheathed. Their skin is not skin but a living crust—dendritic films of oxidized copper, aragonite blooming in milky petals across their chests, faces masked in translucent silica that has preserved the exact expression of the hour they chose to stop. Beneath the mineral casing they remain biological: hearts beat, perhaps, in rhythms tuned to the groaning strata; lungs draw the saturated air in long, tidal breaths that take minutes to complete. The moss adores them. It mats their shoulders into hummocks, sends rootlets into the joins of their elbows, so that when a sleeve of viridian fronds shifts in the condensation-drip, it is impossible to say whether they gestured, or whether the garden simply adjusted itself on their frames.

They do not speak. The roar of a thousand kettles would render any voice futile, and in any case, their mouths are rimed with sinter, throats half-filled with the sediment of centuries. Instead, they communicate by accretion. When one desires another to know something—a shift in a seep, a fresh fracture singing in the basalt—they simply stand in the path of the water and let the minerals record their posture. The others read the new deposits: a thicker band of ochre here means *attention*, a bloom of white salt there means *wait*. Their history is stratified; peel a century back from the flank of the oldest and you would find, like tree rings, the record of thought rendered in chemical bands. They have become a parliament of geodes, half-sentient, half-sedimentary.

The oldest is nearly architecture now. He wanted to oversee the confluence of the seeps, and so he stood on the plaza’s edge and let the calcium and silica rise. Today he is a bell tower, or a pillar; his feet have fused to the causeway, his arms raised in a permanent gesture of directing flow. When the earth settles and the stone cracks, his body rings. The slate-colored bird nests in the hollow of his collarbone, and condensation beads on his raised knuckles to feed the insects that needle the air. He is not dead. The city does not permit that kind of finality. He is simply mid-gestation, as the mountain is mid-gestation, becoming infrastructure while still somehow inhabiting it.

The youngest still moves. Perhaps she is only three centuries old, her casing no thicker than eggshell, and she retains the habit of shifting her weight from one leg to another when the cold deepens—a pointless gesture, since the cold is the same inside her as out. She tends the seeps not with tools but with presence, adjusting her stance to cast a shadow where algae must not grow, letting a shoulder build up to divert a channel from a weaker wall. She eats by filtering the mineral water through the moss that lines her esophagus; she rests by standing deeper, letting the pools climb to her chin, entering a state in which her mineral crust thickens while her dreams, if that is what they are, seep outward into the stone in fans of pale turquoise.

They are not waiting for anything in the human sense. They have outlived the need for arrival. What lives here is a patience so absolute it has achieved consciousness—a distributed, dripping sentience that wears the shapes of its own first scaffolding. They are the city’s metabolism, its living gutters and flying buttresses and foundation stones. When the mist lifts and the basin gleams, you can see them: not ruins, not inhabitants, but the precise point at which the mountain stops and the intention to build begins, still breathing, still growing, still deciding, drip by drip, what it will become.
