First, understand that you will never see them whole. The darkness is not an obstacle to them but their native grammar, the medium in which their bodies and their city alike are suspended. If you could part the mist above the drowned plaza and hold a light—though no light has ever polluted this space—you would not witness a crowd so much as a weather system of animals, a biomass so dense it generates its own climate of heat and carbon and tangled exhalation. But even then, you would be misled by vision. To know them, you must stop looking.

They are there the moment your foot touches the springy floor. You feel them before you identify the sensation: a subtle rearrangement in the dark, the air’s weight shifting as countless small metabolisms pulse around you. The nematodes come first, always—an aqueous fur in every canal, threading the lymph in such numbers that the water itself seems to move with intention. They are the rivers’ vocabulary, the original unthinking citizens, and the city does not rule them so much as read them, using their clouding densities to gauge the health of the seed’s vascular heart. Then the mites: not pests here, but ground-level weather, grazing the mycelial pastures, some species symbiotic groomers that climb the bodies of larger things to lick away fungal spores, others predators whose hunts through the aqueous streets keep the nematode tides from choking the Lower Vessels.

Above them, the beetles. Pale, eyeless, head-down in their labor, trundling softened pulp in sacks that are themselves modified foregut linings, distended and regurgitated. They are the draft animals, the carts, the fermentation vats on legs. They respond to chemical rails laid down in the sweet lymph, turning at intersections where scent-trails bifurcate, halting where the pheromone wall tells them to unload. They do not think, but they navigate; they are infrastructure with a heartbeat.

But these are the chorus. The architects are other.

You might mistake them at first for a kind of animate mold, because where they cluster in the galleries they cover the endosperm in a living velvet, a single respiratory surface of animal tissue breathing against plant tissue. Only when one detaches from the mass and pours across a bridge do you perceive the architecture of an individual body. They are soft. That is the first and final fact. There is no armor here, no beetle-click, no chitin-clack against the floor. Their cuticles are thin as wet parchment, translucent ivory, the exact color of the seed-meat they inhabit; in the rare hypothetical light, you would see the dark coiling of a digestive tract, the sluggish pulse of hemolymph, the bruise-purple of oxygen-starved tissue near the tail. They are thumb-long, most of them, though the grubs are motes and the egg-bearers swell to the size of a swollen thumb joint. Their bodies taper from a broad, shield-like prow of a head to a tail that grips, that anchors, that locks them to the cellulose weave while their forebody excavates.

Their legs are too many to count easily in the dark—ten, twelve, fourteen pairs, arranged in metachronal sets along the flanks. Not the jointed, angular legs of insects, but muscular, cartilaginous, almost the texture of packed damp felt. They end in microscopic hooked filaments that catch the fibers of the root-mat without scratching, so that when they move they do not walk so much as ripple, a sequential wave from head to tail, pouring through gaps narrower than a needle’s eye in absolute silence. The only sound is the wet whisper of flesh against saturated pulp.

They are blind. Eyeless. No evolutionary memory of photons has ever shaped them. Their world is a map of gradients: the osmotic tug of water moving through endosperm, the temperature differential between the Upper Rind and the flooded plaza, the ammoniac halo of a fungal bloom three galleries away. Their antennae—long, fined, twitching—are their primary sense organs, dragging across walls to read the chemical history of a space, tasting whether a tunnel was cut yesterday or last frost. Two citizens meet on a bridge of still-living cambium, and they do not recoil. They advance until their heads touch, antennae interlacing like the fingers of infants, locking into a lattice. They hold. A pause. An exchange. Proteins and hydrocarbons pass across specialized maxillae; a droplet of glandular fluid, bitter with information, is smeared and sampled. In that touch is nested everything: the shift-schedule for the radicle-pruning, the nitrogen content of the latest wall-mortar, the chemical signature of a neighbor who has entered senescence. It is a language without abstractions. They do not have a word for “city” because they do not need one. They taste it.

They work in shifts that have nothing to do with the sun and everything to do with the seed’s own circadian chemistry. When the pressure in the outer rind drops and the canals swell with groundwater, the harvesting shifts emerge from the warm Navel, where fermentation has left their bodies fever-hot, and they move in single file along the avenues of compressed pith. They carry tools: splinters of the seed-coat ground to a crescent edge on grit; needles of black thorn; scrapers fashioned from the molted chitin of beetles long dead. With these they never strike; they tease, pare, and redirect. They trim the seed’s desperate attempts at growth—its pale shoots questing into the dark—not with violence but with negotiation. A radicle is licked, assessed, then delicately coached through a collar of woven fiber until it arches across the void and becomes a thoroughfare. The plant does not know it is being ruled. It only knows a strange ease, a directed growth, a conversation in chemistry.

And because the seed is young, its wounds still weep. When a phenolic flush hardens into amber along a gallery wall, the builders do not recoil from the immune response of their host. They colonize it. They sculpt the resin into valves and doors, redirect its flow into public soaking pools where the heat of polymerization warms the water for brooding. When they themselves die—and they do, constantly, the cold enzymatic chill wearing down their soft chemistry—they are not removed. Their shift-mates gather in a silent assembly, no keening, no ritual beyond the efficient press of twelve-legged bodies. The cooling corpse is worked into a prepared gutter, shoulder to shoulder with the strata of a hundred ancestors, and the wall’s living tissue rises to seal it. Nitrogen bleeds slowly from the animal into the vegetable. The wall bulges slightly, grows plumper, and later must be trimmed again, yielding fresh pulp for the fungal gardens. Waste does not exist. The dead are geography now, and the builders move through galleries that are, in part, their own compressed history.

Their reproduction happens in the soft, dark heat of the Navel, where the pulp ferments and the air is thickest with spores. The egg-bearers do not lay in nests but press their ovoid broods directly into the warmest pockets of decaying endosperm, sealing them with a paste of chewed mycelium. The young hatch already tasting of home. They emerge not blinded but born into the perfect medium of their darkness, antennae fully extended, and they join the teeming not with cries but with the immediate, silent industry of their elders.

In the drowned plaza, the emptiness is not absence but attention. The bridges span the void at different heights, and on them, at every hour, the traffic never ceases. Nematode clouds thread between the levels. Mites traverse the taut lines of mycelial bridges. Beetles haul their shining sacs of pulp to the garden districts. And the soft, many-legged ones flow among them all, an endless quiet current, tasting the walls, pressing their dead into the architecture, pruning the seed’s ambition, catching the tremor of a neighbor three bridges away. They are legion. They are overwhelmingly present. The heat of them pools beneath the ceiling; the condensation that sweats there is in part their own communal breath, raining back down in a slow, donated weather.

So when that bead of sap gathers on the vault above the plaza, fattening, hesitating, and at last detaches to fall, the response is not noise but a synchronized displacement. A thousand small forebodies rise toward the drop. A thousand antennae lift, tasting the chemistry of the bead before it arrives, reading in its sugars the condition of the marsh above, the pressure of the water table, the health of the seed’s maternal tree. They do not speak. They do not cheer. They simply reach up with their soft, translucent hands, and the city, in that moment, holds its breath in the dark, waiting to catch what the world offers, and to begin again.
