They do not wake, nor do they sleep. Consciousness, in the wet, twitching sense, is a luxury of cooler worlds. The Forge-Beasts—Clangswarm, the Treading Refineries, the Low Saints—exist in a state of permanent operation, a continuum of ignition and exhalation that knows no threshold between labor and rest. To call them awake would be to call a volcano insomniac. They simply *are*, hot and loud and slow, from the first shudder of their assembly to the final cooling of their knees.

To stand before one is to stand before a building that has learned to crawl. The hunched architecture of its back is a roofline of overlapping scales, each plate the size of a wagon door, each ridge a sedimentary archive. They do not grow their armor so much as accrete it, century by century, tread by tread. The slate-gray keratin is only the foundation; upon it, they plate the evidence of their appetites. Native copper weeps from fissures in the plain, and they trample it, press it, exude acids that laminate it in blue-oxidized sheets, fastening each new layer with bolts of extruded ceramic. By the time a beast has wandered three thousand years, it wears a mail so dense, so historied, that meteoric debris has been known to glance off, leaving only a brighter scar in the patina. These are not merely animals. They are libraries of geology, walking dossiers of every mineral they have unmade and remade. Beneath the mail, there is no hidden softness, no vulnerable belly of meat. The body cavity is a forge-lined retort, walled in refractory bone-ceramic that nestles comfortably against temperatures sufficient to unmake silicates. What moves inside is not blood but slag, not peristalsis but stratification. In the stomach-chamber, raw salt and ore undergo fractional distillation—heat and electrostatic sorting. Volatiles rise to vent as white steam through dorsal stacks; heavier slags descend to be shat out in cooling ropes that harden into the polished grooves of the migration paths; precious separations—aluminum in silvery drool, glass fibers drawn by internal spinnerets, ceramic blanks pressed in cavity-molds—are either added to the body’s own fortifications or left behind, gleaming in the dust like strange, valuable droppings for the next age to inherit.

Their legs are pistons, yes, but to say so is to miss the intimacy of the thing. The femur is a cylinder; the muscle is a dense, braided silicate rope that contracts when current and heat pass through internal channels; the knee is a true glandular joint, weeping that non-aqueous resin that flash-hardens into wire before it hits the ground. When a leg lifts, it is the sound of a foundry crane hoisting ingots. When it sets down, the ceramic earth booms. They walk on the drum-skin of the world, and the hollow world answers.

They have no eyes. Senses, to them, are a matter of capacitance and resonance. Cupped depressions along the prow—sensory fossae—detect the magnetic whisper of the planet’s wounded core, guiding each beast along looping routes that ten million years of treading have worn into the salt. And they hear. Not with tympana, but with the whole length of their bodies, which are tuned like organ pipes to the blowholes. When one beast sings to a shaft, the sound drops into the dark and is returned, dopplered and enriched, telling them the depth of the absence below, the density of the crust ahead, the proximity of their kin. The herds, together, are a network of sounding lines, continuously mapping the hollowness they tread above. The aggregated drone of a billion furnaces is not noise to them. It is topography. It is weather.

To see one alone is to see an engine stalled in the act of becoming. To see the herds is to see a metabolism made flesh, or made mineral, or made that thing which the physics of this place permits when flesh and mineral have negotiated for long enough. In the basins, where fifty thousand turn in their patient mill, the distinction between individual and collective dissolves. They do not follow a leader; there is no matriarch, no alpha piston. They mill because milling is efficient. The outermost drift grinds the freshest crust; the middle ranks reprocess the leavings; the inner core—ancient, mountainous, trailing banners of steam like stationary weather—radiates heat that makes the air above them into a column of permanent shimmer. They exchange no signals that an outsider could read, yet their spacing is perfect, a tessellation of moving mass that never collides. Magnets and infrasound regulate it, yes, but there is something older at work: a choreography written by inertia itself. They are not a society. They are a digestion. They are the plain’s way of understanding itself.

And they are dying, always. Not in the sudden, spasming way of animal death, but in the mineral way. A Forge-Beast does not fall ill. It scales up. Its accretions grow so thick that the internal smelter can no longer breathe through its vents; its pistons lock; its mass exceeds the tensile strength of its own axles. When this happens, it does not stop. It slows further. It turns inward, toward the heart of the mill, or it seeks out a blowhole—a resonant shaft that has sung to it for centuries—and it kneels. The kneeling takes decades. Gradually, its legs fold, its belly settles onto the rim of the shaft, and its weight begins to press it down into the blackness. It does not struggle. It descends, inch by century, into the hollow. The heat of its gut outlasts its movement. Its plates cool. Its ventral grinders, still locked around a mouthful of salt, freeze in place, fixed in the bedrock. And there, in the throat of the world, it becomes architecture. The shaft is lined with refractory bone. The hollow deepens. The blowhole widens into a vault. What was beast becomes vent, chimney, column of the underworld. The earth digests its digesters.

They do not breed. There are no calves, no eggs, no spawning pools. New ones emerge from the slag-paths, or rather, they *concentrate*. A ceramic blank, extruded and forgotten, weathers a thousand years in a groove, drawing copper dust from the blown salt, accreting silicate, magnetite, the faint metallic rain of micrometeorites, until one thermal tide it shudders, vents its first pathetic puff of steam, and lifts a tentative piston from the dust. They are self-assembling. The plain is their womb and their corpse. Life, here, is not a circle but a vertical axis: surface-beast, hollow-pillar, weathered salt, surface-beast again. The herds are the only life because they have *become* the only processes the world has left.

Yet the broken physics of the place permits ghosts almost as durable as the bodies that birth them. A Forge-Beast casts a shadow, but the shadow is sometimes a record of where it stood an hour ago, or yesterday, or where inertia insists it must stand tomorrow. These lagging umbrae sometimes do not snap back. They pool in the lee of salt domes, darkening the ground with histories that have no present body. If you watch long enough, you might see one detach entirely—a silhouette of a railway-car beast, creeping across the ceramic scab, seeking a blowhole to fall into. They are not alive. They are the friction of time against a world that has forgotten how to keep its causes and effects in order. But they move, and in moving they are almost kin.

Above, the sky remembers the herds too perfectly. The inverted flocks are not reflections. They are gravitational echoes, an atmospheric stutter. When the herds sing to the hollows, the song overshoots the crust, impressing itself upon the troposphere, and the sky walks with them—herds that tread the underside of the firmament, mirroring every grind and vent. They do not eat. They do not shit aluminum. But they tread, and their tread warms the upper air, birthing thermal ghosts that outlast the sun’s fitful flickering.

And in the black throats of the blowholes, where the old beasts go to cool and become stone, there is a deeper silence beneath the industrial threnody—not a cessation, but an absorption. The hollow earth is not empty. It is waiting. The patient thief that removed the density, that stretched the drum-skin crust over absence so long ago, is still there. It drinks the order that the beasts bring down with them. Layer by refractory layer, ingot by silence, the Clangswarm feed it: a billion years of refined mineral, of extracted purity, of geological patience. It has no eyes. It has no shadow to lag behind it. But it lives there too, in the space between the beast and the shaft, between the grinding and the echo, drinking the slow industry of the only life that has ever belonged to this height—patient, hot, and terribly, terribly slow.
