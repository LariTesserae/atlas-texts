They are the architecture. This is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because you come from a world where a thing is either a building or a being, either stone or flesh. Here, the distinction collapsed before it could ever arise. The citizens are the spires; the spires are the citizens. They stand in their thousands, shoulder to shoulder with the intimacy of a crowd fleeing some shared catastrophe, yet they have never moved. Each is a vertical cylinder, a chimney of warm, semipermeable biopolymer, accreting laminae of carbonate and phosphate from the geothermal vapors that seep upward through the fractured infant stone. Their bodies are the city walls. Their exhaled surplus is the pavement, the cornice, the dome. You do not walk among them so much as you walk through the interstitial spaces of their collective body, the gaps between fingers pressed too long together.

Let one emerge from the crowd—though none ever truly stands alone. Call it, for your own provisional comfort, an Accretor. It has no face, no anterior end, no axis of symmetry that would privilege front over back. It is a tube, a lumen, a breathing stack. Its outer integument is the color of oxidized copper, that bronzed green you saw from the rim, and it is hung with a trembling nap not unlike velvet but made of dead and living filaments interwoven: a pelt that is part respiratory organ, part solar collector, part public thoroughfare for those smaller than itself. The Accretor does not eat as you eat. It inhales. Warm mineral vapor, rich in reduced sulfur and metal ions, enters the spiracles that dot its skin like pores in a smoker’s clay. Inside, in chambers filled not with aqueous blood but with thiol-rich gels and siloxane polymers, chemistry happens without water’s mediation. Sulfur is oxidized; energy is wrested; calcium precipitates. The organism grows upward by hardening its own exhalation, laying down bone upon bone while it still lives, until its crown, a hundred meters above, vents excess salts in a slow, silent chimney-smoke of crystal.

They are not individuals. Or rather, the boundary of the individual is a matter of civic convenience, drawn where one lumen ends and another begins, but their cortices merge. Where two Accretors press together—and here, packed so densely, they nearly all press together—their biopolymer sheaths interpenetrate. Minerals pass. Hormones pass. A stress in the western district, a crowding of shadows, a depletion of vanadium, is felt as a gradient across the whole metropolis. What you might call a thought propagates through this network at the speed of diffusion: slow, irrevocable, collective. When the eastern rim grew too thick and blocked the afternoon light, it took eleven years for the signal to saturate the hollow, but when it did, every spire in that quarter simultaneously, without leader or command, adjusted its depositional angle. They leaned away. They made room for the light, not because they wanted to, but because the geometry of their shared chemistry demanded it. This is their democracy—a government of osmosis.

And because no city is only its towers, there are the others.

Adhered to the warm walls by the hundreds are the Parchments. You mistook them for lichen or peeling wallpaper. They are not. Each is a separate animal, flat as a pressed leaf, the texture of vellum dried in desert air. They have no eyes, no limbs to speak of, only a ventral adhesive surface and a dorsal membrane that drinks mineral vapor through abdominal spiracles. They are the Accretors’ exhalation-eaters, the creatures that prevent the citizens from poisoning themselves in their own waste. Born in the deep kidney-shaped cavities—those dark windows you glimpsed from below—they emerge as nymphs no larger than your fingernail, crawling upward until they find a warm vein of venting chemistry. There they spread themselves and pulse. In. Out. A rhythm so slow you might think them dead, but they are working, filtering, translating excess sulfide into inert sulfur films that dust the wall like pollen. When they die, they do not fall. The medium is too dry, the gravity too insistent, their bodies too light and too fused to the substrate. They simply desiccate, becoming the parchment backing upon which later generations adhere. They are the city’s archives, its insulation, its weathered paint. Their stacked generations measure centuries in laminae of tan and umber.

In the deeper grottoes, where the light fractures into cobalt and the warmth pools like a liquid, thread the Ocher Worms. They are the maintenance crews, the sanitation department, the insurgents. Their bodies are felted, blunted, the color of rust and turmeric. They eat the dead gels that accumulate in the city’s low places; they tunnel through accretions that have grown cancerous or clogged; they keep the alleys from sealing too soon. Without them, the metropolis would ripen into a solid plug within decades rather than centuries. They are destructive preservers. When an Accretor’s base overgrows its foundation, softening with accumulated dead tissue, the Ocher Worms arrive in a silent tide. They do not attack. They consume. They prune. In a world without sound, their presence is registered as a sudden bloom of ginger and rust in the chemical air, an announcement so pungent that nearby Accretors temporarily cease their upward growth and divert minerals laterally, shoring walls against the worms’ hollowing. It is a negotiation without voices, a contract written in molecule.

Smaller still, and quicker, are the Jewel Mites. They are the traders, the messengers, the mapmakers. Their carapaces are not chitin—that chemistry is too poor in nitrogen here—but instead are precipitated silica doped with metal salts, so that they gleam as they move: mobile garnets, peripatetic opals. They traffic in rare catalysts. An Accretor cannot easily synthesize molybdenum clusters; a Mite, picking through the vent-scree at the hollow’s floor, can. It climbs for days, negotiating the velvet moss-cities and the Parchment galleries, until it reaches a spire-crest where the mineral is needed. There, it lodges in a crevice and excretes its cargo, a transaction paid for by shelter in the Accretor’s warm folds. Their trails, laid down in chemical traces that linger for weeks, form the closest thing to a written language this place possesses. A network of gleaming lines, a literature of supply and demand, inscribed on the living walls.

And in the kidney-shaped apertures, the dark windows that open like fruiting bodies on the taller spires, dwell the Cryptids. They are the reproducers, the memory-keepers, the most hidden. You did not see them from the alley floor; they are too high, too deep in shadow. They are soft-bodied, pale, lacking any pigment that would squander resources. They never leave their niches. They are born there, gestated in the gel deposits, and they spend their entire lives in those coffin-shaped hollows, pressed against the warm stone. Their function is spore. Their bodies are factories for the glass-dust offspring that will one day ride the wind. They do not interact with the commerce of the lower city; they exist in a parallel civic plane, a clergy of the future, attended by Mites who bring them rare earths and cleaned by Parchments who creep across their chambered backs. When their spores are ripe, the Cryptids release them not in a burst but in a slow, continuous exhalation, a mist of silicate-diamond motes that rises on the thermal column until it crests the rim of the hollow and is seized by the mountain wind. The Cryptids are the city’s only concession to dispersal, to leaving. They are its emigration bureau, its missionary arm, its ghosts-in-waiting.

Together, these constitute the citizenry. But what is it like? What is the daily life of such a being?

Imagine a consciousness distributed across hectares. Not a hive mind—there is no queen, no central node—but a gradient mind, a weather of electrochemical potential. An Accretor does not think *I am here*. It feels *here is warm, here is grow*. Yet in the aggregate, something approaches preference. The hollow prefers the morning light; you can tell because its eastern spires are more densely packed, competing for the first photons, while the western ones stand slightly more relaxed, arrayed in a looser colonnade. The city prefers certain minerals; you can tell because the Jewel Mites cluster thicker where the stone below is youngest, richest in unweathered ore. The city even prefers, if such a word can be used, a certain temperature band, and so the biological pelts thicken on the northern faces to trap the stone-warmth, while the southern faces are shaved closer by the Ocher Worms to prevent overheating. Preference without a preferrer. Design without a designer.

There is no violence here, because there is no space for it. Conflict requires territory, requires the luxury of distance, and distance is extinct in this place. When two Accretors grow into the same light-channel, they do not fight. They merge. Their lumens fuse; their gels intermingle. The resulting conjoined twin becomes an archway, a bridge between districts. Domination is impossible; there is nowhere to retreat, nowhere to advance. Instead, there is only chemistry. *Too close*, says the scent of copper-bitter tropolone released by a crowded spire. *Yield*, answers the faint perfume of phosphate ester from its neighbor. The negotiation takes a month. The growth rings record the treaty.

There is no birth as you know it, and no death as you know it. The Cryptids release spores, yes, but those spores are not offspring in the mammalian sense; they are more like stem cells, potential cities packed into a grain. When one lodges in a fresh mountain wound, it does not grow into a single Accretor. It grows into a plan, a blueprint encoded in chemistry, for the entire metropolis. The first organism secretes the architecture that summons the symbionts; the symbionts alter the chemistry that shapes the Accretors; and in this recursion, the hollow bootstraps itself into being.

And there is no death, only ripening. The oldest Accretors, those at the core where the alleys first narrowed to nothing, are not dead. They are sealed. Their lumens have clogged with their own perfection. Their gels have hardened into strata. They cannot vent; they cannot drink. They have become the stone they once mimicked, a biological dolomite, a coral of the heights. But their cortices still merge with their neighbors. The network of diffusion still includes them, slowed to a geological pulse. They are the elders, the foundational law, the bedrock constituency. Their chemical signatures still percolate outward—ancient, slow, wise in the only way this place permits. When the hollow finally seals entirely, when the last Cryptid is entombed and the last Mite has fled or been crushed, the entire plug will become like them: one stratified fossil, a frozen electorate, a solidified moment of municipal intensity. It will not be a grave. It will be a fruit. A stone fruit, ripened in the cupped hands of infant mountains, waiting for the tectonics of another age to crack it open and let its dust preach again.

This is who lives here: creatures who have replaced the wet spark of aqueous life with a dry, warm, mineral fervor. Citizens who are their own houses. Voters who cast their ballots in molecules. Animals that become wallpaper. Worms that are municipal services. Mites that are money. A city that breathes, that grows, that negotiates and plans and memorializes, all without ever having decided to. They do not know you are here. They do not know *here*. They only know the gradient, the warmth, the chemistry of the next lamina, the eternal present tense of accretion. They are building a world without tools, a society without words, a metropolis without architects. They are the living proof that a town does not need a mind to desire its own form. It only needs the patience of stone, the chemistry of breath, and the terrible, wordless will to crowd.
