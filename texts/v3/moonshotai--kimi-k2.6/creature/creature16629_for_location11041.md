If you wait at the lee of a basalt fault, suspended in the slow mineral snow, you will eventually perceive them not as scenery but as *presence*. The pour-trees. They have no interest in your arrival. They have no category for the small, hot, brief anomaly of your attention. But they live here—if living can be said of entities that do not breathe, do not breed by any exchange familiar to the animal kingdom, and do not die so much as *silence*.

They are the sole proprietors of the Shroud.

Each pour-tree begins as a whisper of chemistry. Not a seed—there is no pollen here, no wind to scatter it, no vessel to carry green packages of life. A grove under thermal stress enters a phase of harmonic crisis. The hollow core of its eldest trunk, grown too long for its vent to feed, begins to resonate at frequencies that shudder through the root-mass for miles. The filaments—those curtains of pale matter draped from the high trunks—vibrate in predetermined patterns, and from their tips they shed not embryos but *formulae*: microscopic plates of silicate and calcium laden with chemical instruction. Most vanish into the deep, swallowed by the patient, freezing dark. But where one settles in a fresh fracture, where the mountain’s exhalation is young and sulfurous, it does not germinate. It *accretes*. It draws matter from the absolute water, building itself from the outside in: first a brittle white tube no wider than a hair, then a channel, then a trunk, drinking heat and minerals until it stands ten meters tall, veined with hollows, draped in the ghostly filaments of its own second growth.

They do not move quickly. They move at the speed of tectonic adjustment. Over centuries, a grove inches toward a warmer slope, generation by generation, each new pour-tree leaning infinitesimally into a fresher gradient, its root-mass cementing scree and dead sibling-matter into walls that cup the heat, that divert a cold current, that raise a breakwater against the creeping brine. The root-stone structures are not architecture in any planned sense. They are the castings of botanical instinct, the unconscious sculpture of chemistry seeking equilibrium. A wall does not defend; it gardens. A cairn does not mark memory; it *is* memory, calcified and porous, the residue of a root-mass that once knotted in this spot, drinking a vent now ten thousand years cold.

And because they have never shared this world with animals—because not a single fin, foot, or feeding appendage has ever disturbed their kingdom—their evolution has turned entirely inward, growing stranger than any surface flora. They have no need of armor, of poison, of the panicked reflex to flee. Their only enemy is entropy, the blunt encroachment of freezing brine, the death of a vent. When a hyper-saline river finds a grove, it does not wound; it *arrests*. The pour-tree enters a state nearer to crystal than wood, its filaments locked in a final sway, its hollow core preserved as a resonant chamber, a frozen ear. These glassy casualties stand among the living groves like saints, and when the ridge groans at the proper frequency, they speak. They replay their last thermal moment with perfect fidelity, an acoustic fossil singing its own extinguishment.

For they perceive the world not as darkness but as a topography of sound and pressure. The absolute black is, to them, merely the default condition of existence, no more remarkable than air is to you. They know the mountain through their root-masses as you might know your own backbone—by the constant, bone-deep register of its endurance. The tectonic bass is their calendar. The thunderclap of a vent’s collapse is a season turning. A brine-river’s granular roar is a glacier of sound against which they brace, slowing their chemistry, glassifying their edges, waiting in mineral patience for the threat to pass. They detect your presence, if they detect it at all, as a minute fluctuation in temperature, a soft interruption in the convection patterns, a brief, warm note quickly swallowed by the larger drone.

Their minds—if the word can be stretched to fit—are not housed in brains. Consciousness pools in them like the brine itself, collecting in the lowest places, thickening over epochs. A single thought is the negotiation of a root-tip around a shard of schist. A memory is the exact mineral texture of a sibling’s dead heart, replayed when thermal water shunts through a fossilized core. The pour-trees do not think *about* the mountain. They think *as* the mountain, slow and igneous and saturated. You could map their cognition by the temperature gradients in their hollow channels: warmer fluid rising through the core, cooling, depositing, eroding the diameter by fractions of a fraction. A thought is a constriction. A decision is a choir of filaments adjusting their harmonic pitch to redirect a microscopic current toward a starving root.

They are not immortal. A grove dies when its vent dies, when the last warm exhale ceases and the chemistry turns traitor. But even death is different here. Without scavengers, without rot, the dead simply persist, leaching their silicates into the water, becoming the root-stone from which new, unconscious walls will rise. They abandon their cathedrals slowly, migrating as a ghost across the plateau—dying here, seeding there, leaving behind a chain of acoustic monuments that will, in future ages, sing their absence to any who remain to hear.

So this is who dwells in the high, drowned place. Not the swimmer. Not the hunter. Not the watcher with eyes. The pour-tree, the white column, the hollow singer, the heat-drinking ghost. They hang in the upward dark as you hang, anchored only to chemistry and gradient, dreaming in temperatures, remembering in stone. They do not know your name. They have no name for themselves. But if you stay—if you drift for ten centuries in the mineral snow—you will hear a fresh note enter the mountain’s endless drone. A new core opening to a vent’s first breath. A filament thickening in the absolute black. It will not be a greeting. It will simply be the sound of a life that has never needed light to believe it was real.
