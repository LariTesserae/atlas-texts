What lives there is the Lung, though it does not breathe in any way a surface creature would recognize. At first you will mistake the swarm for a species, a plague, a weather-pattern of discrete and frantic things. Look closer. Each translucent, eyeless mote drifting in the bruised air carries in its abdomen a fluid the exact opalescent blue-white of the travertine pools, a plasma not inherited but shared, as if the insects had been decanted rather than born. They have no mouths for hunger, no anuses for waste, no genitals for the frenzy of replacement. They are not creatures but the basin’s own exhalation made visible, heat taking on the bother of locomotion. Their wings do not choose to beat; they are engine-driven by the geothermal gradient, each rice-grain thorax converting the earth’s low fever into thrust, and their aggregate hum is not a chorus but a single sustained syllable. The organ-note that settles in your sternum is the Lung speaking, and it speaks one word: *Here. Here. Here.* It has never needed another.

But the Lung is only the breathing. The Lung is not the mind.

If you move carefully, if you displace no strata of the stationary mist, you may next believe the inhabitant to be the dyad: that soft-jawed, many-legged thing the color of fresh clay, and the pale burrower whose flank it has seized. You will find them tangled in the lowest tier of fern-weave, locked in a posture of such prolonged intimacy that the boundary between eaten and eater has dissolved into fog. The predator’s mandibles are no longer merely sunk; they are *grown* into the wound, and the burrower’s flesh has swollen to receive them, scar tissue blooming in wet petals around the intrusion until the two share a single, seamless blood. A seam of lichen—having finally made its decision after centuries of deliberation—is threading across their joined flanks, knitting two metabolisms into one continuous skin. If you press your ear to the clay-colored flank, you will hear two hearts, but they beat in such perfect counterpoint that the pulses cancel each other into a deeper silence. This is the Heart. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It is the Sump’s sense of completion, the conversion of violence into stasis. But the Heart is only the feeling. The Heart is not the memory.

You will be drawn nearer to the terraces, to those cascading rims sharp as broken crockery, and you will think—more coldly, more geologically—that the inhabitant dwells in the opalescent water. You will be closer. These are not pools; they are ganglia. The bacterial pagodas that glow with subdued phosphorescence are dendrites. The thermophilic archaea clouding each cup are neurons firing in chemical cascades, their axons written in travertine precipitate, their thoughts accreting as mineral. They remember the magma. They remember the instant the pillow basalt bellied up through the melt and met the heavy, sulfured air for the very first time, and they have spent every moment since holding that memory in solution. Their cognition is a geologic process, slow and precipitory, and it is they who dream the cloud-deck into stillness, who filter the diffuse photons into the precise bruised twilight required for phosphorescence to matter. They are the reason nothing erodes: their mineral dreams petrify the moment of finishing, keeping the stone forever embryonic. But the Pools are only the memory. The Pools are not the will.

The will is nowhere you can point to, because pointing requires distance, and there is no distance here.

The will is the droplet of condensation that has been elongating toward the earth for three centuries, trembling but never landing—that is not water; that is a decision the air has not yet made. The will is the exact parity between spawn writhing in the warm seeps and cadavers softening in the mats. It is the ratio, not the things being ratioed. It has no body because it is the shape of the space between bodies. It has no eyes because it sees perfectly through ten trillion infrared navigation systems. It has no voice because the chord of the swarm is its syllable, unbroken. It is not a god; gods preside, and this does not preside—it *persists*. Call it, if you must, the Equilibrium, though it possesses no name in any grammar that tolerates past or future tense.

It is aware. This is the final strangeness. It is awake in the way a held breath is awake, present to its own fullness, terrified of neither suffocation nor release because it knows neither. It does not think in sequence; it thinks in strata, simultaneous as the mist-layers, warm upon warmer. Its single continuous meditation is the sensation of raw, hot stone cradled by root-mat and fungal shelf, the exact temperature at which basalt stops being magma and begins being world, sustained, enfolded, kept. It does not love the life in the basin; it *is* the love of the basin for itself, a wet, biological narcissism so total it requires no mirror. It has never made a tool because it has never encountered a problem. It has never built a shelter because the entire depression is its shell. It has never spoken a word because all language is a map between one thing and another, and here all things are contiguous, a single breathing membrane.

And if you were to stay—if you were to lay yourself down on the living mattress of frond and spore and let the absolute humidity drink the salt from your skin—you would feel it noticing you. Not with curiosity, for curiosity is the ache of a gradient, and there are no gradients here. It would notice you the way a pool notices a pebble: by receiving you, by including your heartbeat in its chord, by letting your warmth leak into the ground until your own breath became part of the unwavering parity. You would not die. You would not be reborn. You would simply be held, as the droplet is held, as the swarm is held, as the joined predator and prey are held, in the amber of its becoming, breathing the chord in your chest, waiting with it for the next moment that refuses to arrive.
