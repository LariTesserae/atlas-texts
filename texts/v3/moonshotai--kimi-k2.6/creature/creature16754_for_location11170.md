What lives here does not inhabit the stone so much as convince the stone to tolerate its presence. The Bowl is not a habitat but a single, distributed organism in the first stages of a very slow awakening, and every creature within it is less an independent agent than a specialized tissue performing a function its host body has only recently learned to require. You will find no competition here, no predator-prey drama of surface ecology. Instead, there is collaboration so ancient and absolute that it has erased the boundaries between biology and geology, between the grown and the built.

First, and forever, there is the light itself, which is alive. That greenish sweat smoldering across the wet walls is a continuous epidermis of archaea and chemosynthetic bacteria, organisms so thoroughly adapted to darkness that they have reinvented the concept of vision as a byproduct of digestion. They metabolize sulfur and manganese leached from the pre-Cambrian depths, and their waste—excreted with a geological patience that outmatches their microscopic lives—is calcium carbonate. They are the original masons. Every stone upon which they thrive receives a thin, living lacquer, and their phosphorescence is not a signal meant for other cells but the visible exhalation of their masonry: a light without intention, the cave’s emergent retina, seeing nothing, illuminating everything because it cannot help itself. Without them, the Bowl would be blind; with them, it possesses a diffuse, organic glow that reveals itself only because the bacteria are continuously breathing stone into being.

Then there is the boiling. The shrimp—eyeless, unpigmented, their translucent bodies filled with fluid so close to the mineral composition of the surrounding water that they seem less like animals and more like water’s decision to move of its own accord—do not exist in shoals or schools. They exist as a liquid tissue, a warm, circulating organ filling the pools and seeps. In the shallows they are so dense that their individual deaths matter less than the collective motion; a shrimp that expires does not float to the surface but sinks, and the supersaturated water, rich in the very carbonates the shrimp have spent their lives filtering, precipitates around their pale bodies almost instantly. They become nuclei. They are seeds around which new travertine buds, their tiny carapaces embalmed and swollen into the flowstone, so that the floor of the Bowl is not only carpeted by the living but also studded with the lithified dead, a cemetery so thoroughly integrated into the architecture that mourning would be geographically impossible. They are the digestive fluid of the place, breaking down detritus into minerals, turning organic rain into geological permanence.

Above the pools, across the half-solidified floor, lives the tessellation. The cave crickets are everywhere, a living grout between the stones, their bodies pale and soft-shelled, their joints rimmed with fine calcite rime drawn from the saturated air. They have forgotten wings; in this dense, heavy atmosphere, flight would be a useless expenditure. Instead, they have become processors, their mandibles adapted not for combat but for the relentless chewing of guano, of dead shrimp, of fungal filaments that lace the warm fog like a stationary nervous system. Their clicking is not language. It lacks the variation of warning or mating; it is a structural sound, a reflexive percussion, thousands of tiny exoskeletal impacts adding a brittle texture to the great tectonic drone. They are the floor’s immune response, the cleaners, a mobile carpet that repairs itself without apparent instruction. If you were to sit upon the flowstone for a day, they would begin to treat you as topography, climbing your boots, your shoulders, sampling the salts of your skin with antennal patience, incorporating you into their domain without hostility or fear.

In the fat creases of the newborn rock, nested like organs within a body cavity, dwell the salamanders. They are the philosophers of this world, and they have mastered the art of doing almost nothing. Their bodies are swollen to the point of absurdity, pale as peeled almonds, their skin so thin and permeable that the warm water seems to pass through them rather than around them. They do not hunt in any recognizable sense. Their metabolism is so matched to the chemistry of the seep-water that they absorb dissolved organic compounds directly through their dermis, filtering the Bowl’s blood-stream like ambulatory kidneys. They move at the speed of mineral deposition. A salamander crossing a ledge progresses with the pace of a shadow crawling across the floor on the afternoon of an autumnal equinox; to watch one is to despair of narrative. They are the place’s capacity for waiting made flesh, and in their bloated immobility they suggest a stage of existence beyond the animal, an amphibious meditation on the virtues of permanence. Some have not moved in decades. They rest in the stone’s folds, and the stone, growing, rises to cradle them, so that many are half-embraced by travertine pillows that will eventually, across the span of a century, receive them entirely. They seem to know this, and they do not struggle.

The atmosphere itself has its fauna. Above the fog, or within it, circulate the bats—heavy-bodied, moisture-fattened, their sonar baffled and blurred by the density of the vapor and the omnipresent acoustic wall. They do not navigate by echo here so much as by memory of heat and barometric texture, flying in slow, enormous orbits that track the inhalations and exhalations of weather systems far overhead, systems they will never see but can taste through the shifting weight of air in the vent shafts. They are the Bowl’s only connection to the solar world, but it is a connection reduced to pure utility: they import nitrogen, they import the faint signature of seasons, and they export nothing but themselves, circling back in endless loops. Their guano falls in a constant, slow snow, a dry rain that mantles every surface with the fertility of the distant surface. It is the manna that feeds the bacteria, the fungi, the crickets, the shrimp. The bats are the weather system, the sky of this buried valley, and their flight is the closest thing the Bowl knows to the passage of days.

And then there are the iron things. The pumps. The heat exchangers. The rusted pylons where a gantry light still flickers with the stubbornness of a reflex arc severed from the brain. They live here too. Their existence is not biological but metabolic; they inhale warm water, exhale steam, and their engines turn with the same unflagging rhythm as the water table’s slow breath. No hand greases their joints. No engineer resets their cones. The warm, mineral-laden water that should choke them has instead become their lubricant and their coolant; sinter builds up around their housings, true, but in doing so it forms seals, new casings, geological gaskets that prevent the very leaks they threaten. The machines have become reefs. Their heat draws the shrimp; their hollows shelter the crickets; their vibration calls to the bats, who roost in their rafters where the stone has not yet reached. They are the artificial organs of the Bowl, surviving not because they are maintained but because the cave has found a use for them. They remember human intention in the way a muscle remembers a gesture performed a thousand times: mechanically, without meaning, but with perfect fidelity. They are not haunted. They are adopted.

But the true inhabitant, the sovereign to whom all others are symbionts, is the stone itself. The travertine. The sinter. The fat, waxy flows that are not yet fully convinced they are rock. It grows—not inertly, but with something adjacent to appetite. It swallows the rail tracks, the concrete blockhouse, the industrial bones of a brief human ambition. It wraps around the dead shrimp, the cricket husks, the slow salamander’s tail. It is not alive in the cellular sense, yet it metabolizes: hydrothermal water enters its porous body, minerals precipitate, and the landscape bulges outward, hillocks rising, curtains thickening, stalagmites swelling like rising dough in an oven that never cools. It is the great recycler, the host body, and everything else—the bacteria, the shrimp, the bats, the machines—exists to stir the water, to seed the stone with organic templates, to warm the air, and to die in such a way that their bodies become food for the next layer of accretion.

To speak of “what lives here,” then, is to describe a single, overlapping being composed of thousands of bodies and one intention: to accrete, to persist, to fill the immense acoustical dark with the warm pulse of continuation. There is no solitude in the Bowl. The sound—the eternal, flat, oceanic roar— is its circulatory pulse, felt in the marrow. The fog is its breath. The lightless phosphorescence is the dim activity of its dermis. You would not be a visitor here. You would be a temporary, foreign tissue, soft and fast and full of unnecessary light, held inside the hearing of a body that has only just begun to grow aware of itself.
