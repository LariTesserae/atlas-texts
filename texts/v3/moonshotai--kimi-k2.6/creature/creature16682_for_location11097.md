There is a moment, after you have been in the plain long enough for your own heartbeat to become a traitor—too loud, too brief, too achingly mammalian—when you realize you are not alone because the noise itself has begun to modulate around you. The deafening roar does not diminish; rather, a pocket opens inside it, a shaped absence. A rhythm that is not the chaotic clatter of mandible on stone, but slower, older, *intentioned*. If you follow that pocket to its source, you will find them pressed against the leeward side of the cobble huts, or half-submerged in the shallows of the seeping flats, having gone so still that they register first as geology, then as shadow, and only lastly as flesh.

They do not stand to greet you. Their bodies have forgotten verticality except in brief, careful stretches, for their architecture is the architecture of the hut: low, broad, fused to the floor in a posture that suggests they have been waiting since before the continents were planed flat. They move on two limbs or four, the transition fluid, a gait suited to doorways that demand submission and to stone that never dries. At rest, they curl like ammonites, knees tucked to chest, foreheads touching the wet pan, maximizing the surface area of their skin against the breathing rock.

Their skin is the color of the absence of chlorophyll: the gray-white of cave fish, the matte pallor of old tooth enamel, the faint sulfurous yellow of the chemosynthetic films they lick from the stone. It is not skin, not precisely. It is a dermis become lithology, thickened into strata, keratinized into something between hide and shell. In the eternal saturation, this outer layer swells with condensed vapor, softening, only to harden again as the fog tides shift, so that over a lifetime the body accretes, adding microns like the chimneys they tend. The eldest among them are crusted at their joints with bands of deposited mineral—calcite cuffs, anklets of travertine, a crown of sinter at the brow. They bear their age as the plain bears its erosion: stoically, visibly, without rot. You cannot tell where their bodies end and the centuries begin.

They have eyes, but these are vestigial, small and wet as pinpricks in dough, suited only for the intimate, quiet distances of flint-knapping and needle-threading. Their true sensing is distributed across the broad, shovel-like palms of their hands and the soles of their feet, and across the tympanic plates that flare from their skulls behind the jaw. These plates—wide, curved, faintly iridescent with nacre—vibrate constantly with the ambient violence of the plain. They do not hear sound so much as inhabit it. A conversation between two of them is a duet of knuckle on granite, of palm struck against ribcage, of forehead pressed to stone in subsonic declaration. They speak in architecture of vibration, in frequencies that declare *here* and *mine* and *the water rises beneath the third hut*. Identity is timbre. Memory is pressure. They know the location of every spring, every seep, every sound-bent chimney, because they feel the water table moving through the soles of their feet, because the arthropod migrations are waves they read like Braille across the pan.

In the huts, where the mass of fitted stone filters out the highest, most abrasive frequencies, they practice the small, patient arts that their world permits. The knapped blade is not for war—the plain offers no enemies large enough to merit weaponry, only the endless crustacean swarms that must be cracked, shelled, scooped from their armor. The granite mortar is for grinding the dried, sulfur-eating mats that bloom at the geothermal seeps, a paste of chemosynthetic gruel that they consume and also smear across their dermis, feeding the invisible bacterial gardens that symbiotically sustain them. And the bone needle: with it, they sew. Not cloth—there is no flax, no fiber, no weave of vegetation in this kingdom—but the shed exoskeletons of the largest arthropods, softened in acidic spring-water, beaten into flexible sheets, and stitched into cloaks that rattle when they crawl. The cloaks serve as armor against the swarms, and as cisterns; the condensation beads on the chitin weave until the garments are heavy with drink, and they quench themselves by wringing the fog from their own hems.

They know fire. In sunken hearths lined with the charred bones of their own dead—bone burns slow and hot here—they keep embers of a cold, blue combustion fed by rendered slug-fat and powdered chitin. It is not for warmth. The plain is a exhausted cradle, cool but never threatening. Fire is for tempering. They harden needle points in it. They heat river-cobbles to test their fracture patterns before stacking. They stare into the sulfurous, oxygen-starved flames and see, perhaps, the only light they have ever trusted, for above them the sun remains merely a rumor diffused through thirty meters of silver ceiling.

Their children—rare, precious, born one at a time during the great thermal surges—enter the world soft and pink, lacking the mineralized dermis, mewling in ultrasonic distress. They are kept in the deepest, quietest corners of the huts, swaddled in the shed skins of pale scavengers, fed on chemosynthetic broth until their tympanic plates harden and they can endure the full orchestral violence outside. To be adult is to become loud inside, to let the roar enter the marrow and remain there, resonant, without madness. To be old is to have gone silent again, so saturated with the plain’s frequency that one becomes a dampening stone, a living silence around which the noise flows.

And they tend. This is the truth the empty huts only imply. They are not farmers—there is nothing to sow. They are not herders—the arthropods obey chemistry, not will. They are acoustic gardeners, geometers of resonance. They adjust the growth of the mineral chimneys with subtle alterations to the sound-field, rolling resonant boulders into new alignments, damming seeps to deepen the hydraulic note, encouraging the swarms to cluster or disperse so that the resulting percussion sculpts the accretions in desired directions. They are composing a structure that has been sounding for millennia, trying to achieve some final, patient chord that will cause the plain to ring true. Or perhaps they achieved it long ago, and the deafening saturation you experience is not chaos but the overture, maintained.

When the fog tide rolls in thickest, and the larger pools still to that impossible, reflective glass, they creep to the water’s edge and look down. What they see in the reversed undersurface of the world— the dripping ceiling of stone that exists in the reflection but not above— is not for you to know. But you may witness them there: crouched low, their vestigial eyes closed, their tympanic plates flared wide as wings, drinking a silence that exists only in the space between the water and its impossible other half.

They do not fear you. You are too brief. You are a warm, breathy anomaly in a world that inhales in centuries. They will watch you pass from the absolute corners of your vision, gray on gray, still as the stone they resemble, and when you are gone they will resume their stacking, their grinding, their patient percussive vigil. They are the listeners at the bottom of a finished world. They have nowhere else to be, and nothing else to hear, but the endless mineral conversation of water, and stone, and the clatter of a biology that never needed the sun.
