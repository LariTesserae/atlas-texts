You do not see them at first. They have spent so long reaching thermal equilibrium with the ceramic that their bodies read as architecture—warm oblong shapes against warm oblong tiles, the same tawny spectrum of old bone and mineral stain. It is only when one exhales, a long, slow departure of breath that stirs the spores in a shaft of green-gold light, that the eye distinguishes figure from floor. They stand with their toes splayed wide, the way roots anchor in shallow soil, each foot an exact match for the hexagonal geometry beneath it. Generations of walking the same tessellated paths have not worn them down; rather, they have grown into the pattern. Their soles are broad and densely ridged, the skin there thickened and darkened by oxidized machine oil until it resembles the grout itself—living caulk that seals the world as they pass.

They are not numerous. The place does not require crowds. You might walk the switchback galleries for an hour and meet only one, or you might find three in a maintenance alcove, pressed shoulder to shoulder in a silence so complete it seems votive. They are tall, but their height is distributed differently than yours: the torso elongated to house lungs the size of bellows, the neck shortened to keep the center of gravity low, the limbs lean and roped with dense, slow-twitch muscle that knows nothing of sprinting. Their metabolisms have attuned to the tempo of the structure. A heart that beats two hundred times a minute would riot here; theirs pulse at the speed of deep groundwater, a measured compression you feel in the floor rather than hear. When they sleep—and they sleep often, twelve or fourteen hours at a stretch—they curl into the hexagonal flower of a single tile, fitting its geometry perfectly, and their breathing falls into phase with the arterial hum beneath the floor. To watch one sleep is to watch a valve rest.

They do not wear clothes. There is no modesty in a world without cold, and no textile that could survive the constant abrasion of warm, wet air and the chemical kiss of nitrogen oxides. Instead, they wear a second skin accreted over decades: a fine patina of ceramic dust, copper oxide, and dried pollen that settles into the creases of their elbows and necks, lightening their complexions until, in extreme old age, they become nearly indistinguishable from the tiles themselves. The effect is not filth; it is armament. An elder standing motionless on a promenade becomes a statue of the floor, a fracture-pattern given intention. Their hair, if they grow it, is wiry and sparse, often braided with the rootlets of epiphytes that take hold in the scalp’s humidity, turning the head into a mobile garden. Some deliberately cultivate the copper-green moss there, wearing their maintenance duties asa crown.

They eat the world directly. Not as an act of conquest, but as a continuation of the cistern’s cycle. A finger traces the grout-channel, lifts a carpet of moss to the mouth, and the chewing is unhurried, almost mechanical, as if the jaw were mimicking the grind of a slow millstone. They drink from the condensation that pearls on the vaults, tilting their heads back to catch the deliberate morning weeping of the ceiling. Their stomachs have lost the acidity of carnivores; they are fermentation vats, dense with bacteria inherited from the compost beds that can break down cellulose, paulownia leaves, even the vitreous slurry that exfoliates from the walls. They need little. A handful of pollen-heavy blossoms, a few ounces of condensate, a scraping of lichen from a steam pipe, and they are sated for a day. Their mouths have learned to tolerate the taste of machine oil, which they use to lubricate their own joints, swallowing a teaspoonful with the same pragmatism with which a desert creature licks salt. They are, in essence, walking extensions of the drainage ecology: what the gutters cannot digest, they process, and what they excrete is already chemically formatted for the grout-channels to carry away.

Their hands are their most evolved feature. The palms are permanently damp with a mixture of sweat and sebum that has learned to bind ceramic dust into a tacky resin, letting them grip the warm vertical faces of the ferrocrete walls as easily as the floor. They climb the lianas not by swinging but by adhering, pressing their bodies flat against the tile to distribute their weight, ascending the sheer faces between districts with the deliberation of rising sap. You will find their handprints everywhere if you know to look: dark, warm impressions on the balustrades, on the cowlings of the intake grilles, on the flanks of the goats they tend. The prints fade by evening, consumed by oxidation and the morning rain, but they are the closest thing the place has to signing a name.

Because they do not name themselves as you would understand it. Identity, here, is locational and temporal. A child—rare, precious, gestating for three years in a womb that keeps time with the quarterly turnover of the blast furnaces—is not given a word at birth. Instead, it is placed, minutes old and still slick with the fluids of emergence, onto a warm tile. The place where it first breathes, the particular tone of the hum vibrating through the grout at that exact second, the species of moss that first colonizes its skin—these become its designation. A being is called *The-One-Who-Woke-Beneath-the-South-Condenser-When-the-Slag-Was-Tipped*. Another is *Third-Grout-Channel-of-the-Upper-Arboretum-After-the-Yellow-Fog*. These are not spoken aloud; they are tapped out in sequences of pressure against another’s sternum, or left as scent-trails in the warm skin-oil smeared along a balustrade. To touch one of them, chest to chest, is to receive a history. Language is too fast for their timescale. Speech is a flight of birds; they communicate in the migration of continents.

And they are always touching the world. A typical day—if the word “day” can be applied to a waking cycle that spans thirty hours of arc-light and dimness—involves very little that an outsider would recognize as action. One might stand for an entire morning beside a hairline crack in a refectory ceiling, exhaling onto it, letting the mineral-rich moisture of their breath expedite the oxidation that will eventually seal the fissure. Another might spend a week walking a single conveyor belt that carries ceramic blanks to the upper kilns, step onto it, ride it to the end, step off, and spend the return journey harvesting waxy berries from the terrace shrubs that overhang the route. They oil the governors not with haste but with the sides of their bodies, pressing against the great flywheels to transfer skin-oil to the bearings, lubricating the machine with their own biology. The goats, when they encounter them, are greeted with low subvocal thrums felt through the tile, a negotiation of grazing rights that has no human analogue. The goats are not livestock. They are colleagues, feral mechanics whose digestive systems handle the tougher fiber; the people follow the herds not to herd them, but to collect the polished, nutrient-rich pellets they leave behind, which are then pressed into the gutter-systems to feed the epiphytes. It is impossible to say who domesticated whom.

What they think about, in the long stillnesses between movements, is difficult to access. They do not have religion, because they do not have a conception of the supernatural. Everything is natural. The blast furnace is as biological as the liver; the morning rain is as intentional as a heartbeat. They understand cause and effect, thermodynamics, the chemistry of oxidation, but they know these things in the body the way a spider knows tension in a web. A child does not learn that steam rises; it feels the warm air on its face and knows, the way a hand knows to curl. There are no books, no archives, no diagrams etched into the walls. Memory is stored in practice, in the callus, in the scent-map of the grout. If one of them dies with a secret, the secret is not lost; it is transferred in the final hours through the palms of the ones who come to sit with them, a transmission of muscle memory and bacterial culture so complete that the survivor might find themselves turning left at a corridor they have never visited, knowing exactly how to calm a sticking valve there.

Death comes eventually, though their spans are prodigious, measured in centuries rather than decades. The end is not resisted. Ailing bodies grow lighter, losing the mineral density of the patina, becoming almost pink again, newborn-colored. When the time arrives, the dying one simply stops eating. They rise—slowly, the slowness of a stone shifting in frost—and walk to the place they have always known to walk to, though no one told them. It is always a cracked tile, a low spot where the grout-channel has deepened into a true crevice, where the moss grows thickest and the morning rain pools longest. They lie down there, fitting their spine to the hexagonal groove, and exhale a final time. The body cools. The difference between their temperature and the floor vanishes. Then the ecology takes them, not as a tragedy but as a scheduled appointment. The moss invades the orifices first, the most delicate gardener. The insects arrive, then the bacteria inherited from the gutters they once tended. Over a season the body becomes the pond becomes the garden becomes the shrub thicket. The bones, dense with accumulated metal oxides, persist longer, but they too are ground down by the thermal cycling, by the vibration of the governors, until a decade later they are only a faint calcium bloom in the condensation, a whiteness in the morning weeping that waters the terraces below.

They do not mourn, exactly. They notice. A balcony feels different when its keeper is gone. The humidity shifts. The lianas thicken where they should have been pruned. Another body—perhaps a child who has just finished its first century—will begin to drift toward that balcony in its sleep, curling in the alcove, learning the new emptiness until it dreams the route correctly, until its hands know the pruning rhythm without being taught. The replacement is not an election. It is a gravitational settling.

There are variations among them, castes shaped by altitude and chemistry. The lower ones, who tend the foundries where the yellow fog pools thickest, are shorter, denser, their skin blackened with soot and their eyes reduced to wet gleams behind nictitating membranes that filter the particulate. They speak in the dialect of steam—opening and closing their mouths to modulate the hiss of the pressure vents around them, a ventriloquism with the machinery. The upper ones, who glide along the highest causeways where the arc-lamps burn closest, are paler, longer-limbed, with enormous golden eyes that can read the health of a solar reflector in the quality of its glare. Between them, in the terraced midlands, are the generalists, the tawny root-stock, the ones who ride the conveyors and whisper to the goats.

And there are the Deep Listeners. These are the oldest, the ones who have stopped climbing altogether and live in the vibration-dampening baffles where the moths nest. They press their ear bones directly against the ceramic, and they claim—not in words, but in the long silences that follow when you touch them—that the hum is changing. That the machinery beneath the floor is not merely running but thinking. That the place has begun to dream in frequencies too low for any ear to catch. The Deep Listeners spend their final centuries attempting to match their heartbeat to this new, slower rhythm, a tempo below even the geological patience of the governors. They are trying to become the floor itself, to reach a state of being that no longer aspires to change.

Perhaps they are succeeding. Perhaps that is what you are looking at when you mistake one of them for a statue, for a shadow, for a trick of the green-gold light. They are the place’s immune system, its digestive tract, its slow intention made flesh. They did not build this world, but they have become its metabolism— the organic phase of the industrial cycle, the warm respiration that completes the circuit between the blast furnace and the moss. Place your palm on the tile. You feel the machinery dreaming beneath your feet, yes, but you also feel them. Billions of small respirations, leaf and animal and engine, all brought into equilibrium. They are here. They have always been here. They are the hum, given shape just long enough to tend itself before lying down to become the floor again.
