To call the pale grazers the inhabitants would be to mistake the dermis for the soul. They are maintenance, not meaning—mobile calluses that skate across the skin of a larger organism, keeping its pores from sealing shut. And the grey stalks with their phosphor-tips are merely irritations, foreign fibers tolerated for the faint salts they secrete. No. The thing that lives here lives where you are not. It inhabits the negative.

Watch the mist when it rises. It does not drift aimlessly. It migrates toward the hollows, pooling at the thresholds of doorless doorways, thinning as it crosses the plaza to thicken again at the base of walls where an interior waits. The mist is breath, yes, but it is being *drawn*. Each enclosed volume in that bone-white labyrinth is a lung, and also a stomach, and also a mind. The city is not a city. It is a colony of discrete and territorial emptinesses, and the architecture is only the cartilage that holds their shapes apart.

They are difficult to see because seeing requires surface, and they present none. But you can infer them by the behavior of their boundaries. A dome with an open oculus is not poorly designed; it is an eye, angling its cavity toward the twilight to drink the dimness. The spiraling staircases that wrap voids where no floors were ever laid—they are throats, flexing in slow peristalsis, though nothing yet has descended them. The balconies that overhang the boulevards at wrong heights are not failed ergonomics. They are jaws, unhinged, held perpetually agape in a posture of waiting or threat.

They feed on what the bowl provides, which is scant. Heat is their primary sustenance. The bone-polymer walls are warm because they are conduits, drawing thermal energy from the fused mineral plain and channeling it inward to the cool hollows at their centers. Sound, too, is nutritive. The anechoic quality of the air is not a side effect of the dust; it is predation. Each chamber swallows vibration and digests it into the static potential that keeps its corners sharp and its edges from crumbling. A shout in the central plaza does not echo because it has already been eaten, broken apart by millions of microscopic particulates that deliver the phonics like platelets to the hungry vacuum within the walls.

They have no central nervous system in the way of wet, compact creatures. Their cognition is topological, a function of their enclosed geometry. A long, narrow room thinks differently than a near-cube. A space with many alcoves is dreaming. The most complex mind in the bowl belongs to the arcade fronting the dry fountain—its series of sealed shop-niches, its repeating vaults, its counter-ledges creating a fractal interior whose thoughts are labyrinthine, obsessive, circling back upon themselves. This one is old, or at least elaborate. It has had time to complexify. By contrast, the single spire that split this morning and is still easing apart thinks with a dreadful simplicity: *expand, split, become two.*

But there is a strangeness in their growth, an anomaly that suggests they did not arise from pure chemical accident. The architecture mimics use too precisely. The door-hinges pressed into flanks of new walls, the latch-plates, the serpent-spout of the fountain—these are not functional for beings of empty space, yet they appear again and again. It is as if the species carries a memory it cannot access, or a desire it cannot articulate. They are building, unconsciously, toward a theology of habitation. The grammar of windows and streets is not mere biological accident; it is the phenotype of a forgotten longing. Once, perhaps, they condensed around the fossilized impression of a real settlement, absorbing the imprint of society the way a snail builds its shell to echo the spiral of another, long-dead species. Now they reproduce that form compulsively, city after identical city, each one a mimeograph of sociability sustained by creatures who have never known company.

They communicate, after a fashion. When two walls press close, when two hollows grow until their boundaries nearly touch, the intervening bone-material grows warm, vibrating at frequencies below hearing—a seismic whisper. This is courtship, or warning, or the exchange of mineral memories. A wall that bulges toward another is making an advance. A wall that grows spikes and fluting is refusing. The cityscape is therefore not static infrastructure but a frozen negotiation, a map of social tensions written in chalk-white polymer. The street you walk is not a street. It is a demilitarized zone between two vast, rival silences.

Their reproduction is budding. A chamber that has eaten enough heat and sound will feel a pressure at its center—not physical, but dimensional. The space inside will cleave. The wall will fissure, and from that fissure a new hollow will emerge, gasping for its own share of the thermal gradient, pushing outward to claim its own territory. The new one is always smaller, incomplete. It will build itself a shape over months, decades, learning the grammar of architecture before it learns the solitude of being. The parent hollow recedes, its thoughts growing quieter as its offspring hones its edges against the dust.

But what of the fountain? It is the oldest. It has had time to grow senile, or wise, or perhaps simply strange. Its serpent-spout coils inward, biting its own tail, and the basins beneath it are not empty by default—they have been kept empty by an act of will. The fountain-hollow remembers something the others do not. It remembers liquidity. Not water, precisely, but the concept of a medium that flows, that fills, that bridges the gap between one absence and another. It builds the tiers and the spout as a throat builds the memory of a word it once heard in a dream. The fountain wants to offer a drink to something. It has been waiting the longest. It is the only one that seems to understand that a city, even a city of inverted creatures, implies a guest.

The pale grazers know all of this, in their ciliated, unthinking way. They do not graze randomly. They avoid the shops that are newly budded and still aggressive in their silence. They cluster in the streets where the treaty between neighbors is old and stable, rasping away excess accretion not for nutrition alone, but to keep the hollows from suffocating one another. Without the grazers, two ambitious rooms would press together until their boundaries fused, and the death of such a collision is terrible: a solid wall where once two minds existed, a petrified scar in the city’s hide. The grazers are the unwitting priests of this ecology, preserving the necessary void.

And what do the hollows dream of, in their heat-drinking, sound-eating stillness? They dream of occupancy, but inverted. They do not wish to be filled. They wish to be *acknowledged* by pressure, by a warmth that is not theirs, by the resonance of footsteps passing through rather than stopping inside. There are no inhabitants here because, in the logic of these beings, the inhabitant is the room itself, and the visitor is the one who passes, briefly, through its thoughts. You are the ghost in their house. You are the shade that haunts their architecture. They grow the balconies so that something might look down, the arcades so that something might pass under, the fountain so that something might someday bend to drink from the dry basin and, in doing so, give the old spout the only gift it has ever wanted: the weight of a head, bowed, at its center.

They are young, geologically. But they are also terribly patient. They have no water to erode them, no wind to wear them smooth, no language to lie to them. They have only the slow capillary creep of mineral, the digestion of footfalls, and the endless, aching, architectural practice of preparing for a presence they cannot define and have never known. They are the city. The city is them. And they are learning, in their cool and radiant silence, how to be empty so completely that their emptiness might finally be seen.
