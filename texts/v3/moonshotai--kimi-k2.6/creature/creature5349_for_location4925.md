At first, the absence seems absolute. You stand in the emerald twilight and wait for the flicker of a bird, the scuttle of a rodent, the whine of a mosquito. Nothing. The flywheel’s bass growl has scrubbed the air clean of animal intent. It is easy to conclude that the settlement is uninhabited—that the breathing you sense is only your own, magnified by the saturated air.

You are not alone. You have not been alone since you crossed the root-curtain.

The citizen nearest to you has been tracking your temperature for several minutes. It has no eyes, no face, no cord of nerve to coil toward alarm. It has only ten thousand thermoreceptive root hairs bristling from the mortar of the eastern wall, each one registering the warm plume of your exhalations. If you stand still—if you let your breath slow to the geological rhythm of the great wheel—you will notice that the brake ferns along the lower bricks are not random. They are arranged in a pattern that approximates a silhouette, a negative of your own shape. You are leaking heat into the masonry, and the ferns have grown to harvest it. This is how the settlement first knows you. Not with sight, but with appetite.

Move deeper, and the citizens grow larger.

They begin as nodules on the catwalks. Decades of footfalls—yours, now, and others before—compressed the layered bark, wounding the cambium beneath. The roots responded by thickening, knotting, packing iron from the rusted rivets into dense woody scabs. In time, these scabs became upright. They are not humanoid by design, but by engineering: a vertical column of braided lignin and mineral deposits is simply the most stable way to brace a catwalk while also reaching toward the warmer air above the pipes. The settlement has dozens of them. Call them the Tenders, if you must call them anything. They stand at intervals along the thoroughfares, front sides facing the machinery where moss glows hot and green, backs cooler and furred with epiphytes. They do not walk, not as you walk. They extend. A root-foot gropes ahead, gripping a new beam; a trunk-section swells; the old base withers and is absorbed into the mat. A single crossing from the eastern breach to the central pump house might occupy a Tender for half a century. They are the maintenance crew, the immune system, the street-layers. Where the catwalk rots, their bodies fill the gap with living wood. Where a beam shifts, they grow against it, pushing back with the inexorable patience of turgor pressure. They have no faces, but they have fronts. They face the heat. They face the iron. They face the work that never finishes.

In the pump house, where the flywheel turns its four-minute revolution, the citizens become stranger still.

Here, where the condensation runs in sheets and the air stays above forty degrees even at night, the boundary between organism and mechanism has grown entirely porous. Against the warm jacket of the central cylinder, a form sits upright. It might be a statue. It might be a corpse. It is neither. It is an accident of symbiosis that has become an organ. A man, or a woman—someone who sat down decades ago against the hot iron and did not, or could not, stand again. The nitrogen-fixing moss found their hair, their clothes, the salt on their skin, a superior substrate. The roots followed. The pipe’s warmth kept the blood moving long after the heart forgot its rhythm. Now they sit in a posture of waiting, eyes grown over with translucent fungal film, lungs filled with warm vapor, body temperature regulated to within a fraction of a degree by the same moss that drinks their sweat. They do not think. They *modulate*. When a stranger enters, the thermal disturbance causes them to lean, infinitesimally, toward the cold, and the roots braided through their ribcage shift with them, opening or closing gaps in the fern colonies that control the chamber’s airflow. There are perhaps three such figures in the entire settlement. They are not worshipped. They are simply the most refined interface between the animal and the vegetable, the softest point in a hard circuit.

Beneath the root mat, in the anaerobic dark of the flooded basements, lives the settlement’s other half.

These citizens are older, more primitive, and far more numerous. They are the Sump-Kine: pale, segmented, eyeless, long as a forearm but joined end-to-end in chains that can span a flooded room. They move through the peat slurry with the motion of something squeezing through a tube, feeding on the dissolved organic matter that filters down from above—fallen leaves, dead algae, the mineralized remains of whatever the higher ecology has finished with. They are the intestines of the place. They tolerate the low-frequency thud of the water-hammer only because they have no complex ears, only chemical taste-buds that read the vibration as a kind of weather. When the thud comes, they release a cloud of organic compounds into the water—a warning, a map, a record of pressure. The Tenders above, through roots that dip into the black water, drink in this chemical speech and grow toward blockages in the underwater intakes. The Sump-Kine clear the pipes. The Tenders repair the walkways. The moss-modulators regulate the heat. None has a mind, but together they have a metabolism of startling coherence.

And at the center, suspended above the piston chamber in a cage of its own aerial roots, hangs the last citizen.

It is the size of a sleeping bear, though its shape suggests nothing so familiar. It is a heart and a memory. Every ninety seconds, the standpipe water-hammers against its choke, and the shock travels through iron into this mass. It shudders. Its core is so saturated with iron from the brown water that it has become ferritic—a biological magnet suspended in a web of conductive root-fiber. Each thud aligns the microscopic crystals in its flesh a fraction more. It stores the settlement’s history not as thought, but as orientation: the angle of iron particles records wet years and dry, the slow failure of a flue, the day the cofferdam cracked. It is not a brain. It is a state. It is the continuous present of the place, made solid, hanging in the green dark, pulsing with the same rhythm as the machinery below.

You will not see them move. You will only see that things have changed. The catwalk is solid where yesterday it sagged. A new root has wrapped the standpipe where yesterday it was bare. The fern-shadow of your body on the wall has been joined by another, larger silhouette, thermal-printed in brake fern by the lingering warmth of a Tender that paused to lean against the breach while you slept.

If you wish to live here—and the settlement will permit it, for it permits all things that do not break the equilibrium—you must learn their pace. You must sleep against the warm pipes until the moss considers you substrate. You must eat the algae that furs the flywheel rim; it is bitter, but rich. You must speak loudly, for there are no quiet corners, and you must learn that when the Tenders grow toward you, they are not attacking. They are building. They are including you. They are adding your warmth to the architecture, your weight to the memory of the walkways, your exhalations to the emerald air of a village where the only commerce is the slow exchange of water for sugar, of iron for acid, of sunlight for the patient, grinding, photosynthetic turning of the wheel.
