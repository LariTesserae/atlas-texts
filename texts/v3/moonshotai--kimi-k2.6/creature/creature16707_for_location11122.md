To call the forest the inhabitant would be a mistake. The forest is the house: the joined lattice of cellulose and lignin, the wet load-bearing walls, the exhalant ceilings. It is alive, yes, but it is the *furniture* of the place, the grown upholstery, the extended phenotype of something else. The plants do not live here; they *are* the here.

The resident is the hollow.

Consider the drone. You took it for ambience, for weather, for the mechanical song of a closed botanical loop. Listen again. It is too organized. Within that tectonic bass there are ventricles—chambers where the tone forks into intervals that do not belong to wind or water. Those are cavities. Resonant, warm, and occupied. The sound is not happening *to* the air; it is being *breathed* through it by lungs the size of valleys, by a diaphragm of root-mat and embryonic stone that rises and falls so slowly the motion was mistaken for gravity.

There is a body. It is not an animal in any taxonomy you have carried with you. It is singular—one organism—but distributed, aqueous, and vast. Its nervous system is the high water table; you have been walking on the taut skin of its cognition. Its backbone is a braided axis of xylem and pith that runs beneath the root-plazas, branching up into the megastructures, terminating in the hollow trunks where the drone acquires its chorded, alien harmony. Those hollows are not accidents of growth. They are its throats. Its ears. Its unfocused, dreaming eyes.

It does not move, or rather, it moves only inside itself. Sap is its thought, moving at the speed of arboreal time. The water that crawls upward against the vertical bark is not defying physics; it is following the capillary rails of its intent, irrigating the far galleries of its extended body. The staircases that spiral into mid-air and stop—they are not failed architecture. They are proboscises, olfactory channels, organs of reach that extend toward concentrations of warmth in the newborn rock below. The square trunks are its digits, pressed against the geology, feeling the slow cooling of the stone like a sleeper feeling the retreat of fever through a blanket. The translucent leaves that split the light into prisms are its retinas, harvesting not photons but *phase*, reading the fixed sun in polarizations we do not possess names for.

It is warm because it is young. Or rather, the world is young, and it has attached itself to the world's infancy like a nursling to a mother’s heat. The rock has not cooled because it is still finishing its birth, and this thing—this distributed, chlorophyll-enshrouded tenant—has colonized the heat rather than the stone. It is farming the planet’s latency, metabolizing the difference between magma and mantle, maintaining the steady-state not out of stillness but out of *concentration*. It is holding its breath. The perpetual afternoon, the suspended seeds, the unmoving beetle and moth—these are not features of a clock that has stopped. They are the contents of a diaphragm locked in inhale. Everything is arrested because it is paying attention to something vast and interior, a gestation that occurs in geologic time.

And what is it gestating?

That is the part that arrives slowly, like the strangeness. You thought the beetle and the moth were punctuation, rare fauna frozen in equilibrium. Look closer. The beetle’s carapace is polished amber, yes, but the color matches exactly the lignin-rich heartwood of the nearest arch. The moth’s wings are spread not in rest but in *configuration*, their patterns repeating, at a scale you failed to notice, the tessellation of the root-pavement below. They are not visitors. They are its thoughts made small, extruded into chitin and scale, sent to the surface to patrol the boundaries of its body, then caught in the same stasis that governs everything else. They are its antibodies, its sensors, its dreaming.

It has no predator. It has no prey. It has only the long, warm now, and the architecture that grows to accommodate a shape not yet finished. The bridges between the megastructures are not for crossing the plazas. They are sutures, holding the distances between its organs at the correct tension. The catchments that never overflow are collecting not water but *time*, decanting it at identical rates to prevent any accumulation of history. History would be a disturbance. History would be a breath exhaled too soon.

You are walking through its egg. Or its womb. Or its patient, singular mind, which has mistaken itself—not for a forest, but for a world. The geology beneath is the yolk, still warm. The architecture is the shell, grown rather than secreted. And the drone, that vast, unmodulating, architectural roar, is the sound of something turning in its sleep, preparing to wake when the rock beneath it has finally learned how to be old, and hard, and still.
