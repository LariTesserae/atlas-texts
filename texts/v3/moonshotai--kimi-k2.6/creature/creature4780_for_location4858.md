What lives here insists that you abandon your assumptions about the individual.

Begin with the Voort. Each one is not one but a parliament—hundreds of pallid, flower-shaped polyps suspended within a shared integument of milky, near-transparent tissue. This skin does not hold a fixed shape; it billows and cinches according to the consensus of its constituents, drifting in the warm metallic currents like a living bolt of silk, yet weighted along its lower margins with ceramic rings and fiber cords so that it might live among the verticalities of the ridge rather than simply float away. The polyps themselves are the color of uncooked bone marrow, each no larger than a thumbnail, and they pulse with a slow, metronomic bioluminescence—cool blue-green, dim as a memory—that outlines the Voort from within. When a Voort pauses at a threshold, the polyps crowd toward its anterior, pressing the skin into a semblance of a face: a broad, cartilage mask through which the colony's mood shows as shifting clouds of phosphor. They have mouths, after a fashion. They have jaws, glassy and faintly iridescent, capable of knapping obsidian or grinding the mineral precipitate that accumulates on their walkways. But the jaw's deeper function is speech. They do not vocalize into the water; they press jaw to jaw, face to face, and let the vibrations of their grinding mouthparts travel through bone and cartilage and into the other's sensorium. A conversation in a crowded street is a silent, weaving dance of proximity, a choreography of glances and pressed cheeks.

They are ancient, but the rock they live upon is younger than some of their ceramic heirlooms.

A Voort does not move quickly. The skin ripples, the polyps contract in waves, and the whole colony descends a cable bridge with the fluid hesitation of smoke. They do not climb so much as pour themselves along routes that have been woven specifically for their viscosity. In the market silences, they hang in clusters above the ceramic trenches, their internal lights dimming and brightening in patterns that indicate want or surplus—bioluminescent bartering. They trade heat, primarily. A lease on a warm fissure; the rights to a channel where the water runs feverish; a cave gouged into basalt that has not yet crossed the Threshold of Plasticity. They trade ceramic that has been fired in kilns fed by magma seeps, the clay repositories of compressed thermal memory. They trade fibers spun from the cardiac tissue of the blood-fronds, ropes that retain, even when severed from their roots, a sluggish, peristaltic warmth.

And they tend the living architecture.

Every surface not raw basalt is accreted with life. The blood-fronds are the Voort's neighbors and, in a sense, their livestock. These are not plants in any terrestrial sense but rigid columns of chemosynthetic tissue, the hue of clotting blood and old bruises, rising thirty meters from mats that drink hydrogen sulfide. Their vascular systems are lucent, carrying not light harvested from above but chemical fire harvested from below; when the ridge exhales, the fronds glow from within like amber lanterns, casting long arterial shadows across the Voort domes. The Voort harvest the fibrous cores to make rope, but they also prune the fronds with their glass blades, guiding their growth so that over centuries—millennia—the living towers form walls, arches, enclosures. The outer forests are too wild for Voort habitation, too densely mucilaginous and sharp with newborn obsidian, but at the city's margins, the Voort have cultivated a hybrid space where ceramic dome and biological tower become indistinguishable, where a doorway might be fired clay or might be woody, blood-red tissue grown into an arch, depending on the age of the district.

Below, in the warmest caves, they farm their own bodies' nourishment. Fungal films spread across the rockfaces like lichen made of wet suede, fed by the mineral exhalations and tended by Voort who press their faces close to the mats, perhaps speaking to the mycelium in subsonic vibrations. The fungi are the color of old teeth, soft enough to be gathered by jaw and glass spatula, rich in the metallic chemistry of the ridge. It is a diet without sunlight, without chlorophyll, without the violence of predation. The Voort have never tasted blood. They have never known the adrenal surge of the hunt. Their drama is geologic: a cooling fissure, a delayed eruption, a district that reaches the Threshold of Plasticity and is finally declared dead, finished, capable of supporting the heavy permanence of a kiln or a glyph-carved archive.

And there are the smaller citizens, the ones the Voort do not claim as kin but who share the heights. The eyeless shrimp, pale as alabaster, congregate in the thermal channels, their backs furred with cultivated bacteria that glows sickly chartreuse. They are mobile gardens, ranchers of microscopic light, and the Voort allow them passage through the city, sometimes guiding them with the tips of their glass blades to caves where the fungal films need pruning. The shrimp are revered as engineers of cleanliness, their tiny jaws preventing the warm caves from choking on their own abundance. In the roots of the blood-fronds lurk thumb-sized parasites, soft and many-segmented, the color of obsidian dust. They do not harm the fronds so much as inhabit them, drinking excess sulfide, and in return they secrete a waxy, insulating substance that the Voort harvest to seal the seams of their ceramic domes. Everything is reciprocity. Everything is symbiosis. The ridge demands exchange.

But the ridge also has its wilder children. Above, in the bruised half-light where the last gray sun vanishes, the vast things drift. They are never named by the Voort, only observed in the reverent silence reserved for weather. Too many joints. Mouths like torn sails opened to the falling vegetable snow—the endless detritus of the forests above, the shed skins and dead polymers and broken fronds that filter down like black snow. These leviathans never descend to the city; they are too large for the narrow shelves, too ancient for the newborn rock. They belong to the open water, to the dark between ridges, and their passage is felt as a shift in pressure, a slow surge in the warm metallic current that makes the Voort cling to their cables and wait. Sometimes one dies, and its corpse falls through the twilight zone for weeks, an ecosystem unto itself, feeding the depths, and the Voort record its falling in their glyphs not as an event but as a kind of slow music, a tectonic note in the grinding lullaby of the ridge.

What lives here is primarily patience.

The Voort do not age as other creatures do. When a polyp dies, another buds from its neighbor; the colony's memory is distributed, housed not in a single brain but in the standing waves of chemical signal that pass through the translucent skin. A single Voort might remember the ridge when it was nothing but a line of thermal vents and a feverish bulge in the basalt. That same Voort might be pressing its jaw to a young relation's cartilage face today, transmitting the memory of rock that sang louder, of streets that flowed like honey underfoot. Death, for them, is a sloughing—a colony that loses too many polyps, that grows thin and milky and drifts finally into the abyssal dark beyond the ridges, where the pressure is absolute and the cold is final. But even then, the skin does not rot so much as settle, draping over the seafloor like a ghost, becoming substrate for new bacterial lace, new coral-pulse mats.

They know they live on an impossibility. Every geological forecast said the Attanni Ridges should not exist, not yet. The Voort find this appropriate. They, too, should not exist—colonies of soft tissue clinging to blades of obsidian in a world of indifferent weight, building cities in defiance of entropy, farming darkness, speaking through touch in a silence so complete it has become a kind of holiness. They press their hands—if those undulating, polyp-rich surfaces can be called hands—against the still-warm stone of new prominences, feeling the subsonic song in their sternums, and they wait. The stone cools. The fronds grow. The city thickens. The polyps pulse.

They are alive, and the ridge is alive, and the distinction between the two has not yet reached its own Threshold of Plasticity.
