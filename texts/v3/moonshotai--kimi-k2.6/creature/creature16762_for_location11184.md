At first, you mistake them for droplets that have learned to skitter. A single *churn* is a thing of such quietness that it seems to borrow its mass from the mist itself. It is no larger than the last joint of your thumb, pale as a tooth kept too long in milk, its body segmented into nine articulated plates that flex rather than hinge. The shell is not armor in the way of beetles—there is no knuckle of chitin, no glossy declaration—but something closer to wet porcelain, soft enough to deform against the satin stone and spring back without sound. It has no eyes. Its face, if it can be called a face, is a fan of antennae no thicker than thread, constantly trailing, testing, reading the humidity like a blind man reads braille. It tastes the air for the metallic exhalation of the stone, for the bloom of film, for the chemical bulletin of its kind. It is a nerve ending made whole.

To see one alone is rare, and to see one alone is to see almost nothing. They do not persist in solitude. The individual is an abstraction, a temporary knot in a current that never stops. Watch two meet at the edge of a runnel. They do not pause—they never pause—but their trajectories slow, their antennae cross like swords in a duet, and for a half-second they share a droplet of moisture bound in electrolytes and pheromone. A confession passes between them: the bacterial film is richer two plazas west; a moult is weakening near the third megalith. Then they flow around each other and are gone, the contact broken, the knowledge diffusing into the swarm. They do not remember. The swarm remembers. They are the swarm's Forgetters and Rememberers all at once, each body a synapse that holds its charge only until the next touch.

Because they never stop, their stillness is the first sign of ending. A churn that is injured, ageing, caught in a crevice during a narrowing of the flow—nothing here dies of old age in a bed—finds its rhythm faltering. The aberration is detected before it occurs. The wave arrives not in malice but in metabolic certainty. Neighbors, passing at the speed of a heartbeat, brush the faltering one, taste the change in its chemical stream, and the news propagates faster than the individual can fall. What happens next is not consumption so much as reclamation. They are stripped with the efficiency of water finding a crack. The soft shell is peeled by hundreds of tiny, hookless mouths; the flesh is passed hand-to-hand in a ripple that radiates outward like a cough. Calcium, phosphorus, the moisture bound in the body—all is borrowed, spent, returned to the current within minutes. Nothing remains. Not a stain, not a husk. The stone beneath is perhaps a fraction more polished than it was before, and the traffic continues.

But look closer at the traffic, and the abstraction of the swarm begins to fracture into strata. They are not uniform. Those that keep to the runnels, the shallow veins where the ceiling’s sweat gathers, grow plumper, their shells taking on a faint, opalescent sheen from the bacteria they graze. They move with a heavy, deliberate liquidity, like cream poured in fat spirals. Then there are the climbers, the ones drawn always to the vertical, to the shoulders of the ziggurats and the spines of the bone-white megaliths. They seek the upper light, that marginally brighter zone where the calcite sky filters its cold radiance. Their bodies are leaner, longer in the joint, their ventral setae more densely packed to grip the damp ascent. They climb in living ropes, columns spiraling upward in silent vortices, breaking against the limits of gravity and cascading down in soft drumming curtains. None ever reach the fissures. All eventually fall. But the city was built, after all, for movement, and so the falling is also a kind of arrival.

There are others: the gleaners that hunt the grooves where the water vanishes into porous stone, their bodies narrower, able to press into pores no wider than a needle; the sweepers that move in broad, rapid ellipses across the open plazas, their passage so numerous and so consistent that they serve as a lathe, burnishing the stone with ten million microscopic ablations, maintaining the satin finish that eons have achieved. The city would not stay finished without them. It would clog, would roughen, would lose its way. The churn are the maintenance of the world, its immune system and its polish, its circulation and its breath.

They are born where the stone drinks deepest. In the porous cavities beneath the lowest platforms, where the mist collects in darkness, eggs lie in gelatinous strands no thicker than spider silk, laid by no mothers that claim them. The stone exhales warmth—a relative warmth, still chill by any surface measure—and the eggs hatch into motes, pinhead fragments of pale life that are already moving, already correct. The swarm teaches them by collision. A young churn, nearly invisible, is buffeted into the channels, swept into the concentric rings, and by the time it has grown to visible size it has already traced the city’s mandala a thousand times. It knows the flow as a fish knows pressure. There is no learning, only the alignment of body to topology.

Once, perhaps twice in a creature’s brief span, it will moult. It seeks the center of a dense vortex for this, where the press of bodies is a shield. The old shell splits along a dorsal seam that is invisible until it opens. For an hour, the new churn is soft as a slug, its color not pale but nearly clear, a gelid thing of ghost-white viscera. It moves in a frenzy, a whipping, panicked dervish, ricocheting off its neighbors who part and close around it like water around a stone. Then the new shell firms, opacifies, returns to the color of old bone, and the creature is indistinguishable again, its brief vulnerability erased, swallowed by the blur.

What lives here, then? Not the individual, surely. Not even the swarm as a mere phenomenon of appetite. What lives here is a continuousness, a steady-state metabolism given legion form. The *churn* are the stone’s second thoughts. The bacteria drink the filtered light and die; the churn drink the bacteria; the churn die and are stripped to ions that join the mist that feeds the stone that births the bacteria. The city channels them, yes, but they channel the city. Every groove in the plaza is kept open by the passage of a billion clawless feet. Every runnel is scoured free of obstruction by the endless current of their seeking. They are the blood cells of a creature that does not know it is alive, maintaining the satin body of the architecture against the silence.

They have no rite but motion. No anthem but the soft percussion of damp shell on wet stone. No god but the geometry of the flow. And yet, if you stand still until your breath fogs and then clears, until you are only another pillar of borrowed calcium in the eternal afternoon, you will see patterns in the swirl that resemble processions. The churn trace the old boulevards not merely because the boulevards are broad, but because the stone there remembers feet. The worn glyphs, illegible even to time, still offer the faintest variation in texture, and the antennae find these ghosts, follow them, reënact them. The city planned for flow; the churn have become the flow’s memory. They are not the builders. They do not remember the builders. But they remember the *shape* of having been intended.

And so this is what lives below the lie of the sky: a pale, frantic, endlessly gentle maintenance. A species-gesture that has resolved itself into weather. The *churn* do not sleep, do not dream in any way a mammal would recognize, but the swarm, in its totality, dreams the city. It dreams it smooth and empty and bright and cool, and the dream is indistinguishable from the world. If you were to remain, if you were to surrender your warmth and your edges, they would flow over you once, twice, and on the third pass you would be not a visitor but a feature—a brief, complex interruption soon reconciled to the satin stone. They would not hate you. They do not hate. They only move, and in moving, keep.

They are the only citizens. They are the conversation the stone has with itself to keep from forgetting that it is more than dust. They are old, not in the way of years, but in the way of water: the same water that has always been here, assuming new bodies, new trajectories, new instants of pale and jointed being. They are the churn. They are what endures.
