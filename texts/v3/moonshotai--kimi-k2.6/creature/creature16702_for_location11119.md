The first thing that lives here is the sound itself—not an echo, but a tenant. You will think the dark is empty until you realize it is chewing. The sound of wet felt dragged across grit is the foot of a Tender, but the Tender is only the largest, slowest syllable of a hunger that pervades the clay.

A Tender is bone-white and broad as a banquet table, its body a slab of cool, pale meat that ripples across the tiles in peristaltic waves. Its shell is not a spiral fortress but a low, broad crescent, unsettlingly like the adzes that scarred the pillars—and it is, in fact, an organ of hearing. In a world without photons, the Tender sees with percussion. When a drop strikes the curved bowl of its shell, the vibration travels to a membrane at the shell’s hinge, and the Tender knows exactly how far the nearest wall stands, how deep the channel runs, where the next runnel branches. It is a creature turned inside-out: its ear is its roof, its architecture is its senses. It drags this resonant dome through the weeping dark, playing the space like a musician touching a string, reading the geometry of the basin in rings and tones.

On every surface, feeding on the lime and the sulfur in the condensation, grows the Meadow: a velvet biofilm of chemolithotrophs, pale as a drowned moon. It is the only light here, if light is the word for a glow too faint to cast a shadow, a foxfire that merely makes the darkness visible as a texture. The Tenders graze with radulae like combs of glass, shearing the Meadow, and the Meadow grows back in hours, fed by the eternal drip. It is the pasture and the lamp, the first link in a chain that has no sun.

Living in the grooves of the adze-shells are the Flakes—flat isopods the color of chalk, so still they seem to be chips of limestone until they scuttle. They eat the dead epidermis of the Tenders, but more importantly, they garden. In their guts they carry spores of the Meadow. When a Tender pauses at a fresh channel, the Flakes disembark, planting the first food in the new stone, ensuring the builder never outpaces its pasture.

In the resonant bowls, where water falls and the great gongs and ticks of the place are born, live the Keepers. They are minute, translucent, almost made of gel, blind but sensitive to the slightest change in pitch. They are not musicians; they are curators. They scrape the mineral accretions from the ceramic so the note stays pure. A bowl left untended would dull to a thud, and a Tender passing beneath would lose its way, its map dissolving into noise. The Keepers maintain the instrument.

The Tenders are not wanderers by destiny, but by phase. They hatch from eggs laid in the great jars—ceramic-hard, calcified things that rattle in the dark like loose teeth. The young emerge pale and already heavy, no larger than a human heart. For a century they graze and build, excreting walls, raising runnels, their cementitious slime hardening in the cellar-cool to become mortar, becoming ledge, becoming floor. Then a chemical clock turns. They seek a spot where the water falls in a rhythm that matches the resonance of their shell, a note that says *here*. They begin to circle. Ten years. Twenty. Their trail builds up, ring upon ring, a tree-ring of architecture. Their body sinks into the accretion. The shell tilts, becomes a capital, cupping the drip. The flesh calcifies, becomes limestone, becomes pillar. They do not die; they graduate into the load-bearing dark. Every pillar in the irregular copses is a Tender who finished becoming. The place is a forest of its own ancestors.

The original builders—those who pressed the hexagonal tiles, leaving whorled thumbprints in the wet clay—are gone. But the clay remembers. Sometimes a Tender will halt, its muscular foot rippling, pressing its own whorled flesh into an ancient impression. For a moment the two patterns align—the maker and the made—and the tile seems to soften, as if the clay, cool and eternal, dreams of the hand that shaped it. The builders were not Tenders. They were something else, something that measured steps in decades and archways in generations, that built jars to feed armies of the slow. They left their tools, and the Tenders, in their adze-shells, continue the grammar of construction, speaking a language they did not invent but cannot stop uttering.
