You will not see them at first. You will feel them as a change in the floor’s gossip—a new frequency threading itself through the stone’s endless rumble, something deliberate where everything else is geothermal accident. During the Subsidence, when the roar drapes itself down into a merely thunderous gold, the city ceases to be a dead thing. It becomes a body again. The tremors are footsteps.

They emerge from the dead-zones: chambers padded with ages of woven mineral-fiber, compressed salt-thread and beaten copper felt, built to hoard silence the way a lung hoards air. The doors do not creak. They exhale. And the ones who remain step out into the merely-deafening city with the hesitation of creatures leaving water, their broad, flat feet testing the hexagonal stone as if it were thin ice.

They are tall, but they carry themselves low. Their architecture is vertical, yet their posture is bowed, always reaching down to keep contact with the floor, the walls, the pillars. They have no ears. Where ears would be, there is only a smooth, flaring plate of bone and salt-rind, an empty architecture as deliberate as the amphitheaters they built. They hear nothing of the air. The sound that matters to them travels through the lithic body of the world itself, conducted up through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands.

And what hands. They are enormous, out of proportion even to their elongated frames, each palm broad as a dinner plate and covered in a thin, taut membrane stretched over hollow cavities of bone. Six-fingered, jointed in two directions, these are not grasping organs so much as listening devices. When they press their hands to the salt-stone, they do not merely feel vibration; they read it the way eyes read ink. A footstep three streets away is a sentence. The subsonic groan of a cooling vent is weather. The creak of glass-grass pushing upward during the Hot Phase is a calendar. Their language, such as it is, consists of tapping: a fingernail against a pillar sends a question racing through the stone at the speed of quartz, answered a mile away by a companion’s obliging palm. They do not speak. They resonance. They do not call out. They inquire.

Their bodies are kiln-dry. No water moves in them; water is a fable from the outer dark, a poison of liquidity and rot. What circulates through their segmented limbs is something thicker, stranger—a slow, dark sap of metallic salts and silicate suspensions that moves not in pulses but in tides, responding to pressure rather than rhythm. Wounds do not bleed; they weep a dark, resinous lacquer that hardens into new armor. Over decades, their skin accretes a secondary shell of sintered sodium and verdigris, each individual becoming a walking deposit, a living mineral strata blending into the city they maintain. Their eyes, small and obsidian, are perpetually narrowed against the merciless light, vestigial ornaments compared to the vast sensitivity of their palms. They see, but they trust the stone more.

They move through the overgrown avenues not as trespassers but as tuners. The sonotrophic jungle is their orchard, their instrument, and sometimes their adversary. With bronze wedges and obsidian blades, they prune the copper-oxide curtains that spill from the aqueducts, not for beauty, but for acoustics—a frond in the wrong place deadens a harmonic. They know the exact pitch that will coax the cobalt bell-flowers into opening, and they harvest the rigid blooms when the chord is right, grinding the petals into a metallic paste that fuels their dry metabolisms. They are eaters of secondhand thunder, consuming the sound that the plants have already digested into chemistry. The glass-grass they do not cut, for its cymbal-leaves catch the gas tides and play necessary notes in the city’s endless composition. Instead, they walk carefully between the stalks, setting their feet in the silence between clangs.

The small fauna, those armored earless scuttlers in the high cornices, are not pests to them but percussion. They leave offerings of powdered salt for the creatures, or simply permit them to exist, understanding that the patter of tiny claws along a lintel adds a needed texture to the stone’s vocabulary.

But they are so few. You might walk for an hour and meet none. You might walk for a day and meet one, standing motionless in a forum with both hands and all four of the lesser palps of their feet pressed flat to the ground, receiving the news of the city. They are not solitary by nature; they are solitary by catastrophe. Each one carries the standing waves of a thousand ancestors in its nerves, a history not memorized but resonated. They know that once, the streets thrummed with the footsteps of thousands, the stone alive with conversation. Now the city is a library with only a handful of readers.

When the Hot Phase returns and the vents bellow the white, killing chord, they retreat again into their padded chambers. There, in the dead-zones, the sound is only monstrous, only immense, only a pressure that scrapes the mind—but it is survivable. They curl their tall forms into tight, armored knots, membranes folded, palms tucked against their chests, and wait. In those hours, they are nearly blind, nearly deaf even to the stone, suspended in a thunderous dark that is the closest thing they know to sleep.

They do not dream of water. They dream of silence—not the silence of the outer void, which is death, but a silence so absolute it would be a perfect note, a single pure resonance holding steady forever. It is a dream they do not expect to realize. They are the final harmonics of a chord struck long ago, lingering in the air of a room that has forgotten the hand that struck it. They press their hands to the floor, and the floor tells them they are still here. For now, that vibration is enough.
