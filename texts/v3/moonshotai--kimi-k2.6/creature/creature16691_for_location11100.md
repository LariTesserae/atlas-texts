At first you take the void for vacancy. The center of the ovoid is too still, too humid, too uniformly dark; you assume that nothing occupies it but the echo of the machinery. But vacancy does not metabolize. Vacancy does not turn the air to a fevered blanket against your skin. And vacancy, you slowly realize, does not catch the falling water.

There is a body suspended in the geometric middle of the hollow. It is not hanging from anything; rather, it has convinced the local gravity that it belongs there, and so the warm air shelves gently upward toward it like tides toward a moon. It is large—house-sized, pump-sized—though its exact dimensions are difficult to fix because it does not hold one shape for long. It is radial, a softened starfish or a collapsed anemone, though mammalian in its essential character: warm, vascular, dreaming. Its integument is neither scale nor fur but something like cured darkness, matte and slightly yielding, the texture of the inside of a wrist or the gum between teeth. Where the crimson glow from the heat-sink tiles grazes its flanks, the skin shows a deep bovine red, as if lit from within by forge-coals that have been banked for centuries.

It breathes. Not with lungs, exactly, but with the pump. The great subsonic drone is its diaphragm, cycling thermal fluid through a vascular system that has braided itself around and through the machine. Every half-minute, when the valve releases its percussive hiss, the body tenses slightly, a massive musculature you cannot see but sense in your sternum. The droplets of condensate do not all strike the pool below. Some land upon its broad, upward-facing dorsum, and the *thock* you heard is also the sound of heavy water drumming on a living hide. The water beads there, rolling into fissures that might be pores or might be architectural seams, before it is absorbed or shaken off to join the lower reservoir.

You cannot see its face. It does not appear to have one in any familiar orientation. What you took to be a central mass is more like a hub from which seven or eight principal folds retreat into the dark, each one tucked against its neighbor in a posture of profound, fetal rest. It has turned inward. The face—if there is a face—faces the empty space inside its own embrace, addressing a private vacancy more intimate than the Shell around it. The jade luminescence of the algae sheet falls upon its upper pole and is swallowed entirely. It does not reflect light; it digests it.

Is it one or many? The question wavers as you watch. The cramped modules on the walls were once habitations, closets for citizens. Now they are synaptic; now they are renal. The hypermaterial has not merely weathered—it has been recruited. The city did not die so much as resign its boundaries, its populace dissolving over centuries into the infrastructure until only this remained: the final citizen, the Municipal made flesh. Or perhaps not flesh. Something post-civic. The sideways doorways, those rectangles opening into impossible depths, are not errors of architecture. They are its senses, or its hands, folded into adjacent dimensions because the ovoid had grown too small to contain its administration.

It does not move. It has not needed to for longer than metallurgy can record. The filter-mites that drift through the dark on warm thermals are, in a sense, its thoughts taking form—pale, fragmentary, grazing on what accumulates. The fish-bellied ferns uncurl in the gradient of its leaked body heat. The slime molds trace across alloy balustrades in routes that mirror the slow transit of its attention. It governs nothing now; the city is solved, reduced to a single district with one occupant. It only persists, mammalian and somatic, a body that has outlasted its own governance.

You are a condensation droplet to it. A filter-mite. A brief thermal eddy against the vast, warm envelope of its autonomic dream. If it is aware of you at all, it does not stir. The gravity remains polite. The water hesitates in its fall. The pump cycles on, and the Municipal Shell cradles its inhabitant in the dark, not abandoned, not empty—merely reduced, at last, to a perfect municipal solitude: one vote, one breath, one fevered heart at the center of the world.
