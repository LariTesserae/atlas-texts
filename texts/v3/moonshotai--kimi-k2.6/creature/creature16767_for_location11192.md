At first you think the city is empty. You stand in the corridor, six hundred miles of packed clay rising to a slot of white sky, and you hear only the granular hush, the breath of the chimneys. But emptiness here is a failure of scale. You are looking for something your own size. Lower your eye to the wall. Wait for the shadows to stop moving. There: a grain of polished obsidian has just shifted. It shifts again. It is a mandible.

They are small. That is the first true thing about them. A single body weighs less than a breath of the hot air it displaces. If you cupped your hands around one, you would feel only the faint vibration of its metabolism, a sealed chemistry hoarding water in waxy layers beneath its armor. They are not designed to impress you. They are designed to press shoulder-to-shoulder, to convert appetite into geometry, and to die before their cuticles dry out. They have no name in any language you speak. Call them the Occurrence, or the Process, or simply the Mouths. They do not call themselves anything. They have no ears for it.

Watch now as the surface of the wall begins to seethe. The heat has reached the threshold, and the afternoon shift is emerging. They do not pour forth in a flood—that would be wasteful, chaotic, loud. Instead they extrude. Pores in the clay, each no wider than a needle’s eye, dilate, and the bodies ease out into the corridor with the slow inevitability of sap rising. They are pale where the masonry has shaded them, amber where the galleries run close to the surface, and dark as scorched horn where they work the outer chimneys. Their segmentation is subtle, almost blurred, as if they were halfway dissolved into the material they inhabit. Their legs are not many-jointed instruments but short, strong spurs designed to grip slurry. Their antennae are long, thread-thin, trembling with a constant exchange of touch; in the acoustic tomb of the thicketworks, the walls themselves are the language, and every vibration is a word.

They are not all the same. To treat them as identical would be like treating a city’s inhabitants as one soul replicated. There are those whose heads are dominated by black, forward-jutting mandibles sharp enough to chip fresh scree. These are the Gnawers, though they do not know the title. They move along the base of the newest mineral flutes, cropping the stone where it elbows through the floor. They do not eat in the way you understand. They take the geology into themselves—alkali dust, silica, the young salts of the basin—and their gut is a kiln where stone is unraveled into paste. What they excrete is not waste but mortar, a mineral slurry bound in mucilage, which they daub onto the growing wall with the precise, mindless devotion of calligraphy. They are the oldest caste, or perhaps the original one; everything else is an elaboration of the mouth.

Then come the Carriers. Their abdomens are distended, not with eggs but with water—not liquid exactly, but a humid gel pressed from the succulents that drape between the spires like chandeliers of living tissue. They move in files along the high avenues, their legs finding purchase on paths grooved by a thousand generations of identical steps. Where a Carrier passes, the masonry is left damp for a moment, and rootlets follow hungrily, knitting the new layer into the old. They are the bonds. Without them, the walls would be mere dried mud; with them, the architecture is a body, flexing, healing, growing callus over wound.

There are the Gardeners, smaller, softer, their mouthparts adapted not for stone but for the tender meristems of lithops and aloe. They tend the hanging gardens in the interstices, pruning, pollinating with a dust of body-borne spores, harvesting the swollen leaves not with violence but with surgical tenderness. The vegetation is their livestock and their reservoir. Every fleshy leaf is a cistern they have calculated into the municipal budget of moisture. When a lithops splits and spills its stored chemistry, the Gardeners are there to sponge it up, to carry it in their own bodies until the next layer of masonry is ready to be fed.

And there are the Coolers. You will not see them unless the heat drives you into the deep warrens where the warmth pools. They cling to the inner surfaces of the ventilation shafts, their bodies fringed with feathery lamellae that evaporate what little water they can spare. By beating these fringes in unbroken rhythm—thousands upon thousands of them stacked in the hollow walls—they generate the capillary breath that draws cool air through the base-vents and sends it sighing up toward the chimney-stacks. They are the city’s lungs, individualized, expendable, replacing themselves so seamlessly that the respiration has not paused in millennia. They die of desiccation with their lamellae still fluttering, and their bodies are not mourned but immediately groomed for their last drops of fluid and pasted into the wall, where they become part of the thermal mass they once cooled.

You will look for a queen. You will look for a center. There is none, or there are ten thousand, depending on how you define sovereignty. Reproduction here is not a throne but a weather pattern. In certain districts, in the deep halls where the stone is newest and smells of sulfur, you may find brood matrices—vast combs of waxy cells the color of old teeth, each cradling a larva pale as milk-glass. But the larvae are tended by any mouth that passes, fed on regurgitated stone-paste and the ground pulp of succulent leaves. There is no single mother. The city itself gestates. When a district grows too dense, a column of winged reproductives rises on the hot afternoon drafts, climbing the chimney-stacks toward those soft, dry thunderheads where the static gathers. They do not mate in a nuptial flight of passion but in a diffused cloud, an exchange of genetic material so broad and statistical that the swarm itself is the parent. They are rain that never falls, settling in new crevices miles away to begin again the process of chewing, carrying, cooling.

They are not alone in the structure, though they are its authors. The geckos you sensed, brick-colored and motionless, are their sanitation crews, licking the mineral salts that bloom where the Coolers have worked too hard. The serpents in the hollow walls are their cables of flesh, threading through the ducts in search of the sick and the dried-out, recycling failure back into motion. The membraned hunters drifting between the spires are the pressure valve, trimming the excess of Gardeners and Carriers that accrue in times of unusual plenty. They do not hunt with cry or claw but by hanging in the thermal shadows, waiting for a single misstep on a high ledge. The death is silent; the body is gathered before it cools. Nothing may stockpile, not even corpses.

What the builders have made, more than towers or avenues, is a metabolism without a skin. They are the membrane that separates the raw planet from the work of time. Before the wind can smooth a mineral flute, their mandibles have already tasted it, judged it, bound it into the conversation of the wall. Before a drop of hypothetical rain could strike the ground, a thousand succulent cisterns have already calculated the phantom moisture and adjusted their pores. They are the reason the geology remains embryonic, because they do not allow the world to finish being born. Every spire is a refusal of entropy, a hive-mind argument against erosion made in the medium of chitin and phlegm.

Here is how to understand them: stand in the corridor at dusk, when the light cuts hard and horizontal through the slots above, and place your hand on the wall. It is warm, yes, but it is not the warmth of passive stone. It is the warmth of digestion. The wall is digesting the planet, and the city is digesting itself, and the small dark shapes moving beneath your palm—they are not inhabitants of the place. They *are* the place, in the same way a body is not a house for cells but the emergent shape of their hunger. They have no art, no history, no ritual beyond the endless grooming of stone into clay and clay into chamber. They do not dream of a finished city. To finish would be to die, to become a ruin, and ruins require a stillness that their biology cannot permit.

If you lay down here, if you let your own water seep into the rammed earth, they would come to you. Not with malice. Not with curiosity, exactly. They would come as the tide comes, as calcification comes—to assess, to taste, to determine if you could be made useful. Your bones would be too large for their mouths, but they would wait. In enough time, with enough heat, and enough small mouths working in the granular hush, even you would become part of the architecture. You would become corridor. You would become rumor, from another kind of world, finally and completely at home.
