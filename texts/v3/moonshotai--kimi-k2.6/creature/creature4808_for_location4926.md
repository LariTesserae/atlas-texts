The first inhabitants are the film. Not on the surfaces, but of them—a continuous integument of purple and rust-orange that blurs the boundary between living chemistry and oxidized alloy. To run a hand along a warm pipe is to touch a skin. These bacteria do not colonize; they have acceded. They are the city’s replacement dermis, sensitive to pressure, to pH, to the tremor of approaching feet through the polymercrete. In aggregate, they constitute something like a nervous system, slow and anaerobic, transmitting information not as electricity but as gradients of sulfur and ferrous iron. They remember the last time a valve was opened by the chemical scar it left in their growth patterns. They are the foundational citizenship, the original quorum.

Above them, the isopods. It is tempting to call them a population, but they are better understood as a medium, as ubiquitous and motive as the aqueous air itself. A single isopod is a triviality—a slate-colored comma, a segmented droplet of chitin. But they do not live as single things. In the caldera’s arithmetic, they exist in plurals so vast that the eye gives up and registers them as texture, as weather. When the bronze twilight deepens and the surface temperatures dip toward the merely unbearable, they move in strata. The uppermost layer skims across the plaza in a living scree, grazing on bacterial varnish and airborne precipitates. Beneath them, a second layer flows through the expansion joints, the curb-gutters, the hairline fractures in the roadway, excavating the city with mouths too small to be individually audible, producing in concert a sound like seeds poured across velvet. They are the city’s digestion, its peristalsis, its endless tidying. They do not sleep. They modulate their activity to the pulse of the geothermal bores, and in this way they keep time for a place where clocks have long since corroded.

But there is another presence, one the original municipal plan did not accommodate and the current ecology barely reveals. They live in the old residential blocks—those thick-walled cubes of pumice-concrete that sag at the corners but refuse to fall. They are not numerous. Perhaps a dozen. Perhaps, in some seasons, fewer. The density of their being is so slight that to spot one is to experience a kind of vertigo, as if the architecture itself had briefly assumed a posture of attention.

They are human. Or they began that way. Generations of gestation in the wet heat have refined them into something the caldera finds more acceptable. They are small. Not merely thin, though they are that—low metabolic rates suit an environment where thermoregulation is a losing battle—but compact, with a stoop that brings their heads closer to the cooler breath near the ground. Their skin is the color of the brine that condenses on every surface: a dark, iridescent umber, varnished with mineral salts so that in certain rifts of steam-light they seem to be cast from the same oxidized alloy as the pipes. The bacteria have colonized them too, not as infection but as mantle. In the crooks of their elbows, along the collarbones, the thermophilic film blooms in miniature gardens, thriving on the sulfur in their sweat. They do not wash. To wash would be to strip away a protective layer, to become naked in a place that permits no nakedness.

They do not speak. The air is too heavy for speech; consonants die in the humidity, and vowels expend too much precious moisture. Their language has migrated downward, into the body. They communicate by pressure—the laying on of hands, the transfer of heat from palm to shoulder. They leave messages in the isopod carpet, arranging the crustaceans with gentle brushes of their fingertips into patterns that persist for an hour before the tide erases them. They read the steam. A venting from the eastern stacks means one thing in the morning, another at dusk, and they understand the difference as a farmer understands rain. They have become, in their silence, something almost mineral. They endure. They attend.

What they attend to is the machinery. The generation stations still hum at fifty hertz because these few keep them fed, keep the bores from choking on their own pressure, keep the relief valves from failing entirely. It is not industry that motivates them. The original purpose of the refinery is as lost to them as it is to the rust. They maintain the city because the city maintains the heat, and the heat maintains the life. They are not workers; they are physicians, or perhaps priests, tending to the metabolism of a patient that will never recover and never quite expire. They move through the catwalks with the certainty of blood moving through a vein, knowing which valves to ease open by a quarter turn, which fractures to let weep, which cooling galleries must never be allowed to fall below forty degrees lest the bacterial cathedras collapse.

They eat the city. Not metaphorically. They harvest the bacterial mats from the digesters with ceramic scrapers, pressing the purple slurry into cakes that they dry on the warm flanks of inactive pumps. They collect the blackened snails from the hottest pipes, cracking the iron-impregnated shells with practiced taps. They drink the condensate that weeps from the underside of the pipe bridges, cupping it in their hands, filtering it through mesh woven from shed isopod exoskeletons and polymer thread. Their diet is pure mineral and protein, a chemistry so stripped of plant matter that their biology seems a parallel invention, converging on life by a separate theorem.

They breed, but rarely. A child born here arrives into a world of absolute humidity and twilight, and the first breath is a lungful of lithium brine and sulfur. The infants are smaller than surface norms, with large eyes calibrated not for bright light but for the subtle thermal shadings in the fog. They do not cry. Crying wastes water. They are preternaturally still, learning before they walk that motion generates heat, and excess heat is a debt the body pays in sweat and exhaustion. By the age of six they can read the isopod flows, predicting where the carpet will break around a cooling vent or pool in a warm depression. By twelve they are entrusted with a route—a gallery, a stack, a stretch of pipeline—and they walk it daily, checking with their palms for temperature anomalies, for the wrong kind of vibration, for the silence that means a bore has choked.

They wear little: strips of synthetic weave that do not rot, or nothing at all, their skin having become as adequate a garment as anything woven. When the rare visitor comes—and visitors are rare, though the residents do not think of themselves as remote, only as central to a geography others have forgotten—they do not hide. They stand in the transit plazas or on the catwalks, motionless, letting the steam wrap them. They know that to a stranger’s eye they are indistinguishable from the infrastructure, from the rust, from the negative space. They know they are landmarks.

And they die. The heat is survivable, but it is not merciful. Bodies fail slowly, kidneys crusting with mineral deposits, lungs thickening from decades of aqueous air. The dead are not buried—the ground is too hard, too hot, too full of conduit. Instead they are laid in the warmest seep-fields, where the bacterial film rises up in a sudden, exuberant bloom, and the isopods gather in their billions to perform the final conversion. Nothing is wasted. The calcium goes to the snails. The iron to the brine. The body, in a matter of weeks, becomes part of the municipal hum, another frequency added to the fifty-hertz thrum.

They have no name for themselves. They have no need. In a place where water cannot be owned and heat is the only true topography, identity dissolves into function. They are the ones who turn the valves. The ones who drink the condensate. The ones who, when the sodium arcs gutter to life at dusk, can be seen standing at the edges of the plazas, watching the dark plural mass of the isopods shift and settle, keeping the covenant, ensuring that the hot, wet exhalation continues. They are the city’s final layer, slower than the bacteria, quieter than the crustaceans, more permanent than the machines. They have finished becoming themselves.
