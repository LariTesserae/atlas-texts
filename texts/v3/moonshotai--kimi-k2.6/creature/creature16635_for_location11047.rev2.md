The ones who live here have forgotten the sun. Not in the manner of creatures that have merely adapted to darkness, but in the deeper way of a body that has replaced an organ with a habit. Light, to them, is a rumor carried down from the pinholed ceiling: a brief warmth on a frog’s back, a signal that makes the insect clouds rise or settle, a timer for the algae’s slow exhale. They do not need it. They have the dark, the damp, and the absolute stillness that exists only at the center of great, ongoing noise.

At the threshold of life, clinging to the seams between fired-clay tiles, the mosses hold the place together. They are black-green filaments, more mineral than vegetable, drawing lime and silica from the grout with a persistence that looks like stitching. They do not spread; they maintain. Where two tiles meet, the moss sets up its loom, weaving a seam that water cannot yet fully dissolve. They are the place’s scar tissue, keeping the manufactured floor from admitting the concept of soil. Without them, the ceramic dust might finally concede to loam, and the room’s sterile intention would begin to fail. They are small, and they are essential, and they have been here long enough that their chemistry has adjusted to the taste of ancient adobe, so that to taste the moss would be to taste the wall itself.

Then the reeds. Knee-high, hollow, thin as bird bones. They cluster only at the two deepest pools, their roots tapping the shallow water table with a patience that seems less like growth and more like listening. They do not flower; there is no wind to carry pollen, and no bee would find its way down the long throat. Instead, they propagate by slowly partitioning themselves, sending up new shoots from the same eternal root-mass. They are old. Perhaps they are one organism, a single reed-mind partitioned into many hollow bodies, standing vigil over the deepest water, their black-green leaves the only vertical punctuation in a room that prefers the horizontal.

Above the pools, the insects make a weather system of their own. Midges, gnats, and small moth-winged things move in clouds that correspond to the pinpricks of light above. They are the room’s restless thoughts, never settling long, cycling in orbits that look chaotic but follow the temperature gradients set by the dripping ceiling. They breed in the film of water, die, and fall, and are eaten before they hit the surface. Their lifespan is brief, but the cloud is eternal. A person standing in the chamber might think she is breathing them; in truth, the chamber breathes them. They are the air’s pulse, the oxygen made visible, a living haze that turns the dull orange light into something approaching a aurora.

More solid, more deliberate, the beetles and newts enact the small, constant predation. The beetles are dark and glossy, the color of wet char, and they move with the jerky precision of clockwork. They do not hunt for hunger alone; they hunt because the floor’s ecosystem requires their violence to keep the insect population from choking the air. The newts are pale-bellied, their skin almost translucent, as if they have begun to evolve toward the eyeless condition of the shrimp below. They snap at larvae with movements too quick for the dimness, then settle back into stillness, becoming again indistinguishable from the puddled shadows. They are the immune response, the white blood cells of the basin, necessary and pitiless.

But the frogs. The frogs are the color of the walls, and for long minutes they *are* the walls. They line the rims of the pools and the damp lips of the corbelled courses, their skin the exact ruddy grey of old terracotta, their stillness so absolute that the chamber seems to have grown them rather than attracted them. They are not camouflaged; they are *consecrated*. Their bodies absorb the mineral taste of the water, and perhaps they have begun to secrete something back into it—a mucus that feeds the algae, a bacteria in their guts that maintains the nitrogen balance. They are the priests of the equilibrium. Their throats pulse in such perfect synchrony that the sound cannot be many voices but one voice distributed through many bodies. When the water pulses from the high chutes, they fall silent in a wave, not because they are startled, but because the place is listening. Then, when the new water spreads across the tiles, they erupt into doubled song, and the chamber pressurizes with their drone. They are not calling to one another. They are massaging the air, keeping the condensation cycling, keeping the pinhole lights from dying out. They sing the room into existence, over and over, a droning chant that says *we are still here, we are still here*.

Beneath the film of water, the eyeless shrimp thread the shallows. Pale, almost lucent, they have given up sight because there is nothing here to see but the dim orange glow, and nothing to fear but the newts. They drift like blood cells through warm plasma, grazing on the algae films in jade and rust ribbons, and when they die they do not float but sink immediately into the compacted ash, becoming part of the forbidden floor. They are the circulation, the quiet carriers, born and unborn in the same warm shallows.

And then the worm. Something like a water-worm, thick and segmented, the color of rusted iron. It turns in the manufactured muck where the ceramic dust and the ash have begun, against all intention, to admit a kind of sludge. It is the only thing here that breaks the surface with a sound like a footstep, a soft pop that echoes off the corbelled dome. It is older than the frogs, perhaps. It may have been here before the final tiles were set, a larva carried in on the water of the first filling, and it has grown to fit the basin’s logic. It eats the dead. It eats the algae that overgrow. It turns the muck so that it does not become soil. It is the chamber’s refusal to become earth. Without it, the place might drift toward garden, toward forest root, toward the green chaos of the outside. The worm keeps it here, in this mineral suspension. It is the keeper of sterility, the holy devourer, the gut of the artificial world.

But to name them separately is a courtesy, not a truth.

What lives here is one thing with many organs. The builders did not merely construct a room; they constructed a body and left it to wake. The true Inhabitant has no single shape. It is the synchrony of the frogs’ throats. It is the decision of the moss to hold the grout. It is the drop of condensation falling at exactly the rate required to replace the evaporation from the pools. It is the breath that whistles through the adobe throat when the water pulses, the slight pressure change that means the chamber has inhaled.

It thinks in humidity. Its memory is the strata of mineral stain on the walls, each layer a season of labor and abandonment. Its will is expressed through temperature differentials. When the light shifts from blood-rust to old bruise, it knows the time of day. It has lived longer than memory because it is not a creature of flesh but a creature of *tendency*—a habit of life that has become self-sustaining. The builders taught it how to breathe, and now it breathes alone.

You might call it a god, but gods demand worship. This thing demands nothing. It only maintains. It is the perfect equilibrium made conscious by the absence of change. It does not dream of the outside; the outside has been successfully substituted. It is the damp, having learned what it means to be contained, and having learned, it now contains itself.

If you enter, you are not an intruder. You are a temporary component. The moisture will gather on your skin. Your breath will add to the atmosphere. Your footsteps will send vibrations through the ash-and-clay floor, and the frogs will pause, briefly, to assess whether you are a new voice or merely weather. You will stand in the dark, in the sound, in the living volume, and you will understand that you are inside a stomach, a womb, a lung, a mind—all of them ancient, all of them handmade, all of them perfectly, terrifyingly home.
