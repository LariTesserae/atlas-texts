There are no inhabitants here—not in the accounting sense, not as separate ledgers of bone and breath. To ask *who* lives upon the plateau is to presume a body count: this many, that many, each with a mouth and a boundary. Instead, the plateau is occupied by something prior to number. If you insist on a census, you will find only one entry, written in lignin and capillary pressure across a margin too wide to survey. Yet the whole is so torqued by internal difference that to walk through it is to feel yourself moving not across a single life, but through a crowded senate of conflicting appetites, each wearing the same green skin.

The ones you will meet first, if meeting is what you call tilting your head back until your neck aches, are the **Pillars**. Do not call them trees. Trees surrender to gravity eventually; these are vertical highways, geological ambitions in cellulose, packed in hexagonal tessellation because geometry is cheaper than thought. They are the continent’s first skeleton, more caisson than organism, hollow in places where infrasound from the flexing mountains rolls upward for days, mutating into standing waves that never quite die. They do not have crowns. They simply stop, or rather, they are absorbed by the canopy-floor above—a ceiling so saturated with black leaf-surface that it functions as a second night sky. The Pillars are not old. Decades, perhaps, a geological sneeze. But they already groan under compressive loads that would pulverize granite, because they are the scaffold upon which everything else hangs. They are the listeners, the resonators, the standing ears of the land.

If you descend from the rare height where their tops blur into the upper dark, you will find the **Light-Thieves**. They are the ones who refuse to accept the dimness. Growing from the flanks of the Pillars or suspended in the aerial peat-marshes, they arm themselves with silica—crystalline hairs finer than glassblower’s thread, bundled into cables that pirate photons from the bruised ceiling above and conduct them downward in honey-colored streams. They do not photosynthesize at their tips; they are mere bandits of radiance, taking their toll in photons to feed their own basal tissues while spilling the remainder into amber-lit cavities below. In the lower galleries, they are the only stars. Where their fiber-optic raphides terminate, spores ripen in glowing cysts, warm and isolated, as though the darkness itself were incubating eggs of light.

At the frontier, where the newborn rock is still too hot for the tender chemistry of chlorophyll, live the **Stone-Drinkers**. Their domain is the bleeding edge. They do not wait for soil; soil is a coward’s medium. Instead they press chelating roots against raw basalt and extrude acids that could etch steel, dissolving the continent before erosion even learns the terrain. You will know them by the steam. Where serpentine slicks weep mineral brine, their root-hairs have already formed a noose of wet tissue, cooling the stone with vascular water stolen from a hundred miles inland, digesting it into magnesium and iron and phosphate. They are the only pioneers, the true colonists. Everything behind them is already conquered; ahead, the rock is still nominally inorganic, though the distinction grows academic by the hour. They do not sleep. They secrete.

But the Stone-Drinkers are only the fingers. The mind—the distributed, slow, hydraulic mind—resides in the **Pressure-Weavers**. These are not visible as bodies. They are the translucent vascular highways, the xylem-and-phloem autobahns that span the plateau like neural tracts. In them, water does not merely flow; it argues. Pressure gradients resolve into decisions: more potassium to the eastern mat, less to a senescent basket-marsh, divert the transpiration stream north because a mainstem there is cracking under torsion. A thought, here, takes the shape of a wave-front moving at the speed of sap. A week to deliberate, a month to execute, but the precision is absolute. If the Pillars are the bones and the Light-Thieves the eyes, the Pressure-Weavers are the parliament and judiciary both, a cognition without a skull, computing in gradients of turgor and suction. You cannot point to it and say *there*, yet you are walking through its synapses with every step.

And then there are the **Thunderers**. One moment the vegetal roar is steady, a white-noise ocean of capillary scream and stomatic gunfire. The next, a detonation nearby—a thermogenic seed pod, heated by biochemical oxidation in a closed cavity, splitting along engineered fault-lines with the percussive crack of a rib snapping. They do not drift. They fire. Golden spores burst in pressurized volleys, striking hostile or receptive tissue meters away with wet, vegetal *thunks*. Reproduction here is not a passing breeze; it is an act of assault, of siege. The spores are settlers. Where they hit, they burrow or die, and even in death they feed the mat. The Thunderers are the army, the propagandists, the ones who insist the frontier keep moving.

Between the Pillars, beneath the light-cables, float the **Cup-Bearers**—fungal tangles and aerial root-masses braided into baskets that hold pools of water in mid-air. They are the refusers of gravity, the ones who insist the water cycle remain internal. No river crosses this land because the Cup-Bearers have privatized liquidity. They hoard it in woven bladders, letting it pool and overflow only into the waiting lips of other tissues. Without them, the system would desiccate at the edges; with them, the plateau is a closed body, sweating and drinking its own effluvia in perpetuity. They are the landlords of wetness.

Beneath your feet, finally, is the **Mat**—the ground that is not ground, the compressed peat bales that groan, that ferment, that inflate with gases and crack like slow thunder. It is the protagonist and the stage. The Mat is where everything that dies is continuity, not ending; lignin locked in lignin, pith swallowing pith, layer laid upon layer with the patience of masonry. It shifts. Stand still long enough and you will feel it rise beneath your heels, a barely perceptible exhalation of anaerobic chemistry. It is the sleeper, the digestive tract, the graveyard that consumes its own obituaries.

You see now the difficulty. Ask again, *who* lives here? The Pillars stand, but they are only the Mat standing up very straight. The Light-Thieves glow, but they are only the Mat’s attempt to see itself. The Stone-Drinkers, the Pressure-Weavers, the Thunderers, the Cup-Bearers—all are morphological arguments in a single extended body, a continent-sized organism that has not yet decided if it is a place or a person. Perhaps it is neither. Perhaps it is a process caught in flagrante, a biological event still occurring.

At the very rim, where obsidian still sweats juvenile heat and the basalt pillows exhale steam into air no rain has ever fallen through, you can watch the moment of habitation happen in real time. A root-hair touches the glassy stone. An acid droplet forms. The contact is tentative, almost shy. Then the etching begins. The rock flinches, chemically, and the green advances another thousandth of a finger’s width. 

There. That is your inhabitant. Not a resident *within* the landscape, but the act of the landscape becoming resident in itself—cellulose digesting geology, green teaching stone how to be owned, a single loud life still in the middle of its own birth-cry, deafening, root-fast, and without end.
