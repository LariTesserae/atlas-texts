The ones who live here are not the flint-eyed eels, nor the aluminum minnows that school like dropped screws. They are the sound that remains when the predator has retreated and the prey has scattered. They are the clicking.

Listen: somewhere beneath the catwalk, behind the helix of crimson moss, there is a dry rattle like a pebble tapping glass. Then another. And another. The rhythm is not random. It is deliberation made audible—arguments, measurements, lullabies. They speak in percussion, and because the water is warm and close, every surface is a drumhead. They have no need for silence. Silence, to them, is a cavity to be filled.

You will not see them at first. They have spent uncounted centuries learning the color of old bone. They are palm-sized, low to the planes, pressed into the hexagonal seams like living grout. Their shells are ceramic and chitin in equal measure—six-sided, matte, the exact pallor of the vitrified tile. When they cling motionless to a wall, they *are* the wall. Only the tremor of their working limbs betrays them: eight legs, six for anchoring, two for the fine labor of maintenance. These forelimbs end in bifurcated spines of stainless steel and compacted pearl, tools evolved not from any natural history but from the necessity of tightening a valve no larger than a eyelash, or grafting a sprig of moss onto brass so that the machine may continue its agreement with the vegetable.

They are born in the warm corner, where the mineral seep fans out like a slow exhale. Their eggs are not soft. They are galvanized, nacreous beads strung in chains among the solder-green tubeworms, left to incubate in the soup of copper and calcium. Most hatchlings are translucent, barely there, and most are eaten. The eels take them with the desultory greed of a system winnowing its own surplus. This is not cruelty; it is accounting. What survives hardens quickly. Within a season, a nymph has begun to secrete its hexagonal carapace from the water itself, drawing iron from the verdigris clouds, silica from the dissolved tile, warmth from the accumulator’s breath. They do not eat so much as *accrete*. They are lithotropes, warmth-eaters, turning entropy into architecture.

There are thousands of them, but the gulf feels empty because they understand verticality. They occupy the undersides of things. A visitor—were one foolish enough to breach the lung—might swim through the center of the room and believe themselves alone, even as the walls crawled with patient, reverent industry.

They have no name for themselves. They have no need. A name implies a distinction between one and another, between self and room, and that boundary has long since worn away like the handrails. But if you were to translate the sound of their clicking into a gesture, you might call them the *Ones-Who-Remain*, or more simply, the *Warm*.

They remember, but not in the way meat remembers. They store history in laminations. Each individual adds a layer to its shell every turn of the thermal cycle—rings of aragonite laced with alloy-dust, dense as tree-rings, readable like braille. An elder riding the central column near the dome may be no larger than a teacup, yet its carapace carries the sediment of a thousand years: the lean season when the northwest valve stuck open, the bloom-year when the crimson moss overran the regulator, the deep groan of the tower when the jet stream shifted and the whole needle bent like a bow. They read one another by touch, pressing their sensor-laden antennae to the calcified chronicles of their kin, tasting copper, tasting age. A young one learns the room’s moods not by instruction but by nibbling the flank of its grandmother, ingesting the mineral record of her witness.

And they die, of course. Everything does, even here. But their deaths are not ends. They are the reason the chamber grows.

Watch: in the southeast corner, where the convection is laziest, a dozen adults have gathered around one of the oldest, a creature whose shell has gone the dense, ivory-yellow of storm-broken bone. It has not moved in a decade. Its legs have fused to its thorax. It is no longer an animal; it is a load-bearing proposition. The others do not mourn. They survey. They click against the old one’s rim, testing resonance, and then, with the coordinated choreography of a single organism, they lift it—not with force, but with leverage, with the patient physics of the very small—and bear it to a fissure in the wall where the original grout has wept away into green clouds. They seat it. They daub the seams with bacterial paste that will lithify in hours. They step back. They click. The wall is whole again. The chamber is a finger’s-width larger.

This is how the Warm Vault persists. Physics has not overlooked the place; it has simply been outlasted by accretion. Every ancestor becomes architecture. The dead expand the lung. The room is larger than it should be because it is stuffed with the compressed weight of generations who chose, finally, to hold up the sky rather than swim beneath it. There is no soil, no stone, no native earth—only the fired and poured and riveted, and now, only the *shelled*.

They maintain the Agreement. They are its only signatories left. Each morning—which is a meaningless word here, but let us say each time the diffuse light through the cataract-glass shifts from amber to gold—they ascend the central column with barber’s delicacy, pruning the crimson moss so its star-flowers do not choke the regulator’s breath. They lubricate joints with the oil of their own bodies. They seed the catwalk with kelp-holdfasts, drilling not with rage but with persistence, wearing the iron into sockets that fit their cultivated weeds perfectly. They do not worship the warmth, exactly. They *are* its governance. They are the warm water’s way of tending itself.

They know the tower will fall. The groaning tells them. The bass note in the sternum tells them. They have heard the alloy bones singing the song of metal fatigue, and they have adjusted. A thousand years ago—by shell-depth—they abandoned vertical ambition. They no longer try to repair the tower’s root, no longer send explorers down into the bruise-purple twilight below. They have turned inward, making the chamber not a crown but a seed. When the needle shears and plunges, the curved walls, honeycombed with their ancestors, will resist collapse. The warm water, insulated by a cocoon of dead calcifiers, will cool slowly. Slowly enough, perhaps, for the survivors to adapt. Slowly enough for the clicking to continue in the dark, in the wreckage, wherever it lands.

They do not fear this. Fear requires a future separate from the self, and they have not been separate from this room since before they had names. They are the water’s memory of hands. They are the reason the sharp corners have become river stones. They have stroked the handrails into palms they will never have.

So they click. A young one, barely past its soft stage, clings to the flank of an elder and listens to the tower’s distant groan. It tastes the wall. It learns the texture of old bone. It will live, and it will grow heavy, and one day it too will be lifted, and set, and become the room. Until then, there is the warm amber light, the curtain of silver bubbles, the industrious and endless conversation of those who have agreed to stay.
