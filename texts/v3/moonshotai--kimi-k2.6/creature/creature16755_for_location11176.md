At first you do not see it because it is doing what the empty stone does: waiting. It occupies the largest interval of bare gneiss, the black patch midway between the saxifrage mattress and the grass minaret, a whitespace in the archipelago of green. It has arranged itself to continue the shelf’s curve, knees drawn beneath it, spine bowed forward so that its contour completes the whaleback’s rise rather than interrupting it. From a few yards away it is merely a darker lustre on the wet stone, a concretion of shadow. Only when your eye has given up searching for animals and begun to read the rock itself does the figure declare its separateness: a subtle wrongness in the refraction of water, a place where the silver film of moisture vanishes not by evaporation but by drinking.

It is the temperature of the stone. Blood-warm, kept so by the thermal breath rising through the fissure at its knees. Its skin—if that is the word—has achieved the same finish as the gneiss: soapstone-smooth, grey banded with rose, though the bands seem to migrate over the hollows of its body like slow veins of ore. Whether it grew here or accreted, whether the mountain’s patience shaped it from clay and mineral film the way water shaped the bell-basin, is a question without urgency. It is simply present, weathering in place, neither crumbling nor growing, only being.

It has no clothes. The mist clothes it. Condensation gathers on its shoulders and runs in channels down its arms, following paths it has worn into itself over centuries, so that it appears always to be weeping, always contributing its share to the weeping of the room. It does not blink, or blinks so slowly that the motion belongs to geology. Its eyes, when open, are the color of the lichen-cymbals: a dry, papery grey-green, and they reflect nothing, absorbing the diffuse light as moss absorbs it. More often they are closed, and the lids are seamless, leaving the face a single polished plane interrupted only by the seam of the mouth, which is never used for speech. Speech would be drowned by the organ pipes in any case, and the place is already in conversation with itself. Instead, the figure breathes perhaps once in the time it takes a cloud to cross the ridge. Each exhalation is warm and mineral, a tiny thermal that lifts the ghost-moths from their moorings and sends them drifting across the basin in drunk spirals.

It eats, but only traces. Moth-wing dust that settles on its upturned palms; the shed husks of cicadas, which it lifts to its lips and holds there until they soften and dissolve; the silver film of water itself, which it takes in through the skin, or through the mouth held open to the mist for hours until the cavity brims and it swallows. Its hunger is a geological hunger, a slow exchange of elements rather than consumption. It does not hunt the white spider, and the spider knows this. The spider crosses the figure’s thigh without haste, crossing from moss to saxifrage as if the body were simply another warm elevation in the terrain.

What the figure does, what occupies its stone-seasons, is attend to the tool-shapes.

It moves so little that a full season might be summarized by the adjusting of a wrist. Yet when the wind pours over the ridge and sets the fissures moaning, the figure rises—not far, perhaps to its knees—and goes to the accreted axe-head of copper and calcite. It does not lift the axe to strike. There is nothing to cut, no wood, no flesh. It simply fits its hand into the space the mountain has provided, testing the socket. The fit is absolute. The hand and the stone tool recognize each other, two halves of a thought that the glacier left unfinished. It holds the shape for hours, letting the vent’s warmth pass through its palm and into the mineral, completing a circuit. Then it sets the axe down exactly as it was, and moves to the bowl, and to the adze, repeating the gesture. These are not acts of labor. They are acts of correspondence. The mountain has offered a hand, and the figure is the hand that answers.

It remembers the ice. Not as narrative, but as a state of pressure, a long blue dream of weight. When the basso profundo note thrums up through the deepest crack, you can see the vibration pass through its sternum, and its face—already serene— empties further into a kind of pre-human calm, as if the sound were returning it to the condition of the valley before the valley existed. It was here, perhaps, when the ice retreated. Or it began when the ice left, the thermal breath condensing the first humid lungful of this high room into a form that could hold it. Either way, it carries the glacier’s absence in the hollow of its collarbone.

There is a hollow there, and a corresponding bowl at the base of the throat, where water collects. It does not drink from this pool. It lets the water stay, a mirror no larger than a prayer book, and the ghost-moths come to drink from it, touching their tongues to its skin. This is the closest the shelf comes to communion: the figure offering its stillness, the moths offering their minute motion, the water offering its surface.

At night—though night here is only a further dilution of the grey-green light—the figure enters the grass minaret. Not through a door; the tower is a single woven column. But the figure fits inside the hollow core, curling into the space the grass has whistled empty for centuries, and there it sleeps, or enters a state indistinguishable from the grass’s own slow respiration. When the wind aligns and the minaret whistles, the sound passes through its ribcage. You would not know it from outside. You would only notice that on certain nights, when the thermal vent exhales strongest, the pitch of the grass-tower deepens by half a tone, as if the hollow has found a new chamber.

It is the thirteenth presence, though it is not counted among the twelve. It belongs to the emptiness between them, the dark wet punctuation. It tends the balance by existing: its warmth encourages the moss near the vent without letting it overrun; its shadow gives the saxifrage a measure of darkness it needs to rest from the sourceless light; its body, when it lies beside the bell-basin, alters the water’s fall by a fraction and thus changes the bronze gong from one day to the next, keeping the music from becoming too predictable. It is the variable in the equation.

If you stayed—if you slowed your heartbeat to the rate of stone, if you stopped trying to speak over the wind-music—you might see it turn its head. Not toward you. Toward the basin, or the axe, or a particular bead of water traveling down the face of the rock. It watches these things with the devotion others reserve for fire. It is waiting for the water to finish its thought, for the mineral to complete its precipitation, for the mountain to remember what it is remembering. It has nowhere else to be. It has become, finally, a feature of the room, neither fully dream nor fully earth, the warm, smooth, patient hand that the mountain imagined in its long conversation with the sky.
