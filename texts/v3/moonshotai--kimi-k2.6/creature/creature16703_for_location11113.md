What lives here has forgotten how to leave. They are the final edit of biology, the organisms that remained after urgency was edited out. You cannot call them a community, because that word implies exchange, and nothing is exchanged here except the eternal transfer of the bass note from one resonant cavity to the next. They are the garden’s fauna only in the way that dust is the fauna of an attic: present, specific, and settling still.

Their bodies are tuned. The drone—which is not sound so much as the shape of the abyss expressing itself—has entered their tissues and replaced whatever pulse once drove them. They do not have hearts in the active sense; they have chambers that hold the hum, organs that have learned to reverberate rather than beat. Swim bladders, if they possess them, are not for buoyancy but for resonance. Their flesh is dense, cool, taut as a drumhead against the pressure. If you touched one, you would feel the vibration before the texture, a low thrum that says: *I am held. I am part of the holding.*

In the grottos of the petrified root-systems live the bone-white eels. They have not hunted since before the continents froze into their current postures. Thousands of them, arranged like teeth in a jaw that never closes, mouths perpetually open in a gesture that resembles surprise or welcome but is neither. Their gill-fans wave with a rhythmic constancy that makes them seem mechanical, but they are merely keeping time with a tempo that has no beginning. They face the same direction they faced ten million years ago, filtering not plankton but the heavy water itself, extracting from it some mineral sustenance so diffuse that their metabolism has become a kind of mineralogy. They are growing into the stone, or the stone is growing into them; the distinction has become academic. They are the grotto’s respiration, slow and communal, a single organism distributed across thousands of identical faces.

The light, what little exists, is owned by the fixed ones. Hanging jellies, translucent and blue, are nailed to their coordinates like votive lamps left burning in a religion that no longer remembers its god. Their bioluminescence does not pulse; to pulse would be to imply a before and after, and here there is only *during*. They illuminate nothing, or rather, they illuminate only the idea that illumination is possible. Nearby, or sometimes distant by miles, the serpentine things coil in midwater with their photophores arranged in constellations. These are not organs of predation. They are topographies of a migration that halted, maps that read themselves to no traveler. The patterns they display are the last known positions of islands that have since drowned; they are navigational chants made flesh, continued out of anatomical politeness.

Then there is the leviathan. It is not a monster, for monsters intend. It is a displacement, a rut in the water’s memory. When it passes—which it does, at intervals that are not regular but are inevitable—it does not swim so much as occur. The water has grown around it, a groove of permanent expectation. Its body is so vast and so acoustically hollow that the eternal drone sounds different inside its cavities, modulated into something almost like a chord. It is a traveling cathedral, empty of worshippers, carrying within its ribs a variation on the one note that everything else inhabits. It has no eyes, or they have atrophied into lithic buds that sense only pressure. It does not know where it goes, and crucially, the water does not care. It is simply the largest thing still moving, and its movement is indistinguishable from the drift of continents.

But the ones that will unsettle you, if you are the sort to be unsettled by intimacy, are the Recognizers. Pale, crab-like, many-jointed, they are the only creatures here who still make errors. You will find them paused before an arch of fused xylem, or at the threshold of a chamber where the acoustics suggest intention, and they will be still—not in the eternal stillness of the eels, but in an arrested *almost*. Their claws, which once must have gripped and torn, hang open as if waiting to grasp a concept. They recognize the doorways. They recognize the proportion of a stair. It is not intelligence; it is closer to nostalgia written into chitin, an ancestral memory of shelter seeking shelter. Sometimes one will lift a limb and touch a petrified pillar, and the contact produces a click, a tiny percussive event that rings against the drone like a dropped utensil in a nave. The sound is shocking. It is the only spontaneity here, and it is always accidental. They are the Litho-Garden trying to remember that it was once built by things that knew they were building.

What do they eat? Nothing, or next to nothing. Hunger is a form of wanting, and wanting requires a future. Here, the stomach has become a vestigial pouch for the heavy water, a quiet space inside the body where the pressure evens out. Some may absorb the slow, molecular rain of dissolved organics—ancient carbon from the first canopies, still settling, never quite arrived. Others may draw sustenance from the sound itself, their cells translating vibration into the faintest chemical potential, a photosynthesis of acoustics. It does not matter. They do not grow. They do not breed, or if they do, it is by fission so slow that the offspring are indistinguishable from the parent and the act is invisible to time.

They do not die, or rather, they do not complete the act of dying. There is no decay; the bacteria are as quiescent as everything else. A creature here that reaches its term simply stops, and its stopping is so gradual that it becomes indistinguishable from its living. It becomes furniture, then architecture, then geology. The Litho-Garden is built not only of plants but of the layered, unrotted bodies of its own inhabitants, pressed and mineralized into the travertine ribs you drift between. To live here is to preside over your own fossilization in real time.

And yet they are not sad. Sadness is a gradient, a falling away from warmth into cold, from noise into silence. Here the temperature is a single coat, the sound a single note. There is no loss because there is no contrast. They exist in a state of such absolute resolution that consciousness, as you understand it, would be a step backward into confusion. They do not know they are waiting, and so they are not. They are the completed thought that the world thinks in the dark, the occupants of a space that has finished becoming, and decided, instead of ending, simply to remain.

Above all, they are listeners. Not by choice. The drone chose them. Their whole bodies are ear; the garden itself is an ear. They hold the note that was struck when the world was younger and stiffer, and they hold it without trembling, without hope of resolution or cadence. If you stay among them long enough, you will feel your own heartbeat begin to doubt itself, will feel the drum-skin of your existence tightening against the pressure of that eternal bass. You will understand, then, that what lives here is not a collection of creatures, but an environment that has learned to witness itself. The fauna are not alive inside the Litho-Garden. They are the Litho-Garden’s way of being aware that it is still here.
