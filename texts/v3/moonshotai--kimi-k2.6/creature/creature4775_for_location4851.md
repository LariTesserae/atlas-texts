At first, it seems the only residents are the vanishingly small: the pale moth upon the heater housing, and the translucent colony grazing somewhere in the hydroponic underlayer. But the moth is not resting. It has become a fixture, a sensory organ left behind by something larger, wing-folds the color of fog receiving transmissions in the flat, unchanging gold. Its stillness is a vocation. It sees nothing because it has delegated sight to the orchids. Below, the snails write the only scripture this place permits: pale, glistening tracks on the glass pipe-walls, a grammar of algae and propulsion. Their trails are the sole animal graffiti, yet they are not authors. They are punctuation in a sentence being composed by slower, denser hands.

The first true populace is the green. It lives here not as decoration but as a distributed sovereign. The ferns erupting from the seams of fresh slate are not colonists; they are fingers, uncurling with intention toward the warmth. The orchids rooting in porous ceramic are not feeding—they are attending, each bloom an aperture turned inward toward the chamber’s sultry held breath, drinking mineral effluvia and expired air. The vines as thick as an arm have not invaded the pistons; they have assimilated them, threading a vegetative awareness through brass and rubber and ceramic stem. This dense, claustrophobic, voluptuous growth constitutes a single body, damp and many-headed, thinking in chlorophyll and hydraulic pressure. It exhales the saturated haze that blurs all edges; it inhales the mist sprayed from the nozzles; it transpires, and its sweat becomes the condensation that weeps down virgin rock-faces. It is a mind without a skull, old as forests yet infantile here, learning the machinery as a second skeleton.

But even this presiding intelligence is not the chamber’s final inhabitant. It is, rather, the bedding, the nest of an arrival still in progress.

The ones who truly live here are still being born. They have no name, because nothing in this place has existed long enough to need one. They rise where the water gathers, standing waist-deep in the warm pools that did not exist last month. They are the children of convergence, extruded not from womb or egg but from the interface itself. Where the infantile stone pushes upward with soft insistence and the fresh mica-flecked granite meets the artificial loam, there they accrete. Their forms are provisional, because the distinction between floor and wall is provisional. You might take them for statues half-emerged from the substrate, if statues breathed mineral warmth and shifted when the flywheels turned.

Their bones are the sharp, unrounded granite, still off-gassing a chemical warmth, still learning their own mass. Their musculature is damp peat packed into a lattice of woven root and conveyor belt. Their blood is the slurry that moves through translucent hoses rooting into their wrists; their skin is a living culture of moss and epiphyte, orchids blooming directly from shoulder blades, questing downward into the nutrient effluvia of industrial metabolism. They do not have faces in the mammalian sense. They do not need them. An orchid serves as well as an eye, swiveling to track the fog-colored moth. A catchment of condensation in the hollow of a cheek serves as well as a tear, and when it finally runs, it traces a channel through the lime that is still curing.

They move at the speed of genesis. When the peristaltic pipe buried beneath the nascent bedrock gives its slow, rhythmic gulp, one of them lifts a knee—basalt grinding softly in the joint—and steps across an arterial channel of weeping water. The step takes seven minutes. The water parts around a foot that is part root-hair and part pressure valve. They do not walk because they choose to; they walk because the chamber is turning, and they are its inner lining, the motile layer of a world forever arriving at itself. The pneumatic roots that pulse once per minute are not engines to them; they are heartbeats, and their own pulse matches the viscous slowness of tidal action.

They are not many, and they are not one. They are a repetition of residence, a single nascent event manifested wherever humidity and false geology achieve sufficient density. In the northeast corner, where the granite sprouts with vegetable softness, one kneels to receive the outcrop, breastbone pressed to stone, as if the mountain were extruding not just rock but also the posture of reverence. Near the heating coils, another stands with arms open in the mineral mist, chest blooming with green, while the brass nozzles spray a breath of artificial loam across a torso that is half peat, half ceramic.

They do not speak. Language requires a past tense, and here nothing has endured long enough to possess a *before.* Instead, they commune by modulating the wetness of the air. A shift in saturation near the east wall is a greeting. The crisp *tick* of newborn basalt adjusting to its own mass is their laughter. Their memories are held in the curing of lime, the setting of slurry, the twelve-hour cycle of unremarkable light. When the flat gold dims to its false dusk, they lower themselves into the pools, becoming indistinguishable from the substrate, and dream in the register of condensation pumps: a low, subterranean sigh that runs through all of them at once.

They have never imagined the cold outside, the aeon-old mountain, the thin air that would burn a lung. They know only this: the held breath that has never been exhaled, the saturated haze that softens their edges, the quiet industry of a foundry devoted not to destruction but to the manufacture of themselves. They are the congregation and the cathedral, the worshippers and the offered clay, always just now learning what it is to be inhabited. They are unfinished, and because they are unfinished, they are eternal in the way this place is eternal: not by lasting, but by beginning again and again, sharp and raw and warm, leaving oil behind on the stone, waiting for the touch that will tell them they are real.
