What lives here does not see the valley, and so it is not alien to it. It does not know itself a visitor in a buried place, does not feel the weight of stone above as oppression, does not dream of a sky it never possessed. Instead, it knows the hollow as a single continuous surface, an interior turned outside-in, read entirely by the touch of thick, bristled appendages and the low, resonant frequencies that travel through the floor. To be blind here is to be indigenous. The light is only a rumor, a faint prickling on the silvered buttons where eyes once were; they have atrophied into ornament, into wet glass coins that reflect the bacterial glow without comprehending it. The beasts do not need them. They navigate by brushing their wide, whiskered snouts against the raw stone, and the stone speaks back in textures—here a seam of damp granite, there a lip of sharper flint, ahead a stripe of lichen blooming on a drip-line. Their world is a map of abrasions, of gradients of cold, of taste.

They are low-slung, as though gravity asked them to crawl and they agreed without argument. Their bellies ride close to the rock, not dragging but hovering, suspended on four splayed pillars that end in broad, prehensile feet—living cudgels of horn and callus that spread their impossible tonnage across the sharp edges so that they do not cut themselves. Their skin is the color of wet limestone because it is saturated with the same minerals, caked in the same dust, so that from a distance they are indistinguishable from the floor itself: mobile hillocks, temporary accumulations of geology that happen to breathe. And they do breathe, slowly, in long steaming exhalations that cloud the cool air. Their chests are barrels not of speed but of storage; inside each one is a furnace of fermentation, a slow biological reaction turning the endless sift of dead leaves and root matter from above into heat and motion and the low, grinding hum that gives the valley its permanent bass note. To stand near one is to feel the sound in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. It is the noise of digestion stretched into infinity, of matter being persuaded to become flesh at the speed of stone.

Living in their folds, in the warm, humid creases behind the knees and beneath the roots of their tails, are their attendants. Small, swift lizards with skin the color of old milk and blood like cooled tin. They are not pets; they are employees too primitive to know they are employed. When the great beasts pause at a promising drip-line, the lizards dart from their pockets of living warmth to snap at the pale moths that orbit the giant’s head, drawn to the carbon dioxide of its breath or the salts that crust its lips. The lizards return, swallowing, to press their bellies against the beast’s pulse. The beetles are less excitable. Fat-bodied, armor-plated, they cling to the sparse hairs along the belly or tumble in the wake of the dung, which is not waste so much as processed geology. They work it, kneading it into the fresh cracks of the floor, inoculating the stone with the only fertility it knows. Without them, the organic sift would accumulate too fast, clogging the arithmetic. Without the beasts, the beetles would have nothing to knead. It is a closed loop, a conversation held entirely by touch and chemistry.

And then there are the moths. They are the only creatures here that are not heavy. They are pale, nearly translucent, with wings that fray rather than tear, and they are the wind, since no wind exists. They carry spores from the isolated fungal sentinels to the lichen beds, and they drink the condensation that beads on the beasts’ brows. In the absolute stillness of the air, their flight is a silent riot, a trembling against the mist that makes each giant a drifting island surrounded by a private weather.

The beasts do not gather. They do not herd. They are solitary in the way that boulders are solitary, spaced across the basin according to an equation of appetite and resource. Yet they are synchronized. The hum of their digestion, transmitted through the unweathered stone, allows each to know the position of the others as a vibration in the jaw, a ghost presence in the hollows of their own massive skulls. A map of pressure. When one halts, the others feel the shift in the valley’s timbre. When one finds a new fissure rich with sift, the low rumble of its feeding alters the local geology of sound, and the circuit gradually adjusts. They speak no language, but they are never out of contact. Their paths interweave in a pattern that is not worn by centuries but by the immediate, repeated passage of this week, this month, this identical day. A groove in the fresh rock, knee-deep, not from erosion but from persistence. They follow it not from memory but because the stone has already been tested here and found edible, and to step elsewhere would be to risk the sharp unknown.

They are born rarely, and they die rarely, and the two events are nearly the same. A mother finds a place where the ceiling bleeds the thickest rain of soil, and she lowers herself, groaning in a register that shakes the pools. The calf emerges already huge, slick with mineral broth, its snout bearing the first stiff filaments with which it will read the world. Within hours, the lizards have colonized its folds; within days, the beetles have moved from parent to child, carrying the garden with them. The young one learns the route not by instruction but by seismic inheritance, following the thrum of the adults through the floor until its whiskers recognize the exact same drag against the exact same fissure. It will grow until it occupies the precise amount of space the valley can feed. If a crack widens above, it will fatten; if a crack seals, it will diminish, its bones becoming more hollow, its steps more deliberate, until it reaches the final subtraction.

For when one dies, it does not collapse so much as settle. It lowers itself onto the stone it has known all its life, folding at the joints with a hiss of expelled air like a bellows laid flat. Then the arithmetic shifts. The attendants multiply. The beetles churn the body’s mass into the floor; the lizards bask on the cooling flanks until the flanks are stone-cold; the moths lay their eggs in the rich damp. For a brief span, measured in mere centuries, the site becomes a lush, rotting oasis, a bloom of fungi and accelerated lichen, a surplus in the closed account. But slowly, the nutrients leach. The bones bleach. The skull, broad as a cottage, grinds down under the unhurried feet of the survivors, becoming part of the path, part of the floor, part of the map that the living read with their whiskers.

They have no fear because they have no predator. They have no ambition because they have no future that is not already present. They are not the valley’s inhabitants; they are its organ, its slow metabolism made flesh. The stone is their skin; the mist is their breath; the ceaseless, patient percussion of dripping water is the pulse they match. They tread the circuit, they ferment the sift, they exhale into the waiting air, and the valley, in turn, holds them in its cool, immovable palm—complete, sufficient, and forever exact.
