They are born wet and already listening, though they have no ears to hear with. The infant emerges into a cup of waiting palms, and before the cord is severed, the mother presses the child’s hand to the floor so that the first language entering its body is not light, not image, but the eternal compressed chord of the Basin humming upward through the young stone. They do not cry. There is no acoustics for crying. Instead, the newborn’s chest hitches in silent, airless spasms, and the mother lowers her throat until it brushes the infant’s cheek, letting the muscles beneath her jaw write their first words in puffs of warm, expelled vapor against skin that has never known dryness. This is how they come into awareness: damp, cramped, held, already one body among countless others.

As they mature, their bodies resolve into shapes dictated by the absolute density of their world. They are compact, not small, but dense—thick-wristed and low-jointed, built for the friction of proximity. Their skin is a few shades deeper than the bacterial mats they farm, a soft ceramic grey stained amber and copper by the mineral oils that seep from their pores and from the sweating walls. In a place of eternal wetness, hair would be a cruelty, and so they are bald, smooth as the stone they sleep against, their scalps ridged with the faint coronal sutures of a skull that never needed to harden against weather. Where ears might once have fanned the heat from a face, there is only seamless bone, a smoothness that seems almost deliberately finished, like a pebble that has lost its roughness not by erosion but by an absence of ever having been rough. They hear, if the word can be stretched to fit, through the architecture of their entire skeleton: the hum enters at the soles of the feet, travels the long bones, pools in the hollows of the clavicles, and vibrates in the teeth.

And the teeth—they do not chew leaves or flesh so much as grind the tough, leathery bacterial biscuits and the pale chitin of the insect-analogues, and so the jaw is heavy, the molars broad and flat, but it is the throat that has become the seat of their humanity. It is long, wide, the skin over it thin and almost translucent, mapping each depressor muscle, each ripple of the larynx like currents under ice. When they speak, if speaking is what they do, two individuals press their foreheads together and interlock their fingers, and the speaker pushes air through the glottis in controlled, silent exhalations that do not shape into sound but into visible, tactile gestures of the neck. The listener reads these gusts with their own face, feeling the tiny shifts of temperature and pressure against their lips and eyelids, watching the internal choreography of muscles that once, millennia ago, might have modulated breath into words for ears that no longer exist. Their hands are their other eyes. Broad, with fingers slightly too long, the palms padded with nerve-rich tissue that darkens to a rust color from the constant abrasion of stone and the chemical kiss of sulfides. A builder’s hands are her face in the dark: they read the lineage-trails carved into corridor walls, feeling the record of who owed whom a channeling of water, whose grandmother carved the first drain into that particular warm extrusion. The fingertips trace the graffiti of obligation, tracing spirals and gouges that tell stories only in pressure and contour. To walk the city is to run one’s palms along its flanks like a blind reader consuming braille in a language of geology and debt. Their feet are nearly as articulate as their hands, the toes gripping the half-formed columns as they climb to the higher sleeping-niches, the soles registering the footfalls of a neighbor three terraces below through the continuous stone.

They do not walk through the city so much as circulate through it, shoulder sliding against shoulder in a motion that is less locomotion than peristalsis. There is no gap between bodies large enough to call personal space; the concept would be as foreign as a sky. Warmth radiates from them as it does from the walls, and a stranger’s body heat against one’s flank is indistinguishable from the ambient heat of the Basin itself. They sleep in carved niches, shoulder-width apart, curled into the fetal posture not out of protection but because the architecture demands it, fitting into the stone like pegs into a honeycomb. Children are born when a niche is emptied by death; the population is a liquid transferred from one vessel to the next, never adding volume, never spilling. They are the too-many, and they have always been exactly enough.

The dead do not leave. There is no earth to receive them, no sky to scatter them toward. Instead, the elders, when their hearts finally still against the unceasing thrum, are carried to the lowest packed passages, their bodies laid among the debris of old corridors and sealed behind fresh scrapings of soft basalt, their mineral-heavy oils leaching into the composted stone until the floor rises imperceptibly and the walls thicken. In this way, they do not so much die as are reabsorbed, becoming the medium through which the living move, their calcium and sulfur feeding the slow chemistry of the place. The city is a midden and a womb, a lithic intestine that never fully evacuates but continuously digests its own mass into new form. The builders understand this not as tragedy but as the final intimacy: to become, at last, the same temperature as the floor.

They farm, if it can be called farming, with the delicacy of sculptors. The bacterial sheets in the warm pools must be scraped at exactly the right thickness—too thin and the colony dies; too thick and it overruns the mineral balance of the water. They wade belly-deep through the warm weeping ponds, palms turned upward beneath the grey velvet skins, lifting them carefully and laying them across the flat drying stones where they cure into biscuits dense with the taste of sulfide and heat. Around them, the pale insect-analogues thread through the cracks, and the builders catch these too, crunching them for the trace fats their own bodies cannot synthesize, or else boiling them into a thick paste that smears across the throat to ease the dryness of silent speech. They tend the stone with the same care they tend the bacteria, carving drainage grooves to keep the water moving in its endless loop of arrival and evaporation, leaving living buttresses of uncarved stone to bear the weight of the next generation’s chambers.

Time, for them, does not pass; it accumulates on the skin like mineral scale. They do not measure days, for there is no dawn to divide them. There is only hunger and the easing of hunger, the softening of fresh extrusions when the heat from below surges, and the hardening when it stabilizes. They know age not by wrinkles—the wet keeps the skin plump—but by the density of their bones, which grow heavier to withstand the pressure of the sound, and by the depth of their understanding of the stone’s song. An elder can press her palm to a wall and tell, from the attenuation of the hum, whether a new bubble of magma is swelling a hundred miles below, or whether the water-table has shifted in its subterranean course. They speak less as they grow older, as if the endless internal noise eventually fills them so completely that there is no air left to push into another’s face. In their final years, they often sit motionless in the phosphorescent halos by the water’s edge, feeling the city live through them, their smooth heads tilted as if to catch a sound that has forgotten to stop.

They do not dream of outside. They do not dream of weather, of dryness, of silence. The Basin is not a prison to escape but a completeness that holds them, as the water is held, as the stone is held mid-gesture. They are the soft interval inside the geological note, the warm animal punctuation inside the sentence of the mountain. And when they press together in the steaming dark, face to face, throat to throat, palm to stone, they are only doing what the Basin has always done: touching itself, feeling its own texture, and finding, in the endless held breath, that it is enough.
