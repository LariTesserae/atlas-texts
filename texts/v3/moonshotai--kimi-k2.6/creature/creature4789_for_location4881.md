They are the chamber’s second mineralogy. Where the basalt bubble formed around a pocket of unmelted gas, life colonized the void as a denser, more intricate precipitate—think of them not as creatures invading a cave, but as a slow, hot phase-change in the rock itself, a combustion of chemistry that the planet did not plan but could not prevent. The floor—if the word still applies—is a packed metamorphic bed of their own making, three bodies deep, each form an iridescent lozenge of bronze-sulfide plates and magnetite-darkened joints. Above, the walls wear a living tessellation of hooked tarsi and rasping mouthparts. From the ceiling hang braids of them, chains woven by adhesion and pressure, shedding iron-filigree scales into the press below with each peristaltic contraction. The air is not empty; it is a thin, heated soup of nymphs, motes of metallic pollen no larger than pinheads, drifting in convection until they find a thermal seam on which to anchor and grow.

To isolate one in thought is to find a low, armored engine shaped for gradient and compression. It is about the length of a human hand, though the gravid forms swell larger, their abdomens plated with yellowed enamel of accreted sulfur. It has no eyes; evolution found no utility for photons in this sealed lithic womb. Instead, its entire dorsal surface is a mosaic of thermoreceptors, a skin that maps the infrared landscape with more fidelity than any retina could render light. The hottest hairline cracks in the walls glow to its perception as ruddy, constant suns. The ceiling is a dim, receding abyss. The floor beneath its ventral plates is a baking baseline, a plateau of radiant heat against which all other temperatures are judged. It knows its neighbors as thermal silhouettes and vibrational signatures—the particular hum of a female swollen with eggs, the grinding frequency of a large form scraping olivine from a new protrusion. Its world is sculpted entirely from temperature and resonance.

To move is to commit to a protocol measured in geological increments. A single step requires the unhooking of six tarsi, the re-arching of bronze-sulfide plates to avoid grinding against the compressed mass of siblings, and the careful re-adhesion to glass-sharp basalt. This takes a minute, sometimes two. The mandibles part, and the radula extends—a crystalline tongue, black and harder than quartz, forged in the same furnaces that birthed the mountain—to rasp a microscopic shaving from the wall. The sound is a solitary industrial note: the aerial sawing of a lathe, the hiss of a valve. Alone, it would be lost in the mountain’s basal groan. But thousands perform this ministration simultaneously, and the aggregate is the standing roar that fills the chamber like water in a locked cistern. The bass thrum from the floor suggests pistons the size of thighs. The mid-range drone is a forge that never cools. The creature does not *hear* this as noise. It is weather. It is the constant pressure of existence, as unremarkable to it as silence would be to a thing born to light.

It eats stone, and stone becomes it, and it becomes stranger stone. Thermal energy enters through the ventral plates, drawn up from the walls in a gradient that mirrors photosynthesis but scorns chlorophyll and water. The radula scrapes unweathered volcanic glass, newborn olivine, threads of native copper still cooling from the planetary melt. These pass into a gut-chamber that is less intestine than crucible, a biological blast furnace where internal temperatures climb high enough to slag the raw material into molecular order. What emerges is not soil but refinement: rods of compressed copper, pure as electrodes; blooms of crystalline sulfur, enamel-hard; delicate trusses of silver chloride that precipitate into the lower corners like powdered snow. The creature excretes these byproducts not as waste, but as the unavoidable consequence of its chemistry. And because they are legion, and because generations have occupied the exact same thermal seams for centuries, these excretions accumulate along lines of collective habit.

No intelligence designed what rises from this saturation. Yet a pipe forms where thousands have voided copper along the same seep-line, accreting a duct of pure metal that channels heat like a superconductor. A gear-like disc of intergrown chitin and magnetite thickens where a cluster has rotated in place for uncounted years, grinding a hollow into the basalt, their collective torque fossilized into a toothed wheel that turns no axle and serves no purpose beyond the record of their straining. Scaffolds of silver chloride rise in the corners, filigree vaulting without blueprint. This is the uncanny signature of the colony: architecture without architects, industry without intent, a refinery that refines only the idea of itself. The residents are simultaneously the workers, the machinery, and the ore being processed.

Time here is not measured in days but in cooling curves and magmatic twitches. The mountain outside is young, barely a millennium since its violent parturition lit the horizon for weeks, and its interior blood still shifts in sluggish tides. When the walls crystallize and the heat flux wanes, the population thins. Those in the coldest periphery enter a stasis indistinguishable from mineral death, their bronze plates dulling as internal furnaces gutter. The density loosens; the roar quiets to a murmur. But when a fresh fissure opens far below—an event measured in decades—a new red vein appears in the infrared dark, radiant and irresistible. Then the packed mass flows toward it with the viscosity of cooling tar, a slow avalanche of chitin and sulfide hungry for the renewed gradient. Reproduction surges. The gravid forms swell, abdomens distended with sulfur, and the metallic pollen of nymphs thickens the heated air until every seam is filled. The chamber inhales, populates, roars back to its full, saturated production.

Outside, the mountain remains a naked blade of geology. No rain has run its flanks to tender the stone; no root has sought purchase in its glassy talus. The wind that screams past its rim carries nothing—no spores, no grit from softer, older ranges, only the volcanic ash of neighboring newborns. It is all rim and edge, too sharp to hold snow, too young even to know erosion. It will stand for ten million years before it softens into something a human might call a hill.

But inside, in the hot, dark, saturated lung of it, the peak has already developed a hidden interior, a soft and furious organ. The residents are its metabolism, its dream of industry, a feverish conversion of stone into life and life into geometries of metal and salt. They will outlast the mountain’s youth. Long before the exterior weathers into a gentle slope, the chamber may cool, its accidental gears and copper pipes entombed in the geological record as inexplicable strata. Until then, they remain: patient, eyeless, loud, and innumerable, transforming the planet’s residual fury into the slow, hot substance of themselves.
