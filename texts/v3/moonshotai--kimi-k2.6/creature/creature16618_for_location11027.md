What lives there has no name that could be written in light. They are the ones who answered the void not by filling it, but by learning its acoustics. You will not see them; you will hear them before you understand that you are hearing something *intentional*—a click, a sustained subsonic drone, a percussive rattle of stone on ceramic wood that returns to them as shape, as distance, as hunger or safety.

They are pale as the stalks themselves, lean to the point of being architectural, their limbs elongated for wedging into vertical cracks and spanning gaps where the wind would tear a heavier body to pieces. Their eyes are vestigial, soft and lidless, covered by a translucent caul of skin; in a world where darkness is total, sight is a liability, a memory of a different physics. Instead, their flanks and throats are armored with broad tympanic membranes—thin, quivering plates that drink the chord of the place, mapping the hexagonal tubes and abyssal chambers into a topography of vibration. They strike the walls deliberately, not to wound the wood, but to interrogate it. A heel planted against the cambium sends a report through kilometers of ligneous channel, returning with news of a crack, a predator’s wing-beat, a fog-bank rising three tubes over. They are blind, and they are the only creatures who can truly *see* the size of the world.

They travel by inserting themselves. A platform of lashed fiber and sinew wedged in a vertical crack is not a dwelling in any settled sense; it is a listening post, tuned to the frequency of a particular sap-pulse. A cairn of pale chipped stone stacked on a ledge is not a monument to the dead, but a sonar beacon, its irregular scatter designed to cast an acoustic shadow that reads, in the returning click, as *passage* or *water* or *home*. They understand the regularity of the stalks, the unsettling mathematics of the hexagonal packing, not as geometry but as music—a harmony they move within, never against. They have stone adzes, yes, primitive and patient, but they do not use them to shape the plant. They rasp gently at the inner cambium to tease forth the sluggish, mineralized sap, and they stop before the wood complains. They know better than to reshape the substrate. They are guests in the emptiness it chose not to occupy.

Their bodies have become negotiations with the cold. They are nearly naked, their skin smeared with a waxy exudate harvested from the mite-grazers they tend—not domesticated, but *accompanied*, followed, encouraged to cluster where the condensation pools so they can be milked of their grease. The fog-swimmers, those undulating tadpole-things that ride the pressure fronts, are their omens; when the fog-swimmers rise, the people ascend with them, migrating toward the rim where the apertures breathe. When the cold sinks and the predators bomb through the dark after the snap of a mandible, the people descend into the deeper booming cathedrals, clinging to the wall in absolute stillness, becoming indistinguishable from the pale tissue.

They eat the predators, too, when they can, and the grazers, and the translucent jelly of the fog-swimmers, but their true sustenance is informational. They follow the plant’s adolescence like priests interpreting the tremors of a god. When a new split opens in a stalk-wall, sharp and subsonic, they are the first to sing into it, to test whether the crack is a passage or a death-trap, to claim the fresh resonance with a chord that will guide their grandchildren. Their language is not speech but interference: a greeting is a sustained hum pressed against another’s flank-membrane; a warning is a dissonance that makes the air itself shudder; a lullaby is a frequency that matches the sap-pulse, so the infant clinging to a mother’s back learns to distinguish the living thrum of the world from the silence of falling.

And because nothing decays—because the pure plant mass resists rot, because fungus finds no purchase and bacteria starve in the sterile heartwood—they have solved death by insertion. Their dead are not burned, not buried in any ground that does not exist, but lashed into stress-fractures with cords of dried fiber, pressed against the pale wall where the slow hydraulic push of growth crushes them over decades into the ligneous mass. They become the wall. They become the faint, disquieting phosphorescence at the fracture lines. A grandfather’s ribs, compressed, add a specific dampening to a turn in the tube; a grandmother’s skull, fossilized in the growth rings, changes the way a note returns. The architecture is a palimpsest of their lineage. To navigate is to remember: they press their ears to the tissue and hear the dead still humming with the sap, marking the route.

There are no chiefs, no maps scratched on bark. Their geography is a repertoire of pitches. To ask *where are we?* is to sing a note and wait for the stalk to answer in its specific overtone. Their history is stored in the hollowness of certain chambers, where the geometry perfectly preserves a funeral dirge from centuries past, the sound still cycling in the upper reaches, never fading in the sterile cold. They return to these places to sit on ledges, wrapped in fog, and listen to the past still bouncing between the walls.

What lives there, then, is a species that has understood the central covenant of the place: that the world is not filled but framed, that biomass is less powerful than emptiness, and that survival belongs not to those who conquer the dark, but to those who learn to sing inside it. They are the soft percussion that asks the wood what it is, and they survive by trusting the answer that comes back from the void.
